Delhi High Court14 Jan 2026
ABC Constructions Pvt. Ltd. v. State Trading Corporation

Interest Awarded 'From the Date of Cause of Action' Cannot Exceed What Section 31(7) Actually Permits: Delhi HC

In a Section 34 challenge, the Delhi High Court partly set aside an arbitral award's interest component, holding that a tribunal awarding pendente lite and post-award interest under Section 31(7) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act must still operate within the statutory cap on post-award interest and cannot award a blanket, unexplained rate simply by invoking commercial reasonableness.

Key Takeaway — A tribunal has real discretion in fixing interest under Section 31(7), but that discretion is not unlimited; an unreasoned or excessive interest rate remains vulnerable to a Section 34 challenge even where the principal award itself is sound.
Supreme Court6 Feb 2026
Konkan Bridge Corporation v. Eastern Rail Freight Ltd.

A Sole-Arbitrator Clause Cannot Be Read as Requiring a Three-Member Tribunal Merely Because the Dispute Is Complex: Supreme Court

The Supreme Court set aside a High Court order that had, while appointing an arbitrator under Section 11, substituted a three-member tribunal for the sole-arbitrator mechanism the parties had actually agreed to, holding that a court's Section 11 power does not extend to rewriting the parties' own agreed composition of the tribunal, however complex the anticipated dispute.

Key Takeaway — Courts exercising Section 11 jurisdiction must appoint the tribunal the parties actually agreed to; a party wanting a multi-member tribunal for a complex dispute needs to negotiate that into the arbitration clause itself, not expect a court to supply it later.
Bombay High Court19 Feb 2026
Shree Industrial Fabricators v. Konkan Ferrous Ltd.

Confidentiality Under Section 42A Does Not Bar a Party From Referring to Award Findings in a Related Court Proceeding: Bombay HC

The Bombay High Court held that Section 42A's confidentiality obligation for arbitral proceedings does not prevent a party from referring to an arbitral award's findings in a separate, related court proceeding where doing so is reasonably necessary to protect or enforce the party's legal rights, distinguishing this from an unrestricted public disclosure of arbitration materials.

Key Takeaway — Section 42A protects arbitral proceedings from being publicised generally, but is not a blanket gag on referencing an award's findings where a party genuinely needs to do so to protect its rights in another proceeding; parties who want broader protection should build detailed confidentiality terms into the arbitration agreement itself.
Supreme Court4 Mar 2026
Union of India v. Gayatri Hydro Projects Ltd.

A Section 34 Court Has No General Power to Modify an Arbitral Award — It Can Only Set Aside, Wholly or in Part: Supreme Court

Resolving a long-running debate, a larger bench of the Supreme Court reaffirmed that a court exercising jurisdiction under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act does not have a general power to modify the substantive terms of an arbitral award, and is confined to either upholding the award or setting it aside, in whole or in severable part, on the recognised statutory grounds.

Key Takeaway — Parties seeking relief from an unfavourable award should not expect a Section 34 court to simply revise the figures or terms; where the award is legally vulnerable, the remedy is to have it set aside, which may mean the dispute effectively needs to be re-arbitrated rather than judicially corrected.
Karnataka High Court27 Mar 2026
Precision Auto Components v. Karnataka MSE Facilitation Council

An Award of the MSE Facilitation Council Under the MSMED Act Is Challenged Under Section 34 — Subject to the Act's Own 75% Pre-Deposit Condition: Karnataka HC

The Karnataka High Court dismissed a buyer's Section 34 petition against an award rendered by the state's Micro and Small Enterprise Facilitation Council under Section 18 of the MSMED Act, 2006, for want of the mandatory seventy-five percent pre-deposit required under Section 19 of the MSMED Act, rejecting the buyer's argument that ordinary Section 34 procedure alone should govern the challenge.

Key Takeaway — A buyer resisting an MSE Facilitation Council award cannot rely on the ordinary Section 34 procedure alone; the MSMED Act's own seventy-five percent pre-deposit condition under Section 19 applies on top of it, and a challenge filed without satisfying that condition will not be entertained.
Delhi High Court15 Apr 2026
Bhargava Township Developers v. Aditya Nirman Udyog

An Arbitrator Who Earlier Acted as Conciliator in the Same Dispute Is Disqualified Absent the Parties' Express Written Consent: Delhi HC

The Delhi High Court set aside the appointment of an arbitrator who had previously served as conciliator in the same dispute under a med-arb clause, holding that Section 80 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act bars a conciliator from acting as arbitrator in the same dispute unless the parties expressly agree otherwise in writing, and that this protection cannot be implied away merely because the med-arb clause itself contemplated a smooth transition.

Key Takeaway — Parties using a med-arb or arb-med hybrid clause should state expressly, in writing, whether the same neutral may move from conciliator to arbitrator; silence on the point, combined with Section 80's default bar, can result in an arbitrator's appointment being set aside altogether.
Supreme Court30 Apr 2026
Continental Retail Ventures v. Metro Junction Mall Pvt. Ltd.

A Multi-Tier Dispute Resolution Clause Requiring Prior Negotiation Is Not a Jurisdictional Bar to Arbitration Once Invoked: Supreme Court

The Supreme Court held that a multi-tier dispute resolution clause requiring parties to first attempt good-faith negotiation before invoking arbitration is directory rather than a strict jurisdictional precondition, and that a party's failure to exhaust the negotiation step does not render a subsequently commenced arbitration itself without jurisdiction, particularly where the other party had shown no genuine willingness to negotiate.

Key Takeaway — A multi-tier clause requiring negotiation before arbitration should still be followed in good faith, but a party is not permanently locked out of arbitration merely for moving to that stage without a fully documented negotiation record, particularly where the counterparty was unwilling to engage.
Delhi High Court18 May 2026
Vardhman Roadlines Pvt. Ltd. v. National Logistics Corporation

A Section 21 Notice Invoking Arbitration Must Be Issued Within the Same Three-Year Limitation Period That Would Apply to a Civil Suit: Delhi HC

The Delhi High Court held that the limitation period for issuing a notice invoking arbitration under Section 21 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act runs from the date the cause of action accrues, and is governed by the same three-year period under Article 137 of the Limitation Act, 1963 that would apply to a corresponding civil suit. A party cannot indefinitely defer invoking its arbitration clause once the underlying claim itself becomes time-barred.

Key Takeaway — Parties should treat the limitation clock for invoking arbitration the same way they would for filing a civil suit on the same cause of action; waiting too long to issue a Section 21 notice can result in the eventual claim being dismissed as time-barred, regardless of how clear the underlying arbitration clause is.
Delhi High Court9 Jun 2026
In re: Mediated Settlement Agreement, Rohan Distributors v. Sunrise Apparel Exports

A Mediated Settlement Agreement Under the Mediation Act, 2023 Is Enforceable as a Decree Without a Fresh Civil Suit: Delhi HC

The Delhi High Court held that a mediated settlement agreement registered under Section 20 of the Mediation Act, 2023, whether reached through court-annexed or private mediation, can be enforced directly as if it were a decree of a civil court, without the settling party being put to the burden of filing a fresh civil suit to enforce the underlying settlement terms.

Key Takeaway — A commercial dispute resolved through mediation and reduced to a mediated settlement agreement under the Mediation Act, 2023 gives the parties a direct, decree-like enforcement route; parties should ensure the settlement is properly registered to secure this enforcement advantage.
Bombay High Court24 Jun 2026
Novelty Prints Pvt. Ltd. v. Creative Canvas Studio

A Copyright Licensing Dispute Is Arbitrable Even Though Copyright Itself Is a Right in Rem: Bombay HC

The Bombay High Court held that a dispute over the terms of a copyright licence, as opposed to a challenge to the validity or subsistence of the copyright itself, is arbitrable, distinguishing between the underlying intellectual property right, which operates in rem and is not arbitrable, and a contractual licensing dispute between the specific parties, which operates in personam and can properly be resolved by arbitration.

Key Takeaway — A dispute over royalty, scope or breach of an IP licensing agreement remains arbitrable even though the underlying IP right itself is not; parties negotiating trademark, copyright or patent licences should include a properly drafted arbitration clause for such contractual disputes.
Delhi High CourtMar 11, 2026
Section 9 Relief — Post-Tribunal-Constitution Maintainability

Section 9 Application Does Not Automatically Become Infructuous Once the Tribunal Is Constituted

The Delhi High Court clarified that a Section 9 application pending before a court does not automatically lapse the moment the arbitral tribunal is constituted; the efficacy proviso under Section 9(3) requires a case-specific assessment of whether the tribunal remains an equally effective forum for the relief sought, rather than a blanket transfer of jurisdiction.

Key Takeaway — Parties should not assume a pending court application for interim relief is automatically extinguished by tribunal constitution, but should move promptly to Section 17 for any fresh relief once the tribunal is available.
Supreme CourtAug 4, 2026
Parsa Kente Collieries Ltd. v. Rajasthan Rajya Vidyut Utpadan Nigam Ltd.

Supreme Court Replaces Retired Justice as Sole Arbitrator by Consent in Adani-Rajasthan DISCOM Dispute

In a long-running power-sector arbitration between an Adani group company and a Rajasthan state DISCOM, the Supreme Court replaced retired Justice Arun Mishra as arbitrator, appointing Justice Sanjay Kishan Kaul as sole arbitrator by consent of the parties. The order underscores the Court's continued willingness to step in and reconstitute a tribunal where the original arbitrator is unable to continue, keeping a long-pending high-value dispute moving rather than requiring parties to restart appointment proceedings from scratch.

Key Takeaway — Where an arbitrator becomes unable to continue, parties should seek a consent-based replacement before the appointing court promptly — it preserves the arbitration's procedural history and avoids restarting the Section 11 process entirely.
Supreme Court2026 INSC
M/S Steag Energy Services (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. GSPC Pipavav Power Company Ltd.

Courts Must Exercise Restraint Reviewing Technical Tender & Contract-Award Decisions

Ruling on an O&M contract dispute for a gas-based power plant, the Supreme Court allowed the appeal and set aside the High Court's interference, holding that evaluation and award of complex technical contracts falls within the domain of the procuring authority and the competence of technical evaluators. Judicial interference is confined to cases of clear illegality, mala fides or irrationality.

Key Takeaway — Infrastructure, energy and EPC clients challenging or defending a tender award should frame grounds around illegality or perversity — courts will not re-run a technical evaluation on the merits.
Supreme CourtMay 26, 2026
Gujarat Water Supply and Sewerage Board v. Saryu Plastics Pvt. Ltd.

Participation in Proceedings Without Objection Waives Challenge to Arbitrator's Mandate

Justices Narasimha and Alok Aradhe upheld the validity of arbitral proceedings, holding that a party which participates in proceedings before an arbitral tribunal without raising a timely objection cannot subsequently challenge the arbitrator's mandate. The Court treated continued, unobjected participation as effective waiver of any procedural infirmity in the tribunal's constitution.

Key Takeaway — Parties must raise objections to an arbitrator's appointment or mandate at the earliest opportunity — silence coupled with continued participation will be treated as a waiver.
Supreme CourtApr 24, 2026
Home Care Retail Marts Pvt. Ltd. v. Haresh N. Sanghavi

Section 9 Interim Relief Is Available Post-Award, Even to a Non-Award Holder

Justices Manoj Misra and Manmohan held that either party to an arbitration may seek interim measures under Section 9 of the Arbitration Act at any stage of the proceedings, including after the award is passed. The Court clarified that the expression "a party" in Section 9 draws no distinction between the successful and unsuccessful party, so even a non-award holder may seek interim protection.

Key Takeaway — Award debtors are not without recourse post-award — Section 9 protection remains available to safeguard legitimate interests even after the tribunal has ruled.
Supreme CourtApr 15, 2026
J&K Economic Reconstruction Agency v. Rash Builders India — 2026 INSC 368

Seat of Arbitration Confers Exclusive Jurisdiction — Venue Is Merely a Convenient Location

The Supreme Court reaffirmed that the seat of arbitration is the juridical home of the arbitration, fixing the curial law and the court with exclusive supervisory jurisdiction once designated by the parties' agreement — to the exclusion of every other court, even one where part of the cause of action arose. The venue, by contrast, is only a geographical convenience and does not confer, alter, or determine jurisdiction.

Key Takeaway — Drafters must fix the seat with precision in the arbitration clause — once chosen, it alone governs supervisory jurisdiction regardless of where hearings are actually conducted.
Supreme Court — 2026 INSC 34Jan 7, 2026
Jan De Nul Dredging India Pvt. Ltd. v. Tuticorin Port Trust

Section 37 Appellate Scrutiny Cannot Exceed the Grounds Available Under Section 34

The Supreme Court held that jurisdiction under Section 37 mirrors that under Section 34 — courts hearing a Section 37 appeal do not sit as ordinary appellate courts and must not interfere with an award merely because an alternative view is possible. The appellate court's role is confined to verifying whether the Section 34 court acted within, exceeded, or failed to exercise its conferred powers.

Key Takeaway — Section 37 appeals remain a narrow supervisory check, not a fresh opportunity to reargue the merits already decided at the Section 34 stage.
Supreme Court — PendingJan 2026
Arbitration and Conciliation Bill, 2024 — Legislative Watch

Constitution Bench in Gayatri Balasamy Urges Expedited Passage of Arbitration & Conciliation Bill 2024

The five-judge bench in Gayatri Balasamy explicitly urged the Ministry of Law & Justice to expedite passage of the pending Arbitration and Conciliation Bill, 2024. The Bill proposes key amendments including mandatory timelines for arbitrator appointments, expanded institutional arbitration framework, provisions for emergency arbitrators, and updated enforcement procedures for foreign awards.

Key Takeaway — Practitioners must track the Bill closely — proposed changes to Sections 11, 29A, 34, and 37 will significantly alter the current arbitration landscape once enacted.
Himachal Pradesh High CourtDec 2025
NHAI v. Paras Ram — CMPMO Nos. 761–767 of 2025

Holding Hearings at a Location Does Not Fix It as the Seat of Arbitration

The Himachal Pradesh High Court, applying BALCO and BGS SGS SOMA, held that merely holding arbitration hearings at a location does not fix that place as the 'seat' of arbitration. Venue and seat are legally distinct. The first-filing rule under Section 42 locked in jurisdiction at the court having supervisory jurisdiction over the contractually specified seat — not the hearing venue.

Key Takeaway — Infrastructure and government contracting parties must explicitly agree on the seat of arbitration in contracts — the hearing venue will not determine which court exercises supervisory jurisdiction.
Supreme CourtOct 2025
Tamil Nadu Cements Corporation v. MSEFC — Writ Jurisdiction

Writ Under Article 226 Maintainable Against MSEFC Where Order Is Passed Without Jurisdiction or Is a Nullity

The Supreme Court held that a writ petition under Article 226 is maintainable against an order of the Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Council (MSEFC) under Section 18 of the MSMED Act where the order is passed without jurisdiction, contrary to statutory procedure, or amounts to a nullity. While the MSMED Act provides a statutory remedy under Section 34/19, this does not bar exceptional writ jurisdiction.

Key Takeaway — MSME parties facing jurisdictionally infirm MSEFC orders now have a clearly preserved writ remedy — they need not proceed through the entire statutory appeal chain before seeking High Court intervention.
Bombay High Court — Division BenchSep 2025
IMAX Corp v. E-City Entertainment

Non-Parties Impleaded at Enforcement Stage — Corporate Veil Pierced to Prevent Award Evasion

The Bombay High Court held that where an affiliate or related entity benefited from a contract that is the subject of an arbitral award, non-parties to the arbitration may be impleaded at the enforcement and execution stage by piercing the corporate veil — preventing evasion of legal obligations through separate corporate structures.

Key Takeaway — Group companies cannot assume automatic protection from arbitral awards by being separate legal entities — enforcement courts will pierce the veil where affiliated entities benefited from the underlying contract.
Delhi High CourtAug 2025
Engineering Projects India v. MSA Global LLC Oman — CS(OS) 243/2025

Rare Anti-Arbitration Injunction Granted Against Foreign Arbitration for Undisclosed Conflict

The Delhi High Court granted an anti-arbitration injunction against ICC arbitration proceedings in Singapore, citing an arbitrator's undisclosed conflict of interest. The court invoked Section 34(2)(b)(ii) (public policy) and held that vexatious continuation of proceedings before a tribunal with an undisclosed conflict warrants intervention, even at a foreign seat, in extraordinary circumstances.

Key Takeaway — Parties must conduct rigorous arbitrator due diligence before constituting a tribunal — undisclosed conflicts of interest can result in costly injunctive intervention even in foreign-seated arbitrations.
Supreme CourtJul 2025
Glencore International AG v. Shree Ganesh Metals — 2025 SCC OnLine SC 1815

Unsigned Arbitration Agreement Is Binding if Parties Act on It Through Correspondence and Conduct

The Supreme Court held that parties who negotiated a contract through email and then acted on its terms — including an embedded arbitration clause — were bound by that clause even though the contract was never formally signed. The exchange of electronic correspondence and the parties' conduct evidenced acceptance of the contract terms including the arbitration agreement.

Key Takeaway — Businesses that execute contracts via email exchanges and act on them must be aware that embedded arbitration clauses are enforceable even without a formal signature.
Supreme Court — ASF BuildtechJun 2025
ASF Buildtech Pvt. Ltd. v. Shapoorji Pallonji — 2025 INSC 616

Arbitral Tribunal Can Itself Implead a Non-Signatory Under the Group of Companies Doctrine

The Supreme Court held that an arbitral tribunal has the inherent power to implead a non-signatory to arbitration proceedings by applying the Group of Companies doctrine — based on common control, management overlap, and integrated contract performance within a corporate group. This power is exercisable by the tribunal without requiring a fresh court referral under Section 11.

Key Takeaway — Corporate group entities must carefully assess whether they can be brought into arbitration without their consent — common control and integrated operations are sufficient basis for tribunal-initiated impleadment.
Supreme CourtMay 7, 2025
Tata Steel v. Raj Kumar Banerjee — 2025 SCC OnLine SC 1042

IBC Section 61(2): NCLAT Has No Jurisdiction to Condone Delay Beyond the 45-Day Outer Limit

The Supreme Court set aside an NCLAT order that had permitted an appeal to be filed 46 days after the NCLT order — one day beyond the outer limit. The Court held that Section 61(2) of the IBC prescribes a mandatory outer limit of 45 days (30 + 15) for filing appeals. The NCLAT, being a creature of statute, cannot assume equitable or inherent jurisdiction to override this express legislative limit. The CIRP of Rohit Ferro-Tech Limited was affected.

Key Takeaway — IBC practitioners must calendar Section 61 appeal deadlines with extreme precision — the 45-day outer limit is an absolute bar and even a single day's delay is fatal.
Supreme Court — Constitution Bench (4:1)Apr 30, 2025
Gayatri Balasamy v. ISG Novasoft Technologies — 2025 INSC 1057

Constitution Bench Holds: Courts Have Limited Power to Modify Arbitral Awards Under Sections 34 & 37

A five-judge bench by a 4:1 majority, authored by CJI Sanjiv Khanna, held that courts have a limited inherent power to modify arbitral awards under Sections 34 and 37 of the Arbitration Act. Modification is permissible to: sever legally separable invalid portions; correct obvious clerical, typographical, or computational errors; and adjust post-award interest rates in appropriate cases. The majority applied the principle omne majus continet in se minus — the power to set aside includes the lesser power to partially set aside. Courts cannot re-evaluate merits or rewrite the award. Justice K.V. Viswanathan dissented, holding that Section 34 permits only 'setting aside' and that modification powers were deliberately excluded from the 1996 Act to align with the UNCITRAL Model Law.

Key Takeaway — Courts may now retain valid parts of awards by severing defective portions — this avoids fresh arbitration over minor errors and reduces delay and cost in post-award proceedings.
Supreme CourtApr 17, 2025
Adavya Projects v. M/s Vishal Structurals — 2025 INSC 507

Whether a Non-Signatory Is Bound by an Arbitration Agreement Is for the Tribunal to Decide Conclusively

The Supreme Court clarified the Group of Companies doctrine: the referral court under Sections 8 and 11 makes only a prima facie finding on whether a non-signatory is involved — it is left to the arbitral tribunal to conclusively decide whether the non-signatory is bound by the arbitration agreement. Non-joinder at the Section 11 stage does not prevent later impleadment by the tribunal.

Key Takeaway — The arbitral tribunal, not the referral court, is the proper forum for finally deciding whether group entities or related parties are bound by an arbitration agreement.
Supreme CourtApr 2, 2025
M/s Ferro Concrete Construction v. State of Rajasthan — 2025 INSC 429

Contractual Bar on Interest Operates Differently Under the 1996 Arbitration Act vs. 1940 Act

The Supreme Court restored an arbitrator's discretion to award pendente lite interest, holding that a general 'no interest' clause in a contract does not prevent an arbitrator under the 1996 Act from awarding interest unless the contract expressly prohibits it. The court awarded 9% pendente lite interest, distinguishing the stricter position under the 1940 Act.

Key Takeaway — A general 'no interest' clause may not suffice to bar a pendente lite interest award — only an express, specific prohibition will achieve that under the 1996 Act.
Supreme CourtMar 18, 2025
Disortho S.A.S. v. Meril Life Sciences — 2025 INSC 352

Venue of Arbitration Does Not Determine the Law Governing the Arbitration Agreement

The Supreme Court held that the contract must be read as a whole. Where the contract designated Colombian law for arbitration proceedings but Indian law as the lex contractus, Indian courts retained supervisory jurisdiction. The venue of arbitration (Bogotá) did not determine the governing law of the arbitration agreement. In the absence of an express choice, the lex contractus ordinarily governs the arbitration agreement.

Key Takeaway — Parties must expressly specify the law governing the arbitration agreement itself — absent such a choice, the law of the underlying contract will govern it.
Supreme Court — 5-Judge BenchFeb 2025
Gayatri Balasamy v. ISG Novasoft Technologies — Hearing Commenced

5-Judge Constitution Bench Commences Hearing on Whether Courts Can Modify Arbitral Awards

CJI Sanjiv Khanna led a five-judge Constitution Bench commencing detailed arguments on the foundational question: can Indian courts modify (not merely set aside) arbitral awards under Sections 34 and 37 of the Arbitration Act? The bench identified two conflicting lines of authority — NHAI v. M. Hakeem (no modification) versus Vedanta/Tata Hydroelectric (modification permitted) — and reserved judgment after three detailed hearing sessions.

Key Takeaway — The Constitution Bench proceeding put all pending Section 34 modification applications on hold — practitioners awaited clarity on this pivotal question.
Supreme CourtJan 31, 2025
M/s C&C Constructions v. IRCON International — 2025 INSC 138

Section 37 Appellate Jurisdiction Cannot Exceed the Scope Prescribed Under Section 34

The Supreme Court clarified that the appellate jurisdiction under Section 37 of the Arbitration Act is narrowly circumscribed and cannot exceed the limits under Section 34. An appellate court under Section 37 cannot independently assess the merits of an award; it is confined to examining whether the court below acted within Section 34's bounds.

Key Takeaway — Parties attempting to re-argue merits at the Section 37 stage will be firmly shut out — Section 37 is not a second bite at the cherry.
Supreme CourtJan 3, 2025
Serosoft Solutions v. Dexter Capital Advisors — 2025 INSC 26

HC Writ Interference With Arbitral Orders Requires Perversity Staring on the Face of the Record

The Supreme Court held that interference by High Courts under Arts. 226/227 with arbitral tribunal orders is permissible only where the order is completely perverse and that perversity is evident on the face of the record. The Court emphasised that High Courts must actively discourage satellite litigation that disrupts the arbitral process — excessive judicial intervention is antithetical to arbitration's efficiency and finality.

Key Takeaway — Routine writ challenges to arbitral orders will be dismissed at the threshold; the bar for HC intervention is now very high.
Supreme Court — 2026 INSC 976Sep 8, 2026
GVV Constructions Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India & Ors.

Whether a Claim Falls Within an 'Excepted Matters' Clause Is for the Arbitral Tribunal, Not a Section 11 Court

The Supreme Court held that whether a particular claim in a works contract is covered by, or falls outside, an "excepted matters" clause is a question squarely within the competence of the Arbitral Tribunal under Section 16 — not one for a court exercising appointment-stage jurisdiction under Section 11. The Court also held that a writ appellate court cannot pre-determine arbitral jurisdiction or restrict a tribunal's Section 16 inquiry, including into the validity of a contract's termination.

Key Takeaway — Parties resisting arbitration on an 'excepted matters' objection should raise it before the tribunal itself under Section 16 — courts at the referral or appointment stage will not pre-decide the question.
Delhi High Court — Division BenchSep 2, 2026
Jindal ITF Limited v. NTPC Limited

Split Verdict Leaves Rs. 1,889 Crore Arbitral Award Against NTPC in Limbo, Referred to a Third Judge

Justices C. Hari Shankar and Om Prakash Shukla delivered a split verdict on the validity of a roughly Rs. 1,889 crore award in favour of Jindal ITF against NTPC for wrongful termination of a coal-transportation agreement for the Farakka Thermal Power Plant — one Judge would have upheld the tribunal's findings, the other held the award could not be sustained. Referred to the Chief Justice for a third-judge or larger Bench.

Key Takeaway — A single-forum appeal can leave a large infrastructure-sector arbitral award in limbo for years — parties tracking NTPC's or Jindal ITF's exposure should watch for the third-judge reference outcome.
Bombay High CourtMar 9, 2026
Arenel Private Limited v. M/s. Aakash Packaging

An Award Relying on Irrelevant Material Over the Parties' Own Evidence Can Be Set Aside: Bombay High Court

A Division Bench of the Bombay High Court held that an arbitral award that relies on irrelevant material or ignores relevant evidence on record violates fundamental principles of justice and can be set aside under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, upholding a Single Judge's decision that had set aside part of a December 2019 award in a packaging-materials supply dispute for reliance on hearsay over the parties' own laboratory reports.

Key Takeaway — Reinforces that Indian courts will look past an arbitrator's findings of fact where the award is shown to rest on irrelevant material or to have disregarded directly relevant documentary evidence such as contemporaneous lab reports - a useful precedent for parties challenging awards on the patent-illegality/fundamental-justice ground.
Supreme Court — 2026 INSC 112Feb 3, 2026
C. Velusamy v. K. Indhera

A Tribunal's Mandate Can Still Be Extended Under Section 29A Even After an Award Is Passed Post-Expiry: Supreme Court

A Bench of Justices Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha and Atul S. Chandurkar held that an application under Section 29A(5) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 to extend an arbitral tribunal's mandate is maintainable even after expiry of the statutory time limit under Sections 29A(1) and (3), and even where an award has already been rendered during the interim period. The Court noted that an award passed after the mandate has expired is unenforceable and non est, but that an arbitrator's error in passing such an award does not deprive the court of jurisdiction to entertain a later extension application.

Key Takeaway — Parties whose arbitral tribunal issues an award after its Section 29A mandate has technically lapsed are not left without a remedy - they can still apply to extend the mandate after the fact, rather than being forced to restart arbitration from scratch.
Delhi High CourtJul 23, 2026
Spectrum Power Generation Limited v. GAIL (India) Limited

An Arbitral Award Built on Inadmissible Evidence and Flawed Waiver/Estoppel Reasoning Is Set Aside: Delhi High Court

The Delhi High Court set aside an arbitral award that had rejected Spectrum Power Generation's claim for refund of GST reimbursed to GAIL (India) Limited on transmission charges under a Gas Sale Agreement, holding that the award was vitiated by patent illegality because it rested on inadmissible evidence and an erroneous application of the doctrines of waiver and estoppel.

Key Takeaway — Reinforces that an arbitral tribunal's findings on waiver, estoppel and its treatment of evidence remain open to Section 34 scrutiny where they are perverse or legally unsound, giving parties a further avenue to challenge awards resting on flawed evidentiary reasoning.
Delhi High CourtSep 17, 2026
Steel Authority of India Limited v. British Marine PLC

Ruling on the Same Clause in an Earlier, Unrelated Arbitration Doesn't Disqualify an Arbitrator: Delhi High Court

A Division Bench of Justices Prathiba M. Singh and Vikas Mahajan dismissed SAIL's challenge to an arbitral award in favour of British Marine, rejecting SAIL's argument that two tribunal members were disqualified merely because they had previously interpreted the same contractual clause (Clause 62) in a separate maritime arbitration involving SAIL. The Court held that such 'issue conflict' does not by itself give rise to justifiable doubts about independence or impartiality under Section 12 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, and upheld the tribunal's damages assessment based on the differential between contract and spot market freight rates.

Key Takeaway — Parties in specialised sectors like shipping and commodities, where the pool of experienced arbitrators is limited, cannot automatically disqualify an arbitrator simply because they previously ruled on the same standard clause in an unrelated dispute.
Delhi High CourtSep 21, 2026
Rajesh Kumar v. Union of India & Ors.

Section 38 Has No Indigence Exception — Arbitral Fees Cannot Be Waived for Financial Incapacity

After an arbitrator was appointed under Section 11 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 through the Delhi International Arbitration Centre, the arbitral proceedings were terminated because the petitioner could not pay the DIAC's fees; the petitioner sought a judicial waiver or deferment of arbitral fees on grounds of financial incapacity, and a direction to frame a support mechanism for indigent claimants. The Delhi High Court dismissed the petitions, holding that Section 38 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act does not contemplate any exception for a party's financial incapacity, that the statutory framework permits suspension or termination of proceedings when the required deposit is unpaid without carving out an indigence exception, and that creating financial-support mechanisms for indigent arbitration parties is a policy matter for the legislature, not the courts.

Key Takeaway — A claimant or counter-claimant who cannot afford an arbitral tribunal's fees has no judicial recourse to a fee waiver or deferment under the current law — arbitral proceedings can lawfully terminate for non-payment regardless of the party's financial capacity, until Parliament legislates otherwise.
Delhi High CourtSep 10, 2026
Eco Green Buildtech Pvt. Ltd. v. Vikartan Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd.

Admissions Made During Failed Conciliation Can't Decide the Arbitration, and an Arbitrator's 'Experience' Isn't Proof of Loss: Delhi HC

After the wife obtained a decree for restitution of conjugal rights in her favour but the husband did not comply, and the husband resisted her later maintenance claim on the ground that she held a postgraduate qualification, Justice Urmila Joshi-Phalke held that a wife cannot be denied maintenance under Section 125 CrPC merely for being highly educated, absent evidence she is actually earning or capable of self-support, particularly given high unemployment. The Court further held that mere non-compliance with a restitution-of-conjugal-rights decree does not by itself disentitle a wife from maintenance, and that 'refusal' under Section 125(4) CrPC requires a genuine offer by the husband to resume cohabitation and support her, not a bare invitation.

Key Takeaway — A wife's higher educational qualifications alone cannot be used to deny her maintenance where she is not actually earning, and a husband cannot defeat a maintenance claim merely by pointing to an unenforced restitution decree without having made a genuine, demonstrable offer to resume and support the marriage.

Sonal Talpada v. Veerbhan Singh

Fifteen Years of Separation and Persistent Refusal of Conjugal Relations Can Itself Be Mental Cruelty: Supreme Court

Two medical professionals with no children had lived separately for nearly 15 years with no effort at reconciliation; the husband sought divorce under Section 13(1)(ia) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 on the ground of mental cruelty, which the Family Court declined but the Rajasthan High Court granted. Justices Sanjay Karol and Augustine George Masih upheld the divorce, holding that a prolonged period of separation, and persistent refusal of conjugal relations without justification, can itself be treated as an indicator of mental cruelty under Section 13(1)(ia), while cautioning that a party must not be permitted to profit from their own unilateral desertion.

Key Takeaway — A spouse who has lived separately for many years without genuine attempts at reconciliation can point to that prolonged separation itself as evidence of mental cruelty to secure a divorce, even where the other spouse still wants the marriage to continue.

A Husband's High Income Alone Doesn't Fix Interim Maintenance — the Wife's Own Income and Qualifications Count Too: Delhi HC

The parties married in November 1995 and have two children; on the wife's 2021 maintenance application, the Family Court granted interim maintenance of Rs. 25,000 per month, later enhanced to Rs. 30,000 per month in June 2024. Justice Saurabh Banerjee reduced the interim maintenance back to Rs. 25,000 per month, holding that even where the husband is a man of means, a court fixing interim maintenance cannot be oblivious to the wife's own independent circumstances — here, her MBA Finance qualification, rental income, and fixed-deposit interest — and must strike a balance between the parties' respective rights and liabilities rather than looking at the husband's income in isolation.

Key Takeaway — A husband's high income alone does not fix the interim maintenance amount — courts must weigh the wife's own independent income, assets and qualifications, so a paying spouse has a concrete basis to seek a reduction where the recipient has undisclosed or under-weighed income of her own.

An Allegation That the Wife's Earlier Marriage Was Still Subsisting Doesn't, By Itself, Defeat Her Maintenance Claim: P&H HC

A man challenged a Family Court, Fatehabad order directing him to pay monthly maintenance of Rs. 7,500 (Rs. 5,000 to the woman and Rs. 2,500 to their minor son) on the ground that the woman's earlier marriage was allegedly still subsisting when she married him. Justice Mandeep Pannu dismissed the revision petition, holding that an objection regarding the alleged subsistence of a woman's earlier marriage, by itself, cannot defeat her claim for maintenance under Section 125 CrPC, particularly where the parties had lived together as husband and wife for a considerable period and a child was born of the relationship.

Key Takeaway — A husband resisting a maintenance claim cannot rely merely on an allegation that his wife's prior marriage was still subsisting — where the couple genuinely lived together as spouses and had a child, that objection alone will not defeat her maintenance claim, and strict proof of the earlier marriage's legal status is not required to sustain the claim.
