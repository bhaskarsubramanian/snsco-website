Cross-Border Child Custody Between India and the USA, Canada or the UK

India has not signed the Hague Convention on child abduction. For a parent on either side of a custody dispute with an Indian and a Western connection, that one fact changes almost every strategic choice that follows.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

When a marriage with one Indian and one foreign-based parent, or two Indian parents settled abroad, breaks down, the custody fight rarely stays inside one country's courts. A parent brings a child to India, or keeps a child in the USA, Canada or the UK after a visit, and the other parent is left asking which country's courts can actually do anything about it. The honest answer starts with the fact that India has never joined the 1980 Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, which puts an Indian court in a genuinely different position from a court in any of the three countries this piece covers, all of which are Hague Convention members. What follows is how Indian courts actually approach these disputes, what weight they give a foreign custody order, and what a parent on either side needs to do differently because of where India stands.

Key points

  • India has not acceded to the 1980 Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, despite a 2009 Law Commission recommendation to do so, which means there is no treaty mechanism requiring an Indian court to summarily return a child brought to India from a Convention country.
  • An Indian court faced with a child brought from abroad, or kept in India after a visit, does not ask whether the child was wrongfully removed in the Convention sense. It asks what the child's welfare requires, treating that question as paramount over any foreign order, under the parens patriae jurisdiction long recognised in Indian family law.
  • Habeas corpus under Article 226 or Article 32 is the practical remedy a parent in India actually uses, but the Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that it is a summary remedy meant to test whether a child's custody is lawful, not a substitute for a full custody trial, and courts shift to an elaborate welfare inquiry whenever the facts call for one.
  • A foreign custody order carries real weight but is never automatically binding on an Indian court. Its persuasive value depends on how recently it was made, whether it followed a full and fair inquiry into the child's welfare, and how deep the child's own roots in India have since become.
  • Some Indian courts have begun accepting undertakings from a parent relocating abroad with a child, functioning in practice like the mirror orders used in Hague Convention jurisdictions, though Indian courts remain explicit that such directions bind only the specific case and do not set a general rule for others.
  • A minor's passport generally needs both parents' consent under the Passport Manual, though an applicant can proceed on an affidavit in defined circumstances, and Indian courts can direct the passport authority to issue or withhold a passport once a custody dispute is pending before them.
  • Nothing in Indian law stops a parent from filing in India while a Hague petition or custody case proceeds abroad, and Indian courts have shown no reluctance to continue hearing a habeas corpus petition on its own terms even while a foreign Hague proceeding is live elsewhere.
  • A parent planning to relocate with a child, or contesting a relocation, should treat the Indian welfare inquiry and the foreign court's own custody proceeding as two separate battles that both need to be fought properly, because winning one does not guarantee anything in the other.

Why this is not simply a custody dispute with a longer plane ticket

A domestic custody dispute between two parents living in the same city turns on welfare factors a family court is well placed to assess directly: schooling, each parent's home, the child's own preference once old enough to express one, and so on. A cross-border dispute between India and the USA, Canada or the UK adds a layer that has nothing to do with parenting quality: which country's court gets to decide the question at all, and what happens to an order one country's court has already made once the child, or the other parent, crosses into the other country's territory.

In Hague Convention countries, that jurisdictional layer is resolved by treaty. A parent who wrongfully removes or retains a child away from the country of the child's habitual residence faces a summary return proceeding in the country the child was taken to, and the court there generally does not decide the underlying custody dispute at all, leaving that for the courts of the child's habitual residence once the child is returned. None of that machinery exists between India and any of the three countries covered here, because India has never ratified the Convention, and that absence is the starting point for everything that follows.

The three countries discussed in this piece, the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom, are themselves all Hague Convention members and apply the Convention's return mechanism between each other and with every other member state. That makes India's position genuinely unusual from the perspective of a parent based in any of these three countries, because the legal toolkit that parent would use without a second thought in a dispute with, say, a parent in France or Australia simply is not available once the other parent and child are in India instead.

Why India has stayed outside the Hague Convention

India's own Law Commission recommended accession to the Convention in its 218th Report in 2009, and a draft Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction Bill was prepared in 2016 to give the Convention domestic effect. That bill was never enacted, and reporting from the period indicates the government chose not to proceed with it. Commentary on why centres on a concern that a strict return obligation could work against Indian parents, disproportionately mothers, who leave an abusive or unhappy foreign marriage and return to India with a child, since a summary return order under the Convention does not examine whether the parent seeking return was herself a safe custodian, only whether the removal was wrongful under the law of the child's habitual residence.

Whatever the merits of that policy position, its practical consequence has not changed by the current year: there is still no treaty obligation binding an Indian court to return a child to the USA, Canada or the UK simply because a foreign court or a foreign parent asserts the child was wrongfully brought to or kept in India. Each case is decided on its own facts under Indian domestic law, and a parent on either side of such a dispute needs to plan around that reality rather than assume Convention-style rules will apply by analogy.

The welfare principle as the only real compass

Indian family law treats a child's welfare as the paramount consideration in any custody matter, domestic or cross-border, a position rooted in Section 6 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 for Hindu families and in the court's own parens patriae jurisdiction more generally for others, including under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890. In a cross-border case, this means an Indian court asks what arrangement actually serves this particular child's welfare today, considering the child's age, the length of time already spent in India, schooling and social ties built up here, and each parent's practical capacity to care for the child, rather than mechanically enforcing whatever a foreign court decided before the child arrived in India.

Courts applying this standard in a cross-border setting look at a cluster of practical factors rather than any single determinative one: the child's age and corresponding capacity to express a genuine preference, the stability and suitability of each parent's home and immediate support network, each parent's financial and practical ability to provide for the child's day-to-day needs and education, any history of neglect, violence or substance abuse on either side, and, increasingly, the realistic prospect that the child can maintain a meaningful relationship with the parent who does not get primary custody, wherever that parent happens to live. None of these factors is weighted mechanically against the others, and a court's assessment of how they interact in a particular family is necessarily fact-specific rather than formulaic.

This welfare-first approach cuts in different directions depending on which side of the dispute a parent sits on. A parent who brought the child to India some years ago and can show the child has since built a genuine, settled life here, school, friends, extended family, stands on considerably stronger ground than a parent who did so weeks or months before the matter reaches an Indian court, because the longer the child's Indian roots, the less an Indian court is inclined to treat an abrupt move back to the other country as being in the child's interest, even where the original removal looks questionable on the facts.

Habeas corpus: the remedy of first resort, and its real limits

A parent in India seeking custody of a child from the other parent, including in a cross-border setting, typically moves a High Court under Article 226, or in some circumstances the Supreme Court under Article 32, by way of a habeas corpus petition asserting that the child's custody with the other parent or a third party is unlawful. The Supreme Court has repeatedly stressed in this line of cases, including in Nithya Anand Raghavan v. State of NCT of Delhi (2017) 8 SCC 454, that the principal question in a habeas corpus petition concerning a minor's custody is whether the custody is lawful, and that the proceeding is meant to be a summary one, not a substitute for a full trial on welfare.

That summary character has a well-recognised exception. Courts distinguish between a summary inquiry, appropriate where the child has only recently been brought into the jurisdiction and the facts are reasonably clear, and an elaborate inquiry into welfare, which becomes necessary once the child has lived in India long enough, or the facts are contested enough, that a snap decision on a writ petition would not do justice to the question. This distinction traces back to the Supreme Court's earlier decision in Dhanwanti Joshi v. Madhav Unde (1998) 1 SCC 112, and later decisions, including Sri Nilanjan Bhattacharya v. State of Karnataka and Others (2020) 14 SCR 445, have reaffirmed that habeas corpus in this context is a constitutional remedy for testing unlawful detention with reference to the child's welfare, not a mechanism for mechanically executing a foreign custody decree.

In practice, this means a parent who files a habeas corpus petition expecting a quick, Hague-style return order is likely to be disappointed if the facts are genuinely contested. The High Court will often convert the proceeding, formally or in substance, into a more searching welfare inquiry once it becomes clear that the child's custody and future cannot fairly be decided on the limited record a habeas corpus petition typically produces at the outset.

What weight does a foreign custody order actually carry?

A foreign court's custody order, whether from the USA, Canada or the UK, is not binding on an Indian court the way it would be on a court within the same Hague Convention framework, but it is far from irrelevant. The leading authority remains V. Ravi Chandran v. Union of India (2009) 15 SCR 960, where the Supreme Court, dealing with a habeas corpus petition brought by an American father whose son had been brought to India, held that a foreign custody order can be given due weight, with its persuasive effect depending on the circumstances of the particular case, including how recently the foreign order was made and how substantial an inquiry the foreign court itself conducted into the child's welfare before making it.

This produces a sliding scale rather than a fixed rule. A fresh, well-reasoned foreign custody order made after a proper adversarial hearing, where the child has spent only a short time in India since, will usually carry significant weight with an Indian court, because little has changed on the ground to justify departing from a considered foreign assessment of the child's welfare. An older foreign order, or one made without the other parent having had a real opportunity to be heard, carries far less weight, particularly once the child has spent a substantial period in India and built a life here that the foreign court never had occasion to consider.

A foreign order obtained ex parte, without the other parent having participated at all, generally carries the least weight of all, and Indian courts examining such orders have shown themselves willing to look behind the label and ask whether the absent parent was genuinely given a fair chance to be heard before the order was made, rather than accept the order's existence as proof of a fair process. This is consistent with the broader theme running through this whole area of law: an Indian court treats a foreign custody order as useful evidence of what another court, with its own access to the facts at a particular point in time, concluded, not as a command it is obliged to carry out.

Mirror orders and undertakings: a cautious, case-by-case practice

In many Hague Convention jurisdictions, a parent permitted to relocate with a child can ask the court in the receiving country to issue a mirror order, essentially replicating the terms of the custody arrangement so that both countries' courts are, on paper, saying the same thing, which gives the left-behind parent some confidence that the arrangement will be respected on the other side of the border. Indian courts have no formal, named procedure called a mirror order, but the substance of the idea has shown up in Indian cases through undertakings a parent gives the Indian court before being permitted to take a child abroad, or conditions attached to permission granted to a parent to relocate.

The Supreme Court's decision in Yashita Sahu v. State of Rajasthan (2020) 3 SCC 67 illustrates how this plays out. The dispute arose after the parents, married in India, moved to the United States, where their daughter was born, and a US court (the Norfolk court) had made a custody-related order before the matter reached the Rajasthan High Court and then the Supreme Court by way of habeas corpus. The Supreme Court was careful to note that its own directions in that matter were made for the purpose of disposing of that particular petition and were not meant to bind any other court in any other case, a caution that recurs across this line of authority and that any parent relying on a reported precedent needs to take seriously: these orders are fact-specific, not general rules a later parent can simply invoke by citing the case name.

For a parent negotiating or litigating a relocation, the practical upshot is that an Indian court can be asked to make its permission conditional, for instance on the relocating parent giving an undertaking about visitation, communication or a right to seek the Indian court's further directions if circumstances in the foreign country change. These undertakings function informally as something close to a mirror order, though without the same institutional backing a true Hague Convention mirror order carries between two Convention states, and their value still depends heavily on whether the foreign court is willing to recognise and give effect to them once the parent and child actually arrive there.

Where a custody case can even be filed

Before any of the welfare analysis above becomes relevant, a parent has to establish that an Indian court has jurisdiction to hear the matter at all. Under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, a guardianship petition is generally filed in the district court within whose jurisdiction the minor ordinarily resides, which in a cross-border family usually means wherever the child is actually living at the time the petition is filed, rather than the country of the child's birth or either parent's own domicile or citizenship. This has an important practical consequence: a parent who brings a child to India and keeps the child here for even a modest period can often establish that an Indian court has jurisdiction simply because the child now ordinarily resides in India, which is precisely the feature of Indian jurisdiction rules that a parent abroad finds frustrating and a parent in India can use to advantage.

A habeas corpus petition works slightly differently, since it is not strictly a guardianship proceeding but a constitutional remedy addressed to whichever High Court has territorial jurisdiction over the person said to be unlawfully detaining the child, typically the place where the child is currently physically present. In practice, the two routes often converge on the same forum, the High Court or district court of the place where the child currently is, which is itself a reason why the location of the child at the time proceedings are filed matters so much in these disputes.

To pre-empt this dynamic, a parent abroad sometimes files a guardianship or custody petition in their own country before the other parent can establish settled residence for the child in India, on the theory that an early foreign order, even one India will not treat as binding, strengthens the overall record and gives the Indian court something concrete to weigh when it eventually looks at the question. This is a reasonable tactical step, but it should not be mistaken for a solution on its own, since an Indian court remains free to look past a foreign order entirely once enough time has passed and the child's circumstances in India have genuinely changed.

How much weight a child's own wishes carry, and the limits of doing this by video

Indian courts do take a child's own preference into account once the child is old enough to form and express an intelligible view, but they are notably cautious about how that preference is gathered and how much weight it should carry against the overall welfare assessment. The Supreme Court's decision in Sheetal Vasant Thakur v. Chirag Arora (SLP(C) Nos. 18701-18702 of 2024, decided 11 June 2026, reported as 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 618) set out a detailed set of principles on when and how a child should be subjected to a psychological evaluation in a custody or visitation dispute, holding that such evaluations should not be ordered routinely given the risk of secondary trauma to the child, that a court must record specific reasons before ordering one, and that the process should remain as neutral and minimally intrusive as possible. The Court was explicit that its observations were not meant as a rigid or exhaustive checklist binding every future case in the same terms.

A related practical difficulty arises wherever a child or a parent is abroad and a hearing is conducted, or proposed to be conducted, by video conference. Indian courts have accepted video-conference testimony from witnesses abroad since the Supreme Court's decision in State of Maharashtra v. Dr. Praful B. Desai, and remote hearings have become considerably more routine since the pandemic years, but courts dealing specifically with a child's wishes in a custody matter have noted that gauging a child's genuine preference, rather than a rehearsed or coached answer, is a task that becomes materially harder over a video link than in person, which is one more reason Indian courts tend to prefer, wherever practicable, that a child physically present in India be assessed in India rather than have the exercise conducted remotely from abroad.

Costs and timelines: what a parent should realistically expect

Timelines vary enormously by how contested the matter becomes. A habeas corpus petition can move quickly where the facts are straightforward, sometimes producing an initial hearing and interim directions within weeks of filing, but a genuinely contested cross-border custody matter, particularly one that shifts into an elaborate welfare inquiry, routinely runs for many months and not infrequently for more than a year once psychological evaluation, home study reports, and appeals are factored in. A parent based abroad should expect to need reliable Indian counsel who can appear and act without the parent's constant physical presence, supplemented by video-conference appearances where the court permits them, and should budget for the genuine possibility of the matter going up to the High Court and, in some cases, the Supreme Court before it is finally resolved.

Cost estimates are difficult to generalise because they depend heavily on how contested the matter becomes and how many expert reports and interim applications are needed along the way, but a parent should go in expecting a cross-border custody dispute to cost meaningfully more than an equivalent domestic one, if only because of the added expense of coordinating evidence, witnesses and sometimes documents from a foreign country into an Indian court record in a form the court will accept.

Passports and the practical mechanics of travel

A minor's Indian passport application generally requires the consent of both parents under the Passport Manual, a requirement aimed precisely at preventing one parent from removing a child from the country, or bringing a child into India and then obtaining travel documents, without the other parent's knowledge. Where one parent cannot or will not give consent, a passport can still be issued in defined circumstances on the basis of an affidavit, commonly described by reference to the relevant annexure in the Passport Manual, from the applying parent explaining the absence of the other parent's consent, though the passport authority and the courts have taken varying positions on exactly when that affidavit route is available, and the position is best confirmed with current Passport Manual guidance and local practice rather than assumed.

Where a custody dispute is already before a court, Indian courts have shown themselves willing to step in directly, either directing the passport authority to issue a passport to a parent who has custody, or directing that no passport be issued, or an existing one be impounded, where there is a real risk of a child being taken out of the jurisdiction improperly. A parent who anticipates a passport dispute, in either direction, is generally better served by raising it squarely before the court hearing the custody matter than by dealing with the passport authority in isolation, since a court order gives the passport authority a far clearer basis to act than an unresolved family dispute does on its own.

A parent whose child already holds a foreign passport, from the USA, Canada or the UK, faces a related but distinct concern: an Indian court order restraining departure from India has no direct power to stop an airline or immigration counter outside India from honouring that foreign document, so the practical protection in that scenario usually comes from an Indian lookout notice or similar coordination with Indian immigration and airport authorities at the point of exit, rather than from anything addressed to the foreign passport itself.

Running parallel proceedings: India and the foreign Hague petition at the same time

Where a child has been brought to India from a Convention country, the left-behind parent can, and often does, file a Hague return petition in that country's courts, asserting that India's courts should be treated as having no proper basis to retain jurisdiction over a child wrongfully removed from habitual residence abroad. India is simply not bound to give effect to any order that foreign Hague proceeding produces, because India is outside the Convention framework entirely, but the existence of that foreign proceeding is not irrelevant to the Indian court. An Indian court hearing a habeas corpus petition will typically take note of a parallel foreign Hague proceeding as part of the factual background, including what, if anything, the foreign court has found about the circumstances of the child's removal, without treating itself as bound to await or follow that foreign proceeding's outcome. A foreign Hague return order, once made, can also feed back into the Indian court's own assessment under V. Ravi Chandran, since it is itself a species of foreign order whose persuasive weight the Indian court will consider alongside everything else, even though it was made under a Convention India has not joined.

This creates a real risk of inconsistent outcomes, a foreign court ordering return under the Convention while an Indian court simultaneously permits the child to remain in India under its own welfare analysis, and a parent needs to go into both proceedings aware that winning one does not automatically resolve the other. Coordinating strategy across both, including through counsel in each country who are at least aware of what is happening in the other proceeding, is usually necessary rather than optional once both fronts are genuinely active.

What this means for a parent planning a relocation, and for one resisting it

A parent who wants to relocate with a child from India to the USA, Canada or the UK, or the reverse, should treat the Indian court's welfare assessment as the real contest, not a formality to get past on the way to the move. Evidence of the child's current stability, each parent's practical ability to maintain the relationship after relocation, and a realistic, concrete plan for continued contact with the other parent all carry weight, and a parent who shows up with a vague assurance that things will work out generally fares worse than one who comes with a specific, workable schedule.

On the other side, a parent resisting a relocation, or seeking the return of a child already taken abroad or into India, should focus energy on building the strongest possible case on welfare rather than leaning heavily on the fact that a removal may have been technically wrongful under foreign law, since an Indian court's attention is squarely on the child's present circumstances and future, not on assigning fault for how those circumstances came about, except to the extent fault bears directly on which parent is the safer or more suitable custodian going forward.

When the dispute involves two Indian-origin parents who never lived in India together

A meaningful share of these disputes do not involve one Indian and one foreign parent at all. Two Indian-origin parents, both settled in the USA, Canada or the UK for years, sometimes even married abroad and never resident in India as a couple, can still end up in an Indian court if one parent brings the child to India, perhaps ostensibly for a family visit, and then does not return. The analysis above applies in essentially the same way to this fact pattern, since Indian courts look at the child's current welfare and the lawfulness of the present custody arrangement rather than at where the marriage itself took place or which country the parents call home, but the practical evidence in these cases often looks different, with the Indian court paying close attention to how the trip to India was represented at the outset and whether the parent now resisting return genuinely intended, at the time of travel, to keep the child here.

Where a parent abroad suspects a visit to India is being used as cover for a one-way move, treating early, documented communication, such as agreed return dates recorded in writing before travel, as a meaningful piece of evidence rather than an afterthought is worth doing before the trip, not after. An Indian court assessing the lawfulness of custody will often look closely at what was actually agreed and represented before the child left for India, as distinct from what either parent says happened after the fact, and a parent with nothing in writing beyond a verbal understanding is at a real disadvantage once the dispute turns into a question of who said what and when.

Enforcing an Indian custody order once it is made, including against a parent who leaves again

Winning a custody or habeas corpus order in India is not the end of the practical problem where the losing parent has the means and the inclination to leave the country again with the child, or to simply refuse to hand the child over. An Indian court's order can be enforced through the contempt jurisdiction of the High Court, and a parent who defies a clear direction to produce or hand over a child risks contempt proceedings that can include imprisonment, which gives the order real teeth within India, but none of that machinery extends automatically beyond India's borders.

Where there is a genuine risk that a parent will leave India with the child in defiance of a pending or already-made order, the usual protective steps are to seek directions restraining the removal of the child from the court's jurisdiction, to have the child's passport impounded or a fresh one withheld, and in appropriate cases to request that immigration authorities be alerted so the child cannot be taken out through an airport without the court's knowledge. These measures need to be sought proactively, before a flight is booked, since an order obtained after a parent has already left the country is of limited practical use until the other parent can be found and a fresh enforcement proceeding brought wherever the parent and child have gone.

A parent who does succeed in leaving India with a child in breach of an Indian order faces a genuinely difficult position for the other parent, since India's own courts have no power to compel a foreign government to act, and the left-behind parent is pushed back into the foreign country's own legal system, with whatever remedies exist there, to try to recover the child. This is precisely the gap a Hague Convention relationship would otherwise fill, and its absence here is one more reason the preventive steps described above matter more than they might in a Convention-to-Convention case, where a return mechanism exists as a backstop even after the fact.

Nothing about India's position outside the Hague Convention makes a cross-border custody dispute easier to win for either parent. It removes a shortcut, the Convention's summary return mechanism, that would otherwise decide many of these cases on a narrow jurisdictional question, and replaces it with a fuller, slower welfare inquiry that looks at the child's actual circumstances rather than the legal label attached to how those circumstances arose.

A parent on either side of such a dispute gains the most by accepting that reality early: building a welfare case with real evidence, treating a foreign court order as a strong but not conclusive starting point, and dealing with practical questions like passports and travel through the court rather than around it.

None of this is a reason to delay seeking advice once a cross-border custody dispute looks likely. Early, well-documented steps, an undertaking recorded before a child travels, a clear written record of what was agreed, a prompt filing once a child's custody becomes genuinely contested, tend to matter more to how these cases turn out than any argument made later about which country's law should, in some abstract sense, have applied from the start.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a US, UK or Canadian court order require an Indian court to return a child who has been brought to India?

No. India has not signed the 1980 Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, so there is no treaty mechanism obliging an Indian court to summarily return a child on the strength of a foreign court's order alone. An Indian court will consider a foreign order as part of the overall picture but decides the question independently, based on the child's current welfare.

What is the difference between a summary inquiry and an elaborate inquiry in a habeas corpus petition over a child?

A summary inquiry is appropriate where a child has only recently been brought within the court's jurisdiction and the facts bearing on lawfulness of custody are reasonably clear, allowing the court to decide quickly. An elaborate inquiry, closer to a full welfare hearing, becomes necessary once the child has lived in India for a meaningful period, or the facts are genuinely contested, such that a snap decision would not do justice to the child's welfare.

Does it matter who filed first, the Indian habeas corpus petition or the foreign Hague Convention petition?

Not decisively. An Indian court is not bound to wait for, or defer to, a parallel foreign Hague proceeding, and can proceed with its own welfare-based inquiry regardless of the sequence of filings. A parent should expect both proceedings to move on their own tracks rather than assume the first-filed case will control the outcome in both countries.

Can one parent get an Indian passport for a child without the other parent's consent?

Generally, both parents' consent is required under the Passport Manual, though an applicant can proceed through an affidavit route in defined circumstances where the other parent's consent cannot be obtained. Where a custody dispute is already pending, it is usually more effective to get a direction from the court hearing that dispute than to deal with the passport authority on the strength of the affidavit alone.

Are Indian courts willing to make 'mirror orders' like courts in Hague Convention countries do?

Indian courts do not use that specific label or follow a formal mirror-order procedure, but they have accepted undertakings from a relocating parent, and attached conditions to permission for relocation, that function similarly in substance. These are case-specific directions rather than a general rule, and their value abroad still depends on whether the foreign court recognises them.

Does a parent's gender affect how an Indian court decides a cross-border custody case?

Indian courts apply the welfare principle without a formal presumption favouring either parent based on gender in these cross-border custody matters, though the practical commentary on why India has stayed outside the Hague Convention has noted concerns about outcomes for mothers who return to India with a child after a difficult foreign marriage. Each case still turns on its own facts rather than any fixed rule tied to which parent is the mother or the father.

Is there any country-specific difference between a dispute involving the USA, Canada or the UK?

The underlying Indian law, the welfare principle, habeas corpus procedure and the treatment of foreign orders, applies the same way regardless of which of the three countries is involved, since none of the three has a special bilateral arrangement with India on child custody. Differences in outcome tend to come from the facts of the individual case and from what each foreign court itself has or has not already decided, rather than from the particular foreign country involved.

What should a parent do first if a child has just been brought to India from abroad without consent?

Acting quickly matters, both because the passage of time strengthens the other parent's argument that the child has settled into life in India, and because a habeas corpus petition is more likely to be dealt with as a summary matter the sooner it is filed after the child's arrival. Gathering evidence of the circumstances of the removal and of the child's prior living arrangements abroad, and filing promptly rather than waiting to see how things develop, is generally the better course.
