Hiring a Foreign National to Work in India: Visas, Registration and Tax

An employer who gets the Employment Visa and the FRRO filing right can still fall over the labour code, the provident fund and the withholding tax questions that come after the foreign employee has already landed.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 16 November 2026 · Law stated as on 16 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company placing one of its own people on the ground in India, or an Indian company hiring a foreign national directly, usually treats the Employment Visa as the whole problem. It is only the first of four separate compliance tracks: immigration, labour law, provident fund and income tax, each with its own officer, its own filing and its own deadline, and each capable of holding up the assignment on its own.

Key points

  • An Employment Visa generally requires a minimum annual salary, commonly stated as USD 25,000 (roughly INR 21 lakh), a signed employment contract and a role that calls for skills not easily available in India; a reported move to raise this floor had not been officially confirmed as on the date of this article, so employers should check the current figure with the Ministry of Home Affairs or the Indian mission before relying on it.
  • Employment Visa holders and their dependants must register with the Foreigners Regional Registration Office, or the Foreigners Registration Officer outside the FRRO cities, within 14 days of arrival; this 14-day rule is unchanged, but a 2026 amendment separately tightened the registration deadline for people on shorter visas who want to stay beyond 180 days.
  • Most Indian labour and employment protections apply to a foreign national working in India in the same way they apply to an Indian employee, because the test is usually where the work is performed and how the person is engaged, not the employee's nationality or the employer's place of incorporation.
  • The four Labour Codes came into force on 21 November 2025, and the Central Government notified the Central Rules under all four Codes on 8 and 9 May 2026; State Rules continue to be notified separately and vary by State, so coverage of a specific expatriate role still needs a State-by-State check.
  • A foreign national who works in India for a sufficient number of days in a financial year becomes a tax resident under the ordinary day-count test in Section 6, regardless of nationality, and Indian-source salary is taxable in India whether or not the employee is a resident.
  • A short-stay exemption under Schedule IV, paragraph 3 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (the successor to Section 10(6)(vi) of the 1961 Act) can exempt the remuneration of a non-citizen employee of a foreign enterprise with no business in India, provided the employee's stay does not exceed 90 days in the tax year; this is narrow and conditions must be checked against the current text before an employer relies on it.
  • International Workers under the Employees' Provident Fund scheme are a separate and more technical question, including a live split between High Courts on who counts as an International Worker and what the withdrawal rules are; employers should treat that as its own compliance track rather than an afterthought to the visa process.

Four compliance tracks, not one

An Indian subsidiary told to onboard a foreign national, whether a deputed group employee or a direct hire, tends to route the request to whoever handled the last work visa and assumes the matter ends there. The visa is the entry point, not the destination. Once the person has landed, three further tracks run in parallel: registration with the immigration authorities, coverage under India's labour and social security law, and Indian income tax on the salary actually paid for work done here.

Each track has its own forum, its own paperwork and its own deadline, and missing one does not simply create a paperwork backlog. A lapsed FRRO registration can block an exit from India at the airport. A Provident Fund default can draw interest and damages years after the employee has left. An unreported short-stay exemption claimed without meeting its conditions can turn into a withholding tax shortfall assessed against the Indian entity, not the employee.

This piece works through the four tracks as an employer would meet them in sequence, from the visa application through to the point the foreign employee's assignment in India ends, flagging where a rule changed recently and where the current position genuinely depends on facts that need checking case by case.

The Employment Visa: eligibility, salary floor and documentation

India issues a dedicated Employment Visa category for a foreign national coming to work for an entity registered in India, whether as a direct employee or on secondment from an overseas group company. The standard conditions are a signed employment contract or an assignment letter, a role requiring skills or qualifications not readily available in the Indian labour market, and an annual salary that meets a minimum threshold, commonly stated as USD 25,000, or roughly INR 21 lakh, with a lower floor of around USD 14,000 for specified categories such as teachers at particular institutions.

For an assignment of less than a year the salary condition is applied on a pro-rata basis, and the figure taken into account can include housing and other allowances rather than base pay alone. A report that circulated around October 2026 suggested the floor might rise to roughly INR 24 lakh; that increase had not been confirmed through an official notification as on the date of this article, and an employer should verify the current threshold directly with the Ministry of Home Affairs or the Indian consular post handling the application before relying on either figure.

An OCI cardholder sits outside this entire framework. An Overseas Citizen of India does not need an Employment Visa and is not sponsored by the employer in the way a non-OCI foreign national is, because the OCI card itself carries a lifetime entitlement to live and work in India subject to conditions unrelated to employment sponsorship. The Employment Visa is generally issued for up to one year at a time and is extendable, in practice running up to a ceiling commonly described as five years for a given assignment, though extension is discretionary and tied to the continuing employment relationship rather than automatic.

The Business Visa trap

A Business Visa is not a substitute for an Employment Visa, and the two are regularly confused by employers moving a foreign employee into India quickly for what is described internally as a short deployment. A Business Visa is meant for activities such as attending meetings, exploring a market or supervising an existing project; it is not meant for a foreign national who will actually be employed by, or draw salary from, an Indian entity or an Indian-registered project.

Using a Business Visa to cover what is functionally an employment arrangement exposes the Indian entity and the individual to a finding that the stay was improperly categorised, which can affect future visa applications for the same individual and, in a disputed case, the individual's ability to remain in or re-enter India. Converting a Business Visa into an Employment Visa from within India is not a routine administrative step; current guidance treats conversion as exceptional and subject to Ministry of Home Affairs approval rather than something a Foreigners Regional Registration Office grants on request.

The practical rule employers should apply is to classify the visa by what the person will actually do in India, not by how quickly the posting needs to start. If the role involves drawing salary for work performed in India over a period that goes beyond a short visit, the Employment Visa route should be started well before the person is due to arrive, because the FRRO and tax consequences downstream assume the correct visa category was used from day one.

FRRO registration: the 14-day rule and where it tightened

A foreign national in India on a long-term visa, generally one valid for more than 180 days, including an Employment Visa, must register with the Foreigners Regional Registration Office in the metro cities where one operates, or with the local Foreigners Registration Officer elsewhere, within 14 days of arrival. Registration is what allows later steps such as a visa extension, a change of visa category, or in some cases an exit from India without separate clearance; late registration draws a penalty, and the penalty structure was significantly increased in recent years, making this one of the items an employer's HR function tends to underestimate.

A 2026 amendment to India's foreigners' registration rules changed the position for a different group: foreign nationals who entered on a visa valid for 180 days or less and then want to stay beyond that 180-day period. For that group, the Ministry of Home Affairs removed an earlier grace period that had allowed registration shortly after the 180-day mark was crossed, and now requires registration to be completed before the 180 days run out. Employment Visa holders are not the group this change targets; they, and their eligible dependants, remain on the 14-days-from-arrival rule. The practical lesson for an employer managing a mixed workforce, some on Employment Visas and some on longer Business Visa stays that might extend past 180 days, is to track the two populations against two different registration clocks rather than one.

Registration requires, among other documents, the passport, visa, a local address proof and, for an Employment Visa holder, the employment contract or secondment letter. Dependants travelling on an X (dependant) visa linked to the employee's Employment Visa generally register alongside the employee, and recent amendments have added separate reporting obligations in respect of children of foreign nationals, which an employer sponsoring a family relocation should flag to the employee rather than assume is covered automatically by the employee's own registration.

What getting it wrong actually costs

The four tracks carry different kinds of consequence, and employers tend to underweight the ones that do not show up immediately. On the immigration side, overstaying a visa or failing to register within the applicable deadline can lead to fines calculated per day of default, and in a serious case to deportation or a bar on re-entry; a lapsed registration has, in practice, stopped individuals at the airport on departure until the position was regularised, which is rarely convenient when the departure is for a scheduled business trip rather than the end of the assignment.

On the labour and social security side, the exposure is less dramatic but more persistent. A Provident Fund default accrues interest and statutory damages that compound for as long as the default continues, and because International Worker contributions and withdrawal rules are themselves a live area of dispute between High Courts, an employer who gets the classification wrong at the outset may only discover the error when the employee tries to withdraw the accumulated balance years later, at which point correcting the position is far harder than getting it right the first time.

On the tax side, an Indian entity that under-withholds on a foreign employee's India-source compensation, whether through an unjustified short-stay exemption claim or through simply not grossing up an overseas-paid component, is assessed and penalised as the deductor in default, independent of whether the employee has separately settled their own return. None of these consequences is unique to foreign employees, but the layered nature of an expatriate's compliance position, immigration plus labour plus tax plus, in many cases, a cross-border secondment structure, means a single oversight tends to surface in more than one place at once.

Which Indian labour laws actually cover a foreign employee

The starting assumption employers should work from is that Indian labour and employment protections apply to a foreign national performing work in India in broadly the same way they apply to an Indian national, unless a specific statute or notification says otherwise. The usual tests are where the work is performed, the nature of the establishment, and how the individual is engaged, factors applied to the facts of the role rather than to the employee's passport or the employer's place of incorporation. An Indian subsidiary of a foreign group cannot treat a seconded foreign employee as outside the reach of Indian labour law simply because the parent entity is overseas or because the individual remains on the overseas parent's payroll for part of their compensation.

That coverage is layered rather than uniform. Every State has its own Shops and Establishments Act governing working hours, leave, holidays and similar conditions for establishments within its territory, and these continue to apply to establishments employing a foreign national in the same way they apply to any other establishment, subject to each State's own thresholds and exemptions. Some States have begun narrowing Shops and Establishments coverage for larger or already-regulated establishments as the Labour Codes bed in, so the applicable State's current rules, not a general national position, decide what actually applies to a given office.

A role genuinely at managerial, administrative or confidential level can fall outside the protective core of several of these statutes, in which case the individual employment contract does more of the work in defining terms. This exclusion is assessed by function, not title, and an employer should not assume a senior-sounding designation automatically takes a foreign hire outside statutory coverage; an incorrectly assumed exclusion is one of the more common and more expensive mistakes an employer makes when structuring an expatriate's contract.

The 2025 Labour Codes and where the rules actually stand

The Code on Wages, the Industrial Relations Code, the Code on Social Security and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code were brought into force with effect from 21 November 2025, replacing and consolidating 29 earlier central labour statutes. Commencement of the statutory text is not the same as the regime being fully operational; a functioning compliance framework also needs the subordinate rules that fill in registration formats, thresholds, returns and procedures, and those rules follow separately at both the central and the State level.

The Central Government notified the Central Rules under all four Codes on 8 and 9 May 2026, which brought central-level procedural compliance, registrations, returns and prescribed forms, into effect for establishments where the Centre is the appropriate government. The Ministry of Labour and Employment has clarified that the Central Rules do not apply uniformly to every establishment; for many private-sector employers the appropriate government, and therefore the applicable rule set, is the State, and State Rules have been notified on a rolling basis rather than all at once, with the position still moving through the course of 2026.

For an employer structuring an expatriate's engagement, the practical consequence is that the four Codes now set the substantive framework, but the detail an HR team actually files against, registers under or reports on depends on which State's rules are in force for that establishment as on the relevant date. Where a Code provision and a State Shops and Establishments provision differ on a worker benefit, the Codes are designed so that the position more favourable to the worker prevails, which is the default an employer should apply when the two sources genuinely conflict rather than assuming the newer Code automatically displaces the older State law.

Provident fund and the International Worker question

A foreign national working for an establishment covered by the Employees' Provident Fund scheme is very often classified as an International Worker, a category that was created specifically because ordinary PF rules, built around an Indian career and an Indian retirement, do not map cleanly onto someone who may work in India for a few years and then leave. International Worker status changes contribution obligations and, more importantly for an employer managing an assignment's end, the conditions under which the accumulated balance can actually be withdrawn once the person leaves India.

This is a technical area in its own right, including a current and unresolved split between High Courts on who qualifies as an International Worker and how withdrawal conditions should be read, and it deserves its own dedicated review rather than a summary folded into a visa and tax checklist. An employer bringing a foreign national onto an Indian payroll, or structuring a secondment that touches Indian payroll at all, should treat the International Worker determination as a distinct compliance step to be confirmed with the establishment's PF consultants before the first contribution is made, not after.

Residential status and the salary that is actually taxable

Indian income tax residence is decided by physical presence in a tax year, not by citizenship. Under the ordinary test in Section 6, an individual who is in India for 182 days or more in the tax year is a resident, and a separate 60-days-plus-365-days-over-four-years test can also trigger residence in some circumstances; a foreign national who spends enough time in India on an assignment can become a tax resident in exactly the same way an Indian citizen does, with the usual consequence that the additional 'resident but not ordinarily resident' category softens the position for someone newly resident after years abroad.

Separately from residence, salary that is earned for services rendered in India is generally taxable in India regardless of where the employment contract was signed, which entity pays the salary, or whether the individual is a tax resident at all. This is the point employers most often get wrong when a foreign employee's compensation is split between an Indian payslip and an overseas parent-company payroll: splitting the pay does not split the tax source, and the Indian-source portion remains taxable in India and reportable by the Indian entity even if a smaller amount physically reaches an Indian bank account.

The Income-tax Act, 2025 applies from the 2026-27 tax year onward, with the 1961 Act continuing to govern earlier years; an assignment that straddles the transition needs its tax position checked against whichever Act actually applied in the relevant year, since the two Acts do not always use identical section numbering even where the substantive rule carries forward unchanged.

Withholding, the short-stay exemption and treaty relief

An Indian entity paying salary to a foreign employee is required to deduct tax at source on that salary in the same way it would for any employee, based on the individual's estimated tax liability for the year, and the usual year-end reconciliation and return obligations follow from that deduction. Where part of the compensation is paid overseas by a group entity for work actually performed in India, the Indian entity can still carry withholding exposure on that portion, which is a frequent point of friction in secondment arrangements where payroll is deliberately kept outside India for administrative convenience.

A narrow short-stay exemption exists for the remuneration of an employee of a foreign enterprise that has no trade or business in India, provided the employee is not an Indian citizen and the stay in India in the relevant tax year does not exceed 90 days; this condition sat in Section 10(6)(vi) of the 1961 Act and its equivalent now sits in Schedule IV, paragraph 3 of the Income-tax Act, 2025. The exemption is specific to this fact pattern. It does not extend to an employee of an Indian group entity, and it fails entirely if the foreign enterprise is found to carry on business in India, which is a separate and fact-heavy inquiry an employer should not assume away simply because the Indian entity is a distinct legal person from the overseas parent.

Where the exemption does not apply, a Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement between India and the employee's home country can still relieve double taxation on the same income, typically through a foreign tax credit claimed in the home jurisdiction, and the employee will generally need a Tax Residency Certificate and, for an India-specific claim, the prescribed self-declaration to actually access treaty relief rather than relying on the treaty's existence alone.

Structuring the assignment on paper

A secondment agreement between the overseas and Indian group entities, alongside an assignment or employment letter between the individual and the host entity, is the standard way to fix who is the employer of record for which purpose, how compensation is split and routed, who bears statutory contributions, and what happens to accrued benefits when the assignment ends. Getting this document right at the outset is considerably cheaper than reconstructing the employment relationship after a tax or labour dispute has already started, because an ambiguous or silent secondment agreement tends to be read against whichever party controlled the drafting.

The agreement should also address repatriation of the employee at the end of the term, including the handling of accrued leave, any India-specific benefits, exit formalities with the FRRO, and the practical steps for closing out Provident Fund and tax positions before the person leaves India. An assignment that simply stops, without a documented exit sequence, is the most common source of the straggling compliance gaps, an unfiled final tax return, an unclosed PF account, an un-cancelled visa registration, that surface months later when the employee or the employer least expects them.

Where the foreign employee continues to render services for the overseas parent while physically based in India, even part time, the secondment agreement should also address whether that arrangement creates a service permanent establishment risk for the overseas entity, a separate and well-documented concern under Indian tax and transfer pricing practice that sits alongside, rather than replacing, the individual's own withholding and residence position. Employers who treat the individual's tax compliance and the group's permanent establishment exposure as two unrelated questions often find they are drafting the same secondment facts twice, once for the employee's file and once for the group's tax advisers, when a single well-drafted agreement could have served both purposes from the outset.

Keeping the compliance calendar current

An expatriate assignment is not a single filing event but a set of recurring obligations spread across the year: the Employment Visa extension, the FRRO's periodic requirements, monthly Provident Fund contributions where applicable, monthly or quarterly withholding tax deposits, and an annual Indian income tax return for the employee. A compliance calendar that tracks these dates against the specific individual, rather than a generic template for all expatriates, is the difference between a function that runs quietly and one that discovers a missed deadline only when a penalty notice arrives.

Group employers running more than one expatriate assignment into India at a time should also expect the rules each individual is subject to to diverge over time, because visa validity periods, tax years and PF contribution histories rarely stay aligned across a cohort that arrived at different points. Building the calendar around each individual's own dates, rather than assuming a uniform start-and-review cycle for the whole group, avoids the common failure mode where one person's compliance position is quietly carried forward using another person's deadlines.

Common mistakes and a practical checklist

The recurring errors employers make are consistent across industries: using a Business Visa for what is really an employment relationship; assuming a senior title removes an expatriate from labour law coverage without checking the role's actual functions; treating FRRO registration as the employee's personal problem rather than an employer compliance item with its own deadline; splitting compensation between an Indian and overseas payroll without recognising that the India-source portion remains taxable and withholdable regardless of where it is paid; and claiming the short-stay exemption without actually confirming the foreign enterprise has no business presence in India.

A workable sequence for an employer bringing in a foreign national is to confirm the correct visa category and current salary threshold before the offer is finalised; file the FRRO registration within 14 days of arrival and calendar the renewal and extension dates; determine International Worker status under the Provident Fund scheme before the first payroll cycle; fix residential status and the applicable Act for the relevant tax year; set up withholding on the full India-source compensation, including any portion paid overseas; and document the secondment or employment arrangement, including the exit sequence, before the assignment begins rather than after it is already underway.

None of the four tracks, immigration, labour coverage, provident fund and tax, is optional or satisfied by completing one of the others well. An employer that gets the Employment Visa exactly right can still default on withholding tax because compensation was split across two payrolls, or can still mishandle a Provident Fund exit because International Worker status was never formally determined.

The 2026 changes described here, the tightened registration deadline for extended short-term stays, the Central Rules notified under the Labour Codes, and an unconfirmed report of a higher Employment Visa salary floor, are a reminder that this is a moving compliance area rather than a settled one; an employer running an active expatriate programme should build in a periodic check against current Ministry of Home Affairs, Ministry of Labour and Employment, and Income Tax Department positions rather than relying on a policy written once and left unreviewed.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the minimum salary for an Indian Employment Visa?

The commonly stated floor is USD 25,000 a year, roughly INR 21 lakh, prorated for assignments shorter than a year and sometimes inclusive of allowances, with a lower threshold for specified categories such as teachers. A reported move to a higher figure had not been officially confirmed as on the date of this article. Confirm the current threshold with the Ministry of Home Affairs or the Indian mission handling the visa before relying on any single figure.

Does an OCI cardholder need an Employment Visa to work in India?

No. An Overseas Citizen of India is not required to hold an Employment Visa and is not subject to the salary threshold or employer sponsorship process that applies to other foreign nationals, because the OCI card itself carries an entitlement to live and work in India, subject to the conditions attached to OCI status generally.

How many days does a foreign employee have to register with the FRRO after arriving in India?

Employment Visa holders and their eligible dependants must register within 14 days of arrival, which remains unchanged. A separate 2026 amendment affects foreign nationals on shorter-validity visas who want to extend their stay beyond 180 days, requiring their registration to be completed before the 180 days run out rather than shortly after, but this change does not alter the Employment Visa's 14-day rule.

Can a foreign employee on a Business Visa simply start working for the Indian entity?

No. A Business Visa is intended for activities like meetings, market exploration or supervising an existing project, not for drawing salary from or being employed by an Indian entity. Using it as a substitute for an Employment Visa risks the stay being found improperly categorised, and converting a Business Visa into an Employment Visa from within India is treated as an exception requiring Ministry of Home Affairs approval rather than a routine FRRO filing.

Do India's labour laws apply to a foreign national working for an Indian company?

Generally yes. Coverage usually turns on where the work is performed and how the individual is engaged, not on nationality, so most Shops and Establishments and Labour Code protections extend to a foreign employee in the same way they extend to an Indian one, subject to each statute's own exclusions for managerial, administrative or confidential roles.

Is a foreign national's salary in India taxable even if part of it is paid overseas?

Yes, in most cases. Salary earned for services rendered in India is generally taxable in India as Indian-source income regardless of which entity pays it or where it is physically paid, so splitting compensation between an Indian and an overseas payroll does not remove the India-source portion from Indian tax and withholding obligations.

Is there any exemption for a foreign employee who works in India for a short period?

A narrow exemption can apply to the remuneration of a non-citizen employee of a foreign enterprise that has no trade or business in India, where the employee's stay does not exceed 90 days in the tax year. This sat in Section 10(6)(vi) of the 1961 Act and its successor now sits in Schedule IV, paragraph 3 of the Income-tax Act, 2025. It does not extend to employees of an Indian group entity, and it is lost entirely if the foreign enterprise is found to carry on business in India.

Are the new Labour Codes fully in effect for an employer hiring a foreign national today?

The four Labour Codes took effect from 21 November 2025, and the Central Rules under them were notified on 8 and 9 May 2026, but State Rules continue to be notified separately and vary, so the compliance detail that actually applies to a given establishment still depends on checking that establishment's State as on the current date.
