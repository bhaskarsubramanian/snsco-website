India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme: Compliance Obligations for the Cement, Aluminium, Steel and Refining Sectors

What an obligated entity, or a foreign investor, lender or joint-venture partner in one, actually has to do once BEE notifies its sector's emission intensity target, and how the certificate trading and verification machinery itself is meant to work

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 25 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

India's compliance carbon market went from statute to running scheme in under three years, and it now reaches nearly 500 industrial units across eight sectors, with iron and steel next in line. For a cement, aluminium, refining or steel business, including the Indian unit of a foreign manufacturer, the question has moved from whether this applies to exactly what it requires, who verifies the numbers, how the certificates actually change hands, and what a shortfall actually costs in practice.

Key points

  • The Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022 inserted Sections 14(w) and 14AA, empowering the Central Government to specify a carbon credit trading scheme in consultation with the Bureau of Energy Efficiency; the Ministry of Power notified the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, 2023 on 28 June 2023.
  • BEE has so far notified greenhouse gas emission intensity targets in two batches: aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali and pulp and paper on 8 October 2025, covering 282 obligated entities, and refineries, petrochemicals, textiles and secondary aluminium on 13 January 2026, covering a further 208 entities, bringing the total to roughly 490.
  • Draft targets for iron and steel followed in June 2026, covering 255 units with a combined baseline of about 358.6 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent and required reductions of 2.1% to 9.3% depending on the type of unit, with FY 2025-26 skipped as that sector's first compliance year; targets for fertiliser remained unconfirmed as finalised at the time of writing.
  • An obligated entity that exceeds its emission intensity target must either purchase Carbon Credit Certificates to cover the shortfall or face the consequences set out in the compliance mechanism, reported to include a six-month bar on trading certificates in addition to any penalty available under the Energy Conservation Act, 2001 itself.
  • Certificates are meant to trade through registered power exchanges under CERC's regulatory oversight, with the Grid Controller of India operating the central registry and the Bureau of Energy Efficiency acting as scheme Administrator responsible for registration and transaction procedures.
  • A separate, voluntary offset mechanism, added by a December 2023 amendment to the scheme, lets entities outside the compliance list register mitigation projects across energy, industry, waste, agriculture, forestry and transport and earn tradable certificates under methodologies BEE has been approving and expanding since March 2025.
  • The scheme builds directly on the Perform, Achieve and Trade framework that preceded it, and a business already familiar with PAT's designated-consumer, target-and-verify structure will recognise much of the compliance architecture even though the underlying currency has changed from Energy Saving Certificates to Carbon Credit Certificates.
  • Verification sits between a unit's self-reported emission figures and any certificate or penalty consequence: BEE's July 2024 accreditation framework requires an obligated entity's performance assessment to be checked by an Accredited Carbon Verification Agency and submitted, with that agency's verification report, to BEE and the relevant State Designated Agency within four months of the compliance year ending.

From energy efficiency to carbon: why this scheme exists

India's energy-intensive industries have lived under a mandatory efficiency regime since 2012, when the Perform, Achieve and Trade scheme set energy consumption targets for designated consumers across sectors such as cement, steel and aluminium, let them trade Energy Saving Certificates to meet those targets, and penalised those who missed them. PAT worked on energy consumption, a proxy for emissions rather than emissions themselves, and it was always going to need a successor once India committed, under the Paris Agreement framework, to an economy-wide emissions-intensity target rather than a sector-by-sector energy-efficiency one.

The Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022 supplied the legal basis for that successor. It inserted Sections 14(w) and 14AA into the Energy Conservation Act, 2001, empowering the Central Government, acting in consultation with the Bureau of Energy Efficiency, to specify a carbon credit trading scheme and to designate an agency to issue carbon credit certificates. The Ministry of Power acted on that power on 28 June 2023, notifying the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, 2023, with BEE named as the scheme's implementing agency.

Calling this India's compliance carbon market is deliberate terminology, distinguishing it from the voluntary carbon markets that have operated internationally, and in India to a limited extent, for years without any statutory backing. A compliance market exists because a law requires a defined set of entities to meet a target, with real consequences for missing it, whereas a voluntary market exists only because a buyer somewhere chooses to pay for a credit it has no legal obligation to acquire. The scheme does, as later sections of this guide describe, also carry a voluntary offset limb, but its core identity is the compliance obligation now reaching hundreds of industrial units across the country.

The scheme should also be kept distinct from the separate Green Credit Programme the government has promoted under environmental rules made for a different purpose, rewarding activities such as tree plantation and water conservation. The two run on different legal bases, different administering agencies and different currencies, and a business should not assume credits or certificates from one programme can be used to satisfy an obligation under the other; each needs to be tracked and reported on its own terms.

Who actually becomes an obligated entity

The scheme does not create obligations in the abstract; a unit becomes an obligated entity only once BEE notifies a greenhouse gas emission intensity, or GEI, target for its sector and lists that unit against it, the same mechanism PAT used for designated consumers. That notification has come in stages rather than all at once. The first batch, covering aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali and pulp and paper, was finalised on 8 October 2025 and brought 282 entities within the compliance obligation.

A second batch followed on 13 January 2026, adding refineries, petrochemicals, textiles and secondary aluminium and bringing a further 208 entities into scope, for a combined total of roughly 490 obligated entities across eight sectors. Draft targets for iron and steel were published in June 2026, covering 255 units with a combined baseline of approximately 358.6 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent and required reductions ranging from 2.1% to 9.3% depending on the category of unit, with the sector's first compliance year set at FY 2026-27 rather than FY 2025-26. Fertiliser sector targets had not been confirmed as finalised at the time of writing.

A business operating a unit in any of these sectors, or acquiring one, should check its own name or its target entity's name against BEE's published lists directly rather than assume sector coverage alone settles the question, since the obligation attaches to the specific listed unit and its notified target, not to the sector as a general category. An acquisition due diligence exercise on an Indian cement, aluminium or refining asset that does not check this list is missing a compliance item that can carry real financial consequences from the very next compliance year.

The staged rollout also means a unit's compliance status is not a one-time classification. A unit in a sector not yet notified today could become an obligated entity once BEE finalises targets for that sector, in the same way the first four sectors became obligated in October 2025 and the next four in January 2026. A business in fertiliser manufacturing, where targets remained unconfirmed at the time of writing, or in any other energy-intensive sector not yet covered, should treat the absence of a current obligation as provisional rather than permanent, and should watch BEE's notifications for its own sector rather than assume continued exclusion.

Meeting the target: energy-efficiency measures, certificates, or both

An obligated entity has two broad routes to meeting its GEI target, and most will end up using a combination of the two. The first is doing the underlying work, reducing the actual emission intensity of its operations through efficiency upgrades, fuel switching or process changes, the same kind of investment PAT incentivised for energy consumption. The second is the market route: where a unit beats its target, it earns Carbon Credit Certificates for the surplus, and where a unit misses its target, it must acquire certificates from the market to cover the shortfall.

This dual structure is what gives the scheme its name and its intended efficiency: a unit facing a high marginal cost of further emissions reduction can buy certificates from a unit facing a lower marginal cost, in principle driving overall abatement toward wherever it is cheapest to achieve across the obligated universe, rather than forcing every unit to over-invest in reductions it is relatively expensive for it to make. The market only works as intended, however, if certificates are genuinely available in sufficient volume, which in the scheme's early years, with only two sectoral batches live and a third still in draft, remains to be tested at scale.

A unit deciding how to allocate capital between physical efficiency investment and certificate purchase needs a reasonably reliable view of where certificate prices are likely to sit over its planning horizon, and that view is harder to form in a market still in its first compliance cycles than it would be in an established one with several years of price history. Until the market has run through at least one or two full compliance periods across all its notified sectors, a cautious approach treats efficiency investment as the more controllable lever and certificate purchase as a supplement to be relied on for the residual gap, rather than the other way around.

The cost of falling short

A unit that ends a compliance period without meeting its target through either efficiency gains or certificate purchases faces the consequences the compliance mechanism sets out. Reporting on the detailed compliance procedure describes a six-month bar on trading Carbon Credit Certificates as one consequence of a shortfall in a given compliance period, layered on top of whatever penalty the Energy Conservation Act, 2001 itself makes available for non-compliance, a structure that mirrors how PAT penalised designated consumers who failed to meet their energy-saving targets.

Because the detailed compliance procedure has itself gone through drafting and revision since BEE first notified it, a business should treat the specific penalty terms as something to confirm against BEE's current, finalised procedure for its own compliance period rather than assume the terms reported at the time targets were first notified remain unchanged by the time a shortfall is actually assessed. The practical lesson for a unit approaching its target year is to budget for certificate purchase well before the compliance deadline, rather than treat the trading market as a last-minute buffer, since a market still building its liquidity may not reliably supply certificates on short notice at a predictable price.

A trading bar carries a cost beyond the immediate compliance period it is imposed in. A unit that is barred from trading certificates for six months cannot sell a surplus it earns during that window either, which means a unit that both falls short in one period and then over-performs in the next loses the ability to monetise that over-performance until the bar lifts. Building a buffer into the target plan, rather than running a unit's compliance position close to the line every year, reduces the chance that an ordinary operational dip in one period compounds into a longer stretch of lost market access in the next.

How the certificates actually trade

Carbon Credit Certificates are meant to change hands through registered power exchanges, placed under the regulatory oversight of the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission, which registers the exchanges and approves their trading rules, including how eligibility is checked and how prices are discovered. CERC issued draft regulations for this trading framework in November 2024, and reporting on a later, 2026 version of the regulations describes the Commission retaining the power to set floor and forbearance prices for the compliance market and to issue directions to an exchange or the registry if trading shows abnormal price movements or unusual volumes.

Two other institutions complete the structure. The Grid Controller of India is reported to operate the central registry that records certificate issuance, ownership and retirement, while the Bureau of Energy Efficiency serves as the scheme's Administrator, responsible for registration and the transaction procedures that sit around the trading activity itself. A business engaging with this market for the first time should expect a three-cornered institutional relationship, BEE issuing and administering certificates against its own targets and verification, CERC regulating how they are bought and sold on an exchange, and the Grid Controller recording the resulting holdings, rather than a single regulator handling the whole lifecycle.

This three-way split has an obvious parallel to how India's electricity market itself is regulated, CERC overseeing exchange-based power trading while the Grid Controller, formed from what was previously the National Load Despatch Centre's functions, manages grid operations and related registries, and that parallel is not an accident. The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme was built inside the Ministry of Power's existing institutional ecosystem rather than handed to a freshly created regulator, which has the advantage of reusing institutions with real market-oversight experience but also means a business's existing relationships and familiarity with how CERC regulates power trading translate reasonably well to this new certificate market, a useful advantage for any legal or compliance team that already deals with CERC on the power side.

Verification: the step between a target and a certificate

No obligated entity's self-reported emission figures translate directly into certificates or penalties; an independent verification step sits in between. In July 2024, BEE issued both the Detailed Procedure for Compliance Mechanism and a separate Accreditation Procedure and Eligibility Criteria for Accredited Carbon Verification Agencies, the bodies, referred to as ACV agencies, that actually check an obligated entity's reported emission intensity before BEE acts on it. An ACV agency must be accredited separately for each sector it wants to verify and for each of the scheme's two mechanisms, compliance and offset, it wants to work under.

The eligibility bar for an ACV agency includes a requirement that it hold no pending judicial inquiry for fraud, malpractice or similar misconduct, and the framework leans on ISO 14065 accreditation, the international standard for greenhouse gas validation and verification bodies, as a backstop: an agency given provisional accreditation that does not secure ISO 14065 accreditation within a year faces consequences to that provisional status under BEE's own procedure. BEE has published a list of accredited agencies, reported at around 18 by one count, with firms such as Bureau Veritas accredited specifically for sectors including cement, iron and steel, petrochemicals and petroleum refining.

Procedurally, an obligated entity must submit a performance assessment document to BEE and the relevant State Designated Agency within four months of its compliance year ending, accompanied by a verification report from its chosen ACV agency. A business should treat the choice of verification agency, and the lead time needed to complete verification before that four-month deadline, as part of its annual compliance calendar rather than an afterthought arranged once the raw emissions data is already in hand, since a verification report that is late or incomplete puts the underlying certificate claim or shortfall calculation itself in question.

An obligated entity is free to choose among the agencies BEE has accredited for its sector, and that choice is not purely a compliance formality. Verification agencies differ in the sectors they are accredited for, in their familiarity with a given industrial process, and in practical terms, in how quickly they can turn around a verification report during the busy period immediately following the compliance year's end, when every obligated entity in that sector is likely seeking verification at roughly the same time. A business that engages its chosen agency early, rather than waiting until data collection is complete, is better placed to avoid the scheduling bottleneck that a sector-wide rush to the same small pool of accredited verifiers can create.

The voluntary side: the offset mechanism for entities outside the compliance list

A business that is not, or not yet, an obligated entity is not excluded from the scheme altogether. A December 2023 amendment to the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme notification added an offset mechanism, letting entities across energy, industry, waste, agriculture, forestry and transport voluntarily register mitigation projects and earn their own Carbon Credit Certificates, tradable in the same market structure described above, subject to project eligibility criteria and the scheme's own requirements.

BEE released the first version of its Detailed Procedure for the Offset Mechanism in March 2025, alongside an initial set of eight approved methodologies specifying how a given project type calculates its emission reduction for certification purposes. That list of approved methodologies has grown since, reported at around twelve by mid-2026, and a business considering a voluntary project, a captive renewable energy installation, a waste-to-energy facility, an afforestation programme, should check the current approved methodology list before designing the project, since a project built around an unapproved methodology cannot generate certificates under this mechanism regardless of its genuine environmental merit.

The offset route has an obvious attraction for an obligated entity itself, beyond its use by an outsider to the compliance list: a unit facing a shortfall on its GEI target in one part of its operations can, in principle, fund or run an eligible offset project elsewhere and apply the resulting certificates against that shortfall, giving it a second source of compliance supply beyond simply buying compliance-market certificates on an exchange. A business weighing this option should still verify that the specific project type it is considering sits within an approved methodology and that the project meets the scheme's additionality and eligibility conditions, since an ineligible or poorly structured project can fail certification after money has already been spent building it.

What this means for a foreign-invested manufacturing unit

Several of the sectors already covered, cement, aluminium, refining and petrochemicals among them, carry significant foreign investment in India, whether through joint ventures, wholly owned subsidiaries or technology-licensing arrangements with an Indian manufacturing partner. A foreign investor in such a unit should treat the GEI target and the resulting compliance obligation as a cost line and a governance item that sits alongside, not instead of, the unit's existing environmental clearance and pollution-control obligations, since none of those earlier approvals addresses the carbon-intensity target this scheme separately imposes.

Shareholder agreements and joint-venture arrangements for a unit in an already-notified sector should, going forward, address who bears the cost of certificate purchases in a shortfall year and who retains the benefit of a surplus, a question that older agreements predating the scheme's sector-by-sector rollout are unlikely to have anticipated. A foreign investor negotiating a new joint venture or acquisition in one of these sectors should raise this explicitly during due diligence and documentation, rather than assume the point is covered by general environmental compliance representations that were drafted before this specific obligation existed.

Financing documents deserve the same scrutiny. A lender financing a cement, aluminium or refining expansion in India, whether through project finance, external commercial borrowing or a corporate facility, should expect a borrower's GEI compliance status and certificate position to become a relevant covenant item, in the same way energy-efficiency covenants followed from the PAT scheme for an earlier generation of industrial lending. A foreign lender unfamiliar with this scheme's mechanics should not assume a borrower's existing environmental representations already capture it, and should ask specifically whether the facility covers a unit that is, or is likely to become, an obligated entity under the current or next batch of GEI targets.

Insurance and risk-transfer arrangements around an industrial asset in a notified sector carry a similar gap. A property or business-interruption policy written before the relevant GEI target took effect is unlikely to contemplate a certificate-market cost arising from a target shortfall as an insured event, and a business should not expect existing cover to respond to that exposure without a specific endorsement addressing it, if one is available in the Indian market at all. Treating this as an uninsured operational risk to be managed through compliance planning, rather than assuming insurance will absorb it, is the more realistic starting position for now.

How this sits alongside other environmental and energy obligations

The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme does not replace any of the environmental or pollution-control clearances a manufacturing unit already needs. Environmental clearance under the EIA Notification, 2006, consent to operate from the State Pollution Control Board, and any sector-specific regulatory approval all continue to apply independently, and a unit's GEI target compliance has no bearing on whether those other approvals are in order or need renewal.

What the scheme does change is the internal reporting and monitoring a compliance-minded business needs to run. Where energy and environment compliance may previously have sat with a single EHS function tracking consumption and emissions for regulatory reporting purposes, a unit now facing a hard GEI target and a market-based consequence for missing it has a genuine financial reason to treat carbon intensity tracking as a standing finance and operations item rather than an environmental compliance item alone, given that the consequence of a miss is a cash cost in the certificate market rather than simply a compliance notation.

A unit that already runs a PAT-era energy management system has a head start here, since much of the consumption and process data PAT required overlaps with what a GEI calculation needs, but the overlap is not complete. Emission intensity brings in factors, fuel mix and its associated emission factors chief among them, that an energy-consumption target did not need to isolate in the same way, and a business relying entirely on its existing PAT-era reporting infrastructure without adapting it for emissions-specific data risks a verification report that does not hold up to an ACV agency's scrutiny when the two data sets are compared.

Three years after the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022 created the legal basis for this scheme, India's compliance carbon market has moved from a statutory possibility to a running obligation for close to 500 industrial units, with iron and steel next and fertiliser still to come. The institutional architecture, BEE administering targets and certificates, CERC regulating exchange trading, the Grid Controller running the registry, is now largely in place, even where specific procedural details continue to be revised as the scheme's early compliance periods play out.

For any business in an already-notified sector, or expecting to be added to one soon, the practical task is no longer monitoring whether this scheme will apply, but building the internal systems, target tracking, certificate budgeting, contractual allocation of shortfall and surplus risk, needed to live with it as an ongoing feature of operating in India rather than a one-off compliance exercise to clear and forget.

Iron and steel's progress from draft to finalised target, and fertiliser's targets whenever they arrive, will be the next test of how smoothly this expansion runs, and a business watching from a sector not yet covered should read those next steps as a reasonably reliable preview of its own eventual treatment rather than as news concerning someone else's industry. None of this is a reason to delay engaging with the scheme until a unit's own sector is actually notified: the lead time needed to build reliable emissions data, select and brief a verification agency, and understand the trading market's mechanics is considerable, and a business that starts that groundwork only after its GEI target is published will likely spend its first compliance year catching up rather than competing on equal footing with units that prepared earlier.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, and when did it start?

The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, 2023 is India's compliance carbon market, notified by the Ministry of Power on 28 June 2023 under powers inserted into the Energy Conservation Act, 2001 by the 2022 amendment. It sets greenhouse gas emission intensity targets for notified sectors and lets obligated entities trade Carbon Credit Certificates to meet those targets, building on the earlier Perform, Achieve and Trade framework for energy efficiency.

Which sectors and companies currently have emission intensity targets under the scheme?

BEE has notified targets in two batches: aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali and pulp and paper from 8 October 2025, covering 282 entities, and refineries, petrochemicals, textiles and secondary aluminium from 13 January 2026, covering a further 208 entities. Draft targets for iron and steel followed in June 2026, and fertiliser sector targets remained unconfirmed as finalised at the time of writing.

What exactly happens if a company simply misses its emission intensity target?

An obligated entity that misses its target must acquire Carbon Credit Certificates to cover the shortfall or face the consequences under the compliance mechanism, reported to include a six-month bar on certificate trading in addition to any penalty available under the Energy Conservation Act, 2001. The exact current penalty terms should be confirmed against BEE's finalised compliance procedure.

How are Carbon Credit Certificates actually bought and sold?

Certificates are meant to trade through power exchanges registered with and regulated by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission, which can set floor and forbearance prices and issue directions during abnormal trading activity. The Grid Controller of India is reported to operate the central registry, while the Bureau of Energy Efficiency acts as the scheme's Administrator for registration and transaction procedures.

Can a company that is not yet an obligated entity still participate in the carbon credit market voluntarily?

Yes, through the scheme's voluntary offset mechanism, added by a December 2023 amendment. Entities across energy, industry, waste, agriculture, forestry and transport can register eligible mitigation projects under BEE-approved methodologies and earn Carbon Credit Certificates tradable in the same market, provided the chosen project type sits within an approved methodology.

Does this scheme replace the need for environmental clearance or pollution control board consent?

No. The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme operates independently of environmental clearance under the EIA Notification, 2006 and State Pollution Control Board consents, which continue to apply on their own terms regardless of a unit's emission intensity target status, and it is also a separate scheme from the government's Green Credit Programme.

How is this scheme different from the earlier PAT scheme?

The Perform, Achieve and Trade scheme set energy consumption targets for designated consumers and let them trade Energy Saving Certificates. The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme uses a similar target-and-trade structure but targets greenhouse gas emission intensity directly rather than energy consumption, trading Carbon Credit Certificates instead, though much of the underlying reporting infrastructure carries over from one scheme to the other.

Who verifies an obligated entity's reported emissions before it gets a certificate?

An Accredited Carbon Verification Agency, a body BEE has separately accredited for the relevant sector and mechanism, checks the entity's performance assessment and issues a verification report. That report must be submitted to BEE and the State Designated Agency within four months of the compliance year ending, and BEE's own public list names the agencies currently accredited for each sector and mechanism combination.
