Consumer Complaints Against Foreign Airlines, OTAs and E-Commerce Platforms Operating in India

An NRI whose Emirates flight loses a bag, or a Delhi shopper who never gets a refund from an overseas marketplace seller, usually assumes the foreign counterparty is simply out of reach. Indian consumer law was built around the opposite assumption.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 16 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A traveller flying Emirates or Lufthansa, a family booking a package holiday through an online travel agent headquartered in Singapore or the Netherlands, and an NRI ordering electronics from an overseas marketplace seller all share the same instinctive worry once something goes wrong: the counterparty has no office, and sometimes no obvious presence at all, inside India. Indian consumer law has dealt with versions of this problem for decades, long before online marketplaces existed, because international air travel has always involved a foreign carrier selling a ticket to an Indian resident. The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and the rules framed under it carry that older learning forward and extend it to platforms that never open an office in India but sell into the Indian market anyway. The remedies this framework gives are real, and Indian consumer commissions have used them against foreign airlines more than once, but they come with jurisdictional conditions, limitation periods and a set of international conventions that cut across them in ways a complainant filing without legal advice often gets wrong.

Key points

  • Section 34(2) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 lets a complainant file where he or she resides or personally works for gain, as an alternative to filing where the opposite party carries on business, which is what lets an Indian consumer sue a foreign airline or seller from home rather than travelling to wherever that party is based.
  • A consumer commission in Air France v. O.P. Srivastava (NCDRC, 2018) held that a foreign carrier's cause of action arises, at least in part, wherever its ticket was bought through an accredited agent, upholding a finding of deficiency in service for denying boarding to a passenger with a confirmed ticket.
  • International baggage loss, damage and delay claims against airlines sit inside the liability limits set by the Montreal Convention, 1999 as given effect in India through the Carriage by Air Act, 1972; the per-passenger baggage limit was revised to 1,288 Special Drawing Rights with effect from 28 December 2019 and has since been revised again, so the current figure should be checked before any claim is valued.
  • The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission has applied these Montreal Convention limits while still finding foreign carriers, Emirates among them, liable for deficiency in service on the facts, which means the Convention caps compensation for the baggage loss itself without barring the consumer complaint altogether.
  • The Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 reach an e-commerce entity that is not based in India but systematically offers goods or services to Indian consumers, and require it to appoint someone to handle grievances, acknowledge a complaint quickly and resolve it within a defined period.
  • A clause in an airline's conditions of carriage or an e-commerce platform's terms of use naming a foreign court or arbitration seat does not by itself remove a complaint from an Indian consumer commission's jurisdiction if the commission otherwise has jurisdiction under the Act.
  • Section 69 of the Act gives a consumer two years from the date the cause of action arose to file a complaint, a period a commission may condone for sufficient cause recorded in writing, which matters for NRI complainants who often take longer than resident consumers to discover a problem or decide to litigate.
  • The Central Consumer Protection Authority offers a separate, regulatory route against systemic unfair trade practices by a platform or carrier, distinct from and additional to an individual consumer's own complaint for compensation.

Why the Foreign Counterparty's Location Is Less Important Than It Seems

The first question most people ask when a foreign airline loses their luggage or an overseas online seller takes their money and never ships the goods is whether an Indian court or consumer forum can even touch the dispute. The instinct behind the question is understandable. The airline's registered office is in Dubai or Frankfurt, the marketplace seller operates out of a warehouse in Shenzhen, and the idea of travelling abroad to sue either of them, or of an Indian judgment meaning anything once it is written, feels like enough of a deterrent that most consumers simply write off the loss.

Indian consumer protection law was built to make that instinct wrong in a specific and useful way. It treats a foreign airline that sells tickets to Indian residents, or a foreign e-commerce seller that ships goods into India, as a party carrying on business within India for the purpose of consumer jurisdiction, and it lets the complaint be filed close to the consumer rather than close to the respondent. This piece works through how that jurisdiction actually operates, what the Montreal Convention does and does not limit for airline claims specifically, how the 2020 e-commerce rules reach a platform with no Indian office, and what an NRI or a foreign consumer filing a complaint from outside India needs to get right procedurally.

None of this turns a consumer complaint into a guaranteed result. Enforcement against a respondent with no assets in India remains genuinely harder than enforcement against a domestic company, a point this piece does not gloss over. What the law does give a consumer, reliably enough that Indian commissions have exercised it against major international carriers more than once, is a forum close to home and a cause of action that does not evaporate just because the other side is foreign.

The Three-Tier Forum and Where a Foreign-Carrier Complaint Actually Lands

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 keeps the three-tier structure consumer litigants have worked with since the 1986 Act: a District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission for lower-value claims, a State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission for claims within a defined higher band and for appeals from District Commissions, and the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission at the apex, both for high-value original complaints and for appeals from State Commissions. The pecuniary jurisdiction bands have shifted since the 2019 Act replaced value-of-goods-plus-compensation-claimed with the value of goods or services paid as consideration, and a complainant should check the current threshold notification rather than rely on an older figure, since both the District Commission's upper limit and the State Commission's band have been the subject of more than one notification since the Act commenced.

For most individual airline, travel-agent and marketplace disputes, even a significant one involving a cancelled international package holiday or a materially damaged shipment, the claim value will usually fall within District or State Commission jurisdiction rather than requiring a complaint directly before the National Commission. A complainant should resist the temptation to inflate a claim simply to reach a higher, seemingly more authoritative forum, since pecuniary jurisdiction is assessed on the value actually paid and the compensation genuinely claimed, not on where the complainant would prefer to be heard.

What changes for a foreign-carrier or foreign-platform dispute is not the forum structure itself but the question of where, within that structure, the complaint can be filed, which Section 34(2) and its equivalents for the State and National Commissions answer in a way that works decisively in an Indian-resident complainant's favour.

Section 34(2): Filing Where the Consumer Lives, Not Where the Business Is

Section 34(2) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 allows a complaint to be filed before a District Commission within whose local limits the opposite party resides or carries on business, within whose limits the cause of action, wholly or in part, arose, or, and this is the clause that matters most for a dispute with a foreign respondent, within whose local limits the complainant resides or personally works for gain. This last ground did not exist in quite the same form under the 1986 Act, which generally required the complaint to be filed where the respondent could be found, and its inclusion in the 2019 Act was specifically meant to make litigation easier for consumers rather than easier for the businesses they complain against.

In practical terms, this means an Indian resident who bought an international air ticket, booked a holiday through an online travel agent, or ordered goods from an overseas marketplace seller can generally file the complaint before the District Commission closest to home, regardless of where the airline, the travel agent or the seller is actually based. The State and National Commissions carry equivalent territorial provisions for complaints and appeals that fall within their respective pecuniary bands, so the same principle extends up the forum structure rather than disappearing once the claim value grows.

A separate and older line of authority, developed under the 1986 Act's equivalent provision and still treated as good law for working out what counts as a cause of action, holds that even a small part of the transaction occurring within a forum's local limits is enough to found jurisdiction there. This is the principle the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission applied in 2018 when it held that a cause of action against Air France had arisen, at least in part, in Kanpur because the air tickets in question had been purchased there through an accredited travel agent, even though the airline itself had no office in that city. A foreign airline or an online travel agent that sells through Indian-based agents, affiliate websites or local payment gateways is, on this reasoning, unlikely to avoid Indian jurisdiction simply by having no branch office of its own on Indian soil.

Air France v. O.P. Srivastava: What a Foreign Carrier Actually Faced

The 2018 National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission decision in Air France v. O.P. Srivastava and others is worth setting out in some detail because it is the clearest reported instance of a foreign carrier losing a jurisdictional objection before an Indian consumer forum on facts that will recur for any airline selling tickets into India through local agents. The underlying complaint concerned passengers denied boarding on an Air France flight despite holding confirmed tickets, a classic overbooking scenario, and the airline resisted the complaint on the footing that the State Commission in Uttar Pradesh had no business hearing a dispute involving a French carrier.

The Commission rejected that objection, reasoning that the tickets had been bought in Kanpur through an agent accredited to sell Air France tickets, which meant at least part of the transaction, and therefore part of the cause of action, had arisen within the State Commission's territorial limits. On the merits, the Commission held that denying boarding to a passenger holding a confirmed ticket amounted to a deficiency in service, and it specifically flagged the risk that unchecked overbooking by carriers would otherwise leave ticketholders with no effective remedy, upholding the compensation the State Commission had originally awarded.

The decision does not stand for the proposition that every foreign carrier is automatically subject to Indian consumer jurisdiction regardless of how a ticket was sold. What it establishes is narrower and more durable: a foreign carrier that sells tickets into India through Indian-based agents, whether traditional travel agencies or, by extension, online intermediaries operating from Indian servers and Indian bank accounts, has carried on enough business within India for a consumer commission to treat the transaction as giving rise to a cause of action here.

Baggage, Delay and the Limits the Montreal Convention Actually Imposes

International carriage of passengers and baggage sits under a different and more specific liability regime than an ordinary deficiency-in-service claim, because India is a party to the Montreal Convention, 1999, given domestic effect through the Carriage by Air Act, 1972. The Convention caps an airline's liability for baggage loss, damage and delay at a figure expressed in Special Drawing Rights rather than rupees, reviewed by the International Civil Aviation Organization roughly every five years to keep pace with inflation. The baggage figure was revised upward to 1,288 Special Drawing Rights per passenger with effect from 28 December 2019, and a further ICAO revision took effect from 28 December 2024, so the applicable figure for a claim should be checked against the currently effective ICAO table and its rupee equivalent on the date of the claim rather than assumed from an older report.

The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission's approach to baggage claims against foreign carriers, illustrated by its decision in Gaganjeet Bhullar v. Emirates, has been to accept that the Convention's cap genuinely limits the quantum an Indian consumer forum can award for the baggage loss itself, while still finding the carrier liable for deficiency in service on the facts and awarding compensation within that cap. The Convention caps what can be recovered for the baggage; it does not strip the consumer forum of jurisdiction to hear the complaint, and a carrier cannot treat the existence of the Convention as a reason the complaint should not be entertained at all.

A complainant valuing an international baggage claim should therefore keep two figures separate from the outset: the Montreal Convention cap, which governs compensation for the baggage itself, and any separate claim for the airline's deficiency in handling the complaint afterwards, such as an unreasonably delayed response, a refusal to process a valid claim, or mental agony genuinely flowing from the airline's conduct rather than from the loss of the baggage as such, which Indian consumer commissions have treated as a distinct head of compensation not capped by the Convention in the same way.

Domestic Airlines as the More Frequent, if Less Instructive, Comparator

Most of the reported consumer commission activity against airlines operating in India in the last two years has, unsurprisingly, involved domestic carriers rather than foreign ones, simply because domestic carriers fly far more Indian routes and generate far more complaints. District and State Commissions across the country have held domestic carriers liable for a wide range of service failures, from mishandled group baggage to denied boarding after a schedule change to seating passengers in conditions other than what they paid for, with awards that have run from a few thousand rupees in litigation costs up to figures in several lakhs for more serious failures.

These domestic rulings are useful to a foreign carrier or an NRI complainant mainly as evidence of how consumer commissions actually approach airline disputes procedurally: they treat a passenger's confirmed ticket and boarding pass as establishing the contract and its terms, they treat an airline's own conditions of carriage as relevant but not conclusive on what compensation a consumer is entitled to, and they have shown little patience for an airline's argument that a contractual limitation clause ousts the commission's jurisdiction or caps compensation below what the Act itself would otherwise allow. A foreign carrier facing a similar complaint should expect the same procedural posture, adjusted only by whichever international convention specifically applies to the claim in question.

Online Travel Agents: Working Out Who Is Actually Liable

A booking made through an online travel agent, whether a global platform with no Indian corporate presence or an Indian-incorporated subsidiary of a foreign group, routinely involves at least three potential respondents: the airline or hotel actually supplying the service, the online travel agent that took the booking and payment, and occasionally a further intermediary, such as a wholesale consolidator, sitting between the two. A consumer who files a complaint naming only one of these parties risks the respondent arguing that the real deficiency, a cancelled flight or an unavailable hotel room, was caused by a different party in the chain and that the named respondent is not the right one to answer for it.

The safer practice, and the one Indian consumer commissions have generally been comfortable with, is to name every party in the booking chain whose conduct could plausibly have caused the loss, leaving the commission to apportion liability between them once the facts are established, rather than the complainant guessing in advance which single party was actually at fault. An online travel agent that collected payment and issued a confirmation is rarely able to escape a complaint entirely by pointing to the underlying supplier, particularly where the agent's own platform made representations about availability, price or cancellation terms that turned out not to be honoured.

Where the online travel agent is a foreign entity with no separate Indian subsidiary, the same territorial jurisdiction analysis that applies to a foreign airline applies here as well: a platform that takes bookings from Indian consumers, processes payment through Indian payment gateways, and markets itself to Indian customers has, on the reasoning consumer commissions have applied to foreign airlines, carried on enough business in India to found a complaint here.

The Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 and Platforms With No Indian Office

Online marketplace and travel-booking disputes that do not involve air carriage at all, an overseas seller that never ships the goods paid for, a platform that misrepresents a product's origin or specifications, sit under a separate but related framework, the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020, notified under the 2019 Act. These rules were drafted specifically to reach e-commerce entities that are not incorporated or headquartered in India but that systematically offer goods or services to consumers located in India, rather than letting a platform argue that having no registered office here puts it beyond the rules' reach.

The rules require every e-commerce entity, whether it operates a marketplace model connecting third-party sellers to buyers or an inventory model selling its own stock, to establish a grievance redressal mechanism and to appoint someone responsible for handling consumer grievances, whose name and contact details the platform is required to display. Commentary on the rules consistently describes a requirement that a complaint be acknowledged within forty-eight hours of receipt and resolved within a defined period measured in weeks rather than months, though the precise rule number and any amendments since the 2020 notification should be checked against the current gazette text before being cited in a filing, since this piece has relied on secondary legal commentary rather than the bare notification for this specific procedural detail.

A seller or platform's failure to appoint a grievance officer, or to respond within the time the rules require, does not by itself give rise to a separate claim for damages in an individual consumer's complaint, but it is relevant conduct a commission can and does weigh when assessing whether the platform's overall handling of the dispute amounted to an unfair trade practice, which carries its own, broader remedies under the Act beyond a straightforward deficiency-in-service claim.

Why a Foreign Forum or Arbitration Clause in the Terms Rarely Helps the Platform

Airlines' conditions of carriage and e-commerce platforms' terms of use routinely include a clause naming a foreign court, a specific arbitration seat, or simply the carrier's or platform's home jurisdiction as the exclusive forum for disputes. Indian consumer commissions and courts reviewing their decisions have consistently treated this kind of clause as incapable of ousting a consumer forum's jurisdiction where the forum otherwise has jurisdiction under the Act, on the reasoning that the Consumer Protection Act creates a statutory remedy that private contractual terms cannot simply contract around, particularly where the consumer had no real bargaining power to negotiate the clause in the first place.

This is not a uniquely Indian position; it tracks a broader principle that consumer protection statutes are treated as conferring rights the consumer cannot be made to waive in advance through standard-form terms presented on a take-it-or-leave-it basis. A foreign respondent should not expect a jurisdiction or arbitration clause buried in its terms of service to defeat an otherwise properly filed consumer complaint, and a consumer should not be deterred from filing simply because the ticket or the platform's terms recite a foreign forum.

Limitation Under Section 69 and Why It Matters More for Complainants Abroad

Section 69 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 gives a consumer two years from the date the cause of action arose to file a complaint, and a commission may admit a complaint filed after that period only where the complainant shows sufficient cause for the delay, with the commission's reasons for condoning the delay recorded in writing. Two years sounds generous until the specific pattern many NRI and foreign complainants fall into is accounted for: a problem with a flight or an online order is noticed while the complainant is still abroad, correspondence with the airline or platform's customer service drags on for months with no resolution, and by the time the complainant is back in India, has located Indian counsel, and has decided to litigate, a meaningful portion of the two-year window has already passed.

The safer approach for any complainant in this position, and this applies equally to a resident Indian consumer dealing with a slow-moving foreign respondent, is to treat the two-year clock as running from the date the deficiency or the unfair practice occurred, or from the date a specific refusal of a claim was communicated, and to file well within that period rather than relying on the possibility that a commission will condone a late filing. Commissions have condoned delay in appropriate cases, but condonation is discretionary, requires a specific and credible explanation, and is not something a complainant should treat as a backstop for simply not getting around to filing on time.

The Central Consumer Protection Authority's Separate Route

Alongside an individual consumer's right to file a complaint for personal compensation, the 2019 Act created the Central Consumer Protection Authority with its own power to act against unfair trade practices and false or misleading advertisements that affect consumers as a class, rather than only the individual complainant in front of it. Where a foreign airline or platform's conduct looks less like an isolated service failure and more like a pattern, a recurring refusal to honour cancellation policies advertised to Indian consumers, or a systematic practice of listing products as available when they are not, a complaint to the Authority can trigger a regulatory inquiry and class-level remedies distinct from, and additional to, any individual consumer complaint already filed or still being pursued.

The two routes are not mutually exclusive and an individual consumer with a personal claim for compensation should not treat a report to the Authority as a substitute for filing a complaint seeking that compensation, since the Authority's own proceedings are aimed at systemic conduct and do not, by themselves, deliver a personal remedy in the way an individual complaint before a District, State or National Commission does.

Serving Notice and Getting a Foreign Respondent to Actually Appear

A jurisdictional win on paper is only useful if the foreign respondent can be served and made to appear, or if the commission is prepared to proceed and decide the matter on the available record where the respondent chooses not to. Consumer commissions are not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure's formal service requirements in the way an ordinary civil court is, and in practice most airlines and larger e-commerce platforms maintain an Indian subsidiary, a registered travel agent network, or at minimum a customer-service address and email that a commission will accept for service, particularly once the complainant can show that correspondence was actually exchanged with the respondent at that address over the dispute itself.

Where a foreign respondent genuinely has no Indian presence of any kind, no subsidiary, no agent, no local payment processor, service becomes a more serious practical obstacle, and a complainant in that position should expect the process to take longer and should keep every email, chat transcript and booking confirmation that shows the respondent's awareness of the dispute, since this correspondence often ends up doing double duty: it supports the territorial jurisdiction argument and it supports service where the respondent later claims it was never properly notified of the proceedings.

A foreign respondent that simply does not appear after being served does not stop the commission from proceeding. Consumer commissions routinely decide cases ex parte on the complainant's evidence where a duly served respondent fails to contest the complaint, and an award made this way is enforceable in the same way as a contested one, which is a meaningful practical advantage for a complainant facing a foreign respondent uninterested in engaging with Indian proceedings at all.

Filing From Abroad: What an NRI Complainant Needs to Get Right

An NRI or a foreign consumer who wants to file a complaint while physically outside India can generally do so through a duly authorised representative, typically a family member or an advocate holding a power of attorney specifically authorising the filing and conduct of the consumer complaint, since personal physical presence at every hearing is not required and most commissions accommodate representation through counsel in any event. What matters more than physical presence is ensuring the underlying documentation, the ticket or booking confirmation, payment records, correspondence with the airline or platform, and any formal rejection of a claim, is assembled and retained from the outset, since consumer commissions decide most disputes on documentary evidence rather than oral testimony, and a complainant who has let the paper trail go cold while dealing with customer service abroad often finds it harder to reconstruct months later.

A complainant should also identify, as early as possible, every entity in the booking or purchase chain that could plausibly be a proper respondent, verify the correct corporate name and any Indian subsidiary or agent through which the transaction actually passed, and file in the District Commission closest to where the complainant resides or personally works for gain under Section 34(2), rather than assuming a complaint must somehow be filed where the foreign respondent is based. Doing this correctly at the outset avoids the delay and expense of a jurisdictional objection that, on the authority discussed in this piece, the foreign respondent is unlikely to win in any event, but that it will very often still raise if given the opportunity.

The distance between an Indian consumer and a foreign airline or e-commerce platform is a practical difficulty, not a legal bar. Section 34(2) lets the complaint be filed close to home, the territorial reasoning applied against Air France in 2018 extends naturally to any foreign carrier or platform selling through Indian-based agents or payment channels, and the 2020 e-commerce rules were drafted specifically to prevent a foreign seller from treating the absence of an Indian office as a shield. What a complainant does need to respect is the Montreal Convention's cap on baggage and delay claims against airlines, the two-year limitation period under Section 69, and the practical reality that a documented, well-identified respondent reaches a quicker and more defensible result than a complaint filed in haste against the wrong name.

For a platform or carrier reading this from the other side, the lesson is the mirror image: an Indian consumer commission will not treat a foreign forum clause as a reason to decline jurisdiction, and will readily find a cause of action wherever an Indian agent, payment gateway or marketing channel was actually used, so compliance with the grievance-handling timelines the 2020 rules require is a materially cheaper way to resolve most disputes than defending a jurisdictional objection that the reported case law gives little reason to expect will succeed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I file a consumer complaint in India against a foreign airline that has no office here?

Generally yes. Section 34(2) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 lets you file where you reside or work for gain, and consumer commissions have held that a foreign carrier selling tickets through Indian-based agents or payment channels has carried on enough business in India to found a complaint here, regardless of whether it has its own branch office.

Does the Montreal Convention stop me from suing an airline for lost baggage in an Indian consumer forum?

No, but it limits what you can recover for the baggage loss itself. The Convention, given effect through the Carriage by Air Act, 1972, caps baggage liability at a figure in Special Drawing Rights that is revised periodically; consumer commissions have applied this cap while still finding airlines liable for deficiency in service on the facts.

Is there a time limit to file a complaint against an airline or an online platform?

Yes. Section 69 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 gives you two years from the date the cause of action arose. A commission can admit a complaint filed later only if you show sufficient cause for the delay, recorded in writing, so it is safer to file within the two years than to rely on a late filing being excused.

Can an airline or e-commerce platform avoid an Indian consumer complaint by pointing to a foreign court clause in its terms?

Not usually. Indian consumer commissions have treated such clauses as unable to oust a commission's statutory jurisdiction where the commission otherwise has jurisdiction under the Act, particularly where the consumer had no real opportunity to negotiate the term.

Do the e-commerce rules apply to a foreign seller with no Indian company or office?

Yes. The Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 were drafted to reach an e-commerce entity that is not based in India but systematically offers goods or services to consumers located in India, and such an entity is required to set up a grievance redressal mechanism reachable by Indian consumers.

Who should I name as the respondent if I booked through an online travel agent and the airline cancelled my flight?

It is generally safer to name both the airline and the online travel agent as respondents, since the commission can apportion liability between them once the facts are established, rather than risk one respondent arguing the real fault lies with the other party you did not name.

Can I file and pursue a consumer complaint from outside India as an NRI?

Yes. You can generally act through an advocate or a family member holding a power of attorney specifically authorising the complaint, and most of the case is decided on documents such as tickets, payment records and correspondence rather than requiring your personal presence at every hearing.

What is the difference between filing an individual complaint and reporting a platform to the Central Consumer Protection Authority?

An individual complaint before a District, State or National Commission seeks compensation for your own loss. A report to the Central Consumer Protection Authority addresses systemic unfair trade practices affecting consumers generally and does not by itself deliver personal compensation, so the two routes are complementary rather than alternatives.
