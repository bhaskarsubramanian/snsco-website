Unauthorised Construction, Sealing and Demolition in Delhi: The MCD Process and Its Broken Appeal Route

How Sections 343, 344 and 345A of the DMC Act work together, what the Appellate Tribunal MCD vacancy means in practice, and the remedies open to owners, buyers and neighbours

By Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A demolition or sealing notice from the Municipal Corporation of Delhi sets a short, anxious clock running for the owner, the occupant, and often the neighbours who complained in the first place. The statute sets out a fairly clear sequence of notice, order and appeal, but the appeal forum that sequence depends on, the Appellate Tribunal MCD, has gone without a Presiding Officer often enough through 2025 and into 2026 that owners facing enforcement action today need to know not just what the law provides on paper but what actually happens when the one forum built to hear their appeal is not sitting.

Key points

  • Unauthorised construction within an already-approved colony or plot, the subject of this piece, is a different problem from an unauthorised colony itself; Delhi's 2026 regularisation of 1,511 unauthorised colonies on an as-is basis does not touch illegal building work on a plot that already has sanctioned layout.
  • MCD's enforcement runs through linked but distinct powers: Section 343 for demolition and stoppage of unauthorised building work, Section 344 for stopping ongoing construction, and Section 345A, inserted in 1985, for sealing premises built or used in breach of the Act.
  • Courts have consistently read a show cause notice requirement into this scheme even where the bare text does not spell one out in every section, and notices or orders that do not specify the date and extent of the alleged unauthorised construction have been struck down as bad in law.
  • An order under Section 343 or 347B is appealable to the Appellate Tribunal MCD, constituted under Section 347A, with a further appeal under Section 347D to the Administrator, in practice the District and Sessions Judge.
  • Through much of 2025 and into 2026, the Appellate Tribunal MCD has repeatedly had no sitting Presiding Officer, leaving pending appeals undecided and forcing affected owners to approach the Delhi High Court directly for interim protection pending appointment.
  • Compounding of minor deviations from sanctioned building plans is available under the Unified Building Bye-Laws, but only within a limited tolerance; courts have repeatedly stressed that compounding is an exception to be used sparingly, not a routine substitute for compliance.
  • A neighbour's standing to challenge unauthorised construction is not automatic; the Delhi High Court has held that only a person directly and immediately affected, typically an adjoining occupant whose light, air or access is impacted, can maintain such a challenge.

Two different problems that get confused

Delhi runs two largely separate legal tracks for buildings that do not conform to the rules, and conflating them leads to bad advice. One track concerns entire unauthorised colonies, settlements that grew up without an approved layout plan at all, which the Centre and the Delhi government have been working through a regularisation exercise for years. In April 2026 the Union Housing and Urban Affairs Ministry announced that 1,511 such colonies would be regularised on an as-is, where-is basis, with roughly 220 colonies excluded for lying in protected areas such as the ridge, notified forests or the Yamuna floodplain, and with the Municipal Corporation of Delhi issuing certificates of regularisation while the Delhi government's Revenue Department handles conveyance and authorisation documentation through the PM-UDAY framework. This piece is not about that scheme.

The second track, the one this piece addresses, concerns unauthorised construction on a plot or in a building that sits within an already approved, lawful colony or layout: an extra floor built without sanction, coverage or floor area ratio exceeded beyond what the sanctioned plan allows, a residential structure put to commercial use, or a building put up with no sanctioned plan at all despite sitting on land where plans are required. For this category, colony-wide regularisation is irrelevant; what matters is the Delhi Municipal Corporation Act, 1957, and the specific enforcement and appeal machinery it sets up.

The colony-level exercise is still worth knowing about if only to rule it out correctly. Implementation runs through the MCD, which issues certificates of regularisation, and the Delhi government's Revenue Department, which separately handles conveyance deeds and authorisation slips, with applications filed online through the MCD's SWAGAM portal and district-wise cells headed by Additional District Magistrates set up to speed processing. The Delhi Chief Minister has sought additional central funding to accelerate the exercise, and GIS surveys of the colonies covered were reported to still be ongoing as of the latest available reporting. None of this machinery, however, has any bearing on a building that already sits in a sanctioned colony but has itself been built or altered beyond what its own sanctioned plan permits, which is the situation most owners and buyers facing a demolition or sealing notice actually find themselves in.

The enforcement sequence: Sections 343, 344 and 345A

MCD's core demolition power sits in Section 343 of the DMC Act, under which the Commissioner can order the demolition of a building or part of a building erected in contravention of the Act, typically after a show cause notice calling on the owner to explain the alleged unauthorised work. Section 344 operates alongside it, letting the Commissioner stop construction that is still in progress where it contravenes the Act, so that enforcement proceedings often cite both sections together, one addressed to what has already been built and the other to what is still being built.

Section 345A, inserted into the Act by a 1984 amendment with effect from 10 December 1985, gives the Commissioner a further, more immediately disruptive power: to seal a building or part of it that has been erected or is being used in contravention of the Act. The provision was drafted to work alongside Sections 343 and 344 rather than replace them, letting the Commissioner seal premises either before or after a demolition order under Section 343 or a stoppage order under Section 344 has been made, and the Delhi Municipal Corporation (Sealing of Unauthorised Construction) Rules, 1986 set out the procedure for exercising it. Whether this sealing power also reaches misuse of premises, as opposed to purely structural contravention, was itself disputed for years. A Full Bench of the Delhi courts initially answered that question in the negative in the Bajaj Departmental Stores line of cases, before the Supreme Court took the contrary view and held that the Commissioner is empowered to use the sealing power in a case of misuse of premises too, and is not confined to purely structural violations.

In practice, enforcement teams typically move through this sequence in order: a Section 344 stoppage notice if construction is ongoing, a Section 343 show cause notice and demolition order addressed to what has been built, and a Section 345A sealing action, often following its own separate show cause notice, where the Commissioner decides sealing is the more effective or more immediately available remedy. An owner receiving any one of these notices should check carefully which section it invokes, since the grounds, the appeal route and the available defences are not identical across the three.

Natural justice: the show cause notice courts keep insisting on

None of these three sections, read in isolation, spells out an elaborate procedural code, and MCD has at times argued that urgent enforcement action does not require the same process a civil dispute would. The Delhi High Court has not accepted that argument as a general proposition. Even for Section 345A, which does not expressly mandate a hearing, the courts have read in the principles of natural justice, holding that a show cause notice is required before sealing because the provision, standing on its own text, would otherwise let the Commissioner act without giving the owner any chance to respond.

What that show cause notice must actually say has also been tested repeatedly. Courts have struck down demolition and sealing orders for failing to specify the particulars of the alleged unauthorised construction, including the date on which the construction is said to have occurred and its extent, treating an order that leaves the owner guessing at what exactly is being complained of as bad in law regardless of whether unauthorised construction may, in fact, exist. Owners who receive a vague or generic notice, one that recites the sections invoked without identifying the specific structure, floor, or use being objected to, have a real procedural point to raise, separate from and in addition to any argument on the merits about whether the construction is actually unauthorised.

Recent 2025 and 2026 orders illustrate how far the High Court will go to enforce this in urgent cases: in one matter, the court treated an abrupt notice giving the occupant only forty-eight hours before sealing as itself amounting to a show cause notice, gave the occupant two weeks to respond, and restrained coercive action in the meantime, precisely because the original notice had given no real opportunity to be heard before action. In another, the court granted temporary de-sealing where the property had been sealed without any notice or communication of a final order at all, and where the dates on the sealing paperwork did not add up.

These rulings do not mean every procedural objection succeeds. Where MCD can show that a proper notice was in fact served, that it specified the violation with enough particularity, and that the owner simply chose not to respond within the time given, courts have been equally willing to let the enforcement action stand. The practical lesson for an owner is to respond to a show cause notice promptly and on the merits, since a notice that is procedurally sound leaves little room to resist on natural justice grounds alone, whatever substantive defence may remain available on the underlying question of whether the construction is actually unauthorised.

The appeal route: Appellate Tribunal MCD and the second appeal

A person aggrieved by a demolition order under Section 343, or by other appealable orders and notices that Section 347B brings within the same scheme, including a sealing order, can appeal to the Appellate Tribunal MCD, a tribunal constituted under Section 347A specifically to hear appeals under Section 343 or Section 347B. The Tribunal has power under Section 343(3) to grant interim relief, including a stay of enforcement, while the appeal is pending, though it has no obligation to grant a stay automatically and has in reported matters declined to stay demolition or sealing merely because an appeal is pending.

A further appeal lies from the Tribunal's decision to the Administrator under Section 347D, a role that in practice is exercised by the District and Sessions Judge, against an order of the Tribunal confirming, modifying or annulling the order or notice under challenge. Interim or interlocutory orders of the Tribunal, such as a refusal to grant a stay, have been held not to be independently appealable under Section 347D, which is confined to the Tribunal's substantive orders disposing of the appeal rather than to procedural rulings along the way.

Interim relief pending appeal is discretionary, not automatic

Filing an appeal before the Appellate Tribunal MCD does not, by itself, halt demolition or sealing. The owner must specifically seek interim relief under Section 343(3), and the Tribunal exercises that power on the facts of the individual case rather than granting a stay as a matter of course once an appeal is on file. In reported matters the Tribunal has declined to stay enforcement where it was not satisfied the appeal raised a genuine, substantial challenge, leaving the demolition or sealing to proceed even while the appeal itself remained pending for final hearing.

This matters because owners sometimes treat the act of filing an appeal as protection in itself, only to find enforcement proceeding regardless because no interim order was actually sought or granted. A properly drafted appeal should address the interim relief question as a distinct matter, supported by whatever specific grounds exist for believing the underlying order is wrong, rather than leaving the Tribunal to infer urgency from the mere fact that demolition or sealing has been ordered.

A tribunal that keeps going vacant

This appeal structure depends entirely on the Appellate Tribunal MCD actually having a sitting Presiding Officer, and for long stretches through 2025 and into 2026 it has not. One Delhi High Court order records that the post of Presiding Officer fell vacant from 6 August 2025 following the previous officer's term expiring, leaving pending appeals in limbo. A new Presiding Officer appears to have taken charge by November 2025, based on a court order from that month proceeding on that footing, but the relief was short lived: orders from April and May 2026 again record the Tribunal as not functional and awaiting appointment of a Presiding Officer, with one May 2026 contempt matter noting explicitly that the writ petition before it had been necessitated precisely because, at present, there is no Presiding Officer in the Appellate Tribunal MCD.

For an owner with a demolition or sealing order against them, this is not an abstract administrative footnote. An appeal that cannot be heard because there is no one to hear it leaves the owner caught between MCD's enforcement power, which does not pause simply because the appellate forum is non-functional, and a statutory remedy that exists on paper but cannot be exercised in practice. The Delhi High Court's response in this gap has been to step in directly: in reported 2025 and 2026 matters, the Court has granted interim protection against coercive action specifically on account of the Tribunal's non-functional state, sometimes on the strength of MCD's own assurance that no further action would be taken in the interim, and has in at least one instance left the petitioner free to revive the application once a new Presiding Officer actually takes charge.

The practical lesson for anyone facing a demolition or sealing order today is not to file an appeal before the Tribunal and then simply wait. Given the Tribunal's recurring vacancies, an owner should check, before filing, whether a Presiding Officer is currently in post, and if the appeal cannot realistically be heard in the near term, should consider approaching the Delhi High Court directly by way of a writ petition for interim protection pending the Tribunal's appeal being heard, rather than relying on the appeal alone to hold off enforcement.

Regularisation and compounding: a narrow door, not a routine fix

Separate from colony-wide regularisation, the Unified Building Bye-Laws for Delhi, 2016 allow the sanctioning authority to compound certain deviations from a sanctioned building plan on payment of a compounding fee, bringing the building within the scheme of compoundable deviations set out in the bye-laws rather than treating every departure from the sanctioned plan as grounds for demolition. The Delhi High Court has in reported matters accepted that excess coverage or floor area ratio, where it falls within the compoundable category, can be regularised this way, after which the property is treated as compliant.

This door, however, is deliberately narrow rather than routine. The Supreme Court's reasoning in the Friends Colony line of authority, which Delhi courts continue to apply, treats compounding as an exception to be kept at a bare minimum rather than as an alternative compliance route available by right, and deviations beyond the compoundable tolerance the bye-laws permit remain liable to demolition regardless of an owner's willingness to pay a fee. An owner relying on compounding should also expect MCD to first require removal of whatever deviations are not compoundable before it will even consider regularising the rest, a sequencing that has featured in several 2025 Delhi High Court orders dealing with pending regularisation applications, and should not assume that simply offering to pay a compounding fee will itself halt an enforcement action already in motion.

Owners should also keep in mind that a compounding application does not run on its own clock independent of the enforcement proceeding; a demolition or sealing notice already issued does not automatically go into abeyance merely because a compounding or regularisation application has been filed afterward, and an owner who wants that breathing room should ask for it specifically, either from MCD itself or, where MCD refuses or delays, from the Tribunal or the High Court, rather than assuming that filing the application is itself enough to pause the clock.

Writ jurisdiction: when the Delhi High Court will step in directly

Article 226 writ jurisdiction over MCD action is available throughout this process, but the Delhi High Court has not treated it as a substitute for the statutory appeal in every case. Where the complaint is a straightforward procedural one, no show cause notice, a notice that does not specify the alleged violation, or action taken in the teeth of an existing stay, courts have been willing to intervene directly rather than insisting the owner first exhaust the Tribunal route, particularly now that the Tribunal itself has so often been without a Presiding Officer to actually hear the appeal.

Where the dispute instead turns on a genuinely factual question, whether the construction in fact exceeds the sanctioned plan, whether a particular use amounts to misuse, or whether a compounding application has been correctly assessed, courts are more inclined to leave that determination to the statutory forum, intervening by writ mainly to compel a decision within a reasonable time or to grant interim protection while that forum, or its substitute given the vacancy, actually considers the matter. Owners should accordingly frame a writ petition around the specific procedural or jurisdictional defect in the action taken against them, rather than asking the High Court to redo, as a first-instance fact-finder, the assessment that the Act assigns to MCD and the Tribunal.

Contempt proceedings have also become a recurring feature of this area of practice, precisely because the Tribunal's vacancy keeps forcing owners back to the High Court to enforce protections the Court itself had already granted. Several of the April and May 2026 orders referenced above arose as contempt petitions rather than fresh writ petitions, with owners returning to court to complain that MCD had resumed or threatened enforcement despite an earlier order restraining coercive action pending the Tribunal's appeal being heard. An owner who has already obtained interim protection from the High Court should keep that order readily available and be prepared to invoke it immediately, by way of a contempt application if necessary, rather than assuming the protection will be self-enforcing against a Commissioner's office handling a large volume of unrelated enforcement files.

A neighbour's remedies, and their limits

Neighbours who object to construction next door do not have an open-ended right to litigate it. The Delhi High Court has held plainly that only persons directly affected by the unauthorised construction, generally immediate neighbours living in the vicinity of the property in question, are entitled to file a petition against it, and that simply being a resident of the same locality, without more, is not enough to found standing. A neighbour asserting a concrete harm, commonly interference with light, air or access, or a specific safety concern arising from the construction itself, stands on firmer ground than one objecting in the abstract to a structure that does not directly affect their own property.

A neighbour also has a civil remedy independent of complaining to MCD: a suit for injunction or damages where the unauthorised construction infringes a legal right such as easement of light and air, and the Delhi High Court has been protective of that route, in one 2025 matter dismissing as frivolous, and penalising with costs, an attempt by the party carrying out the construction to use a writ petition to choke off a neighbour's civil suit over the same dispute. At the same time, courts have cautioned against the opposite misuse, treating litigation over unauthorised construction as leverage to extract money from whoever is building, and have been willing to impose costs where a petition looks like exactly that rather than a genuine grievance.

A neighbour who has lodged a complaint with MCD and obtained a stop-construction or demolition notice should not assume the matter ends there if MCD then fails to act. The Delhi High Court has, in such cases, directed the authority to inspect and act in accordance with law rather than closing the file, which gives a neighbour a basis to return to court if MCD's own notice is left unenforced for an unreasonable time, though the court will generally ask MCD to act first rather than stepping into the Commissioner's shoes itself.

What a buyer should check before closing

A buyer of a built property in Delhi should treat the sanctioned building plan, not the structure as it physically stands today, as the real baseline for due diligence. Comparing the sanctioned plan against the actual constructed floors, coverage and use reveals most of the risk that matters: an extra floor built without sanction, a terrace structure, a basement put to commercial use, or coverage and floor area ratio that exceed what was approved. Any of these can expose the property to a Section 343 or 345A notice regardless of how long the deviation has stood unchallenged, and regardless of whether the current owner or a predecessor actually built it.

Where a deviation already exists, a buyer should separately check whether it has been regularised through compounding, since an uncompounded deviation remains a live enforcement risk that transfers with the property rather than staying with whoever built it, and should treat a pending demolition, sealing or compounding proceeding, wherever it is at in the Tribunal's currently uncertain functioning, as a material fact to be resolved or priced in before the transaction closes rather than something to be discovered only after possession changes hands.

A buyer should also ask the seller directly, and verify independently where possible, whether any show cause notice has ever been received for the property, even one that was later withdrawn or not acted upon, since a lapsed or dormant notice can be revived by MCD later and is not erased simply because years have passed without further action. Sale deeds and title documents rarely disclose this history on their own, which is why a specific written query to the seller, backed by an inspection of the sanctioned plan against the built structure, remains the most reliable way to surface the risk before money changes hands rather than after.

The legal structure for unauthorised construction in Delhi is not, on paper, especially complicated: a show cause notice, an order under Section 343, 344 or 345A, and an appeal to a tribunal with a further appeal beyond that. What has made the structure unreliable in practice through 2025 and 2026 is that the tribunal built to hear the first appeal has repeatedly had no one sitting on it, leaving owners dependent on the Delhi High Court's willingness to grant interim protection in the gap rather than on the statutory appeal actually functioning as intended.

Anyone facing enforcement action, buying a property with an unresolved deviation, or objecting to a neighbour's construction needs to engage with both layers of this picture: the substantive grounds and procedural requirements the DMC Act sets out, and the current, practical state of the forum meant to enforce them. Checking whether the Appellate Tribunal MCD actually has a Presiding Officer in post before relying on an appeal to hold off demolition or sealing is, as matters stand, not a precaution but a basic necessity, and a writ petition for interim protection should be treated as the realistic fallback rather than a last resort reserved for exceptional cases.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is unauthorised construction the same issue as an unauthorised colony?

No. An unauthorised colony is a settlement that grew up without an approved layout plan at all, and Delhi has been regularising such colonies, 1,511 of them as of an April 2026 announcement, on an as-is basis under the PM-UDAY framework. Unauthorised construction is illegal building work, such as an extra floor or excess coverage, within a plot or colony that already has sanctioned layout, and it is governed by separate demolition and sealing provisions of the DMC Act rather than by colony regularisation.

What is the difference between Sections 343, 344 and 345A of the DMC Act?

Section 343 lets the Commissioner order demolition of unauthorised building work. Section 344 lets the Commissioner stop construction that is still ongoing. Section 345A, inserted in 1985, lets the Commissioner seal a building or part of it that has been built or is being used in breach of the Act, and can be used before or after a Section 343 or 344 order.

Can MCD seal my property without issuing any notice?

The Delhi High Court has read a show cause notice requirement into Section 345A even though the provision does not spell one out expressly, and has struck down or restrained sealing and demolition action where no proper notice was given or where the notice did not specify the date and extent of the alleged violation. An abrupt sealing notice giving very little time to respond has, in at least one 2026 case, been treated by the court itself as a show cause notice requiring a further opportunity to reply.

Where do I appeal a demolition or sealing order?

To the Appellate Tribunal MCD, constituted under Section 347A to hear appeals against orders under Section 343 and other appealable orders and notices under Section 347B, with a further appeal under Section 347D to the Administrator, exercised in practice by the District and Sessions Judge.

Why can't I get my appeal heard by the Appellate Tribunal MCD?

The Tribunal's post of Presiding Officer has gone vacant repeatedly through 2025 and into 2026, including a vacancy recorded from 6 August 2025 and further vacancies reported in Delhi High Court orders from April and May 2026. When the Tribunal has no Presiding Officer, pending appeals cannot be decided, and affected owners have had to approach the Delhi High Court directly for interim protection in the meantime.

Can I regularise an unauthorised extra floor or excess coverage by paying a fee?

Only within a limited tolerance. The Unified Building Bye-Laws, 2016 allow compounding of certain deviations from the sanctioned plan on payment of a compounding fee, but courts treat compounding as a narrow exception rather than a routine alternative to compliance, and deviations beyond what the bye-laws allow to be compounded remain liable to demolition regardless of an owner's willingness to pay.

Can any neighbour object to construction next door?

No. The Delhi High Court has held that only a person directly and immediately affected by the unauthorised construction, typically an adjoining neighbour, has standing to challenge it; being a resident of the same general locality is not, by itself, enough. A neighbour with a concrete grievance, such as loss of light, air or access, stands on much firmer ground.

What should I check before buying a built property in Delhi?

Compare the sanctioned building plan against what has actually been constructed, checking for extra floors, excess coverage or floor area ratio, and changes of use that were never sanctioned. Check separately whether any existing deviation has been formally compounded or regularised, since an uncompounded deviation remains a live enforcement risk that transfers with the property to a new owner.
