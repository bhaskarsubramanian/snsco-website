Arbitration Practice in Kolkata: Section 9, Section 11, Section 34 and Getting an Award Paid

How the Calcutta High Court's Commercial Division actually handles interim relief, arbitrator appointment, award challenges and enforcement for a Kolkata-seated arbitration

By Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A Kolkata business that writes an arbitration clause into a contract is choosing more than a dispute mechanism; it is choosing which bench of the Calcutta High Court will sit over that arbitration from the first interim order to the last step of enforcement. This piece works through what a Kolkata-seated arbitration actually looks like in practice: how Section 9 interim relief is being granted by the Commercial Division today, how an arbitrator gets appointed under Section 11, what it takes to get an award stayed or paid once a Section 34 challenge is filed, and where ad hoc arbitration, still the ordinary route in Kolkata, differs from the institutional model some contracts assume is already running.

Key points

  • Section 9, Section 11, Section 34 and Section 37 applications arising from a Kolkata-seated arbitration go to the Commercial Division of the Calcutta High Court, and courts have held non-commercial benches lose jurisdiction over them once the Commercial Division exists.
  • Naming Kolkata in a contract as a place where the tribunal may sit is not the same as naming it the seat; the Supreme Court set aside a Calcutta High Court arbitrator appointment on exactly this distinction in Ravi Ranjan Developers.
  • Section 9(1) interim relief is wide, but Section 9(3) stops a party going back to court for the same relief once an arbitral tribunal has been constituted and is able to grant it effectively.
  • Arbitrator appointment under Section 11 in Kolkata, as everywhere else in India, still runs through the pre-2019 test confined to the existence of the arbitration agreement, because the institutional-appointment amendment was never brought into force.
  • Filing a Section 34 challenge does not stay the award automatically; a separate stay application is needed, and since the 2021 amendment an unconditional stay requires a prima facie case of fraud or corruption in how the award was obtained.
  • The Calcutta High Court has required cash deposits running into hundreds of crores as a condition for staying large awards, and has refused unconditional stay where the facts fell short of the fraud-or-corruption threshold.
  • Section 34's limitation period is three months plus a discretionary thirty days and no more; the Supreme Court has held Section 5 of the Limitation Act does not extend it further, even for a lengthy, apparently genuine delay.
  • An award debtor's assets, not the seat of arbitration, decide where an award can be executed; the Supreme Court held in Sundaram Finance that execution can be filed in any court where the assets are, without first transferring the award from the supervising court.

Why the seat question comes before everything else

Before any of the procedural questions in this piece arise, a Kolkata-seated arbitration has to actually be a Kolkata-seated arbitration, and that is a narrower thing than a contract simply mentioning the city. The Supreme Court's 2022 decision in Ravi Ranjan Developers Private Limited v. Aditya Kumar Chatterjee set aside an arbitrator appointment the Calcutta High Court had itself made, holding that neither the cause of action nor the seat of arbitration was connected to Kolkata on the facts of that case. The clause in question referred to Kolkata only as a place where the tribunal's sittings might be held, which the Court treated as naming a venue rather than fixing the juridical seat, and it held that a Section 11(6) application has to go to the High Court exercising supervisory jurisdiction under Section 2(1)(e) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, over the court that would otherwise have had jurisdiction on the facts, which was not the Calcutta High Court.

Practitioner commentary summarises a later Calcutta High Court ruling that took a more generous view on a different set of facts, treating Kolkata as having become the seat by conduct once the parties actually held multiple sittings there and expressed a choice of Kolkata as the seat, even though the clause itself had referred to either Delhi or Kolkata. Read together with Ravi Ranjan Developers, the lesson for anyone drafting a Kolkata arbitration clause today is to say expressly that Kolkata is the seat of arbitration, not merely a venue, a city for hearings or one of two named cities, since the difference decides which High Court will hear every one of the applications discussed in the rest of this piece.

A related complication is that the choice of seat, once genuinely fixed, is not easily displaced later by a party filing its first application in a different court. The Calcutta High Court has held that once parties have chosen a seat before the first application is filed, Section 42 of the Act, which gives jurisdiction over later applications to the court where an earlier application was made, cannot be used to shift that jurisdiction to a different court merely because a party chose to file there first.

The practical takeaway for anyone drafting or reviewing a Kolkata-facing contract is to resolve the seat question on paper, long before any dispute arises, rather than leaving it to be argued out once money is already at stake. A clause that says the arbitration shall be seated in Kolkata, with the Calcutta High Court having exclusive jurisdiction over all applications under Part I of the Act, leaves far less room for the kind of seat-versus-venue argument that reached the Supreme Court in Ravi Ranjan Developers, and it is a small drafting cost against what that litigation, spread across a Section 11 appointment, an appeal and a fresh application elsewhere, actually ended up costing the parties in that case.

The Commercial Division's grip on arbitration matters

Once Kolkata is genuinely the seat, the next question is which bench of the Calcutta High Court actually hears the application, and the settled answer since the Commercial Division's constitution is that Section 9, Section 11, Section 34 and Section 37 applications all go to the Commercial Division rather than an ordinary civil bench of the Original Side, even where a party files on the Original Side without routing the matter there directly. A 2024 ruling held that a Section 11(5) application filed on the Original Side still had to be decided by the Commercial Division, reasoning that Section 10(2) of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 uses the word shall and is accordingly mandatory, and a 2026 ruling went further, holding that non-commercial benches lose jurisdiction over commercial arbitration matters entirely once the Commercial Division exists. A Section 34 challenge decided by the wrong bench was itself set aside by a Division Bench in 2025 on this basis, which shows that getting the bench right is not a formality a later court will simply overlook.

This concentration of arbitration work in one division has a practical consequence for where within Kolkata's wider commercial-court structure an application actually lands. A party has, in at least one 2024 matter, been sent away from the Commercial Division at the principal seat and told to approach the Commercial Court at Rajarhat instead, since not every Kolkata-seated arbitration dispute is large enough, in pecuniary terms, to sit with the High Court's own Commercial Division rather than with a district-level commercial court exercising the same jurisdiction under the Commercial Courts Act. An international commercial arbitration, by contrast, is defined under the Act in a way that sends it to the High Court regardless of value, so a Kolkata business contracting with a foreign counterparty should expect its Section 9 or Section 34 application to sit with the Commercial Division even where a similar domestic dispute of the same size would go to a Rajarhat-type commercial court instead.

Where two applications touching the same arbitration end up filed in different forums, such as a Section 9 application at a district commercial court and a related Section 42 argument about which court should hear the Section 34 challenge, the Calcutta High Court has in practice had to sort out the resulting overlap rather than treating the first-filed application as automatically controlling everything that follows. A party planning litigation strategy around a Kolkata-seated arbitration should identify, at the outset, which forum the value and nature of the dispute actually points to, rather than defaulting to the Commercial Division by assumption and having to unwind the choice later.

This structure also means a Kolkata business should expect its arbitration-related filings to sit alongside, and sometimes behind, the Commercial Division's wider commercial-suit docket, rather than in a dedicated arbitration list running on its own separate timetable. The same judges who decide Section 12A pre-institution mediation questions, written-statement timelines and summary-judgment applications in ordinary commercial suits also decide Section 9, Section 11 and Section 34 applications, so the Commercial Division's overall workload, and not some arbitration-specific calendar, is the realistic constraint on how quickly an application is likely to be listed and heard.

Section 9: how wide the interim power runs, and where it stops

Section 9(1) gives a court wide power to grant interim measures of protection, before, during or after arbitral proceedings but before enforcement of the award, and Calcutta High Court orders over the past two years show the Commercial Division using that power readily where a party can show the arbitral process itself is at risk of being rendered ineffective without interim protection. A December 2025 order applied the established principle that Section 9 exists to protect the subject matter of the arbitration and to keep the arbitral process meaningful, rather than to give a party a second shot at relief it could just as easily seek from the tribunal once constituted.

That last point is Section 9(3)'s real function. A 2023 ruling in a matter described as Jaya Industries treated the constitution of the arbitral tribunal as a limit on the court's own continuing role, holding that the court's power under Section 9(1) is very wide but is restrained by Section 9(3), which exists to prevent the same relief being argued at two levels, first before the court and then again before the tribunal. In practice this has meant the Commercial Division will sometimes grant short interim protection, on the order of a few months, while directing the applicant to invoke the arbitration clause and constitute the tribunal in the meantime, rather than leaving the Section 9 order in place indefinitely as a substitute for the arbitration itself.

A party seeking Section 9 relief in Kolkata should therefore treat the application as a bridge to arbitration rather than a parallel track to it: moving quickly to invoke the arbitration clause once interim protection is secured, and expecting the court to ask, at the first opportunity, why the tribunal has not yet been approached for the same relief if the tribunal is already in a position to grant it effectively.

Section 9 also remains available after an award has been made but before enforcement, a stage the subsection covers expressly, though in Kolkata practice most post-award interim applications have tended to arrive dressed as a stay application under Section 36(3) rather than as a fresh Section 9 petition, since a party resisting enforcement usually has the Section 34 challenge already on file and the stay mechanism built around that challenge to hand. A party genuinely needing interim protection of assets after an award but before a Section 34 challenge has even been filed should not assume the Section 36 route is the only one open and should consider whether a standalone Section 9 application better fits the urgency of what is being sought.

Appointing an arbitrator under Section 11: the test the Calcutta High Court still applies

Once a party in Kolkata needs the court's help to appoint an arbitrator, the relevant test is not the one many contracts, drafted after 2019, assume is already in force. Parliament's 2019 amendment would have substituted Section 11(6A) and shifted appointments to arbitral institutions designated by the Chief Justice, moving the High Court out of the appointment business entirely for most cases. The Supreme Court held in Mayavati Trading Private Limited v. Pradyuat Deb Burman, reported at (2019) 8 SCC 714, that this substitution never actually came into force because the notification needed to bring it into effect was never issued, so courts, Calcutta's included, continue to apply the earlier, narrower version of Section 11(6A), which confines the court to examining only the existence of the arbitration agreement rather than its validity on the merits.

The practical effect for a Kolkata party filing a Section 11 application is that the Commercial Division will not, at this stage, go into whether the underlying contract is valid, whether a condition precedent to arbitration such as a mandatory negotiation clause was satisfied, or whether the claim itself has merit; those questions are generally left to the arbitral tribunal once constituted. What the Commercial Division will look at is whether an arbitration agreement exists between the parties before it, which has in recent years also meant engaging with the separate line of Supreme Court authority on whether an unstamped arbitration agreement can still found a Section 11 appointment, a question this piece does not attempt to re-answer given that it is addressed in detail elsewhere on this site.

Budgeting for ad hoc appointment by the High Court itself, rather than through an institution, is therefore the safer assumption for a Kolkata party: the institutional regime some commercial contracts assume is operating, because the clause was drafted with the 2019 amendment in mind, is simply not the regime actually in force, and a Kolkata business should not be surprised when its Section 11 application proceeds through the ordinary court-appointment route instead.

Section 34: the limitation clock, and why it rarely bends

A Section 34 application to set aside an award has to be made within three months of the award being received by the party making the application, and a proviso allows the court to extend that period by a further thirty days if satisfied the applicant was prevented by sufficient cause from applying within the original three months, but the proviso ends with the words but not thereafter. The Supreme Court's decision in M/s Simplex Infrastructure Ltd. v. Union of India, (2019) 2 SCC 455, decided 5 December 2018, relying on its earlier ruling in Union of India v. Popular Construction Co., (2001) 8 SCC 470, held that Section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963, which allows condonation of delay on sufficient cause shown, has no application to a Section 34 application at all, and set aside a Calcutta High Court order that had condoned a 514-day delay by the Union of India in filing its Section 34 application, holding that the 131 days left uncondoned even after excluding time spent in the wrong forum under Section 14 of the Limitation Act could not be condoned.

How the court vacation and festival holidays interact with that outer limit is a separate point of Calcutta-specific interest. Reporting on the issue describes the Calcutta High Court holding that where the thirty-day grace period for condonation under Section 34(3) falls during a court vacation, that period is still counted as running, rather than being extended to account for the court being closed, a position the Supreme Court is reported to have examined on appeal from a West Bengal matter in which the limitation period was found to have expired just before the Durga Puja vacation began. Because the reports on this point are not fully consistent with each other on the exact dates and holding, a party close to the three-month or thirty-day boundary around a long vacation period should not assume the vacation buys any extra time, and should file before the vacation begins rather than relying on it to extend the window.

A 2024 Calcutta High Court ruling in a matter involving Srei Equipment Finance addressed a different kind of extension, holding that the moratorium period under Section 14 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, during a corporate debtor's insolvency resolution process, is excluded when computing the limitation period for a Section 34 application, which is a useful point for a party whose counterparty has gone through insolvency proceedings in the interval between the award and the challenge. None of this changes the basic rule that the three-months-plus-thirty-days ceiling under Section 34(3) itself is rigid; what these decisions address is what counts, and does not count, within that window, not whether the window can be stretched beyond it.

Staying an award once Section 34 is filed

Filing a Section 34 application does not, by itself, stop the award from being enforced. Section 36(2) makes that explicit, and a separate application for stay under Section 36(3) is needed, with the court having discretion to grant it subject to conditions. Before a 2021 amendment, that discretion was read generously, and an award debtor could in practice obtain a stay without being made to deposit anything by way of security. The Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Act, 2021, applied retrospectively from 23 October 2015, added a second proviso to Section 36(3) under which the court may grant an unconditional stay only where it is satisfied, on a prima facie basis, that the arbitration agreement or contract on which the award is based, or the making of the award itself, was induced or affected by fraud or corruption.

The Calcutta High Court's own practice since the amendment shows both sides of this test in operation. In the Damodar Valley Corporation matter, a December 2021 order modified an earlier stay to direct the award debtor to deposit a very large sum, reported as running into hundreds of crores of rupees, by way of cash security with the Registrar, Original Side, before the stay would take effect, which is the conditional-stay route the 2021 amendment left intact for cases outside the fraud-or-corruption exception. In a South Eastern Railway matter, by contrast, an August 2023 order is reported to have granted an unconditional stay on a prima facie finding of fraud and a failure to fulfil contractual prerequisites, which falls squarely within the amendment's own exception. In the WBSIDC v. Kaushalya Infrastructure matter, decided the same year, the court dismissed a plea for unconditional stay, reportedly on the basis that the admitted facts did not meet the fraud-or-corruption threshold even though the award debtor had argued its case fell within that limb.

A later Calcutta High Court decision is reported to have gone further on what will not suffice: a claim of bias in the arbitral process was held not to meet the Section 36(3) threshold, since bias is not itself one of the two grounds, fraud or corruption, that the second proviso lists. Commentary comparing High Courts on this point notes that the Bombay and Delhi High Courts have been reported as taking the view that an unconditional stay can still be granted on grounds outside the second proviso in appropriate cases, while Calcutta is grouped with the Punjab and Haryana High Court as taking the stricter view that it cannot. An award debtor in Kolkata planning a stay application should proceed on the stricter reading unless and until the Supreme Court resolves the split, since assuming the more generous approach followed elsewhere will apply in Calcutta is not a safe litigation strategy.

Getting paid: enforcement once the award survives challenge

Once the Section 34 window has closed without a successful challenge, or a challenge has been dismissed, Section 36(1) treats the award as enforceable in the same manner as a decree of the court. The question that then matters to an award holder is not which court supervised the arbitration, but where the award debtor's assets actually are, because the Supreme Court settled a long-running dispute among High Courts on exactly this point in Sundaram Finance Limited v. Abdul Samad, decided on 15 February 2018. The Court held that an award is deemed to be a decree for enforcement purposes, that execution proceedings can be initiated before any court in India where the judgment debtor's assets are located, and that there is no requirement to first obtain a transfer of the award from the court that would otherwise have had supervisory jurisdiction over the arbitration.

For a Kolkata award holder, this means an award made under a Calcutta-seated arbitration, even one that went through a full Section 9, Section 11 and Section 34 cycle before the Commercial Division, does not have to be executed in Kolkata at all if the award debtor's recoverable assets sit somewhere else in India. The practical sequence is to identify where the assets are first, and only then decide where to file for execution, rather than assuming the Calcutta court that handled the arbitration is also the natural, let alone the only, place to enforce the result. Where the award debtor does have assets in West Bengal, the execution itself proceeds broadly along the lines of executing a money decree under the Code of Civil Procedure, since Section 36 treats the award as a decree for this purpose without converting the arbitral tribunal itself into a court capable of executing its own award.

An award holder should also keep an eye on how long enforcement can be delayed by a conditional stay of the kind described above: a large deposit ordered as a condition of stay, such as the sum ordered in the Damodar Valley Corporation matter, does give the award holder some practical comfort even while the Section 34 challenge remains pending, since the deposit sits with the Registrar rather than with the award debtor, but it is not the same as actual payment and an award holder should not treat a conditional stay as functionally equivalent to having been paid.

Executing an award as a decree also means the award holder inherits the ordinary tools available to a decree holder under the Code of Civil Procedure, including attachment of movable and immovable property, garnishee proceedings against money owed to the judgment debtor by a third party, and, in an appropriate case, examination of the judgment debtor as to their assets. None of this is unique to Kolkata or to arbitration; it follows from treating the award as a decree once Section 36(1) applies, and an award holder planning enforcement strategy should think in exactly the same terms a civil decree holder would, rather than expecting any special procedure because the underlying claim happened to be resolved by arbitration rather than by a civil suit.

Costs of the arbitration and of the court applications along the way are a further practical consideration an award holder should not overlook, since an arbitral tribunal's own costs order, usually made as part of the final award itself under Section 31A, is enforceable along with the rest of the award once Section 36 applies, while the costs of separate Section 9, Section 11 or Section 34 proceedings before the Commercial Division are dealt with by the High Court under its own rules for commercial litigation rather than under the arbitral tribunal's award. A party budgeting for a Kolkata-seated dispute end to end should keep these two cost streams, the tribunal's own costs order and the court's costs on the ancillary applications, separate in its own accounting, since they are recovered, if at all, through different mechanisms.

Arbitrating against an insolvent counterparty: what Kolkata's Srei-related litigation shows

Kolkata's Commercial Division has, in recent years, had to work through an unusually large volume of arbitration-related litigation touching Srei Equipment Finance Limited, a Kolkata-based non-banking financial company that went through insolvency proceedings before the National Company Law Tribunal, and the resulting decisions are a useful illustration of how arbitration and insolvency law interact in practice rather than only in theory. Once a corporate debtor enters the corporate insolvency resolution process, Section 14 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 imposes a moratorium that bars, among other things, the institution or continuation of suits or proceedings against the corporate debtor, and courts across India have treated an arbitration, including a Section 34 challenge to an award against the corporate debtor, as falling within that bar for as long as the moratorium lasts.

The Calcutta High Court's 2024 ruling in the Srei Equipment Finance line of matters addressed what happens to the Section 34 limitation clock during that moratorium period, holding that the time during which the moratorium was in force is excluded when computing whether a Section 34 application was filed within the three-months-plus-thirty-days window described earlier in this piece. This is a sensible accommodation, since a party cannot realistically be expected to file or pursue a Section 34 application against a corporate debtor while Section 14 of the IBC bars exactly that step, but it is an exclusion of time within the window rather than an extension of the window's own outer limit, which remains governed by the Simplex Infrastructures line of authority discussed above.

A separate strand of the same litigation, in the Sajarul Rahaman matter, dealt with a more mundane but recurring problem: a Section 34 application filed with a shortfall in the court fees paid, where the Corporation or counterparty argued the application should be treated as not properly filed at all for limitation purposes until the deficit was made good. A party dealing with any insolvent or formerly insolvent counterparty in Kolkata arbitration-related litigation should treat both points together, tracking the moratorium period carefully for exclusion purposes while also making sure the court fee on the Section 34 application itself is correctly calculated and paid in full from the outset, since a fee shortfall has been used as an independent limitation argument quite apart from the moratorium question.

Institutional versus ad hoc arbitration with a Kolkata seat

Ad hoc arbitration, where the parties and, if necessary, the court appoint the arbitrator and set the procedure themselves rather than handing the process to a standing institution, remains the ordinary way arbitration is conducted with a Kolkata seat, and the Section 11 position described above, where court appointment rather than institutional appointment is still the operating regime nationally, is a large part of why. This research did not find a confirmed, dedicated Kolkata chapter or regional office of the Indian Council of Arbitration, which operates out of New Delhi, or of any comparable national arbitral institution with a standing presence in the city, and no such claim is made here; a Kolkata business wanting institutional administration of its arbitration should check directly with whichever institution it is considering rather than assume a local office exists.

This does not mean institutional arbitration is unavailable to a Kolkata party, only that it has to be chosen deliberately, by naming an institution and its rules in the arbitration clause, rather than assumed to be the default. Sector-specific arbitration mechanisms do exist with a Kolkata or eastern-region presence, such as the regional arbitration arrangements maintained by commodity and securities exchanges for disputes within their own membership, but these are narrow, sector-specific facilities rather than general commercial arbitration institutions, and a business outside the relevant sector should not expect to use them.

The Calcutta High Court's own Mediation Centre, referred to in connection with Section 12A pre-institution mediation for ordinary civil and commercial suits, is a separate facility again, administering court-referred mediation in pending litigation rather than administering arbitration proceedings, and should not be confused with an arbitral institution merely because both involve alternative dispute resolution. A party drafting a Kolkata arbitration clause today, in the absence of a developed local institutional ecosystem, is usually better served by a carefully drafted ad hoc clause, naming the seat expressly as discussed earlier in this piece, than by assuming an institution will be available to administer the process without that being separately arranged.

Cost and timeline, and not administrative convenience alone, should factor into a Kolkata business's choice between institutional and ad hoc arbitration. An institution typically charges its own administrative fees on top of the arbitrators' own fees, and in exchange supplies a fixed procedural timetable, model clauses and, in the larger institutions, a panel of arbitrators to choose from. Ad hoc arbitration avoids the institutional fee layer but leaves the parties, and eventually the Commercial Division if a Section 11 application becomes necessary, to work out procedure and appointment from scratch. For a smaller Kolkata commercial dispute, the ad hoc route's lower upfront cost is often the more realistic choice; for a larger or more complex dispute, particularly one with an international element, naming an established institution in the clause, even one without a Kolkata office, can be worth the administrative fee for the procedural certainty it buys.

A practical sequence for a Kolkata-seated dispute

Anyone anticipating or facing a Kolkata-seated arbitration dispute should start by confirming the seat question addressed at the outset of this piece, since every forum question that follows depends on it, and should resist the temptation to assume a clause naming Kolkata as a venue or one of two possible cities has already settled where the supervising court sits. Where urgent protection is needed before or during the arbitration, a Section 9 application to the Commercial Division is the usual route, but it should be framed, and argued, as a bridge to the arbitration rather than a substitute for it, given Section 9(3)'s restraint on relief once the tribunal is constituted and able to act.

If an arbitrator needs to be appointed through the court, the Section 11 application in Kolkata will be decided on the narrow existence-of-agreement test rather than on the merits of the underlying dispute, so a party should not expect, or try to use, the appointment stage to argue points that properly belong before the tribunal itself. Once an award is in hand, the three-month-plus-thirty-day Section 34 window should be treated as effectively absolute, with no reliance on vacation periods or a sympathetic explanation for delay to rescue a late filing, and a separate stay application under Section 36(3) will be needed if enforcement is to be held off pending the challenge, with an unconditional stay realistically available in Kolkata only on a prima facie showing of fraud or corruption.

Finally, enforcement planning should begin before an award is even made, by identifying where the counterparty actually holds assets, since Sundaram Finance frees an award holder from having to enforce in the court that supervised the arbitration and makes the location of recoverable assets, not the seat of arbitration, the decisive question once Section 34 has run its course.

Where the counterparty is, or has become, a corporate debtor in insolvency, the sequence above has to be read alongside the Section 14 moratorium point discussed earlier: an award holder should check whether the counterparty is subject to an active corporate insolvency resolution process before assuming the ordinary Section 34 or enforcement timetable applies, since the moratorium can suspend both the challenge and, separately, the enforcement step itself until the insolvency proceeding resolves one way or another.

Arbitration with a Kolkata seat runs through one procedural gateway, the Commercial Division of the Calcutta High Court, but the substance of what happens at each stage, from interim relief through appointment, challenge and enforcement, tracks national law more closely than it departs from it, with Calcutta's own contribution lying mainly in how strictly it has applied the fraud-or-corruption threshold for an unconditional stay and in a handful of decisions on how the bench structure within its own commercial-court system has to work.

The points this piece has left open, such as the precise current status of a Kolkata-based institutional arbitration ecosystem and the exact reported citation for the Simplex ruling on Section 34 limitation, are flagged rather than glossed over, and a party with a live dispute at any of these stages should treat this piece as an orientation to the law as it stands today rather than as a substitute for advice on the specific facts of its own arbitration.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does naming Kolkata in an arbitration clause make it the seat of arbitration?

Not necessarily. The Supreme Court in Ravi Ranjan Developers set aside a Calcutta High Court arbitrator appointment because the clause named Kolkata only as a place where the tribunal might sit, which the Court treated as a venue rather than the juridical seat. A clause should say expressly that Kolkata is the seat if that is what the parties intend.

Which court in Kolkata hears Section 9, Section 11 and Section 34 applications?

The Commercial Division of the Calcutta High Court for matters that meet the applicable pecuniary threshold or involve an international commercial arbitration, and a district-level commercial court such as the one at Rajarhat for smaller domestic disputes. Courts have held that non-commercial benches lose jurisdiction over these matters once the Commercial Division exists.

Can I get interim relief from the Calcutta High Court even after my arbitral tribunal is constituted?

Section 9(3) limits this. Once a tribunal is constituted and able to grant the same relief effectively, courts in Kolkata have shown reluctance to keep hearing fresh Section 9 applications for relief the tribunal could grant itself, treating Section 9 as a bridge to arbitration rather than a parallel track running alongside it.

How is an arbitrator appointed by the Calcutta High Court under Section 11?

Through the pre-2019 test, which confines the court to examining whether an arbitration agreement exists between the parties, not whether the underlying contract or claim has merit. The 2019 amendment that would have shifted this to institutional appointment was never notified into force, so the older, court-centred process still applies.

How long do I have to challenge an arbitral award under Section 34?

Three months from receipt of the award, extendable by the court for a further thirty days on sufficient cause, and no further. The Supreme Court has held that Section 5 of the Limitation Act does not apply to extend this period beyond the thirty days, even where a long delay might otherwise seem excusable.

Does filing a Section 34 challenge automatically stop the award from being enforced?

No. Section 36(2) makes clear that filing a Section 34 application does not by itself stay the award, and a separate application for stay under Section 36(3) has to be made and granted, usually subject to conditions such as a cash deposit.

When will the Calcutta High Court grant an unconditional stay of an award?

Only on a prima facie showing of fraud or corruption in how the award or the underlying agreement was procured, under the second proviso to Section 36(3) added in 2021. Calcutta has been reported as taking a stricter line than some other High Courts, declining to grant unconditional stay on grounds such as bias that fall outside the fraud-or-corruption test.

Do I have to enforce a Kolkata-seated award in Kolkata?

No. The Supreme Court held in Sundaram Finance v. Abdul Samad that an arbitral award can be enforced as a decree in any court in India where the award debtor's assets are located, without first transferring the award from the court that supervised the arbitration.
