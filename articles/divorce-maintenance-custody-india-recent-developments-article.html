Divorce, Maintenance and Custody in India: Where the Law Stands After Article 142, BNSS and the 2025 Custody Rulings

The grounds for divorce, the formula for maintenance and the test for custody have not been rewritten, but a run of 2023-2025 rulings and the shift to the BNSS have changed how each of them actually plays out in a family court today.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 5 November 2026 · Law stated as on 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family law practice built only on the text of the Hindu Marriage Act, the Guardians and Wards Act and Section 125 of the old Code of Criminal Procedure would now be working from an incomplete picture. None of those statutes has been rewritten wholesale, but a Constitution Bench ruling on Article 142, the quiet transfer of maintenance enforcement into the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and a cluster of 2025 Supreme Court decisions on custody have each changed a piece of how these matters are actually argued and decided. This piece is purely informational, setting out the grounds for divorce, the mutual consent route and its cooling-off period, how maintenance is actually quantified and enforced, how custody and guardianship differ, and where a petition should be filed, as that law stands today.

Key points

  • A Hindu marriage can still be dissolved only on the fault-based grounds listed in Section 13 or by mutual consent under Section 13B; a separate line of Supreme Court rulings lets the Supreme Court itself, not a trial court, dissolve a marriage for irretrievable breakdown under Article 142.
  • The six-month cooling-off period in a mutual consent divorce is directory, not mandatory, and can be waived by a family court on facts the Supreme Court has described as illustrative rather than an exhaustive checklist, though lower courts do not apply this consistently.
  • Maintenance has no fixed formula. Rajnesh v. Neha requires a uniform affidavit of assets and liabilities from both sides and lists status, needs, qualifications, independent income, standard of living and litigation costs as the factors a court actually weighs.
  • Criminal maintenance under the old Section 125 CrPC now runs through Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita for any application filed on or after 1 July 2024; a case already pending on that date continues under the old section.
  • An unpaid maintenance order is enforced under Section 147 BNSS, formerly Section 128 CrPC, through attachment of salary or property and, in last resort, detention, and that enforcement is treated as a separate proceeding from any challenge to the order itself.
  • Custody and guardianship are legally distinct: guardianship is the bundle of rights and responsibilities over a child's person and property, custody is physical care, and the child's welfare is the paramount, though not the only, consideration a court weighs.
  • 2025 Supreme Court rulings have added a more structured, factor-based welfare test for interim custody and have reinforced that cross-border custody disputes should proceed through a guardianship petition rather than habeas corpus, with welfare taking priority over deference to a foreign court's order.

Three statutes, one family in crisis

A divorce rarely arrives as a single legal question. The same facts usually raise, almost simultaneously, whether a ground for divorce actually exists, how much one spouse must pay the other and the children while the case is pending and after it ends, and who the children live with and who decides for them in the meantime. Indian law answers these three questions through three different statutes that were never drafted to work together: the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 for the marriage itself, a mix of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, the Hindu Marriage Act's own Section 24 and 25, and criminal maintenance law for money, and the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 together with the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 for the children.

Each of those three tracks has moved in the last few years without any of the underlying Acts themselves being rewritten. The Supreme Court has carved out a route to dissolve a marriage that Section 13 does not itself list as a ground. The criminal maintenance provision every family lawyer once called Section 125 has a new number and a change to who can claim under it. And the test for deciding where a child should live while the proceedings continue has been given more structure by a run of 2025 rulings. This piece works through each track as it actually stands today, for general understanding rather than as advice on any specific dispute.

A preliminary point on scope: this discussion follows the Hindu Marriage Act's framework because it governs the largest share of matrimonial litigation in India, with comparative references to the Special Marriage Act, 1954 where the position differs materially. Readers governed by a different personal law, such as Muslim law, Christian law under the Indian Divorce Act, 1869, or Parsi law, should treat the general structure described here, particularly on maintenance enforcement and custody, as broadly comparable but should check their own governing statute separately, since the grounds for divorce in particular differ considerably across these laws.

Fault-based grounds: what Section 13 actually requires

Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act lists the grounds on which either spouse may seek a divorce, and all of them remain fault-based: adultery, cruelty, desertion for a continuous period of two years immediately before the petition, conversion to another religion, incurable unsoundness of mind or a mental disorder of a kind and degree that makes it unreasonable to expect the petitioner to live with the respondent, a virulent and incurable form of leprosy as historically understood before its removal by a later amendment in most reported commentary, venereal disease in a communicable form, renunciation of the world by entering a religious order, and not having been heard of as alive for seven years or more. Cruelty and desertion dominate the reported case law by a wide margin, and both are judged on the specific facts rather than against any closed list of acts, which is why courts repeatedly caution against treating any single precedent's facts as a template for a different marriage's facts.

A separate and narrower route sits in Section 13(1A). Where a court has already passed a decree of judicial separation under Section 10 or a decree of restitution of conjugal rights under Section 9, and the parties have not resumed cohabitation for one year or more after that decree, either spouse may petition for divorce on that ground alone, including the spouse against whom the original decree was passed. The Supreme Court has held that a restitution decree left unobeyed for over a year can found a divorce decree without the petitioner needing to show any further effort to bring the other spouse back, though an older and still-cited line of authority cautions that granting divorce on this ground remains a matter for the court's discretion rather than an automatic consequence of the year having passed.

The Special Marriage Act, 1954, which governs civil marriages regardless of religion, lists a substantially similar set of fault-based grounds in its own provision for divorce, with the same basic structure of adultery, cruelty, desertion and the rest. A marriage solemnised under one statute is generally dissolved under that same statute's grounds, so the first practical question in any contested matter, often overlooked by a litigant representing themselves, is which Act actually governs the marriage before even reaching which ground might apply.

Cruelty and desertion deserve a further word precisely because they generate the bulk of contested litigation. Cruelty, whether physical or, far more commonly litigated today, mental, has deliberately never been defined by a closed statutory list, and courts have consistently held that it must be assessed on the cumulative effect of a course of conduct rather than any single incident viewed in isolation, with the test asking whether the conduct is of such a nature that the petitioner cannot reasonably be expected to continue living with the respondent. Desertion, for its part, requires both an actual separation and an intention to permanently bring the matrimonial relationship to an end, called animus deserendi, continued without reasonable cause and without the deserted spouse's consent for the full two-year statutory period; a temporary separation for work, health or family reasons, without that underlying intention to abandon the marriage, does not on its own amount to desertion however long it lasts. A petitioner relying on either ground should expect a court to examine the full history of the marriage rather than a handful of selected incidents, which is why these cases are rarely decided quickly and why the underlying evidence, contemporaneous messages, medical records, witness accounts of specific events, matters more to the outcome than the general characterisation either side puts on the relationship.

The irretrievable-breakdown route: what Article 142 adds and does not add

Irretrievable breakdown of marriage is not, and has never been, a ground for divorce under Section 13 itself. Parliament has considered adding it on more than one occasion following Law Commission recommendations going back decades, and has not done so. What exists instead, since a 2023 Constitution Bench ruling, is a power the Supreme Court alone exercises under Article 142 of the Constitution, its power to pass whatever order is necessary to do complete justice, to dissolve a marriage on this ground even though no provision of the Hindu Marriage Act authorises it. The Court held that this power is not confined by the fault-based structure that governs an ordinary Section 13 petition, and that it may also dispense with the statutory waiting periods that would otherwise apply.

A 2025 ruling applied the same reasoning, holding that the absence of a statutory ground for dissolving a Hindu marriage is not itself a bar to the Supreme Court exercising its Article 142 power once irretrievable breakdown is established on the facts. What this does not do is create a new ground that a district or family court can apply on its own. The power belongs to the Supreme Court specifically, exercised typically in a matter already pending before it, commonly a transfer petition or an appeal arising out of matrimonial proceedings, rather than through a fresh petition filed directly in the Supreme Court seeking only this relief.

The practical consequence is a two-track system that looks similar from a distance but is not. A family court still decides every ordinary divorce petition strictly on whether a Section 13 ground, or mutual consent under Section 13B, is actually made out. Irretrievable breakdown as an independent basis for ending a marriage against one spouse's continuing objection is available only where a matter has reached the Supreme Court and that Court is satisfied the marriage is beyond saving, commonly after the parties have in substance already separated for a long period with no prospect of reconciliation. Commentary has noted the scope of this power remains the subject of continuing argument, including on whether it can be used over one spouse's sustained objection rather than as a vehicle for parties who are, in substance, both ready to separate but cannot clear every technical requirement of a fault-based or mutual consent petition.

Mutual consent and the cooling-off period: directory, not mandatory

Section 13B lets both spouses jointly seek a divorce where they have lived separately for a year or more, cannot live together and have mutually agreed the marriage should end. The provision requires two motions separated by a period commonly described as six months and not more than eighteen months from the date of the first motion, during which either party may withdraw consent, a safeguard meant to give a reconciliation window before the marriage is finally dissolved.

The Supreme Court held, in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, that this waiting period is directory rather than mandatory, and that a family court, and not the Supreme Court alone, can waive it where the statutory one-year separation period has already run, genuine efforts at mediation or reconciliation have failed, the parties have settled ancillary issues such as alimony, custody and other disputes between them, and the waiting period itself would only prolong their suffering without serving any purpose. A later Constitution Bench decision has been reported as confirming that these considerations are illustrative rather than an exhaustive checklist a party must satisfy point by point, giving family courts somewhat more latitude on the facts of a given case.

That latitude has not translated into uniform practice on the ground. Reported orders show family courts in different states reaching different conclusions on materially similar facts, with at least one reported case where a waiver application was refused at the trial court and the refusal was itself challenged before a High Court. A party seeking a waiver should accordingly treat Amardeep Singh's conditions as a strong starting checklist to satisfy on the record, through a joint application supported by evidence of the separation period and any mediation attempted, rather than assuming the waiver will follow automatically merely because both spouses agree they want it.

Interim maintenance: an answer to the most urgent question first

Before a divorce petition is even close to final hearing, a spouse without independent income needs money to litigate and to live. Section 24 of the Hindu Marriage Act lets either spouse apply for interim maintenance and litigation expenses during the pendency of any proceeding under the Act, which can include a petition for judicial separation or restitution and is not confined to a divorce petition alone, and courts are expected to decide such applications with urgency precisely because the relief is meant to address an immediate need rather than to wait behind the main case for a final hearing. A parallel criminal remedy exists through the maintenance provisions discussed in the next section, and the two can run alongside each other, though a court awarding maintenance under one is generally expected to account for what has already been awarded under the other so the same need is not compensated twice over.

What interim maintenance is not, and this is a recurring source of dispute, is a preview of the final maintenance figure decided through the same detailed, document-heavy inquiry a permanent award requires. Courts routinely work from a more summary assessment at the interim stage precisely because the application is meant to be decided quickly, which is also why appellate courts have shown some willingness to revisit interim figures where new facts about either party's actual income or assets come to light while the main petition remains pending.

Permanent maintenance: the Rajnesh v. Neha framework

The leading authority on quantifying maintenance, whether sought under the Hindu Marriage Act, the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, the Domestic Violence Act, or the criminal maintenance provision discussed below, is Rajnesh v. Neha, decided by the Supreme Court in November 2020 and reported at (2021) 2 SCC 324. The Court expressly held that there is no straitjacket formula for fixing the amount, which is itself the single most important point to carry into any negotiation or hearing: a party expecting a mechanical percentage-of-income calculation, the kind sometimes discussed informally, will not find one in the governing authority.

What the Court did lay down, instead of a formula, is a uniform disclosure mechanism. Every maintenance proceeding is now expected to open with a standardised affidavit of disclosure of assets and liabilities from both the applicant and the respondent, filed within four weeks, with courts permitted to grant at most two further opportunities to file it where there is genuine delay. A false disclosure exposes the deponent to separate proceedings for a false affidavit. The Supreme Court has since directed that the judgment itself be circulated to all judicial officers after noting with concern that many courts were not actually following the disclosure guidelines in practice, and more than one High Court has since held that the guidelines apply equally to matters that were already pending when the judgment was delivered, and are not confined to fresh applications filed afterward.

On quantum itself, the factors Rajnesh v. Neha directs a court to weigh, several of them drawn from the Delhi High Court's earlier decision in Bharat Hegde v. Saroj Hegde, include the status of the parties and the standard of living the applicant was accustomed to in the matrimonial home, the applicant's reasonable needs, whether the applicant has independent income or assets and what her, or his, educational and professional qualifications are, any sacrifice of career or employment the applicant made for the marriage or children, the non-applicant's own income and financial obligations, and the reasonable cost of litigation for a non-working spouse. These factors are weighed together on the specific record rather than run through any fixed sequence, and a party preparing for a maintenance hearing should expect to substantiate each one with actual documents, income tax returns, bank statements, employment records, rather than argue the point in the abstract.

Two further directions from the same judgment matter as much as the quantum factors themselves. The Court identified the overlapping jurisdiction of different maintenance statutes, the Hindu Marriage Act, the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, the Domestic Violence Act and the criminal provision, as a real risk of inconsistent or duplicated awards, and directed that where a party has already been granted maintenance under one statute and later claims it under another, the later court should account for, and set off against, what has already been paid, rather than awarding the same relief twice over for the same period. Separately, on the recurring question of when maintenance actually starts running, the Court held that maintenance in all cases should be awarded from the date the application for maintenance is filed, not from the date of the eventual order, departing from the inconsistent practice of different courts anchoring the start date to the application, the order, or even the date of service of summons on the respondent. That direction matters in practice because contested maintenance applications can take months or longer to reach an order, and a start date tied to the order rather than the application would otherwise reward a respondent for delaying the proceedings.

Maintenance under the new criminal code: Section 144 BNSS replaces Section 125 CrPC

Alongside the civil remedies under matrimonial statutes, a wife, child or parent unable to maintain themselves has long had an independent, faster criminal remedy. That remedy sat at Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 for five decades. With effect from 1 July 2024, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 replaced the CrPC, and the equivalent provision now sits at Section 144 BNSS. On substance, commentary describes the provision as carried over largely as it stood, with one change worth flagging specifically: the BNSS version is reported to drop the word "minor" from the category of children entitled to claim, extending the right in terms to an adult child who remains unable to maintain themselves, where the CrPC's own wording had tied a child's claim more directly to minority except for a disabled adult child.

Which provision actually governs a given application depends on timing rather than on when the underlying marriage or dispute began. An application filed on or after 1 July 2024 is filed and decided under Section 144 BNSS. An application already pending on that date continues under the old Section 125 CrPC rather than being required to restart under the new numbering. A litigant filing today should accordingly cite Section 144 BNSS as the governing provision, while anyone with an older, still-pending application should not assume the new section number has silently replaced the one their case was actually filed under.

Enforcement: making an order of maintenance actually pay

An order of maintenance is only as useful as a court's willingness to enforce it against a defaulting spouse, and the enforcement provision sits separately from the provision that grants the maintenance in the first place. Under the criminal track, enforcement of an order made under Section 144 BNSS now runs through Section 147 BNSS, the successor to Section 128 of the old CrPC, which lets a magistrate issue a warrant for levying the due amount in the manner provided for fines, including attachment and sale of property, and, where that still fails, order imprisonment for a term of up to one month, or until payment, whichever is earlier, for each month's default. In practice, courts have also used attachment of salary through the employer or, for a government servant, through the department concerned, as a more reliable route than attempting to seize movable property directly.

A genuinely useful point for a party enforcing an order, and one that is argued more than most litigants expect, concerns limitation. The CrPC's Section 125(3) carried a one-year bar on claiming arrears, and courts have split on whether that same one-year limit also caps how far back enforcement under Section 128 can reach once a default has occurred, with one line of authority holding the enforcement power is not itself time-barred by that provision and another reading the two provisions together more restrictively. No source consulted for this piece confirms how this specific split is being resolved under the BNSS's renumbered Sections 144 and 147, so a party relying on an old arrears calculation going back several years should treat the point as genuinely contested rather than settled, and should raise it explicitly rather than assume the full arrears will be recovered without objection.

A challenge to the underlying maintenance order itself, whether on appeal or through a revision, is treated as a separate proceeding from the enforcement application, so the mere pendency of such a challenge does not, by itself, halt enforcement unless the challenging party has separately obtained a stay of the order under challenge.

Custody and guardianship: a distinction that still gets blurred

Custody and guardianship are not the same thing, even though the words are frequently used as if they were. Guardianship is the broader legal authority and responsibility over a minor, covering decisions about the child's upbringing, education, property and general welfare, and is governed primarily by the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 together with, for Hindus, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956. Custody is the narrower, more concrete question of physical care, which parent or other person the child actually lives with day to day. A parent can hold guardianship rights over a child without having physical custody, and the law treats these as separable questions that a single order can, and often does, decide differently.

The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act directs a court to have regard to the child's age, sex, and wishes, among other considerations, when awarding custody, while Section 25 of the Guardians and Wards Act frames the welfare of the minor as the paramount consideration in deciding who should be guardian, without treating welfare as the sole relevant fact to the exclusion of everything else, including a parent's own legal claim and the practicalities of the arrangement proposed. The Juvenile Justice Act, 2015 separately defines "best interest of the child" to cover the child's physical, emotional, intellectual and social well-being, a definition courts increasingly draw on even in matrimonial custody disputes that do not themselves arise under that Act.

Proceedings for custody and guardianship are meant to proceed through the statutory machinery, ordinarily a petition under the Guardians and Wards Act, rather than through an extraordinary writ remedy such as habeas corpus, which the Supreme Court has reiterated is not the proper vehicle for resolving an ordinary, non-urgent custody dispute between parents, as distinct from a case of genuinely illegal or unlawful detention of the child. Courts have been firm that habeas corpus is particularly unsuited to a transnational custody dispute carrying its own complex facts about foreign residence, school enrolment and the child's own settled life, which a summary writ proceeding is not well equipped to examine.

Guardianship itself is not a single, undivided role either. The Guardians and Wards Act distinguishes between a guardian of the person, responsible for the child's care and upbringing, and a guardian of the property, responsible for managing whatever assets a minor holds, and a court can and sometimes does appoint different people to these two roles where the facts call for it, for instance where a parent is a suitable custodian day to day but is not considered the right person to manage a substantial inheritance the child has received. A natural guardian under the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, ordinarily a parent, does not need a court's separate appointment to act for the child in most routine matters, but certain transactions involving the minor's immovable property still require the court's prior permission under the Act regardless of who holds natural guardianship, a requirement that is frequently overlooked until a sale or mortgage involving a minor's share in family property is challenged years later for want of that permission.

The 2025 refinements: a more structured welfare test and cross-border priorities

Welfare of the child has always been described as paramount, but 2025 brought a more structured articulation of what that actually means in practice at the interim stage, when a court has to make a provisional decision on incomplete information while the main guardianship petition is still being heard. In Arathy Ramachandran v. Bijay Raj Menon, reported at 2025 INSC 587, the Supreme Court is reported to have supplemented the existing interim custody power under Section 17 of the Guardians and Wards Act with a more holistic set of practical factors going beyond the broad welfare standard alone, including matters such as the child's diet, access to counselling or mental health support where relevant, the bond with siblings, and the geographic practicality of an arrangement for the child's schooling and routine.

Two further 2025 decisions addressed custody disputes with a cross-border element specifically. In Komal Krishan Arora v. Sandeep Kumar, reported at 2025 INSC 1123, the Court is reported to have placed the child's welfare ahead of deference to a foreign court's existing order, granting interim custody with monitoring through the juvenile justice system and directing the parties to pursue a full guardianship determination under the Guardians and Wards Act rather than treating the foreign order as conclusive. In Manoj Dhankar v. Neeharika, reported at 2025 INSC 1068 and decided 2 September 2025, the Court did not revisit that procedural point; it instead granted the father, who had been pursuing the matter under the Guardians and Wards Act while the child lived with the mother in Ireland, regular video-conferencing visitation, observing that a child should not become a casualty of the parents' conflict, and left custody with the mother.

A separate but related development concerns shared physical custody arrangements. A High Court decision has been reported as holding that Indian law does not recognise an arrangement styled as "joint parenting" in the sense of a strict, mechanically equal fifty-fifty division of a child's time between separated parents, quashing an order that had imposed one, on the reasoning that welfare has to be assessed on the child's own circumstances rather than achieved through an arithmetically equal split imposed as a default. Taken together, these decisions suggest Indian courts are moving toward a more individualised, evidence-heavy custody inquiry rather than either a rigid maternal or paternal preference or a mechanical equal-time formula, which in practice means a party on either side of a custody dispute should expect to substantiate the practical, everyday detail of the arrangement they are proposing rather than rely on a general assertion that welfare favours them.

Jurisdiction: where a petition can actually be filed

Section 19 of the Hindu Marriage Act lists the places where a petition under the Act may be presented: where the marriage was solemnised, where the respondent resides at the time of presentation, where the parties last resided together, or, following a 2003 amendment inserting a further clause, where the wife is residing on the date of presentation, where she herself is the petitioner. Courts have treated this wife's-residence option as an additional available forum rather than as displacing the other three, so a wife petitioning for divorce can choose among all four options rather than being confined to her own residence alone, and a respondent cannot defeat a properly filed petition merely by showing that some other clause of Section 19 would also have supported jurisdiction elsewhere.

That additional forum does not apply in quite the same way to a joint mutual consent petition under Section 13B, since both spouses are petitioners together rather than one party invoking a provision designed to protect the other, so the ordinary venues under Section 19 govern a mutual consent filing in the usual way. For custody and guardianship, the position is different again: a petition under the Guardians and Wards Act is generally required to be filed where the child ordinarily resides, a rule High Courts have enforced even where a parent might otherwise prefer to litigate closer to their own residence, on the reasoning that the child's actual residence, not either parent's convenience, is what the statute anchors jurisdiction to.

For a litigant managing all three strands of a dispute at once, divorce, maintenance and custody, this can mean genuinely different courts or at least different, carefully pleaded jurisdictional bases for each, particularly where the spouses and the children are not all currently living in the same city. Getting this wrong at the outset, by filing a custody petition in the forum convenient for the divorce proceeding rather than where the child actually lives, risks a return of the petition for want of jurisdiction well after the matter has already been contested on the merits. The maintenance strand adds a further wrinkle. The civil remedy under Section 24 or Section 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act is tied to whichever court is seised of the matrimonial proceeding itself, while the criminal remedy under Section 144 BNSS carries its own, separately generous venue rule in Section 145 BNSS, the successor to the old Section 126 CrPC: a maintenance application can be filed where the husband is, where the wife resides, where the parties last resided together, or, under a clause the BNSS is reported to have added, where a claimant parent resides. A spouse weighing which track to pursue should factor in which of these available forums is actually the more convenient and the faster one to approach, rather than assume the venue for the maintenance claim must follow wherever the divorce petition happens to be pending.

None of the three underlying statutes discussed here, the Hindu Marriage Act, the Guardians and Wards Act, or the criminal maintenance provision now renumbered as Section 144 BNSS, has been rewritten in its basic structure. What has changed is denser and easier to miss: a constitutional power that lets the Supreme Court alone dissolve a marriage no statutory ground reaches, a disclosure and enforcement regime for maintenance that courts are now under explicit instruction to actually apply rather than treat as aspirational, and a custody jurisprudence that has moved from a general welfare slogan toward a more itemised, evidence-driven inquiry, especially once a foreign jurisdiction or a foreign court order enters the picture.

The practical thread running through all of it is documentation. A disclosure affidavit under Rajnesh v. Neha, a separation timeline supporting a Section 13B waiver, the practical detail a 2025-style welfare inquiry now expects, and a jurisdictional basis pleaded correctly for each of the three proceedings, are all, at bottom, paperwork done early and done carefully rather than argument alone. This article is general information on how that law stands today and is not a substitute for advice on the specific facts of any individual matter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a divorce in India just because the marriage has broken down, even if my spouse disagrees?

Not through an ordinary family court petition. Irretrievable breakdown is not a ground listed in Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act. The Supreme Court has held it can dissolve a marriage on this basis using its own constitutional power under Article 142, but that power belongs to the Supreme Court specifically and is generally exercised in a matter already before it, not available as a fresh ground to argue before a trial or family court.

Can the six-month waiting period for a mutual consent divorce be skipped?

Yes, in principle. The Supreme Court has held the period is directory rather than mandatory and that a family court can waive it where the one-year separation requirement has already run, genuine reconciliation efforts have failed, and the parties have settled issues like maintenance and custody between themselves. In practice, lower courts apply this waiver inconsistently, so it should be sought through a properly supported joint application rather than assumed as automatic.

How is the amount of maintenance actually calculated?

There is no fixed formula. Following Rajnesh v. Neha, courts require both spouses to file a standardised affidavit disclosing their assets and liabilities, and then weigh factors including the status and standard of living of the parties, the applicant's reasonable needs, independent income or assets, educational and professional qualifications, any career sacrifice made for the family, and reasonable litigation costs, on the specific facts of the case.

Is Section 125 CrPC still the law for claiming maintenance?

Only for applications that were already pending before 1 July 2024. For any application filed on or after that date, the governing provision is Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, which is reported to carry the substance of the old provision forward, including, on at least one reported reading, extending the right to an adult child unable to maintain themselves.

What can I do if my spouse simply refuses to pay court-ordered maintenance?

Enforcement is a separate proceeding under Section 147 BNSS, formerly Section 128 CrPC, which lets a magistrate issue a warrant to recover the amount as if it were a fine, including by attaching salary or property, and allows a period of imprisonment for continued default as a last resort. A pending appeal or revision against the maintenance order itself does not automatically stop this enforcement unless a separate stay has been obtained.

What is the difference between custody and guardianship of a child?

Guardianship is the broader legal authority over a child's upbringing, education, property and general welfare. Custody is the narrower question of physical care, who the child actually lives with. A parent can hold guardianship rights without having physical custody, and courts frequently decide the two questions differently within the same proceeding.

Does a foreign court's custody order automatically bind an Indian court?

No. Recent Supreme Court rulings have held that an Indian court assessing custody, including in a cross-border dispute, gives priority to the child's welfare over automatic deference to a foreign court's existing order, and that such disputes should proceed through a statutory guardianship petition rather than through a habeas corpus writ, which is not the proper remedy for an ordinary custody dispute between parents.

Where should I actually file my divorce, maintenance and custody matters if we live in different cities?

Each has its own jurisdictional rule. A divorce petition under the Hindu Marriage Act can generally be filed where the marriage was solemnised, where the respondent resides, where the parties last lived together, or, if the wife is the petitioner, where she currently resides. A custody or guardianship petition, by contrast, is generally required to be filed where the child ordinarily resides, which may not be the same place as any of the divorce venues, so each filing needs its own jurisdictional basis checked separately.
