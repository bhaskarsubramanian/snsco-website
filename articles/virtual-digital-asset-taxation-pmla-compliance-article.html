Taxation of Virtual Digital Assets in India: The 30% Tax, 1% TDS and the PMLA Compliance Layer

What Section 115BBH, Section 194S and the 2023 money-laundering notification actually require of an Indian crypto investor, an NRI holder and the exchange handling the trade

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 25 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

India never banned cryptocurrency, it taxed it heavily and then brought the industry under the same anti-money laundering regime that covers banks. Four years into that framework, the rules are settled enough to plan around but harsh enough that most of the tax planning available for other assets simply does not apply to a virtual digital asset held by an investor, a trading family office, or an NRI moving between residency statuses.

Key points

  • Section 115BBH of the Income-tax Act taxes income from transferring a virtual digital asset at a flat 30% plus surcharge and cess, with no deduction allowed except the cost of acquisition, applicable from Assessment Year 2023-24.
  • A loss on one virtual digital asset cannot be set off against a gain on another virtual digital asset or against any other income, and such a loss cannot be carried forward to a later year, which rules out the ordinary loss-harvesting strategies available for shares or mutual funds.
  • Section 194S requires 1% TDS on payment for transferring a virtual digital asset to a resident, with a lower reporting threshold of Rs 10,000 a year for most payers and Rs 50,000 a year for a 'specified person' as defined for this section alone.
  • A Finance Ministry notification of March 2023 brought virtual digital asset exchanges, wallet providers and similar businesses within the Prevention of Money Laundering Act as reporting entities, requiring FIU-IND registration, a designated director, a principal officer and KYC and customer due diligence programmes.
  • FIU-IND has taken the position that these PMLA obligations are activity-based and apply to an offshore platform serving Indian users regardless of whether that platform has any physical presence in India, and it has issued show-cause notices and compliance directions to multiple offshore platforms since December 2023.
  • The Income-tax Act, 2025 carries the same substantive scheme forward from 1 April 2026, reportedly renumbering the 30% tax rule and the TDS rule rather than changing their substance, though the exact new section numbers should be confirmed against the enacted text before being relied on in a filing.
  • Gifting a virtual digital asset, receiving one as a gift, or receiving one through an airdrop or a hard fork, all carry their own, separately worked out tax treatment, and an NRI or HNI family moving these assets between members should not assume ordinary gift-tax exemptions apply without checking the specific VDA provision first.
  • Beyond the income tax return's Schedule VDA, a Resident and Ordinarily Resident individual holding virtual digital assets through a foreign exchange generally has a separate Schedule FA foreign-asset disclosure to make, and the Enforcement Directorate has reported attaching or freezing proceeds of crime worth roughly Rs 4,189.89 crore across crypto-related PMLA cases as of a December 2025 parliamentary reply, a track distinct from, and in addition to, any income-tax exposure on the same assets.

India's answer to the ban-or-regulate question: tax it instead

For years, the open question in Indian crypto policy was whether the country would ban virtual digital assets outright, as the Reserve Bank of India's 2018 circular had tried to do before the Supreme Court struck it down in 2020, or regulate them properly. The Finance Act, 2022 answered differently: it neither banned the asset class nor built a dedicated regulator for it, it simply inserted a tax regime severe enough to discourage casual trading while leaving the activity itself legal.

That regime, built around Section 115BBH and Section 194S of the Income-tax Act, 1961, remains the core framework today, now carried forward into the Income-tax Act, 2025. Layered on top of it since March 2023 is a second, separate obligation under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, which treats exchanges and similar intermediaries the way it treats banks rather than the way it treats an ordinary trading platform. Anyone holding, trading or advising on virtual digital assets in India needs to treat these as two distinct compliance tracks, a tax track and an anti-money-laundering track, because meeting one does nothing to satisfy the other.

Neither track amounts to a licensing regime for the underlying activity itself. India has, as on the date of this guide, no dedicated statute regulating virtual digital asset exchanges as financial market intermediaries the way SEBI regulates stockbrokers or the RBI regulates banks; what exists instead is a tax charge and an anti-money-laundering overlay bolted onto an activity that otherwise remains, in a narrow sense, unregulated. That gap matters practically: a dispute between a customer and an exchange over a frozen account or a failed trade generally has to be fought as an ordinary contractual or consumer dispute, since there is no sectoral regulator with the kind of grievance-redressal mandate SEBI or the RBI would bring to an analogous dispute in a regulated financial product.

The 30% tax: Section 115BBH and why it bites harder than it looks

Section 115BBH taxes income from the transfer of a virtual digital asset at a flat 30%, to which surcharge and cess are added, applicable from Assessment Year 2023-24, covering income earned from 1 April 2022. The only deduction permitted against this income is the cost of acquiring the asset; no other expenditure, not brokerage, not transaction fees beyond what is treated as part of acquisition cost, not any other business expense, can be set off against it.

The provision's real weight comes from what it refuses to allow rather than from the headline rate. A loss on one virtual digital asset cannot be set off against a gain on a different virtual digital asset, let alone against income from any other source, and a loss that cannot be used in the year it arises cannot be carried forward to a later year either. For an investor used to the ordinary capital-gains regime, where a loss on one stock offsets a gain on another and unused losses carry forward for years, this is a sharp departure: a trader who nets an overall loss across a portfolio of several tokens in a year can still owe 30% tax on whichever individual tokens happened to show a gain, with no relief from the losses sitting alongside them.

Because the tax applies per transfer and per asset rather than on a netted portfolio basis, record-keeping becomes the practical battleground. An investor who cannot show the acquisition cost of each specific unit transferred, particularly where the same token was bought in multiple lots at different prices, risks the assessing officer applying a less favourable costing approach, and reconstructing this after the fact from exchange statements years later is considerably harder than keeping the record contemporaneously.

Trading within the asset class, swapping one token for another on a decentralised exchange without ever converting to rupees, falls within the regime just as much as a sale for cash does. Each such swap is itself a transfer attracting Section 115BBH on any gain embedded in the token given up, which surprises investors who think of a token-to-token trade as merely rebalancing a portfolio rather than realising a taxable event. A family office or HNI investor running an active multi-token strategy should assume every swap is a separate taxable transfer unless specifically advised otherwise, rather than waiting for year-end to work out which trades counted.

The 1% TDS: Section 194S and who actually has to deduct it

Section 194S requires whoever pays a resident for the transfer of a virtual digital asset to deduct 1% TDS from that payment, a mechanism designed less to collect meaningful revenue at the point of each trade than to create a paper trail the tax department can match against the taxpayer's own return. The provision applies subject to threshold exemptions: a 'specified person,' a category defined specifically for this section and distinct from how that term is used elsewhere in the Act, need not deduct TDS where the aggregate consideration paid in a financial year is below Rs 50,000; for anyone else making such payments, the threshold is lower, at Rs 10,000 in the financial year.

In practice, most retail trading runs through an exchange, and the exchange, rather than the individual buyer, typically handles this deduction as part of executing the trade, filing the specified-person variant of the return on Form 26QE rather than the standard Form 26Q used by other deductors. Where a transaction happens peer to peer, outside a registered exchange, for instance paying an individual seller directly for a token, the payer is the one responsible for deducting and depositing the TDS, and overlooking this because the transaction felt informal does not relieve the payer of the obligation or the penalty exposure that follows from missing it.

Where a transaction would otherwise attract TDS under both Section 194-O, which covers e-commerce operator payments, and Section 194S, the statute resolves the overlap by requiring deduction under Section 194S specifically, which matters for a platform that also functions as a broader e-commerce marketplace alongside its virtual digital asset trading function.

A buyer who fails to deduct TDS where required faces the usual consequences that attach to a TDS default under the Income-tax Act generally: interest for the period of default, a penalty, and in a serious case the risk of disallowance of the related expenditure where the payment is otherwise deductible in the buyer's own hands. A business making periodic virtual digital asset payments as part of its operations, rather than an individual making an occasional purchase, should build Section 194S compliance into its payment workflow itself, rather than treat it as a year-end reconciliation exercise, since that is what avoids the default arising in the first place.

Gifts, airdrops and the edges of the regime

A virtual digital asset received without consideration, as a gift, an airdrop, or through a hard fork, is not automatically outside the tax net simply because no money changed hands at the point of receipt. The general provision on gifts of property without adequate consideration, which taxes the recipient on the fair value received above a specified threshold, extends to virtual digital assets, meaning a family transferring tokens between members, a common HNI succession-planning move for other assets, can trigger a tax charge on the recipient that would not arise from gifting, say, listed shares within the exempt categories ordinarily available for gifts between relatives.

The exemptions that ordinarily soften gift taxation, gifts between specified relatives, gifts on the occasion of marriage, gifts under a will or by way of inheritance, generally continue to apply in their usual form to virtual digital assets as they do to other property, but a family planning to move meaningful crypto holdings between members should confirm the specific relationship and occasion fits one of the recognised exemptions rather than assume the transfer is informal enough to escape attention. Once the asset is later sold by whoever received it, Section 115BBH's 30% rate and the bar on deducting anything beyond cost of acquisition apply to that eventual sale in the ordinary way.

The second track: bringing exchanges under the PMLA

On 7 March 2023, the Finance Ministry notified specified virtual digital asset activities as a 'designated business or profession' under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002, placing them in the same reporting-entity category the PMLA already applied to banks, financial institutions and certain other businesses. The activities covered include exchange between a virtual digital asset and fiat currency, exchange between different virtual digital assets, transfer of virtual digital assets, and safekeeping or administration of virtual digital assets or instruments enabling control over them, when carried out for another person in the course of business.

An entity caught by the notification must register as a reporting entity with the Financial Intelligence Unit-India, disclose its relevant bank and financial institution account details, appoint a designated director and a principal officer, and implement anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing programmes including know-your-customer and customer due diligence procedures, broadly the same compliance architecture a bank or NBFC already carries. For a business that had been operating as a technology platform rather than a regulated financial intermediary, this is a materially heavier compliance burden than the tax rules alone impose, covering governance structure and ongoing monitoring rather than just transaction-level deduction.

The FIU-IND guidelines that took effect alongside the notification, in force from 10 March 2023, spell out how these obligations translate into day-to-day practice: maintaining records of transactions and client identification for the period the PMLA prescribes, filing suspicious transaction reports where a transaction pattern warrants one, and cooperating with the Unit's information requests. A reporting entity that treats registration as the end point, rather than the start of an ongoing monitoring obligation, is the kind of entity FIU-IND's later show-cause activity has tended to target.

Why an offshore exchange cannot simply stay offshore

A natural response for an exchange serving Indian users without a registered Indian entity is to assume the PMLA notification, being an Indian law, cannot reach it. The Financial Intelligence Unit-India has taken the opposite position: the obligations are activity-based rather than presence-based, meaning a platform offering the covered services to users in India can fall within scope regardless of whether it has any office, server or registered presence here.

FIU-IND has acted on that position in stages since the notification took effect. Reporting describes show-cause notices issued to a group of offshore virtual digital asset service providers in December 2023, with further notices and compliance directions issued to additional offshore platforms over 2025, though the reported counts and dates vary across sources and should be checked against FIU-IND's own releases rather than relied on from secondary reporting alone. The direction of travel, however, is clear: an offshore platform with a meaningful Indian user base should expect FIU-IND engagement rather than assume distance from India is a shield, and several platforms have registered as reporting entities precisely to avoid that exposure.

What this means for an NRI or a foreign-resident holder

An NRI who trades virtual digital assets through an Indian exchange, or who transfers a virtual digital asset to a resident counterparty, falls within the same Section 115BBH and Section 194S framework as a resident would for that transaction, since the provisions are not limited to resident taxpayers on the recipient side of a deduction obligation. Where the NRI is the seller receiving payment from a resident buyer, the 1% TDS under Section 194S is typically still deducted at the point of payment, and the NRI should account for this when working out whether a further return needs to be filed in India to claim credit for that deduction or to settle the final 30% liability on the transfer.

An NRI holding virtual digital assets through a foreign exchange with no Indian nexus sits largely outside the Indian TDS mechanism for that specific trade, since there is no resident payer within India's reach to deduct anything, but this does not necessarily take the underlying income outside Indian tax altogether if the holder remains an Indian tax resident for the relevant year or if the gain is otherwise treated as accruing or arising in India. Residency status, not just the platform used, is what actually determines the Indian tax exposure, and an NRI whose residency position for a given financial year is close to the borderline should resolve that question before assuming foreign-platform trading falls outside Indian tax altogether.

A related complication arises when an NRI moves between residency statuses within a short span, a common pattern for someone who returns to India for an extended work assignment or moves abroad mid-career. Gains realised while non-resident generally fall outside Indian tax on that specific footing, but the same holdings, if sold after the individual becomes resident again, are taxed on the ordinary Section 115BBH basis applicable to a resident at the time of that later sale, with the cost of acquisition carried over from the original purchase regardless of the residency status held at that earlier date. Treating the residency timeline and the transaction timeline as two separate questions, rather than assuming the status at acquisition governs the eventual sale, avoids a common and costly misreading of the position.

Where the Income-tax Act, 2025 changes the paperwork, not the substance

The Income-tax Act, 2025 takes effect from 1 April 2026 and reportedly carries the virtual digital asset regime forward largely unchanged in substance, while renumbering the provisions as part of the Act's broader restructuring. Commentary available at the time of writing describes the 30% tax rule moving to a new section and the TDS rule moving into a consolidated TDS table rather than standing alone, with the definition of virtual digital asset itself reportedly expanded to capture a wider description of cryptographically secured, distributed-ledger-based value.

Because this mapping comes from secondary commentary rather than from having checked the enacted Act's final numbering, a taxpayer or adviser preparing a return or structuring a transaction that straddles 1 April 2026 should confirm the actual section numbers and any substantive changes against the Income-tax Act, 2025 itself, or against the Income Tax Department's own published guidance, rather than rely on a pre-enactment or early commentary mapping that may not match the final text.

What is unlikely to change in the transition is the underlying policy choice the regime reflects: a government that has repeatedly declined to either ban or formally regulate the asset class, preferring instead to tax it at a rate well above the capital-gains rate on most other assets and to police the money-laundering risk through the exchanges themselves. Nothing in the public commentary on the 2025 Act's restructuring suggests any softening of that stance, and a taxpayer or business planning on a multi-year horizon should assume the current combination of a high flat tax rate with no loss relief, plus active PMLA supervision of exchanges, is the baseline to plan around rather than a transitional arrangement likely to ease.

Schedule VDA and the separate foreign-asset disclosure most NRIs miss

The Income Tax Department requires virtual digital asset income to be reported in a dedicated Schedule VDA within the income tax return, applicable to ITR-2, ITR-3, ITR-5, ITR-6 and ITR-7, and the schedule calls for transaction-level detail rather than a single net figure: the acquisition date, the transfer date, the cost of acquisition, the sale consideration and the resulting income for each transfer during the year. A taxpayer who has been netting trades informally and reporting only a summary figure is not meeting this requirement, and the transaction-level format makes it straightforward for the department to cross-check a return against data it receives from exchanges under their own reporting obligations.

A separate and easily overlooked requirement sits alongside Schedule VDA for anyone who is a Resident and Ordinarily Resident for the relevant year: Schedule FA, the foreign-assets schedule, requires disclosure of any asset held outside India at any point during the financial year, which on an ordinary reading extends to virtual digital assets held through a foreign exchange or in a self-custodied wallet connected to a foreign platform. An NRI who becomes resident in a later year, or a returning NRI who continues to hold crypto on a foreign platform from before, needs to assess this Schedule FA obligation independently of Schedule VDA, since the two schedules serve different purposes and a VDA transaction reported correctly under one does not excuse a missed disclosure under the other.

When a tax problem becomes a money-laundering problem

The PMLA framework described above is not limited to registering exchanges as reporting entities; it also gives the Enforcement Directorate power to investigate, attach and seek confiscation of the proceeds of crime where a virtual digital asset is itself the instrument used to launder money generated from some other offence. In a reply to Parliament in December 2025, the government stated that the Enforcement Directorate had attached, seized or frozen proceeds of crime worth approximately Rs 4,189.89 crore across crypto-related PMLA cases, arrested 29 persons, and filed 22 prosecution complaints, figures that had grown substantially from the roughly Rs 907 crore reported in an earlier 2022 reply.

Reported enforcement action gives a sense of the pattern the Directorate is pursuing: cases involving layering of fraud proceeds through numerous short-lived bank accounts before conversion into stablecoins such as USDT via peer-to-peer trades, seizure of cryptocurrency held in exchange wallets connected to online gaming and cyber-fraud schemes, and at least one case tracing proceeds moved through crypto to a foreign jurisdiction. None of this enforcement activity is a substitute for the ordinary tax assessment described earlier in this guide; a person can face an income-tax demand under Section 115BBH for undisclosed VDA gains without any suggestion of money laundering, and separately face PMLA exposure only where the underlying funds or the method of moving them meets the PMLA's own, distinct definition of proceeds of crime. Conflating the two risks either underselling a client's real exposure or overstating it, and the two statutes should be assessed on their own separate terms.

A practical compliance checklist for an investor and for a business

An individual investor should keep a transaction-level record of acquisition cost and date for every virtual digital asset held, separately for each lot where the same asset was bought at different times, since this is what Section 115BBH's limited deduction actually depends on. The investor should also track TDS credits from Form 26QE or 26Q certificates against the actual tax computed under Section 115BBH, since the two are calculated differently, TDS at 1% of consideration, final tax at 30% of gain, and reconciling them at return-filing time avoids either an unexpected shortfall or an unclaimed credit.

A business operating an exchange, wallet or custody service touching Indian users should treat FIU-IND registration as a threshold question to resolve before launch or expansion, not as something to address only after a show-cause notice arrives, given the Unit's stated activity-based reach. That means appointing a principal officer and designated director, building a KYC and customer due diligence programme, and filing the required reports, as genuine operational commitments rather than a one-time registration exercise, since the PMLA framework expects ongoing monitoring and reporting rather than a single compliance event.

An NRI or HNI family with significant virtual digital asset holdings should, before the next filing season rather than after a notice arrives, work out the residency-driven tax position, the Schedule FA question for foreign-held assets, and whether any PMLA-relevant exchange activity is involved, as three separate strands of the same portfolio review rather than a single question answered once and forgotten.

Four years after the Finance Act, 2022 first brought virtual digital assets into the tax net, the regime has stabilised around two unforgiving features: a 30% tax with almost no deductions or loss relief, and a PMLA compliance layer that treats exchanges as seriously as banks. Neither feature is likely to soften, and the planned transition to the Income-tax Act, 2025 appears set to preserve both rather than relax either.

For an investor, the practical lesson is that ordinary portfolio tax planning, loss harvesting, netting gains and losses across positions, carrying losses forward, simply does not work for this asset class in India, and decisions about how much to hold and when to transfer need to be made with that constraint in mind rather than discovered at filing time. For a business in the space, the PMLA obligation is not a formality that can be deferred; FIU-IND's willingness to pursue offshore platforms shows the compliance question needs answering before, not after, a serious Indian user base is built.

Families and individuals who hold a meaningful virtual digital asset position as part of a broader portfolio should treat it as a distinct asset class requiring its own tax and reporting plan, separate from the plan built around equities, real estate or conventional financial instruments, precisely because so many of the ordinary rules governing those other assets do not carry over to this one. The Income-tax Act, 2025's arrival in 2026 is a moment to revisit that plan, not because the substance is expected to change, but because the renumbering alone is reason enough to recheck every cross-reference a return or an advisory note relies on.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the tax rate on cryptocurrency and other virtual digital asset gains in India?

Section 115BBH of the Income-tax Act taxes income from transferring a virtual digital asset at a flat 30%, plus applicable surcharge and cess, with no deduction allowed other than the cost of acquiring the asset. This applies from Assessment Year 2023-24 onward.

Can I set off a loss on one cryptocurrency against a gain on another in my tax return?

No. Section 115BBH does not allow a loss from transferring one virtual digital asset to be set off against a gain from transferring a different one, or against any other income, and such a loss cannot be carried forward to a future year.

Who deducts the 1% TDS on a crypto transaction, the buyer or the exchange?

Under Section 194S, whoever pays for the transfer is generally responsible for deducting 1% TDS, but on a registered exchange this is usually handled by the exchange as part of executing the trade. In a direct peer-to-peer transaction outside an exchange, the payer carries the deduction and deposit obligation personally.

Is there a minimum transaction value below which TDS on virtual digital assets does not apply?

Yes. A 'specified person' as defined for Section 194S need not deduct TDS where total payments in a financial year are below Rs 50,000. For anyone else making such payments, the threshold is Rs 10,000 in the financial year.

Do crypto exchanges in India have to comply with anti-money laundering law?

Yes. A March 2023 Finance Ministry notification brought virtual digital asset exchanges, wallet providers and similar businesses within the Prevention of Money Laundering Act as reporting entities, requiring FIU-IND registration, a designated director, a principal officer, and KYC and customer due diligence programmes.

Can an offshore crypto exchange with no office in India ignore Indian PMLA rules?

FIU-IND has taken the position that these obligations are activity-based, not presence-based, meaning a platform serving Indian users can fall within scope regardless of physical presence in India. It has issued show-cause notices and compliance directions to multiple offshore platforms since the notification took effect.

Is gifting cryptocurrency to a family member tax-free in India?

Not automatically. A virtual digital asset gift is generally taxed on the recipient under the same rules that apply to gifts of other property without adequate consideration, though the usual exemptions for gifts between specified relatives, on marriage, or by way of inheritance continue to apply in their ordinary form.

Does trading one cryptocurrency for another, without converting to rupees, attract tax in India?

Yes. Each token-to-token swap is itself treated as a transfer under Section 115BBH, and any gain embedded in the token given up is taxed at 30% at that point, even though no rupee proceeds were received. Investors running an active multi-token strategy should track each swap as a separate taxable event.
