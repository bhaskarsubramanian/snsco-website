Municipal and Building-Law Remedies in Kolkata: Sanction, Demolition and the Building Tribunal

What the Kolkata Municipal Corporation Act 1980 actually lets the Corporation do when a building goes up without sanction, and the narrow window an owner, tenant or neighbour gets to answer it

By Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A stop-work notice pinned to a gate in Behala or a show cause letter from the Building department in Ballygunge sets off one of two very different statutory tracks under the Kolkata Municipal Corporation Act, 1980, and the owner's first mistake is usually treating them as the same thing. One track runs through sanction of the building plan and what happens when construction departs from it. The other runs through demolition, change of use and the Municipal Building Tribunal that hears appeals from both. This piece sets out what the Act and the reported decisions actually say about each track, where the Calcutta High Court has stepped in and where it has refused to, and what a neighbour who did not build anything can and cannot do about someone else's construction next door.

Key points

  • Building plan sanction under Sections 393 to 398 gives the Commissioner three choices on an application: grant, grant provisionally, or refuse on grounds confined to Section 396(2), and a sanction obtained by fraud or misrepresentation can be cancelled under Section 397.
  • Unauthorised construction is enforced through Section 400, and courts have repeatedly set aside demolition orders where no proper show cause notice or hearing preceded them under Section 400(1).
  • A stop-work notice under Section 401 carries its own teeth: continuing work after it is served can lead to an FIR under Section 401A, independent of the civil demolition proceeding.
  • Change of use of a sanctioned building is a separate offence under Section 416, with its own notice and appeal route distinct from Section 400.
  • Dangerous and ruinous buildings are handled under Sections 411 and 412A, not Section 400, and the Corporation's choice of track affects what notice and hearing the occupant is entitled to.
  • Appeals from orders under Sections 400 and 416 go to the Municipal Building Tribunal under Section 415 within thirty days, and the Calcutta High Court has treated that window as largely unforgiving in practice.
  • A party unhappy with the Tribunal's order has gone to the High Court by revision under Article 227, and at least one 2026 single-bench matter records a live dispute over whether a writ under Article 226 lies instead.
  • The Corporation does not adjudicate a boundary or ownership dispute between neighbours; its job is to check compliance with the sanctioned plan and the Building Rules, and a neighbour's deeper grievance usually has to go to a civil court.

One Act, two different kinds of trouble

The Kolkata Municipal Corporation Act, 1980 does two jobs that owners, tenants and neighbours in the city tend to run together. The first is a licensing job: nobody may erect, re-erect or materially alter a building in Kolkata without the Municipal Commissioner's sanction of a plan, and Sections 393 to 398 govern how that sanction is sought, granted, refused or cancelled. The second is an enforcement job: once construction has started, or finished, without sanction or at variance with a sanctioned plan, Section 400 and a cluster of related provisions let the Corporation stop it, and if necessary pull it down.

Keeping the two apart matters because the remedies and timelines are not the same. A refusal of sanction under Section 396 is answered by applying afresh or taking the refusal up in a writ petition, since the Act itself does not give a statutory appeal against a refusal in the way it does against a demolition order. A demolition order under Section 400, by contrast, carries its own appeal to the Municipal Building Tribunal with a thirty-day clock running from the date of the order. Confusing the two, or waiting for the wrong clock to run out, is one of the more common ways a Kolkata building dispute goes wrong before it has even properly started.

This piece works through both tracks in the order an owner usually meets them: first the sanction process and what can go wrong with it, then unauthorised construction and the demolition machinery, then the separate regime for dangerous buildings and change of use, and finally the Tribunal, the High Court's supervisory role over it, and what a neighbour who has built nothing can actually do.

Getting a plan sanctioned, and what the Commissioner can refuse

Section 393 requires a person intending to erect a building in Kolkata to give written notice to the Municipal Commissioner and apply for sanction, accompanied by the fee prescribed, which includes a Drainage Development fee. For larger or more complex buildings, Section 393A adds further requirements, and practitioners' checklists for this stage commonly list the title deed, a site plan, a key or location plan and, for certain categories of building, a soil investigation report, among the documents an applicant has to put in with the notice.

The Commissioner then has three options once the papers are in: grant the sanction, grant a provisional sanction, or refuse it under Section 396. Refusal is not an open-ended discretion. Section 396(2) confines the Commissioner to specific grounds, among them that the proposed building or the plan would contravene the Act or the rules made under it, or that a layout plan required under Sections 364 or 365 for the land in question has not itself been sanctioned. Whichever of the three courses the Commissioner takes, the decision has to be communicated to the person who gave the notice under Section 393 or Section 394, so a sanction that simply sits unissued in a file without being conveyed to the applicant does not do the Corporation's work for it. Builders working to a financing or construction timeline should not treat silence from the Building department as equivalent to a grant, since the Act's own scheme assumes an affirmative communication rather than a deemed sanction by lapse of time.

A plan that has already been sanctioned is not beyond challenge. Section 397 lets the Corporation cancel a sanction obtained by fraud or misrepresentation, a power the Calcutta High Court engaged with in the Larica Inn matter, where a Division Bench in 2024 held a writ challenging a KMC sanction for additional construction to be maintainable and went on to find the sanction granted without the other co-owner's consent to be illegal, upholding an order for demolition of the resulting construction. Section 398 adds a procedural safeguard going the other way: where a plan has to be redone because an earlier notice under Sections 393 or 394 cannot stand, the applicant is entitled to a fresh notice rather than being left to guess at what the Corporation now expects.

Two practical points follow for anyone applying for sanction in Kolkata today. The refusal grounds in Section 396(2) are a closed list on the face of the Act, so a refusal resting on a requirement outside that list, such as a land-use conversion condition that is not itself tied to the Act, the rules or an unsanctioned layout plan, is open to challenge on that basis alone, a submission petitioners have in fact made before the High Court, though this account does not treat an unresolved party argument as a settled holding. And a sanction once obtained is not immune from being unwound later if fraud or misrepresentation in procuring it comes to light, which matters for anyone buying a flat or a floor on the strength of someone else's sanctioned plan.

Buying into a building: what a sanctioned plan does and does not guarantee

A buyer looking at a flat or a floor in Kolkata often treats a sanctioned plan, once seen, as the end of the enquiry. It is not. Because Section 397 lets the Corporation cancel a sanction obtained by fraud or misrepresentation, a plan that looked clean at the time of purchase can still be unwound later if it turns out that the developer concealed a co-owner's objection, misstated the land's area or title, or otherwise misled the Building department when applying for it. The Larica Inn matter discussed above is a reminder that this is not a theoretical risk: a sanction granted without a co-owner's consent was found illegal years after it was issued, with demolition of the resulting construction upheld.

Due diligence by a buyer should therefore go beyond asking to see the sanctioned plan and should extend to who actually applied for it, whether every co-owner of the underlying land consented, and whether the construction on the ground matches what was approved. A mismatch between the sanctioned plan and the built structure is exactly the Section 392 and Section 400 exposure discussed through this piece, and it does not disappear simply because a buyer purchased in good faith; the demolition or stoppage power the Corporation holds runs against the building itself, and reaches the person who originally built it just as readily as a later purchaser.

None of this is a reason to avoid buying in Kolkata, but it is a reason to have a lawyer check the sanctioned plan against the completion certificate, the mutation record and the physical structure before money changes hands, rather than relying on a developer's assurance that everything is in order. A deviation that later surfaces, whether through a neighbour's complaint, a routine inspection or litigation between co-owners, can expose even an innocent buyer to the stop-work, demolition and change-of-use machinery set out in the sections that follow.

Unauthorised construction: Section 392 and what counts

Section 392 is the provision the Corporation and the courts point to when describing construction as unauthorised in the first place: erecting a building without the Commissioner's sanction, or at material variance from a sanctioned plan, falls within it. This is the trigger that sets the rest of the enforcement machinery, Sections 400, 401 and 401A among them, in motion, and it covers both the person who never applied for sanction at all and the person who applied, got a plan sanctioned, and then built something materially different from what was approved, whether that is an extra floor, a changed footprint or a structure that eats into a setback.

What counts as a material variance, as against a minor deviation that might later be regularised, is not spelled out in Section 392 itself with the precision an owner would like. The Corporation's own 2015 Regulations on regularisation, discussed later in this piece, are built around the idea that some deviations are minor enough to be brought within sanction after the fact and others are not, but the line between the two is drawn case by case rather than by a fixed statutory formula, which is one reason disputes over whether a given structure is regularisable or has to come down end up before the Tribunal and the High Court rather than being resolved at the Corporation's counter.

An owner who has not applied for sanction at all is in a weaker position than one who applied, received a sanction and then deviated from it, since the first scenario gives the Corporation nothing to point to except the structure itself, while the second at least leaves a sanctioned plan on record against which the extent of the deviation can be measured and, potentially, argued down to something the regularisation Regulations can reach. This is a reason for an owner mid-construction to apply for sanction even belatedly, rather than waiting to be caught, since a pending or recently granted sanction changes the character of the Corporation's response from pure Section 392 enforcement to a narrower question about variance from an approved plan.

The stop-work notice and the criminal exposure behind it

Before the Corporation moves to demolition, Section 401 gives it a stop-work notice, directing the person erecting a building or executing work to halt immediately, and the practice recorded in reported orders is that KMC also sends intimation of the notice to the local police station, so that continued activity on site does not go unnoticed by the police as well as by the Corporation's own building inspectors.

Section 401A then supplies the sanction behind that notice: a person who keeps building after a stop-work notice under Section 401 has been served exposes themselves to an FIR under Section 401A, which runs on a separate track from the civil demolition proceeding under Section 400. This is a point that an owner mid-construction in Kolkata often underestimates. Ignoring a stop-work notice is not simply a risk that the structure might eventually be pulled down; it is a distinct criminal exposure that attaches to the act of continuing work once the notice is in hand, whatever happens later to the building itself.

Section 400: the demolition order, its hearing requirement, and its appeal

This is the provision the Calcutta High Court has itself described, in terms quoted directly from the Act, as dealing with the order of demolition and stoppage of buildings and works in certain cases, and appeal, meaning Section 400. It runs across eight subsections, and the two that matter most in practice are Section 400(1), which is the power to direct stoppage of construction and demolition of unauthorised building, and Section 400(8), which is the power actually used to carry out demolition once an order has been made and has not been complied with.

The Calcutta High Court has been consistent, across a run of decisions through 2023 and 2024, that Section 400(1) carries a hearing requirement before an order can be made, and that skipping it is not a technicality a court will overlook. In Suresh Prasad Gupta, a Division Bench in August 2023 set aside a demolition order because nothing on record showed that a proper proceeding under Section 400(1), including notice to the owner, had actually been initiated, and directed the Corporation to start the process afresh with proper service on the appellant. A 2024 ruling in Shree Shyam Projects took the same line, holding that the Act requires a show cause notice to the person affected before an order of demolition can be passed. Earlier rulings in the same line have held that an order of demolition may only be passed after hearing the person at whose instance the construction work was commenced, and have faulted notices under Section 400 for being too vague about which construction they actually concern.

Once an order under Section 400(1) is made, Section 400(3) gives the person aggrieved thirty days from the date of the order to appeal to the Municipal Building Tribunal, a route discussed in more detail further on. Demolition itself, under Section 400(8), is typically carried out with the help of the police where resistance is expected, and at least one reported case records that the Corporation's internal process for authorising demolition under this subsection runs through its own political and administrative leadership before workmen are sent to site, though this account treats that detail as confirmed only for the specific matter in which it was recorded rather than as a universal procedural rule stated in the Act itself.

A separate pair of notices, issued under Sections 544 and 546, comes into play once a demolition order already exists: these tell occupants when the Corporation's own workmen will enter the building to carry out the demolition that has already been ordered. Courts have treated these notices as parasitic on the underlying order, so that a notice under Sections 544 and 546 that misstates the date of the demolition order it is meant to execute, or that is issued where no proper Section 400(1) proceeding preceded the order at all, has been held incapable of being acted upon. An owner served with a Section 544 or 546 notice should check it against the order it claims to execute before assuming the only option left is to comply.

Change of use and dangerous buildings: two tracks that look like Section 400 but are not

Section 416 deals with something different from unauthorised construction: it prohibits changing the use of a building, or part of one, from what was sanctioned, for example converting a residential flat into a commercial office or a godown without the Corporation's approval for that change. An order under Section 416 carries its own appeal to the Municipal Building Tribunal, and reported decisions describe that appeal as running within thirty days of the order, mirroring the Section 400(3) window even though the underlying wrong being punished, unsanctioned change of use rather than unsanctioned construction, is a different one.

Dangerous and ruinous buildings sit on a third track again. Section 411 lets the Corporation require an owner or occupier to repair a ruinous or dilapidated building under a notice issued under Section 411(1), or to vacate it under a notice issued under Section 411(2), and Section 411(4) has been used to authorise demolition of the most vulnerable part of a structure where the danger is immediate. The Kolkata Municipal Corporation (Amendment) Act, 2017 added Section 412A, which lets the Commissioner declare a building or part of it condemned and order its demolition where the Section 411 route would itself cause hardship to occupants, typically after a show cause notice inviting the occupants to explain why the building should not be treated as condemned or dangerous. Section 518 separately empowers the Corporation to require that a building, well or similar structure be rendered safe, a more general safety power distinct from both Section 411 and Section 412A.

The practical reason to keep these tracks separate is that the notice, the hearing and the appeal available differ from one to the other, and a tenant served with a Section 411(2) notice to vacate is in a materially different position, with different rights to insist on repair instead of vacation, from an owner facing a Section 400 demolition order over unauthorised construction. Courts have in fact stepped in on the Section 411 side too: in one 2019 matter a Calcutta High Court judge stayed a notice to vacate under Section 411(2) after the occupants gave an undertaking to carry out the repairs the Corporation had itself called for, which is a reminder that compliance, not just litigation, can sometimes be the quicker route out of one of these notices.

The Municipal Building Tribunal: who sits on it and how fast an appeal has to move

Section 415(1) requires the State Government to appoint a Municipal Building Tribunal for the Kolkata Municipal Corporation, and the Tribunal's job, under Sections 400(3) and 416, is to hear appeals from the Commissioner's orders on demolition, stoppage and change of use. Following a 2001 amendment, each bench of the Tribunal has to include at least two members, one a judicial member and one a technical member, the technical member being someone with knowledge or experience in a field such as town planning or civil engineering. The Chairman and the other members are appointed by the State Government on terms it determines and are paid out of the Corporation's Municipal Fund, and the Tribunal has its own establishment of officers and staff for this purpose. Sources consulted for this piece differ on the current ceiling on the total number of members the Tribunal may have, so that figure is deliberately left out here rather than stated as settled.

The thirty-day window to appeal under Section 400(3) or Section 416 is not merely a guideline. The Act applies Parts II and III of the Limitation Act, 1963, which deal with computing the limitation period and with condonation of delay, to appeals under this provision, so an appellant who misses thirty days is not automatically shut out and can in principle seek condonation under Section 5 of the Limitation Act. In practice, however, the Tribunal has shown little patience with weak explanations: at least one reported order records the Tribunal declining to condone a delay where the explanation offered for it was found insufficient, and the High Court has in a separate matter had to direct the Tribunal to decide a pending condonation application rather than simply sit on it. An appellant who has missed the thirty-day window should treat condonation as available in principle but unreliable in practice, and should not delay filing on the assumption that a good enough story will always rescue a late appeal.

Even a decided appeal does not always get the last word from the Tribunal itself, since courts have stepped in where the Tribunal's own process has gone wrong, as in a property-related matter where the Calcutta High Court set aside a Tribunal order for lacking reasoning and sent the appeal back to be decided afresh. That relationship between the Tribunal and the High Court, and what happens once a party has exhausted the Tribunal appeal, is discussed next.

After the Tribunal: revision under Article 227, and an unsettled question about writs

Because the Municipal Building Tribunal is a creature of statute rather than an ordinary civil court, a party dissatisfied with its order has historically gone to the Calcutta High Court by way of a revision petition under Article 227 of the Constitution, which gives the High Court supervisory jurisdiction over tribunals and courts subordinate to it. A 2008 matter decided on exactly this basis records the High Court treating a challenge to a Municipal Building Tribunal order as a revision under Article 227, following the Tribunal's dismissal of an appeal from a Deputy Chief Engineer's order, and that remains the route most commonly used to take a Tribunal order further.

Whether a writ petition under Article 226 is instead available against a Tribunal order, as a separate route from Article 227, is not something this research can state as settled. A single-bench matter from 2026 lists, as one of the issues before it, whether a challenge to a Tribunal order would lie before the High Court under Article 227 at all, which suggests the point was still being actively argued as recently as this year rather than treated as closed. Readers dealing with an unfavourable Tribunal order should take legal advice on which remedy to pursue rather than assume either route is automatically open, and should in any event keep the limitation clock for whichever remedy is chosen firmly in view, since neither Article 226 nor Article 227 comes with the kind of built-in condonation regime that Section 415 borrows from the Limitation Act for the Tribunal appeal itself.

Separately from any appeal or revision against a specific order, the Calcutta High Court's general approach to a straight writ petition filed instead of pursuing the statutory appeal is that the Court ordinarily declines to interfere where a statutory appellate forum is available, while treating that as a rule of practice rather than an absolute bar. A 2021 decision in Tapan Kumar Mukherjee recorded the Corporation arguing precisely this, that because the Act provides an appeal against a demolition order, a writ court ought not to have set the order aside in the first place, which shows the argument cutting both ways depending on who is seeking to use or avoid the statutory route in a given case.

What a neighbour can and cannot do

A neighbour watching unauthorised construction go up next door is not without a remedy, but the remedy runs through two different doors depending on what is actually being complained of. The Corporation can act on a complaint from a member of the public, a neighbour included, or on its own initiative, where construction breaches the Building Rules or departs from a sanctioned plan, and a neighbour's complaint of that kind is squarely the Corporation's business under Sections 400 and 401.

What the Corporation will not do is resolve a private dispute between neighbours over ownership, boundaries, shared walls or easements such as a claimed right to light or air. In a matter involving exactly this kind of complaint, the Calcutta High Court held that the Corporation's function is to check whether construction follows a validly sanctioned plan and the Building Rules, not to adjudicate a private dispute between owners or co-owners, and that the appropriate remedy for the private dispute, if any, lies in a civil suit rather than in pressing the Corporation to decide it. That civil suit and the Corporation's own enforcement proceeding are not mutually exclusive: courts have treated them as running on separate, parallel tracks, so a neighbour can both press a complaint with KMC over an unsanctioned structure and separately sue for a declaration, injunction or damages over whatever private right, such as an easement, the construction is said to infringe.

For a neighbour, the practical sequence is usually to lodge a written complaint with the Corporation first, since that can trigger Section 400 or Section 401 action without the cost and delay of a civil suit, while keeping the civil suit route in reserve for whatever the Corporation's enforcement either cannot or will not reach, such as a dispute over an easement of light and air or over where exactly a boundary runs. Treating the Corporation as the forum for every grievance against a neighbour's construction, including ones that are really about private rights rather than planning compliance, is a common and avoidable source of delay.

Regularisation: a narrow door, not a general amnesty

The older position in Kolkata was that the Act gave the Corporation no discretion at all to regularise unauthorised construction once it had gone up, a reading the Calcutta High Court applied in a 2014 decision holding that the 1980 Act, as it then stood, did not confer any power to regularise. The Kolkata Municipal Corporation (Amendment) Act, 2014 changed this for a defined category of cases, and the third proviso to Section 400(1) now covers minor unauthorised erection or work, read together with the Kolkata Municipal Corporation (Regularisation of Building) Regulations, 2015, which set out the scheme for bringing that minor category within sanction after the fact.

Litigation has itself tested the scope of this regularisation route. Courts have distinguished it from a general amnesty, holding in one matter that unauthorised construction cannot be regularised by a general resolution of the Corporation, and that statutory permission under the specific proviso, together with whatever limited compounding the Regulations allow, is what the scheme actually permits, rather than a broad discretion to wave through any deviation after the fact. This research did not find a reliable, independently confirmed fee schedule or compounding charge for regularisation, and no figure is given here for that reason; an owner considering this route should obtain the Corporation's current schedule directly rather than relying on a number quoted secondhand.

A separate development worth flagging, though its precise contours are not settled by the sources gathered for this piece, is a Supreme Court matter reported as having allowed a tenant to apply for regularisation of unauthorised construction under the 2015 Regulations, which if accurately reported would extend the practical benefit of the scheme beyond the owner who built the deviation to an occupant with a lesser interest in the property. Because this account relies on a single reported summary of that ruling rather than the order itself, a tenant hoping to rely on it should confirm the current position with a lawyer before assuming the route is open, rather than treating a secondhand report as the final word.

What this means for an owner facing a notice over a deviation from a sanctioned plan is that regularisation is worth raising as a possibility, particularly for something that looks minor on its face such as a small projection or an internal change that does not alter the building's footprint, but it should not be assumed to be available as of right, and an owner should not delay responding to a Section 400 or Section 401 notice on the strength of an unconfirmed assumption that the deviation will simply be regularised later.

A practical sequence once a notice arrives

For an owner who receives a stop-work notice under Section 401, the first and most urgent step is to stop work on site immediately, given the FIR exposure under Section 401A for continuing after the notice, and only then to turn to the merits of whether the construction is in fact unauthorised. The second step is to check whether a Section 400(1) proceeding, with proper notice and an opportunity to be heard, has actually been initiated, since the run of Calcutta High Court decisions discussed above shows that a demolition order made without that hearing is vulnerable to challenge on that basis alone.

If a demolition order is in fact made under Section 400(1), the thirty-day clock under Section 400(3) to appeal to the Municipal Building Tribunal starts running from the date of the order, and that date should be checked carefully against any later notice issued under Sections 544 and 546 to carry the order out, since a mismatch between the two has itself been a ground on which such notices have been quashed. Missing the thirty-day window is not necessarily fatal, given that Parts II and III of the Limitation Act apply to the appeal, but condonation should be treated as a fallback to argue for rather than a safety net to rely on.

This research did not find a reliable published figure for how long, in practice, the whole sequence from a stop-work notice to an actual demolition typically takes in Kolkata, and no such figure is given here, since enforcement is frequently contested at the Tribunal or the High Court and the time any individual matter takes varies with how hard-fought it is. A dispute that goes no further than the Corporation's own process can move in weeks; one that is appealed to the Tribunal, and then taken further by revision to the High Court, can run for a year or more once adjournments, interim stays and the ordinary pace of listing are accounted for, though no study known to this research puts a reliable average figure on that range.

What can be said with confidence is that the thirty-day appeal window is fixed by statute, that a proper Section 400(1) hearing is a precondition the courts have enforced strictly, and that an owner, tenant or neighbour dealing with any of these notices is better served by moving quickly within those fixed points than by assuming there is an indefinite amount of time to respond. Engaging a lawyer at the stop-work notice stage, rather than waiting for a demolition order to actually issue, is usually the difference between having options under Sections 400 and 401 and being left to argue only about the Tribunal appeal after the event.

The Kolkata Municipal Corporation Act, 1980 gives the Corporation real teeth against unauthorised construction, but it gives the person on the other end of a notice real procedural protections too, chiefly a right to be heard before a demolition order is made and a defined, if narrow, window to appeal once one is. The Calcutta High Court's own decisions over the past several years have policed those protections more than they have policed the Corporation's substantive power to act, which is a useful thing for an owner, tenant or neighbour to know before assuming that every notice from the Building department is the last word.

Where this account has left a point unresolved, such as the current ceiling on Tribunal membership or whether a writ under Article 226 lies against a Tribunal order in addition to a revision under Article 227, that reflects a genuine state of the sources rather than an oversight, and a reader facing a live notice or appeal should treat those as questions for direct advice rather than as settled by anything written here.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the Kolkata Municipal Corporation demolish a building without any hearing?

Not lawfully, if the demolition is being carried out under Section 400. The Calcutta High Court has repeatedly set aside demolition orders where no proper show cause notice or hearing under Section 400(1) preceded them, including in decisions from 2023 and 2024 that sent the Corporation back to start the process afresh.

What happens if I keep building after a stop-work notice under Section 401?

Beyond the risk to the structure itself, continuing work after a Section 401 stop-work notice has been served can lead to an FIR under Section 401A. That criminal exposure is separate from, and does not depend on the outcome of, any later demolition proceeding.

How long do I have to appeal a demolition order to the Municipal Building Tribunal?

Thirty days from the date of the order under Section 400(3), or from the date of a change-of-use order under Section 416. Parts II and III of the Limitation Act, 1963 apply, so condonation of a short delay is possible in principle, but the Tribunal has in practice declined to condone delays where the explanation offered was weak.

Can unauthorised construction in Kolkata be regularised instead of demolished?

Only within a narrow category. The third proviso to Section 400(1), read with the Kolkata Municipal Corporation (Regularisation of Building) Regulations, 2015, covers minor unauthorised erection or work. Courts have held that regularisation cannot happen through a general resolution and that the statutory route, with whatever limited compounding the Regulations allow, is what is actually available.

Will the Corporation step in if my neighbour's new construction blocks my light and air?

The Corporation's role is to check compliance with the sanctioned plan and the Building Rules, not to decide a private dispute over an easement of light and air. The Calcutta High Court has held that this kind of dispute belongs in a civil suit, which can run alongside, rather than instead of, a complaint to the Corporation about the construction itself.

What is the difference between a Section 400 notice and a Section 544 or 546 notice?

Section 400 is the provision under which the demolition order itself is made, after notice and a hearing. Sections 544 and 546 are used afterwards, to notify occupants that the Corporation's workmen will enter to carry out a demolition order that has already been passed. Courts have quashed Section 544 and 546 notices that cite the wrong order date or that follow a Section 400 order made without a proper hearing.

Is a Municipal Building Tribunal order final, or can it be challenged further?

A Tribunal order has been challenged before the Calcutta High Court by way of revision under Article 227, which is the route most commonly used. Whether a writ under Article 226 is also, or instead, available was still an actively argued question in at least one 2026 High Court matter, so this is not a settled point.

Does Section 416 cover unauthorised construction, or something else?

Section 416 deals with unsanctioned change of use, such as converting a residential flat into commercial space without the Corporation's approval for that change, which is a different wrong from unsanctioned construction under Section 400, even though an order under Section 416 carries a similar thirty-day appeal to the Municipal Building Tribunal.
