US and UK Estate Tax Exposure for NRIs and HNIs: What Happens to Foreign Assets After Death

India stopped taxing an estate at death in 1985. The United States and the United Kingdom did not, and an NRI or HNI family that assumes Indian succession law is the whole story usually finds that out at the worst possible time.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 16 November 2026 · Law stated as on 15 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An Indian family with a US brokerage account, a London flat, or a green-card-holding son is usually planning its succession around the Indian Succession Act and the Hindu Succession Act, because those are the statutes an Indian lawyer reaches for first. Neither statute has anything to say about what the United States or the United Kingdom does with those foreign assets the day the owner dies. This piece sets out what actually happens, as the law stands today, so that an NRI or HNI family can plan around it rather than discover it from an executor's letter.

Key points

  • India abolished estate duty with effect from 16 March 1985 and has not reintroduced it; there is no Indian tax on an estate simply because the owner has died.
  • The United States taxes a non-resident, non-citizen's US-situs assets above a USD 60,000 exemption at rates up to 40%, and that threshold is not indexed for inflation.
  • US-situs assets include US real estate and shares of US corporations (including US-listed stock held through an ordinary brokerage account), regardless of where the owner lived or died.
  • India and the United States have no estate tax treaty, so an Indian-domiciled NRI gets none of the treaty relief that nationals of some other countries can claim.
  • The United Kingdom replaced its domicile-based inheritance tax test with a residence-based 'long-term resident' test from 6 April 2025, built around UK tax residence in 10 of the preceding 20 tax years.
  • UK inheritance tax is charged at 40% above a nil-rate band of GBP 325,000, with a further residence nil-rate band for a home left to direct descendants.
  • US-situs exposure and UK long-term-resident status both turn on facts, what is held, and how long someone has been resident, that a family can still influence before death but not after.

The assumption that causes the trouble

Most of the NRI and HNI families we advise have already absorbed the basic point that Indian succession law depends on religion and domicile, and that a will drafted for Indian assets does not automatically govern assets sitting in a Schwab account in New York or a flat in Kensington. What they have usually not absorbed is that the country where those foreign assets sit may tax the estate itself, as a one-time charge triggered by death, independently of whatever India does or does not do.

That gap matters because India's own position creates a false sense of security. There is no Indian estate tax, no Indian inheritance tax, and no Indian gift tax on most transfers between relatives. An Indian lawyer reviewing a will correctly tells the family that the transfer of Indian assets to heirs will not itself attract tax. The same reassurance does not travel to Florida condominiums, US brokerage accounts, or London property, and a family that extrapolates India's tax-free succession regime onto foreign holdings is extrapolating from the wrong jurisdiction.

Two regimes come up most often for Indian families with cross-border wealth: the United States estate tax on non-resident aliens, and the United Kingdom's inheritance tax, now reformed from a domicile test to a residence test. Both are explained below as they stand today, with the figures and thresholds that are actually in force rather than the ones that circulate informally among NRI communities.

A third point is worth flagging before the detail: these are not India's problems to fix. No Indian statute, no Indian treaty negotiation, and no clause in an Indian will can switch off a foreign country's own estate tax rules. The only variables a family actually controls are what it holds, through what structure, and for how long a given family member remains tax resident somewhere, and those are precisely the variables this piece works through.

Why India itself is not the problem

The Estate Duty Act, 1953 taxed property passing on death in India. It was abolished with effect from 16 March 1985, a decision driven by the view that it had become administratively expensive to collect relative to the revenue it raised, and it has not been revived despite periodic political debate about reintroducing some version of an inheritance or estate tax. India also does not levy a general inheritance tax or a death duty under any other statute currently in force.

Gift tax has a more complicated history. The Gift Tax Act, 1958 was withdrawn in 1998, reintroduced in a different form from 2004 as a provision taxing certain gifts received without consideration as income in the recipient's hands under the Income Tax Act, subject to exemptions for gifts from specified relatives, on the occasion of marriage, and under a will or by way of inheritance. None of this is an estate tax in the sense used by the United States or the United Kingdom. It is relevant to lifetime gifting as a succession-planning tool, but it does not change the basic position that death itself does not trigger an Indian tax charge on the estate.

Periodic news cycles about a proposed Indian inheritance tax tend to unsettle HNI families more than the underlying proposals warrant. As matters stand today, none of those proposals has become law, and a family's planning should be built on the law actually in force, revisited if and when that changes, rather than on anticipated legislation.

The practical consequence is that an NRI or HNI family planning succession for Indian assets alone genuinely does not need to budget for an Indian estate tax, because none exists. The planning problem begins the moment the estate includes assets situated outside India, because those assets are taxed, if at all, under the law of wherever they sit, not under Indian law.

The US estate tax trap: who it catches and at what threshold

Unlike India, the United States taxes the worldwide estate of its citizens and of individuals domiciled in the US at death, with a federal exemption that, following the One Big Beautiful Bill Act signed in 2025, stands at USD 15 million per person for deaths and taxable gifts occurring on or after 1 January 2026, made permanent and indexed for inflation from 2027. That exemption is large enough that most Indian families never think about US estate tax as a serious exposure, and for a US citizen or a US-domiciled green-card holder, it genuinely is not, unless the estate is very large.

That generous exemption, however, belongs to citizens and US domiciliaries, not to an ordinary NRI. Matters change entirely for someone who is a non-resident alien for US estate tax purposes, meaning neither a US citizen nor domiciled in the United States, which in this context turns on where the person actually lives with no definite intention of leaving, not on immigration status or how often they visit. For a non-resident alien, the exemption against US-situs assets is USD 60,000, a figure fixed by statute rather than indexed for inflation, and it has stayed at that level for decades while the citizen exemption has climbed into the tens of millions.

Above that USD 60,000 threshold, US-situs assets are taxed at graduated rates reaching 40% on the excess. An Indian parent living in Delhi who holds a USD 400,000 position in US-listed shares through an ordinary international brokerage account, with no other connection to the United States beyond that account, is squarely a non-resident alien for this purpose, and the family's first exposure to US estate tax typically surfaces only when the brokerage freezes the account on notice of death and asks for an estate tax clearance before releasing the funds to the heirs.

It helps to say plainly what this is not. It is not a tax on the heirs for receiving an inheritance, in the way an inheritance tax works in some countries. It is a tax on the estate of the deceased non-resident alien, assessed against the US-situs assets that person owned at death, before those assets pass to anyone. Confusing the two leads families to assume, wrongly, that an heir who is themselves outside the United States has nothing to worry about.

What counts as US-situs property

Situs rules decide which assets fall inside the USD 60,000 net, and they do not track intuition. US real estate is US-situs, which is unsurprising. Shares of a US corporation are also treated as US-situs property for a non-resident alien, and this is the rule that catches people off guard, because it applies to the share itself, issued by a US company, regardless of where the shareholder lives, where the shares are custodied, or whether the holding is direct or through an ordinary brokerage account rather than a US-based one.

Other categories cut the other way. Funds on deposit with a US bank are generally treated differently from US corporate stock for estate tax situs purposes, and many practitioners structure a non-resident alien's liquid US-dollar holdings through bank deposits rather than brokerage positions in US-listed equities for that reason, though the precise treatment depends on the type of account and deserves a case-by-case check rather than a blanket assumption. Debt instruments, insurance proceeds on the decedent's own life, and interests in certain foreign (non-US) corporations holding US assets each have their own situs rules that a US estate tax specialist, not a general Indian succession lawyer, needs to confirm.

Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds domiciled outside the United States raise a related question that a growing number of advisers to Indian families now flag deliberately. An Irish-domiciled fund that itself holds US stocks is argued by some practitioners to fall outside US situs because the fund, not the underlying US stock, is what the non-resident alien actually owns, but this is a position advanced in advisory commentary rather than a settled rule confirmed here against primary IRS authority, and a family relying on it for a meaningful holding should get that specific structure checked rather than assume it.

The planning lever that follows from this is situs mapping, not panic. A family holding US-listed equities directly should know, before death rather than after, roughly how much of the portfolio is US-situs, whether that exposure is worth restructuring into a non-US-situs fund wrapper, and whether the cost and complexity of restructuring is justified by the size of the holding relative to the USD 60,000 threshold.

Form 706-NA and the compliance side

A non-resident alien's estate that holds US-situs assets, together with US-situs adjusted taxable gifts, exceeding USD 60,000 generally has to file Form 706-NA, the United States Estate (and Generation-Skipping Transfer) Tax Return for a non-resident not a citizen of the United States, within nine months of death, with an extension available on application. The filing obligation attaches even where a result turns out to be nil tax, because the exemption only shelters the amount up to USD 60,000 and the return is how that calculation is reported and verified.

In practice, the US custodian or broker holding the asset is usually the first party to insist on this. A brokerage or transfer agent asked to release a deceased non-resident alien's US shares to Indian heirs will typically require a US estate tax closing letter, a transfer certificate from the IRS, or equivalent clearance before releasing the asset, which means the family cannot simply route the matter entirely through an Indian probate court and expect the US asset to follow automatically. The Indian succession certificate or probate establishes who the heirs are; it does not, on its own, satisfy the US estate tax compliance the custodian is asking for.

This is also where cost surprises families. Engaging a US estate tax preparer, obtaining date-of-death valuations for US-situs holdings, and dealing with the IRS on a cross-border estate is rarely cheap relative to the value of a modest US holding, and the delay this introduces can run to many months, during which the asset sits frozen and unavailable to heirs who may have immediate financial needs.

Executors in India handling this for the first time also discover a practical obstacle: most Indian executors and family members have no US taxpayer identification number, no familiarity with IRS forms, and no existing relationship with a US-licensed tax preparer, so the first weeks after a death are often spent simply finding someone qualified to file Form 706-NA, rather than filing it. Identifying that adviser in advance, while the account holder is alive, removes a predictable source of delay.

No India-US estate tax treaty

Some countries have estate and gift tax treaties with the United States that can substitute a prorated share of the much larger citizen exemption, or shift the operative test from situs to domicile, for a non-resident alien who is domiciled in the treaty partner country. India is not one of those countries. India and the United States have an income tax treaty, the Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement, but that agreement addresses income tax and does not extend to estate or gift tax. An Indian-domiciled NRI's estate therefore gets no treaty-based relief and is assessed purely on the statutory USD 60,000 threshold against US-situs assets.

The absence of a treaty is not a technicality a family can work around with paperwork. It means the planning has to happen on the asset side, through situs mapping, holding structure, and insurance-funded liquidity for the heirs to meet any US tax before the custodian releases the asset, rather than through a treaty claim that simply is not available on an India-US estate.

Families sometimes ask whether routing a US holding through a company or trust set up in a third country that does have a US treaty changes the result. It can, in principle, but doing so correctly requires coordinated advice from a US estate tax specialist and the relevant third-country adviser, because a poorly structured arrangement can create its own tax or reporting problems, in India, in the third country, or both, that outweigh whatever US estate tax saving was intended.

The UK's move from domicile to residence

The United Kingdom has run its inheritance tax for decades on a domicile test: a UK-domiciled or deemed-domiciled individual's worldwide estate was within scope, while a non-UK-domiciled individual was taxed only on UK-situated assets. From 6 April 2025, that test changed. The Finance Act 2025 replaced the domicile-based test with a residence-based one, built around a new status called the long-term resident, and the change applies regardless of the individual's common-law domicile.

Practitioners generally describe the headline rule as a 10-out-of-20-year test: an individual who has been UK tax resident for 10 of the preceding 20 tax years becomes a long-term resident and is, from that point, within the scope of UK inheritance tax on their worldwide estate, not merely their UK assets. Residence for this purpose is measured under the UK's Statutory Residence Test, a separate and detailed set of day-count and connecting-factor rules that is applied year by year.

This has a direct bearing on an NRI who has spent a career moving between India and the UK, or an Indian-origin family with a UK-resident adult child who manages family wealth from London. Someone who assumed that staying non-domiciled in the English law sense kept their worldwide estate outside UK inheritance tax is now assessed on time spent as a UK tax resident instead, and ten years of UK residence accumulated over a career, even with gaps, can be enough to cross the threshold.

A transitional point deserves a mention for anyone who was already resident in the UK before the change: commentary on the reform describes transitional provisions for individuals whose UK residence history straddled the old and new regimes, and a family in that position should have their specific history checked against the Finance Act 2025 text rather than assume the ten-year count simply starts from 6 April 2025.

Exiting long-term resident status, and the position on gifts

Long-term resident status does not end the moment someone leaves the UK. The general position is that an individual remains a long-term resident, and therefore within the worldwide scope of UK inheritance tax, for a tail period after leaving that scales with how long they were UK resident: as little as three tax years of non-residence for someone resident for 10 to 13 of the preceding 20 years, rising band by band to a full ten consecutive tax years of non-residence for someone who was UK resident for 20 years or more, a considerably longer tail than many families expect when they plan an exit, especially at the long end. A spouse who is not themselves a long-term resident can also elect into long-term resident treatment in certain circumstances, and that election is subject to the same tapered exit rule.

Lifetime gifts interact with this status at the time the gift is made, not merely at death. A gift of non-UK assets made while the donor was not a long-term resident is generally kept outside the UK inheritance tax net even if the donor dies within the usual seven-year look-back period while by then a long-term resident, because the status that mattered was the donor's status when the gift was made. This makes the timing of lifetime gifting, relative to UK residence history, a live planning question for a family with a member moving in or out of UK tax residence.

Trusts add a further layer that deserves specialist advice rather than a general summary here: trust property's exposure to UK inheritance tax under the reformed rules now turns substantially on the settlor's long-term resident status at the relevant time, which means a trust set up years ago by someone who was then non-UK-resident can be drawn back into scope if the settlor later becomes a long-term resident, or kept out of scope if the settlor never does.

Under-18s and short-residence cases are treated differently again, with the relevant test tied to UK residence for at least half of the tax years since birth rather than the ten-out-of-twenty rule used for adults, which matters for a family planning around a child or grandchild who has been raised partly in the UK.

The nil-rate band, the residence nil-rate band and the 40% rate

Once an estate (or a relevant lifetime transfer) is within scope, UK inheritance tax is charged at 40% on the value above a nil-rate band currently set at GBP 325,000, a figure that has been frozen for several years and, according to the sources checked for this piece, is expected to stay frozen for some further years yet, so the current freeze date is worth confirming against the Finance Act in force at the relevant time rather than assumed to be fixed indefinitely. A reduced rate of 36% applies where at least 10% of the estate, measured after deducting the nil-rate band, is left to charity.

A further residence nil-rate band, separate from the main nil-rate band, is available where a home passes to direct descendants, and unused nil-rate band and residence nil-rate band can generally be transferred between spouses on the first death, which in combination can shelter a meaningfully larger value for a married couple's estate than the headline GBP 325,000 figure suggests on its own. The residence nil-rate band tapers away for larger estates, so it is not available without limit.

For an Indian family with a UK-resident member, the arithmetic that matters is not just the nil-rate band figure in isolation, but whether that person has already become, or is approaching, long-term resident status, because that status is what decides whether the 40% rate applies to UK assets alone or to the entire worldwide estate.

Putting the two regimes together for a real family

Consider a family with a patriarch resident in Delhi, a US-listed equity portfolio held through an international brokerage account, a flat in London occupied by an adult child who has lived and worked there for twelve years, and the rest of the estate in Indian property and bank deposits. The Indian assets pass under Indian succession law with no Indian estate tax. The US-listed shares are US-situs property exposed to US estate tax above USD 60,000, assessed on the patriarch's own death, regardless of the London-resident child's position. The London flat and any assets held through the UK-resident child's own name are a separate question turning on that child's personal long-term resident status, assessed on the child's own eventual death, not on the patriarch's.

Three separate death-triggered tax events can therefore arise out of what the family experiences as a single succession, each governed by a different country's law, on a different person's status, with different thresholds and no treaty bridging the Indian-American leg. Treating this as one problem to be solved by one Indian will is the mistake that causes the delay and cost when it eventually surfaces.

A family of this kind also tends to underestimate how fast professional fees accumulate once three jurisdictions are in play simultaneously. A single-country estate can usually be administered by one firm; a three-country estate needs an Indian succession lawyer, a US estate tax preparer and a UK inheritance tax adviser working in parallel, and the coordination cost between them is itself a real expense that nobody budgets for until the family is already inside probate.

None of this argues for abandoning US or UK holdings. It argues for mapping situs and residence exposure asset by asset and person by person, well before the question becomes urgent, and for building liquidity, typically through term insurance proceeds payable outside the taxed estate, so that heirs are not forced to sell an illiquid asset under time pressure merely to pay a foreign tax bill before a custodian will release the asset to them.

What a family should actually do

Start with an inventory that tags each asset by country of situs and, for any family member who has spent time abroad, by residence history against the relevant foreign test, whether that is the US situs rules or the UK's ten-out-of-twenty-year count. This inventory, not the Indian will, is what tells you which foreign tax regime is actually in play.

For US exposure, get a specific estimate of US-situs value against the USD 60,000 threshold, and discuss with a US-qualified estate tax adviser whether restructuring into non-US-situs wrappers, adjusting how US equities are held, or simply budgeting insurance-funded liquidity for a Form 706-NA filing and any resulting tax, makes sense given the size of the holding. For UK exposure, track the family member's actual UK residence years against the Statutory Residence Test, because the ten-year clock is the single fact that decides whether worldwide assets are at stake.

Each adviser involved should also be told, explicitly, about the other jurisdictions in play, so that an action taken for US purposes, such as retitling a brokerage account, is checked against its UK and Indian consequences before it is carried out, rather than after.

Keep the Indian succession documents, the US compliance, and the UK position coordinated through advisers who talk to each other, rather than three separate professionals each assuming the other jurisdiction is someone else's problem. The structural fix is not complicated once the exposure is mapped; the expensive outcomes we see are almost always the result of nobody having mapped it before death.

Sequencing the three jurisdictions when a death has already happened

A family that reads this after a death, rather than before one, is working against a clock rather than planning around it, and the sequencing matters because the three jurisdictions do not wait for each other. Indian probate or letters of administration can usually be initiated immediately, since Indian courts do not require foreign estate tax clearances before granting Indian relief, and getting that Indian order in hand early gives the family a document every other adviser will eventually ask to see.

On the US side, the nine-month filing clock for Form 706-NA starts running from the date of death regardless of how long the Indian probate takes, so a family should engage a US-qualified preparer within the first few weeks, gather date-of-death valuations for every US-situs holding while records are fresh, and apply for an extension early if the Indian probate is clearly going to take longer than nine months, rather than letting the US deadline lapse while waiting for Indian paperwork that the US filing does not actually depend on.

For the UK leg, the family's first task is establishing, as a fact, whether the deceased (or the relevant UK-connected family member) had actually crossed the long-term resident threshold, because that single determination decides whether UK advisers are assessing UK assets alone or the entire worldwide estate. This is a residence-history exercise, built from visa records, tax returns and travel history, not a judgment call, and it should be done before instructing a UK probate solicitor on scope.

Running these three tracks in parallel, with each adviser copied on what the other two are doing, is slower to set up than letting each proceed in isolation, but it is markedly faster overall, because it avoids the common failure mode where a US custodian releases funds only after UK probate is produced, while the UK solicitor is simultaneously waiting on the US clearance letter before finalising their own account, and nobody on either side was told the other was waiting.

Record-keeping during this period is worth more than it seems at the time. Every valuation obtained, every form filed, and every email exchanged with a foreign custodian or revenue authority should be kept in one shared file accessible to the Indian lawyer, the US preparer and the UK solicitor alike, because disputes that arise later between co-heirs, or queries raised on a subsequent audit in either country, are resolved far more cheaply from a complete contemporaneous record than from reconstructing events years after the estate has closed.

India's decision to abolish estate duty in 1985 was a decision about India. It did not, and could not, change what the United States does with a non-resident alien's US-situs shares, or what the United Kingdom now does once a family member crosses ten years of UK tax residence under the post-2025 regime. An NRI or HNI family with assets or people connected to either country is managing two or three separate succession-tax systems at once, whether or not anyone has told them so.

The answer is not to avoid holding foreign assets. It is to know, asset by asset and person by person, which foreign regime applies, what threshold or test triggers it, and what liquidity will be needed to satisfy it without forcing a distress sale. That mapping is cheap to do now and expensive to skip.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does an NRI living in India owe Indian tax when they inherit money or property?

No. India abolished estate duty in 1985 and has no general inheritance tax, so the act of inheriting Indian assets does not by itself attract Indian tax. Separate rules on capital gains apply later if an inherited asset is sold, and separate gift-tax provisions under the Income Tax Act can apply to certain lifetime transfers, but the inheritance itself is not taxed in India.

I am an NRI with US-listed shares worth about USD 100,000. Will my estate owe US tax?

Possibly, yes. As a non-resident alien, your US estate tax exemption against US-situs assets is USD 60,000, not the much larger amount available to US citizens and residents. US-listed shares are treated as US-situs property, so the value above USD 60,000 can be taxed at rates up to 40%, and your estate will generally need to file Form 706-NA whether or not tax is finally payable.

Can my family rely on the India-US tax treaty to reduce US estate tax?

No. The India-US Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement covers income tax, not estate tax, and the two countries have no separate estate or gift tax treaty. Some other countries do have such treaties with the United States, which can change the calculation for their residents, but an Indian-domiciled estate gets no equivalent relief.

How long does someone have to live in the UK before their worldwide estate is taxed there?

Under the regime in force since 6 April 2025, a person generally becomes a 'long-term resident', and is taxed on their worldwide estate rather than only UK assets, once they have been UK tax resident for 10 of the preceding 20 tax years, as measured under the UK's Statutory Residence Test.

If my adult child moves back to India after years in the UK, do they immediately stop being taxed on worldwide assets?

No. Once someone becomes a long-term resident, that status continues for a tail period after leaving that depends on how long they were UK resident: as little as three tax years of non-residence for someone just over the 10-year threshold, rising to a full ten consecutive tax years only for someone who was UK resident for 20 years or more. Leaving the UK starts that clock; it does not end the exposure immediately, and for long-resident individuals the tail can run considerably longer than many expect.

Are bank deposits in the US treated the same as US shares for estate tax purposes?

Generally not. US bank deposits are usually treated differently from shares of US corporations for situs purposes, and many non-resident aliens hold US-dollar liquidity in deposit form partly for that reason, though the precise treatment depends on the account type and should be confirmed for the specific holding rather than assumed.

Does probate or a succession certificate obtained in India release US or UK assets to the heirs automatically?

No. Indian probate, letters of administration or a succession certificate establish who the lawful heirs are under Indian law, but a US custodian releasing US-situs assets will typically still require US estate tax compliance such as an IRS transfer certificate, and a UK institution may require separate UK probate or confirmation depending on the asset and the deceased's status.

Does life insurance help with this kind of cross-border estate tax exposure?

It is one of the more common tools used. Term insurance proceeds, structured so they are payable outside the taxed estate and quickly accessible to heirs, can fund a US Form 706-NA liability or give a UK long-term resident's family liquidity, without forcing the heirs to sell an illiquid asset such as a house under time pressure.
