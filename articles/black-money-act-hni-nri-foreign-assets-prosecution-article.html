The Black Money Act and HNI/NRI Exposure: Why a Missed Foreign Asset Entry Can Mean Prosecution

A salaried employee who forgets to tick a box for employer stock options, and a returning NRI who still holds an old overseas bank account, sit under the same statute as a person who deliberately parked money abroad to hide it. The Black Money Act does not, on its face, distinguish very well between the two.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 16 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

The Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015 was written with a specific target in mind: Indian residents who moved wealth abroad and never told the tax department it existed. In practice, the Act's reach has turned out to be considerably wider than that description suggests. A salaried employee at the Indian arm of a multinational who received stock options from the foreign parent, a returning non-resident who still holds a small bank account opened decades ago abroad, and a high-net-worth individual with an offshore family trust all fall within the same statutory language, and the last two years have made it clear that the tax department is actively using data from foreign governments to find exactly these people. What follows is as much about how to avoid triggering this Act in the first place as it is about what happens once a notice arrives.

Key points

  • The Black Money Act applies to a person who is 'resident' in India for the relevant assessment year and taxes undisclosed foreign income and the value of undisclosed foreign assets at a flat 30% under Section 3, with no deductions, exemptions or set-off of losses available against that tax.
  • Section 41 imposes a penalty equal to 300% of the tax so computed, on top of the 30% tax itself, which means an undisclosed foreign asset can expose its owner to tax and penalty together exceeding its full value before any criminal consequence is even considered.
  • Separate flat penalties for simply failing to report a foreign asset or account in the income tax return, even where no tax is actually due on it, sit at Sections 42 and 43; the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 raised the threshold below which this penalty does not apply, from Rs 5 lakh to Rs 20 lakh in aggregate value of foreign movable assets, effective from 1 October 2024, though this relief does not extend to foreign immovable property and does not remove the underlying duty to report.
  • Sections 49, 50 and 51 create separate criminal offences, for wilfully failing to furnish a return, for failing to disclose a foreign asset in a return that is otherwise filed, and for wilfully attempting to evade tax, with the most serious of these carrying rigorous imprisonment of three to ten years.
  • Unlike offences under the Income-tax Act, which can be compounded under Section 279(2) of that Act, the Black Money Act contains no compounding provision of its own, and the CBDT's own compounding guidelines expressly exclude offences connected to a Black Money Act prosecution, which means a prosecution under this Act generally cannot be settled by payment once it has been launched.
  • A tax department 'nudge' campaign through the end of 2025 specifically targeted salaried employees of multinational companies who held foreign stock options and RSUs, using data received under the Common Reporting Standard and FATCA, and urged revised returns disclosing these holdings in Schedule FA before a year-end deadline.
  • The Karnataka High Court held in 2024, in a ruling reported as Dhanashree Ravindra Pandit, that prosecuting a person under Section 50 of the Act for non-disclosure in income tax returns filed before the Act came into force violates Article 20 of the Constitution; this question appears to remain under further appellate consideration, so the retrospectivity position should be treated as unsettled rather than conclusively decided.
  • A returning NRI becoming an Indian tax resident should close out or formally account for every foreign account, policy and holding before the financial year in which residency changes, since the reporting duty attaches to the person's status as a resident for the relevant year and genuine inadvertence after the fact is a weaker defence than a clean position established in advance.

A Different Kind of Tax Statute

Most Indian tax disputes, a reassessment, a disallowed deduction, a transfer pricing adjustment, run through the ordinary machinery of the Income-tax Act, with appeals, settlement avenues and a working system of compounding for the handful of situations serious enough to carry criminal exposure. The Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015 was deliberately built outside that machinery, as a separate code with its own flat tax rate, its own penalty structure stacked on top of that tax, and its own prosecution provisions that do not plug into the Income-tax Act's compounding scheme at all.

The policy reason for building a separate, harsher statute is not hard to see: ordinary reassessment timelines and penalty structures were judged too weak a deterrent against money moved outside the country specifically to escape Indian tax. The difficulty for an HNI family, a returning NRI, or an employee who happens to hold foreign equity compensation is that the Act's language does not confine itself to deliberate evasion. It reaches an undisclosed foreign asset however it came to be undisclosed, and the burden of showing that an omission was innocent rather than wilful falls on the person who made it, not on the department that found it.

Who the Act Reaches and What Counts as an Undisclosed Foreign Asset

The Act applies to a person who qualifies as 'resident' in India under the Income-tax Act's residency rules for the relevant assessment year, which matters immediately for an NRI: the Act's harshest provisions generally do not reach a genuine non-resident's foreign holdings, but they switch on the moment that same person's stay in India in a given year crosses the threshold that makes them resident, including a resident but not ordinarily resident in some contexts, so a family that treats its NRI status as a permanent shield rather than a year-by-year question is working from the wrong assumption.

'Undisclosed foreign asset' and 'undisclosed foreign income' are defined broadly enough to catch more than offshore bank accounts in the traditional sense. Foreign bank accounts, shares and securities in foreign companies, interests in foreign partnerships and trusts, foreign immovable property, and financial interests in any foreign entity generally fall within scope, and the definition does not require that the underlying funds be illegitimately earned; an entirely legitimately earned amount of foreign income or an asset bought with disclosed, tax-paid money can still become an 'undisclosed' asset for this Act's purposes simply by not being reported where the law requires it to be reported, typically in Schedule FA of the income tax return.

The 30% Flat Tax and the 300% Penalty Stacked on Top of It

Where an undisclosed foreign asset or foreign income is actually assessed under the Act, Section 3 charges tax at a flat 30%, computed without the benefit of deductions, exemptions, set-off of brought-forward losses, or any of the ordinary reliefs a taxpayer would expect under the Income-tax Act. The flat rate is deliberately punitive by comparison with the ordinary slab-based or corporate rates that would otherwise apply, and it applies regardless of how the underlying income or asset would have been taxed, if at all, had it simply been disclosed in the first place.

Section 41 then layers a penalty on top of that tax, fixed at 300% of the tax so computed, which means the combined tax-and-penalty exposure on a single undisclosed foreign asset can run to roughly four times the tax figure itself, well in excess of the asset's own value once the underlying amount is modest and the penalty multiplier is applied. This is the exposure that makes voluntary, accurate disclosure before any notice arrives dramatically cheaper than correction after the department has already found the asset through its own channels, since the 300% penalty, unlike some penalty provisions elsewhere in Indian tax law, is not framed as a discretionary ceiling a tax officer can simply decline to impose.

Sections 42 and 43: The Flat Penalty for Simply Not Reporting, and the 2024 Relief

A separate and, in volume terms, far more common exposure arises even where no tax is actually due on the foreign asset at all, because the asset was bought entirely with already-disclosed, tax-paid money and generates no income the department has any claim to. Sections 42 and 43 of the Act impose a flat penalty, historically set at Rs 10 lakh, for failing to furnish a return disclosing a foreign asset or interest, or for failing to disclose such an asset in a return that was otherwise filed, regardless of whether any tax liability actually arises on the asset itself.

The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 responded to years of criticism that this flat penalty was wildly disproportionate when applied to a modest, long-dormant foreign bank account or a small shareholding, by raising the threshold below which the penalty does not apply from Rs 5 lakh, the exemption a March 2022 CBDT instruction had set, to Rs 20 lakh in aggregate value of foreign movable assets, with effect from 1 October 2024. Two limits on this relief matter for anyone relying on it: it covers movable assets only, so foreign immovable property remains outside the relief regardless of value, and it changes the penalty exposure, not the underlying duty to report, which the Central Board of Direct Taxes has been explicit continues to apply in full even where the value falls below the new threshold.

A person who has learned of this relief secondhand sometimes draws the wrong conclusion from it, treating a foreign account under Rs 20 lakh as something that no longer needs to appear on the return at all. That reading goes further than the amendment actually supports. The safer practice is to continue reporting every foreign asset in Schedule FA regardless of value, and to treat the Rs 20 lakh threshold as protection against the flat monetary penalty for an inadvertent, low-value omission, not as a reporting exemption in its own right.

Sections 49, 50 and 51: Where Criminal Exposure Actually Begins

Beyond the tax and the monetary penalties, the Act creates distinct criminal offences under Chapter V, aimed at wilful non-compliance rather than at the underlying existence of a foreign asset by itself. A wilful failure to furnish a return of income at all, where a foreign asset or income existed that should have triggered the filing obligation, is treated as an offence carrying a substantial term of rigorous imprisonment. A failure to disclose a foreign asset or interest in a return that was otherwise properly filed is treated as a separate offence, and a wilful attempt to evade tax, penalty or interest chargeable under the Act is punished most severely, with rigorous imprisonment of three to ten years together with a fine, under Section 51.

The common thread the department relies on to move from the civil penalty provisions to a criminal complaint under these sections is wilfulness, and reported case law has treated a person's own disclosure and payment of the tax and penalty due, availed of properly and before enforcement action, as taking the person outside the Act's penal provisions altogether, which is the clearest signal available that voluntary correction, however painful the 300% penalty makes it, remains meaningfully better than waiting to be found out.

A person facing an actual notice or summons under these provisions should treat the wilfulness question as the entire battle, since the civil tax and penalty exposure under Sections 3 and 41 is largely mechanical once an undisclosed asset is established, but whether the omission was wilful, as opposed to a genuine oversight, a reliance on professional advice that turned out to be wrong, or a lapse that came to light and was corrected before any enforcement action began, is where a competently defended case is actually won or lost.

Why a Black Money Act Prosecution Cannot Simply Be Settled

An offence under the ordinary Income-tax Act carries a safety valve that often surprises people encountering Indian tax prosecution for the first time: Section 279(2) of that Act lets specified senior tax officers compound many offences, before or after a prosecution is launched, effectively allowing a case to be resolved by payment and an undertaking rather than proceeding to trial. The Black Money Act contains no equivalent compounding provision of its own, a gap that was confirmed publicly when the Act was first brought into force and that remains the position today.

The Central Board of Direct Taxes' own compounding guidelines for offences under the Income-tax Act, most recently updated in October 2024, go a step further and expressly exclude from compounding any offence that is directly connected to an offence under the Black Money Act, closing off even the possibility that a related Income-tax Act charge arising from the same facts could be compounded as a workaround. The practical consequence is that once a prosecution complaint is actually filed under Sections 49, 50 or 51, the matter proceeds through the ordinary criminal trial process to its conclusion, acquittal, conviction, or withdrawal of the complaint itself, rather than being resolved by a negotiated payment, which raises the stakes of getting the disclosure and wilfulness questions right well before that stage is reached.

The NUDGE Campaign: Why Employees With Foreign Stock Options Started Getting Emails

Through the latter part of 2025, a significant number of salaried employees at Indian subsidiaries and branches of multinational companies began receiving emails from the Income Tax Department flagging apparent foreign assets that had not been disclosed in Schedule FA, most commonly shares, restricted stock units or options received from a foreign parent company as part of compensation. Reported coverage of this campaign describes the department working directly with the human resources functions of a significant number of multinational employers to raise awareness among staff, alongside the emails sent to individual taxpayers, and urging revised returns for the relevant assessment year by a year-end deadline at the close of December 2025.

The data behind these emails comes substantially from automatic exchange-of-information arrangements, the Common Reporting Standard among many jurisdictions and the separate FATCA arrangement with the United States, under which foreign brokers, custodians and in some cases the foreign parent companies themselves report holdings linked to Indian taxpayers back to Indian authorities, who then cross-check that data against what was actually disclosed in Schedule FA. An employee whose employer-granted equity sits in a foreign brokerage account is, on the evidence of this campaign, now meaningfully more likely to be identified for a mismatch than they might have assumed even two or three years ago, and employer TDS already deducted on the value of the equity compensation does not substitute for the employee's own, separate duty to report the underlying foreign asset.

An employee who receives one of these emails or notices should treat it as an opportunity to correct the record through a revised return rather than as an accusation to be ignored, since the Act's own case law draws a sharp line between a person who corrects an omission once it is identified, even where the identification came from the department's own data matching, and a person who is found out and continues to resist disclosure, with the latter position carrying materially greater exposure under Sections 49 to 51.

Search and Seizure Under the Income-tax Act 2025 as the Usual Trigger

Outside the NUDGE-style data-matching campaigns, the more traditional route by which an HNI family's Black Money Act exposure actually surfaces is a search and seizure action under the Income-tax Act, 2025, carried out at a residence or business premises on suspicion of undisclosed domestic income, during which documents or electronic records referencing a foreign bank account, an offshore structure, or foreign property come to light incidentally. Once that happens, the investigating officers typically refer the foreign-asset angle for separate assessment under the Black Money Act, running alongside whatever domestic assessment the search itself was originally aimed at, and the family then faces two parallel sets of proceedings rather than one.

A family under search should assume from the outset that any foreign connection found in its papers, a foreign bank statement, correspondence with an offshore trustee, a foreign property deed, will be referred for Black Money Act scrutiny regardless of whether the search warrant itself mentioned foreign assets, and should take legal advice on the Black Money Act angle specifically and immediately, rather than treating the foreign-asset question as a secondary issue to the primary domestic search once the domestic matter is under control.

The Retrospectivity Fight: Where Dhanashree Ravindra Pandit Leaves Things

One of the more consequential open questions under the Act concerns how far back it can reach. The Act came into force with effect from 1 July 2015 and applies, by its own terms, from the assessment year commencing 1 April 2016, but the department has in a number of cases sought to prosecute non-disclosure that occurred in income tax returns filed for years well before 2015, on the theory that the asset remained undisclosed on the date the Act commenced even though the original return predates it.

The Karnataka High Court, in a 2024 ruling reported as Dhanashree Ravindra Pandit and involving non-disclosure in returns filed for assessment years 2007-08 and 2009-10, held that prosecuting a person under Section 50 of the Act for conduct, the original non-disclosure, that occurred years before the Act even existed violates Article 20 of the Constitution, which bars conviction for an act that was not an offence under the law in force at the time it was committed. The ruling sits alongside an earlier Supreme Court order in a separate matter involving Gautam Khaitan, decided in 2019, which held that the Act's commencement date did not itself make the Act retrospective and sent the underlying constitutional challenge back for decision on its merits rather than resolving it.

Reported commentary following the Karnataka High Court's 2024 ruling notes that the question has moved further up the appellate chain, and this piece does not treat the retrospectivity question as conclusively settled in either direction as of the date given above. A family or individual facing a Black Money Act prosecution for conduct substantially predating 2015 should raise the retrospectivity point as a serious, live defence, while recognising that its ultimate fate depends on how the appellate proceedings following the Karnataka High Court's ruling are eventually resolved.

Offshore Trusts and Family Structures: Where the Definitions Get Harder

A straightforward foreign bank account or brokerage holding is relatively easy to test against the Act's definitions: either it belongs to the resident taxpayer and was not reported, or it was. An offshore family trust, a foreign holding company sitting between an Indian family and underlying foreign investments, or a foreign life insurance or annuity policy with an investment component, raises a harder question that the Act does not answer with the same clarity: whose asset is it for reporting purposes, the settlor who funded the trust, the trustee who legally holds it, or a beneficiary who may never receive a distribution at all.

Indian tax authorities have, in practice, tended to look through a foreign trust structure to the Indian-resident settlor or a beneficiary with effective control or a vested beneficial interest, treating that person as the one who should have reported the underlying assets, rather than accepting the trust's separate legal personality as a reason nobody in India needs to report anything. A family that set up an offshore trust years ago for entirely legitimate succession-planning reasons, and that has since had its structure challenged on exactly this reporting question, should not assume that the trust's foreign situs or its professional trustee's independence will, by itself, answer the question of who within the family carries the Schedule FA reporting obligation for the assets inside it.

The safer approach for any family maintaining this kind of structure is to obtain a specific, written analysis of who within the family is required to report what, revisited whenever the trust's beneficiaries, the settlor's residency, or the underlying assets change materially, rather than relying on a single opinion obtained at the time the structure was first set up and never revisited since.

Is There Any Voluntary Disclosure Route Left Outside the Original 2015 Window?

The Act's own one-time compliance window, under Section 59, ran for a few months after the Act commenced in 2015 and let a person declare previously undisclosed foreign assets on payment of tax and a reduced penalty without facing prosecution for that specific disclosure. That window closed in 2015 and has not been reopened, so a person discovering an old reporting gap today has no equivalent statutory amnesty to fall back on under the Black Money Act itself.

What remains available is the ordinary mechanism of filing a revised or belated income tax return disclosing the asset going forward, paying the tax and whatever penalty follows under Sections 41 to 43, and relying on the voluntary nature of that correction as the strongest available argument against the wilfulness the criminal provisions require. This is a materially weaker position than a formal amnesty would have been, since it carries the full 300% penalty exposure rather than a reduced compliance-window rate, but reported case law treating voluntary, pre-enforcement disclosure as taking a person outside the Act's penal provisions altogether makes this the only realistic route available for a family that wants to correct the position now rather than wait to be found.

What a Returning NRI Should Do Before, Not After, Becoming Resident

The single most common fact pattern behind an entirely avoidable Black Money Act exposure is an NRI returning to India for good, becoming tax resident from a particular financial year onward, and simply carrying forward the same foreign bank accounts, brokerage holdings and insurance policies they held while living abroad, without reassessing what Indian tax residency now requires them to report. The reporting obligation is tied to residency status for the relevant assessment year, not to when the asset was originally acquired, so an account opened decades earlier while genuinely non-resident becomes a reportable, and potentially 'undisclosed', foreign asset from the very first year that person counts as resident.

A returning NRI should, well before the move, take stock of every foreign account, policy, shareholding and property interest still held, decide deliberately which will be closed, repatriated or formally retained, and ensure that whatever is retained is accurately reported in Schedule FA from the first return filed as a resident, rather than treating old accounts as a settled matter simply because they predate the move. Where a family trust, an offshore company, or a foreign retirement account is involved, the question of how that specific structure should be reported, and whether it even falls within the Act's definitions, is genuinely technical enough to warrant advice before the first resident-year return is filed, not after a department notice has already arrived asking why it was not.

Defending a Black Money Act Notice: What Actually Moves the Needle

A notice or summons under the Act is not the point at which to start building a defence; by that stage, the facts of what was and was not reported are usually already fixed, and the available arguments narrow considerably. The two threads that recur across reported outcomes are the timing and manner of disclosure relative to any department action, and whether the underlying asset genuinely falls within the Act's definitions at all, since not everything a family holds abroad, a foreign pension entitlement with no present withdrawal right, for instance, necessarily counts as a reportable foreign asset in the way a bank account or a shareholding plainly does.

A family or individual who has received a notice should assemble, as a first step, a complete and honest account of every foreign holding, however small, cross-checked against what was actually reported in each year's Schedule FA, before engaging with the department at all, since an incomplete initial response that is later shown to have omitted something else found through the department's own data matching does more damage to the wilfulness defence than almost anything else that can happen in one of these cases. Professional advice at this stage should come from someone who has actually handled a Black Money Act matter through to resolution, since the statute's interaction with ordinary income tax procedure, and its complete absence of a compounding safety net, make it a meaningfully different exercise from an ordinary tax dispute even for an experienced tax litigator encountering it for the first time.

The Black Money Act's severity is not evenly distributed across the people it actually reaches. A family that deliberately concealed wealth abroad to escape Indian tax faces exactly the consequences Parliament intended. An employee with unreported foreign stock options, or a returning NRI with an old account nobody thought to close, faces the same flat 30% tax, the same 300% penalty, and exposure to the same criminal provisions, with the department's own data-matching under the NUDGE campaign now materially more likely to find the omission than it would have been even a few years ago.

The practical lesson for either group is the same: disclosure before detection is dramatically cheaper than disclosure after it, since the Act's penal provisions turn on wilfulness and a voluntary correction is the clearest evidence against it, and because, unlike an ordinary Income-tax Act offence, a Black Money Act prosecution that has already been launched generally cannot be settled by payment once it is underway.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Black Money Act apply to an NRI's foreign bank account?

Generally not while that person remains genuinely non-resident for Indian tax purposes. The Act's reporting and prosecution provisions turn on residency status for the relevant assessment year, so the exposure arises once, and if, that person becomes an Indian tax resident while still holding the foreign asset.

I have a foreign bank account worth less than Rs 20 lakh that I forgot to report. Do I still need to disclose it?

Yes. The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 raised the threshold below which the flat monetary penalty under Sections 42 and 43 does not apply, to Rs 20 lakh for foreign movable assets, but it did not remove the underlying duty to report the asset in Schedule FA, which continues to apply regardless of value.

Can I pay my way out of a Black Money Act prosecution the way I could compound an ordinary income tax offence?

No. The Black Money Act has no compounding provision of its own, and the CBDT's compounding guidelines for Income-tax Act offences expressly exclude offences connected to a Black Money Act prosecution, so a prosecution under this Act generally has to run its course once launched.

Why am I getting an email from the Income Tax Department about my employer's stock options?

The department has been using data received under international exchange-of-information arrangements, including the Common Reporting Standard and FATCA, to match foreign equity compensation, shares, RSUs and stock options, against what was actually disclosed in Schedule FA, and has emailed employees, and in some cases worked with employers, where a mismatch appears.

What is the difference between the tax under Section 3 and the penalty under Section 41?

Section 3 charges tax at a flat 30% on undisclosed foreign income or the value of an undisclosed foreign asset. Section 41 imposes a separate penalty equal to 300% of that tax on top of it, so the combined exposure is considerably larger than the tax figure alone.

Can I be prosecuted for not disclosing a foreign asset in a tax return I filed before 2015, when the Act did not yet exist?

This is genuinely contested. The Karnataka High Court held in 2024, in a case called Dhanashree Ravindra Pandit, that this would violate Article 20 of the Constitution, but the question appears to remain under further appellate consideration, so it should be treated as an unsettled, live defence rather than a resolved point of law.

What should I do before moving back to India permanently if I still hold foreign accounts or investments?

Take stock of every foreign account, policy and holding before the financial year in which your Indian tax residency changes, decide which to close or retain, and ensure whatever is retained is accurately reported in Schedule FA from the first return you file as a resident, taking advice in advance on anything structurally complex such as a trust or offshore company.

If a tax search finds a foreign bank account by accident, will I automatically face Black Money Act proceedings?

The foreign-asset angle is typically referred for separate assessment under the Black Money Act once found, regardless of what the original search warrant covered, so yes, a family under search should expect any foreign connection found in its papers to trigger a parallel Black Money Act inquiry.
