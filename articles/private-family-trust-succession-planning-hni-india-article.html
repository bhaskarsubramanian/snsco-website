Private Family Trusts in India for HNI Succession Planning

Why families with businesses and significant assets are increasingly using a private trust alongside a will to plan succession, and what the Indian Trusts Act, the tax law and the Benami law actually require of that trust

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 14 November 2026 · Law stated as on 14 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family business with shares to protect across a generational transition, or a family simply unwilling to leave succession to a will and a probate process it cannot control, increasingly turns to a private trust instead. This article sets out what the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 actually requires of such a trust, how a revocable trust is taxed differently from an irrevocable one, why a discretionary trust can cost more in tax than a specific one, how a trust sits against the Hindu Undivided Family structure many of these same families already use, and where the Benami law and ordinary creditor and coparcenary rights still apply regardless of the trust.

Key points

  • A private family trust lets a family decide, while its members are alive and in agreement, how assets are to be managed and distributed, and can operate immediately, unlike a will, which takes effect only on death and becomes a document open to inspection once probated.
  • A trust is created under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882; a trust of immovable property must be in a written, signed instrument and registered under the Indian Registration Act, 1908, and stamp duty applies under the law of the relevant state.
  • Whether a trust is revocable or irrevocable changes who is taxed on its income: a revocable trust's income is taxed in the settlor's hands under Sections 61 to 63 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, while an irrevocable trust is taxed under the ordinary trust provisions.
  • A specific trust, where beneficiaries' shares are fixed, is taxed in each beneficiary's hands at their own applicable rate; a discretionary trust is taxed on its entire income at the maximum marginal rate under Section 164 of the 1961 Act, subject to narrow exceptions, carried forward in renumbered form into the Income-tax Act, 2025 from 1 April 2026.
  • A properly constituted trust, with a registered deed and contemporaneous documentation of the transfer, falls within the trustee or fiduciary exception to the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988 under Section 2(9)(A)(ii), but families need to keep that documentation from the outset, not reconstruct it later.
  • A trust does not override coparcenary rights in ancestral Hindu property, dependants' maintenance claims, or a court's power to set aside a transfer made to defeat an existing creditor, though it does generally protect trust assets from a beneficiary's own later, personal creditors.
  • Many families are moving family wealth from a Hindu Undivided Family into a private trust because the HUF's karta carries unlimited personal exposure for the family business's debts, while a trust separates management from entitlement more cleanly, though the two structures can also coexist.

What a private family trust actually does that a will does not

A private family trust lets a family move the ownership of its assets, shares in a family business, real estate, securities, out of individual names and into a structure that a chosen set of trustees manages for the benefit of family members, on terms the family itself writes down in advance. The appeal to high net worth families and to the owners of family businesses is not that a trust avoids tax or defeats a rival claimant's rights, it generally does neither, but that it lets the family decide, while everyone is alive and in agreement, exactly how wealth is to be managed and distributed across generations, without waiting for a death and a probate process to answer that question.

A will only takes effect on death, has to be probated or at least proved in many cases before it can be acted on, and becomes a public document once it is probated, since probate proceedings and the will itself are open to inspection. A trust set up during the settlor's lifetime can start operating immediately, keeps its terms private between the settlor, the trustees and the beneficiaries, and can keep a family business running under professional or family management through a transition that a sudden death would otherwise disrupt. Families with a business to protect, a beneficiary who is a minor or otherwise not yet ready to manage assets directly, or members living outside India, tend to find the trust route solves problems a will cannot.

None of this means a trust is the right choice for every family or every asset. Setting one up costs more, in professional fees, stamp duty and ongoing compliance, than writing a will, and an improperly structured or poorly maintained trust can create its own disputes, sometimes worse than the ones it was meant to prevent, if the deed is unclear or the family does not actually follow its own governance terms once the founder is no longer directing things personally. The right starting point is almost always the family's actual goals, continuity of a business, provision for a vulnerable beneficiary, simple orderly succession, rather than a trust deed assembled from a template without reference to what the family is actually trying to achieve.

The legal basis: the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, and what makes a trust valid

A private trust in India, as distinct from a public charitable trust, is created and governed under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882. The Act requires a lawful purpose, a settlor competent to contract, one or more trustees willing to accept the office, and a clearly identified beneficiary or class of beneficiaries, together with property that is actually transferred to the trust. A trust of movable property can, in principle, be created without a written instrument, but a trust of immovable property has to be declared in a written and signed instrument under Section 5 of the Act, and that instrument has to be registered under the Indian Registration Act, 1908 when it is a non-testamentary disposition of immovable property, since such instruments fall within the categories of documents that compulsory registration under Section 17 of the Registration Act requires.

The trust deed is the document that does the real work. It names the settlor, the trustees and their powers, the beneficiaries and their respective entitlements or, in a discretionary trust, the class from which the trustees may choose, the duration or the events that bring the trust to an end, and the mechanism for appointing a successor trustee if one dies, resigns or becomes unable to act. A badly drafted deed, one that leaves the beneficiaries' shares genuinely unclear, or that does not properly provide for what happens if a named trustee cannot continue, is a common source of family disputes years later, generally at the point when the original settlor is no longer there to clarify what was intended.

Stamp duty is payable on the trust deed under the stamp law of the state where it is executed or where the trust property is situated, and the rate and basis of calculation differ from state to state and depending on whether the property settled is movable or immovable. Because stamp duty legislation is amended frequently and varies by state, a family setting up a trust should have the duty calculated for the specific state and the specific assets involved at the time of execution, rather than relying on a figure quoted for a different state or an earlier year.

Choosing the structure: revocable or irrevocable, specific or discretionary

A settlor who retains the power to revoke the trust and take the assets back, directly or indirectly, has created a revocable trust, and a settlor who gives up that power permanently, or for a defined period tied to a beneficiary's lifetime, has created an irrevocable trust. The choice has a direct tax consequence. Under Sections 61 to 63 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, income arising from a revocable transfer is taxed in the hands of the settlor, not the trust or the beneficiary, because the law treats a transfer the settlor can undo as not having really parted with the income at all. Section 62 carves out transfers that are irrevocable for a specified period, including transfers that are irrevocable during a beneficiary's lifetime, which take the trust's income out of the settlor's hands and into the ordinary trust taxation rules instead.

Separately from revocability, a trust is either specific or discretionary depending on how the beneficiaries' shares are described in the deed. A specific trust states each beneficiary's share precisely and leaves the trustees no choice in the matter, while a discretionary trust gives the trustees the power to decide, within the class of beneficiaries the deed names, who receives what and when. Families choosing between the two are usually balancing certainty for the beneficiaries against flexibility for the trustees to respond to circumstances, a beneficiary who later proves irresponsible with money, a change in a family member's needs, that could not be fully anticipated when the deed was signed.

How a specific trust is taxed

Where the trust deed fixes each beneficiary's share with certainty, the Income-tax Act taxes that beneficiary directly on their share of the trust's income, generally at the same rates that would apply if the beneficiary had earned that income personally, under the representative assessee provisions in Sections 160 to 163 of the Act. This pass-through treatment is one of the reasons a specific trust is often the simpler structure where the family's main goal is holding and managing an asset for named, settled beneficiaries, a minor child, for instance, rather than exercising ongoing discretion over who benefits.

A specific trust for a minor beneficiary also avoids a separate complication that arises when a minor simply holds an asset in their own name, namely the clubbing of a minor child's income with a parent's income under the Act's provisions on minors' income, since the income is instead assessed in the hands of the trustee as representative assessee for the minor beneficiary rather than attributed back to the parent in the first instance, though the exact interaction between these provisions and a given family's facts is worth confirming with a tax adviser before the trust is relied upon for that purpose.

The trustee, as representative assessee, is responsible for filing the trust's returns and paying the tax assessed, and the beneficiary is separately liable only to the extent the trustee has not already discharged the liability, which is why the trust's own compliance history matters even though the tax is, in substance, the beneficiary's.

How a discretionary trust is taxed

A discretionary trust is taxed differently because, until the trustees actually exercise their discretion, no beneficiary has a determinate, identifiable share of the trust's income. The Income-tax Act responds to that uncertainty by taxing the entire income of a discretionary trust at the maximum marginal rate under Section 164 of the 1961 Act, carried forward in renumbered form into the Income-tax Act, 2025 that took effect from 1 April 2026, subject to limited statutory exceptions, including a proviso that allows slab-rate taxation for a trust created under a will for the exclusive benefit of a dependent relative in specified circumstances.

The maximum marginal rate treatment is the single most important planning fact about a discretionary trust, and families sometimes set one up without appreciating that it can produce a materially higher tax bill on the trust's income than a specific trust or direct ownership would, unless the trust falls within one of the narrow exceptions the Act allows. Whether a specific trust, a discretionary trust, or a combination, different classes of beneficiaries under separate specific sub-trusts, for example, is the right structure is a question that has to be worked out against the family's actual income profile and goals, not assumed from a template.

A deed can also combine both approaches in stages, for instance by holding a founder's wealth in a single discretionary trust during the founder's lifetime so that the trustees retain flexibility to respond to each beneficiary's changing circumstances, and then converting that trust, or settling a fresh one, into specific shares for the next generation once the founder is no longer there to exercise informal influence over how the trustees' discretion is used. Families that expect the needs of their beneficiaries to change substantially over a long horizon, young grandchildren, for example, whose education and career paths are not yet known, tend to prefer keeping discretion available for longer even at the cost of the higher tax rate that comes with it, while families whose beneficiaries' needs are already settled often move to a specific trust structure as soon as it is practical to do so.

Trust or Hindu Undivided Family: why many families are moving from one to the other

Hindu families have traditionally held business and property through a Hindu Undivided Family, with the senior member acting as karta and managing the HUF's assets on behalf of the coparceners. An HUF is simple to create, in the sense that it can arise automatically on marriage and the birth of children within a Hindu family, and succession within it follows the ordinary rules of Hindu succession among the coparceners without a separate deed. Its drawback is that the karta's authority to manage HUF property does not come with limited liability, and the karta's own personal assets can be exposed where the HUF's business runs into debt, while coparceners such as married daughters may find their practical relationship with HUF property more complicated than a trust beneficiary's would be.

A private trust, by contrast, lets the family decide, rather than have Hindu succession law decide for it, exactly who benefits and on what terms, and lets the family separate management, through the trustees, from entitlement, through the beneficiaries, more cleanly than an HUF structure does. Several advisers now recommend moving family wealth out of an HUF and into a private trust precisely for this reason, though partitioning an HUF and resettling its assets into a trust is itself a transaction with its own tax and stamp duty consequences that has to be planned rather than done informally. Neither structure suits every family, and many families continue to run a trust and an HUF side by side, with the HUF sometimes named as a beneficiary of the trust rather than the two being treated as competing alternatives.

Funding the trust: moving family business shares and property into it

Settling shares of a family company into a trust is usually the more straightforward transfer, since it is typically done by a share transfer deed and attracts stamp duty on that transfer at the rate the relevant state's stamp law prescribes for share transfers, together with any transfer restrictions in the company's articles or any shareholders' agreement that has to be checked and complied with before the trustees can be registered as members. Settling immovable property is more involved, since the trust deed itself, to the extent it deals with that property, has to be registered, stamp duty has to be paid on the value of the property settled under the state's stamp law, and in many states this can be a materially larger cost than the duty on a share transfer, which is a reason many family trusts are funded primarily with financial assets and company shares rather than directly held real estate, with real estate sometimes held through a holding company whose shares are then settled into the trust instead.

Whatever is settled, the transfer to the trustees has to actually happen, in the sense of title passing to the trustees in the trust's name, and not merely be recorded on paper while the settlor continues to deal with the asset as their own. Trusts that are poorly funded in this sense, where the settlor keeps operating a bank account or dealing with shares as though the trust were never really created, are vulnerable to being challenged later as a sham, with the attendant risk that the arrangement is treated as never having taken effect for either tax or succession purposes.

The practical steps that evidence a genuine transfer differ by asset but follow the same logic throughout: shares have to be transferred on the company's own register, with the trustees, not the settlor, shown as the registered holder and exercising voting rights accordingly; bank balances settled into the trust have to actually move into an account opened and operated in the trustees' names rather than merely being labelled as trust money while sitting in the settlor's personal account; and immovable property has to be conveyed by a registered instrument in the trustees' favour, with municipal and revenue records updated to reflect the change, rather than left in the settlor's name on the understanding that it is really the trust's.

Trusts and the Benami law: why documentation at the time of settlement matters

The Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988 defines a benami transaction broadly enough that, without a specific exception, holding property in a trustee's name for the benefit of someone else could fall within its reach. Section 2(9)(A)(ii) of the Act carves out property held by a person in a fiduciary capacity, which includes a trustee as defined by reference to Section 3 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, for the benefit of another person to whom that fiduciary stands in such a capacity. A properly constituted family trust, with a registered deed where the property is immovable, clear identification of the settlor, trustees and beneficiaries, and contemporaneous documentation of the transfer, falls within this exception and is not a benami arrangement.

The practical risk is less about the legal exception existing and more about being able to prove, years later, that it applies. Advisers who have dealt with Benami Act proceedings consistently point to the same requirement, transparent documentation created at the time the property was transferred into the trust, rather than an explanation of a fiduciary relationship put together after a dispute or an enforcement action has already started. A family that keeps its trust deed, the share transfer forms or registered conveyance into the trust's name, and the trustees' resolutions accepting the property, in good order from the outset is in a materially stronger position than one that later has to reconstruct how and why an asset ended up in a trustee's name, particularly where the question is raised many years after the transfer, by which time the people who arranged it may no longer be available to explain it.

What a trust does not change: coparcenary rights, maintenance claims and creditor protection limits

A private trust does not override rights that exist independently of it. If a family settles ancestral property that is subject to coparcenary rights under Hindu succession law into a trust, a coparcener whose consent was required and not obtained, or whose rights were not properly accounted for in the settlement, can still challenge the transfer, and the trust structure does not insulate the settlor from that challenge merely because the property has moved into a trustee's name. Similarly, a trust is not an automatic shield against a dependant's maintenance claim or against a genuine creditor's claim where the settlement into the trust was itself designed to defeat an existing, known creditor, since transfers made with intent to defraud creditors can be set aside under general civil law principles regardless of the vehicle used to make them.

Asset protection against a beneficiary's own, later difficulties, a beneficiary who runs into business debts or a matrimonial dispute after the trust was already validly set up for their benefit, is a different and generally stronger case, since by that point the assets belong to the trust and not to the beneficiary personally, and the beneficiary's creditors or spouse ordinarily cannot reach trust assets the beneficiary does not own outright. Families should not, however, assume that settling assets into a trust shortly before or during an existing dispute will achieve the same protection, since the timing and the circumstances of the settlement are exactly what a court will examine if the arrangement is challenged.

A related limit is that a trust cannot be used to defeat a spouse's rights on divorce simply by relabelling marital assets as trust assets shortly before or during matrimonial proceedings, since a family court faced with such a transfer will generally look at the substance and timing of the settlement rather than treat the trust label as conclusive. A trust settled years before any dispute arose, for the benefit of children rather than as a device to shield assets from an estranged spouse, stands on much firmer ground than one set up once a marriage has already begun to break down.

Using a trust to hold family business shares across a generational transition

Family businesses in India commonly reach a point where the founder wants the business to continue under professional or next-generation management while keeping ownership within the family and avoiding a dispute among siblings or cousins over who controls what. Settling the founder's shares in the family's holding company into a private trust, with the founder's children or grandchildren as beneficiaries and a mix of family members and an independent professional as trustees, is one way of separating the question of who manages the business from the question of who, in the end, benefits from it, since the trustees can continue to exercise the voting rights attached to the shares according to the deed's terms even as beneficiaries change through births, marriages or deaths.

A trust holding structure also gives a founder a way to keep a business undivided across a generation that would otherwise be entitled, on the founder's death, to inherit the shares directly and potentially split them among several heirs with different views on how the business should be run. Because the shares sit with the trustees rather than with each heir individually, the trust deed can specify how disputes among beneficiaries over business decisions are to be handled, or simply leave those decisions with the trustees and the company's own board, rather than leaving the business exposed to a direct, immediate division of shares the moment the founder is no longer there to manage the transition.

This use of a trust works only if the company's own articles of association and any existing shareholders' agreement permit the transfer of shares to the trustees in the first place, and if the trust deed itself is drafted with the company's specific governance arrangements, board composition, reserved matters, drag and tag rights, and the like, in mind rather than as a generic succession document. Families attempting this kind of restructuring without coordinating the trust deed with the company's constitutional documents frequently find, only when a dispute arises, that the two sets of documents do not actually work together.

NRI and foreign-resident family members as settlors or beneficiaries

Many of the families considering a private trust in India today have at least one member living abroad, whether as the settlor, a trustee, or a beneficiary, and this changes some of the practical mechanics without changing the basic legal framework. A settlor who is a non-resident Indian or a person of Indian origin can generally still settle Indian assets into an Indian trust, but any transfer of funds into or out of the trust structure that crosses the border has to separately satisfy the Foreign Exchange Management Act and the rules made under it, and the trust's dealings with an NRE, NRO or foreign currency account have to be structured with those rules in mind from the outset rather than treated as a detail to sort out after the deed is signed.

A beneficiary who is non-resident does not generally prevent a trust from working as intended, but distributions to that beneficiary, and any eventual need to repatriate trust assets to a beneficiary living abroad, will go through the same outward remittance checks, including a chartered accountant's certification and the tax position on the distribution, that apply to any other cross-border payment out of India. Families with beneficiaries in more than one country sometimes also need to consider whether the trust or a beneficiary's receipt of trust distributions creates a tax reporting obligation in the beneficiary's own country of residence, since several countries impose their own disclosure requirements on residents who are beneficiaries of, or who receive distributions from, a foreign trust, quite apart from anything Indian law requires. This article does not attempt to answer that question for any particular foreign jurisdiction, and it should be taken up with advisers qualified in the beneficiary's own country of residence before the trust makes a distribution across the border.

An Indian resident settlor planning to move abroad after setting up the trust, or a family anticipating that a trustee currently resident in India may relocate, should also think through how a change in a trustee's tax residence could affect the trust's own residential status for Indian tax purposes, since a trust's place of effective management and the residence of its trustees can both bear on that question. This is a further reason to involve tax advisers at the design stage rather than only at the point a family member's residence actually changes.

Governance in practice: trustees, successor trustees and the protector role

A trust is only as good as the people running it, and the deed's provisions on who the trustees are, how they are to act, jointly or by majority, and how a successor is appointed when a trustee dies, resigns or becomes incapacitated, matter as much as the tax and stamp duty planning that usually gets more attention. Many family trusts name a mix of family members and an independent professional, a chartered accountant or a trust company, as co-trustees, precisely so that the trust's administration does not depend entirely on family members who may themselves be beneficiaries with their own interests in how discretion is exercised.

Some family trust deeds also create a protector, a person or small committee with the power to consent to major trustee decisions, replace a trustee, or otherwise supervise the trustees without taking on trustee duties directly. A protector role is not a creature of the Indian Trusts Act itself and derives entirely from what the deed provides, so its powers and limits have to be drafted with care, since a protector given powers that are too broad can end up functioning as a shadow settlor who never really let go of control, which can undermine the very irrevocability the family was relying on for tax treatment.

Disputes among trustees themselves, rather than between trustees and beneficiaries, are also common enough to plan for, particularly where co-trustees are siblings or cousins with different views on how actively the trust should manage its investments or how generously discretion should be exercised in a discretionary trust. A deed that specifies how a deadlock among trustees is resolved, whether by majority vote, by the protector's casting vote, or by referring the question to an agreed independent person, avoids the trust becoming paralysed at precisely the moment family members most need it to function smoothly, which is usually in the years immediately after the founder who originally held the family together is no longer there to informally settle such disagreements.

A practical sequence for setting up a family trust

Families generally start by deciding what the trust is actually for, continuity of a family business across a generational transition, provision for a minor or a family member with special needs, or simply orderly succession planning that avoids probate, since the answer drives every later choice, including whether the trust should be revocable or irrevocable and specific or discretionary. The deed is then drafted to match that purpose, with particular care on the beneficiaries' shares or the discretionary class, the trustees and succession of trusteeship, and the events, if any, that bring the trust to an end.

Once the deed is settled, it is executed, stamped under the applicable state law, and registered where the property involved requires registration, after which the actual transfer of assets, shares, property, or financial investments, into the trustees' names has to be completed and properly recorded. The trust will also need its own PAN and, where its income crosses the applicable threshold, its own tax filings, kept current every year in the same way an office or a company would be expected to keep its filings current, since a trust with a poor filing history faces the same kind of scrutiny on any later transaction as any other taxpayer with gaps in its record.

Families often underestimate how much ongoing administration a trust actually needs once it is set up, bank and demat accounts in the trustees' names, periodic trustee meetings or resolutions properly minuted, annual accounts prepared and shared with beneficiaries where the deed requires it, and a tax return filed every year regardless of whether the trust made any distribution that year. Treating the trust as a one-time signing exercise rather than an ongoing entity with its own obligations is, in practice, the most common reason a family trust that looked well designed on paper ends up in difficulty a decade or two after it was set up, usually because nobody kept the paperwork current enough to answer a perfectly straightforward question when a bank, a tax officer or a disgruntled beneficiary eventually came and asked it.

What remains unsettled and worth confirming before a family commits

The exact stamp duty payable on a given trust deed and on the transfer of specific assets into it depends on the state and the asset, and families should have this calculated for their own facts rather than rely on a general figure, since state stamp laws are amended frequently and this article does not state a single rate as applicable everywhere. The renumbering of the discretionary trust taxation provision and the clubbing provisions under the Income-tax Act, 2025, from Section 164 and Sections 61 to 63 of the 1961 Act into their new numbered equivalents, should also be checked against the current official text at the time any trust is actually being set up, since this article describes the position as it is understood to stand after the 2025 Act's commencement on 1 April 2026 rather than reproducing the new Act's section numbers with full certainty in every instance. A family weighing whether to set up a trust at all should also keep in mind that no article, this one included, substitutes for advice given on the family's own deed, its own assets and its own beneficiaries, since the difference between a trust that works smoothly for three generations and one that becomes a source of litigation almost always lies in details specific to that family rather than in the general law described here. Building that advice in from the start, rather than treating the trust deed as a document to be finalised quickly and revisited only once a problem has already arisen, is the single habit that distinguishes the family trusts that still work decades later from the ones that end up being argued about in court.

A private family trust is a tool for a family that wants to make its own succession decisions early and have them take effect without waiting for anyone to die, not a device for avoiding tax or defeating a legitimate claim. Getting the structure right, revocable or irrevocable, specific or discretionary, properly funded and properly documented from the day the trust is settled, matters considerably more to how well it works for a family than any single clause in the deed, and is best worked out with advisers who will look at the family's actual assets and goals rather than start from a standard form.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a private family trust better than a will for passing on wealth in India?

Neither is universally better. A trust can start operating during the settlor's lifetime, keeps its terms private, and can keep a family business running through a transition without waiting for probate, which suits families with a business, minor beneficiaries, or members abroad. A will is simpler and cheaper to make but takes effect only on death, generally needs probate, and becomes open to inspection once probated. Many families use both, with a will covering what a trust does not.

Do I need to register a family trust deed in India?

A trust of immovable property must be created by a written, signed instrument under Section 5 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, and that instrument must be registered under the Indian Registration Act, 1908. A trust of only movable property can, in principle, be created without a written instrument, though in practice a written and executed deed is still used for clarity and for opening bank accounts or demat accounts in the trustees' names.

How is a discretionary family trust taxed in India?

The entire income of a discretionary trust, where the trustees have discretion over who among the beneficiaries receives what, is generally taxed at the maximum marginal rate under Section 164 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, carried forward in renumbered form into the Income-tax Act, 2025 from 1 April 2026, subject to narrow statutory exceptions such as certain trusts created under a will for a dependent relative. This is usually higher than the tax a specific trust or direct ownership would attract.

Can a family trust be challenged under the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988?

A properly constituted trust, where a trustee holds property for a beneficiary's benefit, falls within the fiduciary exception in Section 2(9)(A)(ii) of the Act, since a trustee as defined under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 is specifically excluded from the benami definition. The practical safeguard is documentation created at the time the property was transferred into the trust, a registered deed, transfer forms and trustee resolutions, rather than an explanation produced after a dispute arises.

Is a private trust better than a Hindu Undivided Family for holding a family business?

It depends on what the family needs. An HUF arises without a separate deed and follows Hindu succession rules automatically, but its karta carries personal exposure for the family business's debts and some coparceners, such as married daughters, may find their position less straightforward. A trust lets the family set its own terms and separates management from entitlement, but needs a properly drafted and funded deed. Some families run both together, naming the HUF as a trust beneficiary rather than treating the two as rivals.

Can the person who sets up a family trust still control it?

A settlor who keeps the power to revoke the trust and take the assets back has created a revocable trust, and its income is taxed in the settlor's own hands under Sections 61 to 63 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, rather than being taxed as the trust's or the beneficiaries' income. A settlor who wants the trust taxed on its own terms generally has to give up that degree of control and make the trust irrevocable, or irrevocable for the beneficiary's lifetime, within the meaning the Act requires.

Does putting property in a family trust protect it from a family member's creditors?

Generally, once a beneficiary's interest in a trust is validly created, a creditor pursuing that beneficiary personally cannot usually reach assets the trust itself owns, since the beneficiary does not own them outright. This protection does not extend to a settlor who sets up the trust specifically to defeat an existing, known creditor, or to ancestral property a coparcener had rights in before it was settled, both of which a court can look behind regardless of the trust structure.

What happens to a family trust if a trustee dies or cannot continue?

The trust deed should name a mechanism for appointing a successor trustee, and most properly drafted family trusts do so, often naming a co-trustee or an independent professional alongside family members for exactly this reason. Where the deed is silent or the mechanism fails, the Indian Trusts Act and the general law on trusts provide for a court to appoint a new trustee so that the trust does not fail purely because a trustee cannot continue.
