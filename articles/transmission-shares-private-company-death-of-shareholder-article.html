Transmission of Shares on the Death of a Private Company's Shareholder

A shareholder's death does not transfer their shares to anyone automatically; it opens a separate, often slower process called transmission, and families running a closely held company frequently discover its rules, and the board's real powers under the articles, only after a dispute has already started

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 28 November 2026 · Law stated as on 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

When a shareholder of a private limited company dies, the shares do not pass to the heirs the way cash in a bank account might pass under a nomination, and they are not simply reissued in a new name on production of a death certificate. The Companies Act calls this process transmission, deliberately distinct from an ordinary transfer, and the rules governing it sit partly in the statute, partly in the company's own articles of association, and partly in a run of tribunal and court decisions that settle how much say the board actually has over a family's inheritance.

Key points

  • Transmission of shares, under Section 56 of the Companies Act 2013, happens by operation of law on death, succession, insolvency or inheritance, and does not require the instrument of transfer or the stamp duty that an ordinary share transfer needs; the company instead acts on an intimation of transmission.
  • A private company's articles of association, not the Act itself, usually fix what documents the board may insist on before registering transmission: a succession certificate, probate, letters of administration, a legal heir certificate, or an indemnity bond, depending on how the estate passed and what the articles say.
  • A nomination made under Section 72 and Form SH-13 lets a company deal with the nominee without waiting for succession proceedings to conclude, but the Supreme Court held in December 2023, in Shakti Yezdani v Jayanand Jayant Salgaonkar, that a nominee does not thereby become the absolute owner; the nominee holds the shares for the benefit of whoever succession law actually entitles to them.
  • Restrictions on transfer written into the articles, such as a right of first refusal in favour of existing shareholders, generally bind only a voluntary transfer and do not, without more specific wording, block transmission to a deceased shareholder's legal heirs, though the board's power to call for proof of title is not affected by that distinction.
  • If a private company refuses to register a transmission, Section 58 gives the aggrieved person a right of appeal to the National Company Law Tribunal within thirty days of the company's notice of refusal, or within sixty days of lodging the transmission request if the company sends no notice at all.
  • Legal heirs frequently discover that the board's silence, rather than an outright refusal, is the practical obstacle; an application that simply never gets decided can, depending on the facts, be challenged before the Tribunal under Section 59 or, where the delay forms part of a wider pattern, through a minority shareholder remedy under Sections 241 and 242.
  • Shares held by a Hindu Undivided Family, or shares that sit at the centre of a family business succession plan, raise the same transmission questions layered on top of partition and succession-planning issues that a plain shareholding does not carry, so the two should be worked through together rather than treated as separate problems.

Transfer and transmission are not the same event

Most people who deal with company shares for the first time, including a fair number of first-generation entrepreneurs who incorporated their own private limited company without much thought for what happens to it after they are gone, use the words transfer and transmission interchangeably. The Companies Act 2013 does not. A transfer, governed by Section 56(1), is a voluntary act: a shareholder who is alive and of sound mind chooses to move their shares to someone else, executes an instrument of transfer in the prescribed form, pays stamp duty on it, and delivers it to the company along with the share certificate.

Transmission is what happens instead when the shareholder is no longer in a position to choose anything, because they have died, or because some other event outside their control, such as succession, inheritance or insolvency, has by operation of law moved the right to the shares to someone else. Section 56(2) recognises this directly: nothing in sub-section (1), which otherwise bars the company from registering a transfer without a proper instrument, prevents the company from registering a transmission on receipt of an intimation of that transmission by operation of law, without any instrument of transfer at all.

The distinction is not academic. A transfer needs a signed instrument and stamp duty; a transmission needs neither, because there was no bargain between two living parties for an instrument to record. What a transmission does need is proof, to the company's reasonable satisfaction, that the right to the shares has actually passed to the person claiming them, and it is at that point, deciding what counts as sufficient proof, that most of the friction in this area of company law actually arises.

Who can claim transmission, and on what

The most common trigger is the death of an individual shareholder, but it is not the only one. Transmission also covers shares passing on the insolvency of a shareholder to an official assignee or resolution professional, shares passing by inheritance where succession has already been settled, and, less commonly for a private company, shares held by a company that has itself been wound up or merged. This article deals with the death of an individual shareholder, which is by far the most frequent source of disputes between a private company's board and a deceased member's family.

On death, the claim to transmission can come from more than one kind of person, and the company is entitled to know which one it is dealing with. An executor named in a registered will, once probate has been granted where probate is required, stands in the deceased's shoes for the purpose of administering the estate, including the shares. An administrator appointed by a court where there is no will, or where the will names no executor who is willing or able to act, performs the same function under letters of administration. Where no formal grant has been obtained at all, a legal heir claiming directly under intestate succession law, or under a will that has not been probated where probate is not legally required, can still apply for transmission, but is the person most likely to be asked for additional proof.

A joint holding complicates the picture further. Where shares were held jointly by two or more people and one of them dies, the shares ordinarily survive to the remaining joint holder or holders by the law of survivorship applicable to joint holdings, rather than passing to the deceased joint holder's own heirs, unless the company's articles or the original application form recorded a different intention. Families who assumed that a joint holding with a parent meant an automatic half-share passing to their own branch of the family are often surprised to learn that survivorship, not inheritance, governs that specific holding.

What the board is entitled to ask for

The Companies Act 2013 does not itself prescribe a fixed list of documents for transmission, which is why the question of what a private company can insist on is answered mostly by looking at the company's own articles of association, and failing a specific provision there, at a settled line of tribunal authority built up under both the 1956 and 2013 Acts. Where the articles require a succession certificate, probate or letters of administration before transmission is registered, the company is generally entitled to insist on it, and the Company Law Board, in cases such as Hajee Shaik Abdul Kader Labbai Sahib, has recognised that a company's power to call for this kind of proof flows from the articles rather than from a general discretion the board invents for itself.

Many private companies' articles also give the board a dispensing power: authority to accept a lesser form of proof, such as an indemnity bond coupled with a legal heir certificate from the revenue authorities, where the estate is modest or the heirs are undisputed, instead of insisting on a court-issued succession certificate that can take months to obtain. Where the articles grant this discretion, tribunals have treated the board's exercise of it as reviewable for good faith and consistency rather than untouchable, so a board that accepts an indemnity bond from one family and then insists on a succession certificate from another, on materially similar facts, exposes itself to a claim that the refusal was not a genuine exercise of discretion.

What the board cannot generally do, once a succession certificate, probate or letters of administration has actually been produced, is keep inventing fresh conditions. Tribunal decisions under the earlier Act, including the Company Law Board's reasoning in the Hajee Shaik Abdul Kader Labbai Sahib line of cases, treat registration as the company's duty once the document the articles call for has been furnished, and have been unsympathetic to a company that then asks for a revised valuation certificate, additional consent letters from relatives who are not otherwise entitled to object, or an estate duty clearance that the law no longer requires at all. A refusal dressed up as a request for more paperwork is still, in substance, a refusal, and is reviewable as one.

Nomination under Section 72: faster, but not final

Section 72 of the Companies Act 2013 lets any holder of securities nominate, in the manner prescribed by Rule 19 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules 2014, a person to whom the securities shall vest on the holder's death. The nomination is made in Form SH-13, filed with the company, and can be varied or cancelled during the holder's lifetime using Form SH-14. Where shares are held jointly, Rule 19 requires all the joint holders to nominate together, and a nomination made validly in this way is meant to let the company deal with the nominee immediately on death, without waiting for the nominee or anyone else to go through succession proceedings first.

For years, a real dispute ran through Indian company law over what that vesting actually meant: whether a validly nominated person became the absolute owner of the shares, to the exclusion of whoever would otherwise inherit under a will or intestate succession, or merely a conduit through whom the company could safely deal pending the outcome of succession. The Supreme Court resolved this question in Shakti Yezdani and another v Jayanand Jayant Salgaonkar and others, decided on 14 December 2023. The Court held that a nomination under the Companies Act does not confer absolute title on the nominee; it is a mechanism that lets the company recognise someone to receive or deal with the securities without being drawn into a succession dispute itself, and the nominee holds what has vested for the benefit of the persons actually entitled under the deceased's will or under the law of intestate succession, not in their own unqualified right.

The practical effect for a private company's board is that registering a nominee as the holder of transmitted shares resolves the company's own exposure to being sued twice over the same shares, but it does not resolve the underlying family dispute if one exists. A nominee who is not also a legal heir, or who is a legal heir entitled to only part of the estate, can still be called to account by the other heirs in a separate succession proceeding, and a well-advised board, aware of the Salgaonkar position, treats registering a nomination as administratively convenient rather than as a final answer to who actually owns the shares.

Do pre-emption and transfer-restriction clauses apply to transmission

Private company articles routinely contain a right of first refusal in favour of the existing shareholders, requiring any shareholder who wishes to transfer their shares to first offer them to the others at a price fixed by a formula or a valuer. The Supreme Court's decision in V.B. Rangaraj v V.B. Gopalakrishnan, decided in 1991 and reported at (1992) 1 SCC 160, held that the only restriction on the transfer of a company's shares is the restriction actually written into the articles, so a separate private understanding between shareholders that is not reflected there cannot bind the company, however binding it may be as a contract between the shareholders themselves.

Rangaraj was decided in the context of a voluntary transfer, not a transmission, and the distinction between the two concepts matters again here. A right of first refusal clause, on its ordinary wording, is triggered by a shareholder who chooses to transfer; it does not, without express words extending it to death or inheritance, apply to shares that pass by operation of law to a deceased shareholder's heirs, because there is no voluntary transfer for the clause to catch. Well-drafted articles for a family-owned or promoter-controlled private company often address this gap directly, extending the right of first refusal, or a separate buy-out mechanism, specifically to the event of a shareholder's death, precisely because the default position otherwise lets the shares pass straight to the heirs regardless of whether the surviving shareholders would have chosen those particular heirs as business partners.

Where the articles are silent on transmission and only restrict transfer, the heirs are generally entitled to be registered as holders without first offering the shares to anyone, though the board retains its separate and distinct power to call for proof of title before registering them. A family running a closely held business is well served by checking, well before any succession issue actually arises, whether its own articles treat death as triggering the buy-out and pre-emption machinery or leave that gap open, because the answer decides whether an heir who has no interest in or aptitude for the business can simply step into the shareholding, or whether the other shareholders have a mechanism to buy the shares out instead.

Refusal to register: the appeal route under Section 58

Section 58 of the Companies Act 2013 gives a private company the power to refuse registration of a transfer or transmission, but only for reasons the company's articles actually permit, and it builds in a time-bound right of appeal against that refusal. Where a private limited company refuses to register, it must communicate the refusal, together with reasons, to the person who lodged the transmission request, within thirty days of the lodgement. The aggrieved person may then appeal to the National Company Law Tribunal against the refusal within thirty days of receiving that notice, or, if the company never sends a notice of refusal at all, within sixty days from the date the transmission request was delivered to the company.

A public company's shareholders have a longer runway: Section 58(4) gives the Tribunal appeal a sixty-day window from the refusal, or ninety days from delivery of the transmission intimation where no notice of refusal was sent, reflecting the wider shareholder base a public company typically has to notify and the correspondingly longer process that can take. For a private company, the shorter thirty and sixty day windows mean that heirs who simply wait for a response, assuming the company will eventually act, can find the clock has already run before they realise a formal refusal, or a deemed refusal through inaction, has occurred.

Section 59, dealing more generally with rectification of the register of members, does not carry its own fixed limitation period in the way Section 58 does, and has been used by tribunals as a backstop where a company's conduct falls short of an outright refusal but still leaves a legitimate claimant unregistered, for instance where the board simply never places the transmission request before itself for a decision. Both sections route the complaint to the same forum, the National Company Law Tribunal, by way of petition rather than a civil suit, which is generally faster than ordinary civil litigation but still requires the petitioner to prove the underlying entitlement to the shares, not merely the fact of the company's inaction.

When silence, not refusal, is the real problem

In practice, a private company controlled by one branch of a family rarely sends a crisp, reasoned letter of refusal, because doing so starts the appeal clock running against itself. The more common pattern is delay: the transmission request sits with the company secretary, is said to be under the board's consideration, and is never formally decided one way or the other, often for years, while the shares in question continue to be voted, or kept off the register entirely, by whichever faction currently controls the board.

This kind of indefinite delay is harder to litigate than an outright refusal, precisely because there is no single refusal letter to date the appeal clock from, but it is not left unremedied. Where the delay is part of a broader pattern of a controlling group excluding a deceased shareholder's heirs from information, dividends or decision-making that the shareholding would otherwise entitle them to, the facts can support a petition alleging oppression or mismanagement under Sections 241 and 242 of the Companies Act 2013, a remedy this firm has examined in detail elsewhere on this site in the context of minority shareholder disputes generally. The Supreme Court's decision in World Wide Agencies (P) Ltd v Margaret T Desor, reported at (1990) 1 SCC 536, recognised that the legal representatives of a deceased member, even before registration as members themselves, are not without standing to bring such a petition where the company's conduct toward them warrants it.

Heirs facing this kind of stalling are generally better served by formally lodging the transmission request in writing, with the supporting documents the articles require, and keeping a clear paper trail of the date of lodgement and every subsequent communication, than by relying on informal assurances from a company secretary or a director who happens to be a relative. A documented lodgement date is what fixes the sixty-day fallback period under Section 58 if the company never sends a notice of refusal, and it is also the evidence a tribunal will look for first if the dispute eventually escalates into an oppression petition.

Shares held through a Hindu Undivided Family or as part of a family business

Where the shares in question are recorded in the company's register in the name of the karta of a Hindu Undivided Family rather than in an individual's personal name, transmission on the karta's death raises a question the Companies Act does not directly answer: the shares themselves do not automatically pass to the next karta, because a company's register of members recognises only the individual whose name appears on it, and HUF property is not a legal entity capable of being a member in its own right under company law in the way it is treated as an assessee under tax law. In practice, the shares continue to be held in the deceased karta's name until the family either effects a formal partition, addressed in detail elsewhere on this site, or agrees among itself, and communicates to the company, who the new karta or the registered holder on behalf of the family should be.

A second, separate layer of questions arises where the shares sit at the centre of a family business, because succession through transmission then interacts with issues that a plain shareholding in a listed company would never raise: whether the deceased's role as a director, rather than merely as a shareholder, also needs to be filled, whether a shareholders' agreement or a family constitution contains its own succession or buy-out mechanism running in parallel with the articles, and whether the business's trademarks, goodwill and key contracts, often not held by the company at all but personally by the founder, need their own separate transmission exercise. This site's earlier articles on partitioning a Hindu Undivided Family's property and on what happens to a family business's trademarks and patents when the founder dies work through those adjacent questions, and a family dealing with a shareholder's death is generally better served reading the shareholding issue together with those, rather than treating the share certificate as the only asset that needs attention.

Family arbitration clauses, increasingly common in shareholders' agreements for Indian family businesses as a way of keeping succession disputes out of open litigation, do not by themselves solve the transmission question either, because the company's statutory duty to register or refuse transmission under Sections 56 and 58 is owed to the person entitled under succession law, and an arbitration clause between the shareholders cannot by itself override that duty or deprive the Tribunal of the jurisdiction Section 58 gives it, though it can and often does resolve the underlying dispute over who is entitled in the first place before the company is ever asked to act.

Tax treatment of shares passing by transmission

The transmission of shares to a legal heir, executor or administrator is not itself a taxable event for the recipient. Property received by inheritance or under a will is excluded from the gift provisions of the Income-tax Act, a point this site's article on the tax treatment of gifts addresses in more detail, and the same exclusion applies to shares passing on death, whether the deceased held them personally, jointly, or as karta of a Hindu Undivided Family.

What transmission does not do is reset the tax clock. Under Section 49(1) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, an heir who later sells the inherited shares is treated, for capital gains purposes, as having acquired them on the date and at the cost the deceased originally paid, with the holding period tacked on from that original date rather than restarting from the date of death. For unlisted shares in a private company, this carried-forward cost is frequently far below current value, particularly where the company has grown substantially since the shares were first allotted or acquired, which means a sale by the heir years after transmission can trigger a capital gains liability calculated over a holding period and cost base the heir may never have seen any documentation for at the time of transmission itself.

Families going through transmission are, for this reason, better served preserving the deceased's original share application, allotment or purchase records, rather than treating the share certificate and the transmission paperwork as the only documents worth keeping. A transmission file that records the date and cost of the deceased's original acquisition, alongside the succession certificate or probate used to register the transmission itself, saves the next generation a considerably harder reconstruction exercise if and when those shares are eventually sold or the company is valued for any other purpose, including a future family settlement.

A practical sequence for the heirs and for the board

For the heirs, the sequence that tends to avoid unnecessary delay starts with checking the company's articles of association before approaching the company at all, specifically the provisions on transmission, any dispensing power given to the board, and any pre-emption or buy-out clause that might apply on death. The next step is assembling whichever of the succession documents the articles call for, which may be a succession certificate obtained from the civil court under the Indian Succession Act, probate or letters of administration where a will is involved, or, where the articles' dispensing power allows it and the estate is straightforward, a legal heir certificate from the revenue authorities together with an indemnity bond and a no-objection letter from the other heirs.

Lodging the request formally, in writing, addressed to the company and acknowledged with a date, matters more than most families realise until a dispute makes the date relevant, because it is that date which starts both the company's own thirty-day window to respond under Section 58 and the heir's fallback sixty-day window to approach the Tribunal if the company never responds at all. Where the board's response raises conditions the articles do not actually support, pressing for the specific provision in the articles that is said to justify the condition, rather than simply supplying whatever additional document is demanded, is usually the more effective response, because it forces the board to identify a textual basis for the refusal that a tribunal can then test.

For the board, the safer course is almost always the mirror image: identify the article that governs transmission before responding at all, apply it consistently across different families and different holdings rather than case by case, and put any refusal in writing with specific reasons well within the thirty-day window, because a reasoned refusal that turns out to be wrong is far easier for the company to defend, and far less likely to escalate into an oppression petition, than silence that leaves a family guessing for months about whether its claim has even been considered.

Listed companies and demat holdings: a different, SEBI-regulated track

Everything described so far concerns shares of a private, unlisted company, where the company itself maintains the register of members under Section 88 of the Companies Act and decides, subject to its own articles and to Sections 56 to 59, whether to register a transmission. Shares of a listed company held in dematerialised form sit in a materially different system: the depository participant and the registrar and share transfer agent, not the company's own board, process the transmission request, under documentation standards set by the Securities and Exchange Board of India rather than by the company's articles.

SEBI has moved repeatedly over the past several years to simplify that documentation, including a 2026 framework that eased some of the earlier requirements for straightforward cases, among other things reducing reliance on a mandatory probate in certain circumstances and standardising the affidavit and no-objection formats claimants use. The exact monetary thresholds that separate a simplified, affidavit-based claim from one still requiring a succession certificate, probate or letters of administration have been revised more than once in recent years and depend on the value and nature of the specific holding, so an NRI or HNI family with both listed and unlisted holdings should check the registrar or depository's current requirements for the listed portion separately, rather than assuming the private-company rules described in this article, or the reverse, apply across both.

The constant across both tracks is the underlying principle this article has traced throughout: transmission depends on operation of law, not on an instrument of transfer, and the paperwork a claimant is asked for exists to let the company, the depository or the registrar satisfy itself that the person in front of it is actually the person succession law has made entitled, not to create an independent hurdle of the institution's own choosing.

A shareholder's death puts a private company's board in a position the Companies Act gives it real, but not unlimited, power to manage. The board can insist on the proof of title its own articles call for, and can refuse registration where that proof is missing, but it cannot invent fresh conditions once genuine proof has been furnished, cannot treat a nomination under Section 72 as settling a succession dispute the Supreme Court has said it does not settle, and cannot sit on a transmission request indefinitely without exposing itself to an appeal under Section 58 or, in a sufficiently serious case, a petition over oppression of the shareholders being kept off the register.

For the family on the other side of that board table, the practical lesson is to treat the articles of association, not general assumptions about how inheritance works, as the first document to read. A family business with shares that matter, whether in value or in control, is well served reviewing its own articles for how they treat transmission before any death actually occurs, because the gaps in that drafting, a missing buy-out clause on death, no dispensing power for the board, no clarity on what happens to shares recorded in an HUF's name, are precisely the gaps that turn an inheritance into years of avoidable dispute.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a private company need a succession certificate before registering transmission of shares?

It depends on the company's own articles of association. Where the articles require a succession certificate, probate or letters of administration, the board is generally entitled to insist on one before registering the transmission. Where the articles give the board a dispensing power, it may accept a lesser form of proof, such as a legal heir certificate with an indemnity bond, for straightforward cases, but it has to exercise that discretion consistently rather than requiring different proof from different families on similar facts.

Can a company refuse to register transmission of shares to a deceased shareholder's legal heirs?

Yes, but only for reasons its articles actually support, and the refusal is appealable. Under Section 58 of the Companies Act 2013, a private company must communicate its refusal with reasons, and the aggrieved heir can appeal to the National Company Law Tribunal within thirty days of receiving that notice, or within sixty days of lodging the request if the company sends no notice of refusal at all.

Does a Section 72 nomination mean the nominee becomes the absolute owner of the shares?

No. The Supreme Court held in Shakti Yezdani v Jayanand Jayant Salgaonkar, decided on 14 December 2023, that a nomination under the Companies Act lets the company deal with the nominee without being drawn into a succession dispute, but does not give the nominee absolute ownership. The nominee holds what has vested for the benefit of whoever is actually entitled under the deceased's will or under intestate succession law.

Do a company's pre-emption or right of first refusal clauses apply when shares pass on death?

Generally not, unless the articles say so expressly. A right of first refusal is ordinarily triggered by a voluntary transfer, and transmission on death happens by operation of law rather than by the shareholder's own choice, so the same clause does not automatically catch it. Well-drafted articles for a family or promoter-controlled company often extend a buy-out or pre-emption mechanism specifically to death, precisely to close this gap.

How long does transmission of shares in a private company usually take?

The Companies Act does not fix a single timeline for the board to decide a transmission request, though a private company must give reasons for any refusal within thirty days of the request being lodged. In practice, the time taken depends heavily on how quickly the claimant can obtain whatever succession document the articles require, which, for a succession certificate or probate obtained through the civil court, can itself take several months to over a year depending on the forum and whether the claim is contested.

What happens if a private company simply never responds to a transmission request?

Silence, as distinct from a reasoned refusal, still starts a fallback limitation period: the claimant can appeal to the National Company Law Tribunal under Section 58 within sixty days of lodging the request if no notice of refusal is ever sent. Where the inaction forms part of a broader pattern of excluding the heirs from information or decision-making the shareholding would otherwise carry, the facts can also support a petition alleging oppression or mismanagement under Sections 241 and 242.

Is tax payable when shares are transmitted to a legal heir after a shareholder's death?

No tax is payable on the transmission itself, because property received by inheritance or under a will is excluded from the gift provisions of the Income-tax Act. The heir does, however, inherit the deceased's original cost of acquisition and holding period under Section 49(1), so capital gains tax calculated on that carried-forward basis becomes payable only later, when the heir actually sells the shares.

Do shares recorded in the name of a Hindu Undivided Family's karta pass automatically to the next karta on death?

Not automatically in the company's own records. A company's register of members recognises only the named individual, so the shares continue to stand in the deceased karta's name until the family either effects a formal partition or agrees among itself, and communicates to the company, who should now be registered as the holder on the family's behalf.
