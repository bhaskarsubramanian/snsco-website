Succession Planning for Live-In and Unmarried Partners in India

Why Indian law still will not treat a live-in partner as a spouse or an heir, and what a Will, and only a Will, can actually do about it

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 28 November 2026 · Law stated as on 28 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Two people who have lived together for fifteen years, raised a child together and built a home together can still find, the day after one of them dies, that the survivor is a legal stranger to the estate. Indian succession law has not caught up with how many couples actually live, and the gap is one that only a properly drafted Will can close.

Key points

  • A live-in partner is not a spouse or an heir under the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, the Indian Succession Act, 1925, or any other personal law, so intestate succession gives the surviving partner nothing by default.
  • The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 recognises a 'relationship in the nature of marriage' and gives a woman in such a relationship rights to residence and protection, but this is not an inheritance right and does not survive the partner's death in the way a spouse's statutory share does.
  • D. Velusamy v. D. Patchaiammal (2010) 10 SCC 469 set out the test for a relationship in the nature of marriage, and Indra Sarma v. V.K.V. Sarma (2013) 15 SCC 755 confirmed that not every live-in relationship qualifies.
  • Children born of a long, stable live-in relationship benefit from a rebuttable presumption of marriage for legitimacy, and the Supreme Court's 13 June 2022 ruling in Kattukandi Edathil Krishnan v. Kattukandi Edathil Valsan, building on Tulsa v. Durghatiya (2008) 4 SCC 520, granted such a child a share even in ancestral family property, reversing the older position in Bharatha Matha v. R. Vijaya Renganathan (2010) that had confined such children to self-acquired property alone.
  • A testator's freedom to bequeath self-acquired property to a live-in partner by Will is not in doubt as a matter of law; the practical risk is a post-death challenge by the family on grounds such as undue influence or lack of testamentary capacity.
  • A nominee on a bank account, insurance policy or provident fund is a trustee for the legal heirs, not an owner, so naming a live-in partner as nominee is not a substitute for naming that partner as a beneficiary under a Will.
  • The protection available differs by personal law: a Muslim testator's bequest to a partner outside the heirs is capped at one-third of the estate absent the heirs' consent, while a Will made under the Indian Succession Act by a Christian, Parsi or interfaith couple faces no such ceiling.

The question that arrives after the relationship, not during it

Couples who choose to live together without marrying rarely ask a lawyer what happens to their property if one of them dies. The question tends to arrive from the survivor, after the fact, when a family that never fully accepted the relationship is already moving to take possession of a flat, a joint account or a business the couple built together. By then the options have narrowed considerably.

This is not a fringe scenario confined to any one city or community. Long-term live-in relationships are common across Delhi, Noida and Kolkata among working professionals who have not married for reasons ranging from a pending divorce from an earlier marriage, to religious or caste differences within their own families, to a simple decision that marriage is not for them. The law that governs what happens to their property on death has not moved nearly as far as social practice has.

The stakes rise sharply where the couple is also financially successful, running a business together, holding a jointly used flat worth a significant sum, or maintaining investments in both names informally without the paperwork to match. A family that disapproved of the relationship while the deceased was alive often has every incentive to assert the full force of the law once the deceased is gone, and a survivor without a Will to point to is left arguing equity against a family holding the statute.

Why this is squarely an estate-planning problem, not a litigation problem to solve later

Couples sometimes assume that if a dispute ever arises, a court will eventually do what feels fair and recognise the surviving partner's contribution to the relationship and the property. That assumption is risky for two reasons. First, courts apply the statute as it is written, not as a judge might personally think it should read, and the statute, as shown below, simply does not list a live-in partner as an heir. Second, litigation to establish an equitable claim, through a resulting trust, an estoppel argument or a claim for the value of contributions made, is slow, expensive and uncertain even where the underlying facts are genuinely sympathetic.

Planning in advance, through a Will and consistent documentation of financial contributions, converts an uncertain, years-long dispute into a straightforward administration of an estate according to a document the deceased signed while alive and of sound mind. The cost of doing this properly while both partners are alive is a small fraction of the cost, in money and in years, of litigating the same question after one of them has died.

Why Indian succession law still does not see a live-in partner as a spouse or heir

Every major personal succession law in India, the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 for Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists and Jains, the Indian Succession Act, 1925 for Christians, Parsis and persons married under the Special Marriage Act, and Muslim personal law for Muslims, defines the surviving spouse's share by reference to a valid marriage. None of them recognise a live-in partner as a spouse, and none of them list a live-in partner among the class of heirs entitled to inherit when a person dies intestate.

This means that if a partner dies without a Will, the entire estate devolves on that person's legally recognised heirs, parents, children, siblings or more distant relatives depending on the personal law and the class structure it uses, and the surviving partner receives nothing as of right. A partner who contributed financially to a jointly used flat, paid half the home loan instalments, or ran a business alongside the deceased has no statutory inheritance claim arising from the relationship itself, however long it lasted.

Unmarried cohabitation has not been criminalised in India for decades and the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that two consenting adults are free to live together without marrying, but the Court's protection of that choice as a matter of personal liberty has not been matched by any legislative extension of inheritance rights to the couple that makes that choice. The gap is deliberate legislative inaction rather than an oversight nobody has noticed; several law commission and academic proposals to codify live-in property rights have not been enacted.

D. Velusamy and the test for a relationship in the nature of marriage

The leading authority on when a live-in relationship attracts any statutory recognition at all is D. Velusamy v. D. Patchaiammal (2010) 10 SCC 469. The Supreme Court was asked to decide a maintenance claim and used the occasion to lay down criteria for what counts as a relationship in the nature of marriage under Section 2(f) of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005: the couple must hold themselves out to society as spouses, must be of legal age to marry, must be otherwise qualified to enter a legal marriage including being unmarried, and must have voluntarily cohabited for a significant period.

The Court was equally clear about what does not qualify. A relationship where a man keeps a woman he financially maintains mainly for companionship or domestic service, without any element of a marriage-like partnership, does not meet the test, and the Court said as much in terms that have been quoted in nearly every later case on the subject. A casual arrangement, a weekend relationship, or a short cohabitation before either party has tested whether the relationship will last is unlikely to qualify either.

Indra Sarma v. V.K.V. Sarma (2013) 15 SCC 755 applied the same test and held that a relationship is not automatically one in the nature of marriage merely because the parties lived together for a period, particularly where one partner was already married to someone else at the time. The Court's own framing was that all live-in relationships are not relationships in the nature of marriage, so each case still turns on its specific facts rather than on length of cohabitation alone.

What the Domestic Violence Act gives a partner, and what it does not

Where a relationship does meet the D. Velusamy test, the surviving or aggrieved partner gains real protections during the relationship: the right to reside in the shared household regardless of ownership, protection orders against violence or harassment, and maintenance for as long as the relationship subsists or under its terms of breakdown. These are meaningful rights and should not be understated for a partner facing an abusive or controlling relationship.

None of this, however, is an inheritance right. The Domestic Violence Act protects a partner's position while the relationship exists and while the respondent partner is alive; it was never drafted to allocate a deceased partner's estate, and no court has read it that way. Once the partner who owned the property or held the assets has died, the surviving partner's claim to inhabit the home or to receive support from that estate has to be found somewhere else, most realistically in a Will the deceased left behind.

A partner relying solely on the Domestic Violence Act for long-term security, without any estate planning alongside it, is relying on a statute built for a different problem. It is worth treating the two needs, protection during the relationship and provision after death, as entirely separate projects that require separate documents.

Maintenance under Section 144 BNSS and why it rarely reaches a live-in partner

Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which replaced Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, allows a wife, among others, to claim maintenance from a husband able to support her but neglecting to do so. D. Velusamy itself arose from an attempted maintenance claim under the earlier Section 125, and the Supreme Court's answer was that a woman in a relationship that did not meet the relationship-in-the-nature-of-marriage test could not claim maintenance under that provision as though she were a wife.

Even where the relationship does qualify, a maintenance order of this kind runs against the living partner's income and ability to pay. It is not a claim against an estate, and it does not survive the paying partner's death in the way a decree for permanent alimony attached to specific assets might in some other contexts. A surviving partner cannot convert an earlier maintenance order into a claim on the deceased partner's property or bank balance after death; that door, once the relationship-holder has died, is closed by this route and has to be approached, again, through testamentary planning instead.

Children of the relationship: the presumption of marriage and its limits

The law has moved further and more consistently to protect children born of a live-in relationship than it has to protect the adult partners themselves. Courts have long applied a presumption under Section 114 of the Evidence Act, 1872 that where a man and woman have cohabited for a long, continuous period and held themselves out as husband and wife, a marriage between them is presumed, a line of authority going back to Badri Prasad v. Deputy Director of Consolidation (1978) and restated in Madan Mohan Singh v. Rajni Kant (2010) 9 SCC 209, which held that a long-running live-in relationship cannot be dismissed as a walk-in, walk-out arrangement.

Tulsa v. Durghatiya (2008) 4 SCC 520 applied this presumption specifically to protect the legitimacy and inheritance claims of children born of such a relationship, holding that prolonged cohabitation as husband and wife raises a presumption in favour of the children's legitimacy, rebuttable but not easily displaced. The Supreme Court revisited the point in June 2022 in a ruling that children of a long-cohabiting couple can inherit in the parents' property, reversing a Kerala High Court decision that had gone the other way.

The older view, from Bharatha Matha v. R. Vijaya Renganathan (2010), had confined such a child to the parents' self-acquired property and denied any share in ancestral coparcenary property. That limit no longer reflects the law as it stands: the Kattukandi Edathil Krishnan ruling itself concerned ancestral partition property and reversed a Kerala High Court decision that had denied the child a share in it, and the Supreme Court's 2023 ruling in Revanasiddappa v. Mallikarjun expressly rejected the narrower Bharatha Matha line for children legitimated under Section 16 of the Hindu Marriage Act. A family with meaningful ancestral property and a child born outside a formally solemnised marriage should still take specific advice on how the presumption of marriage, and its effect on coparcenary status, plays out on its own facts.

Why a Will is the only real route to providing for a partner

Every gap described above points to the same conclusion. Intestate succession law excludes the partner entirely. The Domestic Violence Act protects the partner only while the relationship is alive. Maintenance law reaches a living respondent's income, not a deceased one's estate. The one instrument available to a person who wants their live-in partner to actually receive something after death is a Will disposing of self-acquired property in the partner's favour.

This is not a novel or uncertain proposition. Indian law has long recognised that a person is free to bequeath self-acquired property to anyone, including a complete stranger to the family, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly treated testamentary freedom over one's own property as an incident of the right to property that courts will not lightly interfere with. A live-in partner named as a beneficiary is in exactly the same legal position as any other named beneficiary under a validly executed Will.

What a Will cannot do is reach ancestral or coparcenary property the testator does not own outright, since a coparcener's interest in joint family property is constrained by the character of that property itself, and a testator cannot will away a share that has not been carved out by partition. For most urban professionals whose principal asset is a self-purchased flat, a salary-funded investment portfolio and bank accounts, this limitation rarely bites, but it is worth checking specifically where a family business or ancestral land is involved.

Testamentary freedom over self-acquired property, and the coparcenary wall

A Will in favour of a live-in partner should be drafted with the same rigour as any Will likely to face a contest from a disappointed family, because the family almost always has standing to object and frequently does. The drafting should identify the specific self-acquired assets being bequeathed, confirm in the document itself that those assets are self-acquired and not ancestral, and record, briefly and factually, the nature and duration of the relationship so that a probate court later assessing the Will's genuineness has context rather than a bare, unexplained bequest to a person outside the family.

Testamentary capacity, free will and the absence of undue influence are the grounds a family will most often raise to challenge such a Will, and courts have treated a bequest to someone outside the ordinary circle of heirs as a circumstance that invites closer scrutiny of these grounds, not as a ground for invalidity by itself. A Will executed before independent witnesses who are not themselves beneficiaries, ideally including the testator's own treating doctor's note on capacity where age or health is a live issue, and registered where the law of the state in question makes registration advisable, gives the Will a materially stronger footing against this kind of challenge.

A second, often overlooked protection is for the partner to keep clear, dated records of financial contributions to jointly used property during the relationship itself, since these can support an independent claim in the nature of a resulting trust or unjust enrichment over specific assets even if the Will itself is ever successfully challenged. This is a fallback, not a substitute for proper testamentary planning, but it has mattered in reported disputes where a Will's validity was genuinely in doubt.

Nomination is not inheritance, and why that gap catches people

Couples who have thought about what happens on death often believe they have solved the problem by naming each other as nominees on a bank account, a life insurance policy, a mutual fund folio or a provident fund account. This feels like protection and in the short term it functions as one, because the nominee is typically the first person the institution will pay out to or hand custody of the asset to on production of a death certificate.

The legal position is narrower than that convenience suggests. A nominee holds the asset, in most settings, as a trustee for the legal heirs entitled under succession law, not as its owner in their own right, a principle the courts have applied consistently to bank deposits and company shares alike. A live-in partner who receives insurance proceeds or a provident fund payout as nominee can still be called upon to account for and hand over that money to the deceased's legal heirs if those heirs assert their statutory entitlement, unless the Will itself, or the specific statute governing that asset class, gives the nominee an independent beneficial right.

The safer sequence is to treat nomination as an administrative convenience that speeds up who first receives an asset, and to treat the Will as the document that actually decides who is entitled to keep it. A couple that has updated their nominations but never executed a Will has done only half the job, and the half they have done is the less legally secure one.

What this means differently for a Hindu, a Muslim and a Christian couple

The route through a Will works differently depending on which personal law governs the testator, and a cross-community live-in relationship, increasingly common in Delhi and Kolkata, needs this checked carefully rather than assumed. A Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist or Jain testator making a Will under the Indian Succession Act, 1925, which governs testamentary succession generally regardless of the testator's personal law for succession purposes, faces no statutory ceiling on how much self-acquired property can be left to a partner outside the family.

A Muslim testator does face a ceiling. Under Muslim personal law, a bequest, or wasiyat, to a person who is not already an heir is generally limited to one-third of the net estate after debts and funeral expenses, unless the heirs consent to a larger bequest after the testator's death, a rule with authority stretching back through classical Islamic jurisprudence and still applied by Indian courts today. A Muslim testator who wants to leave more than one-third of the estate to a live-in partner needs either the heirs' documented consent or a lifetime gift structured outside the framework of a testamentary bequest altogether, and that structuring needs careful, community-specific advice rather than a generic Will template.

A Christian, Parsi or interfaith couple, or a couple married to other people under personal laws that do not recognise their current relationship, generally falls back on the Indian Succession Act, 1925 for both intestate and testamentary purposes, which again imposes no ceiling comparable to the Muslim one-third rule, though it carries its own formal requirements for execution and attestation that a Will drafted casually can easily fail to satisfy.

A family settlement after the fact: what a live-in partner can and cannot be given

Where a partner dies without having made a Will, all is not necessarily lost if the deceased's actual legal heirs are willing to recognise the surviving partner's moral claim. The heirs can choose, entirely voluntarily, to transfer part of the estate to the surviving partner through a gift deed, a release deed in the partner's favour, or a family settlement that includes the partner even though no law compels the heirs to include them.

This route depends entirely on the heirs' goodwill, which is precisely the goodwill that is often in shortest supply where the family disapproved of the relationship to begin with. It also attracts stamp duty and registration requirements like any other transfer of immovable property, and unlike inheritance under a Will probated in the ordinary way, it can be structured in a manner that invites gift tax scrutiny under Section 56(2)(x) of the Income-tax Act if the recipient is not a relative as that provision defines the term, since a live-in partner does not fall within the closed list of relatives exempted from that provision.

None of this should be read as a reason to skip a Will on the theory that the family might come around later. It is, at best, a safety net for a family that is already cooperative, and families that are already cooperative rarely need the safety net in the first place.

Where the partner's financial contribution to a specific asset, such as a flat held in the deceased's sole name but part-funded by the survivor, can be clearly documented through bank transfers, loan repayment records or a contemporaneous written acknowledgement, an independent claim for that specific contribution can sometimes be pursued even against unwilling heirs, on principles distinct from succession law altogether. This kind of claim is fact-heavy, depends entirely on the quality of the documentation kept during the relationship, and should be treated as a last resort rather than planned around from the outset.

A practical checklist for an unmarried couple doing estate planning in India

Each partner should execute an individual Will disposing of their own self-acquired assets, naming the other partner as a specific beneficiary for specific assets rather than relying on vague or aspirational language, and naming an executor who is likely to actually see the Will through rather than defer to family pressure. Where either partner's family is likely to contest the Will, registration and careful attestation are worth the modest extra cost and delay at the time of execution.

Separately review every nomination on bank accounts, insurance policies, demat holdings and provident fund accounts to confirm they are consistent with, and clearly subordinate to, what the Will actually says, so that a nominee who is also a Will beneficiary is not left arguing about which document controls. Where a jointly used flat is held in one partner's sole name, consider whether a joint purchase, a documented loan, or an express declaration of beneficial interest better reflects the couple's actual contributions, since this can matter enormously if the Will itself is ever challenged.

Finally, keep the conversation current. A Will executed early in a relationship should be revisited after any material change, the birth of a child, the purchase of a new property, a parent's death that changes the family's own composition, because a stale Will that no longer reflects the couple's actual life is almost as vulnerable to challenge as having no Will at all.

Couples who have been married before, to someone else, and are now in a live-in relationship pending or after a divorce should take particular care, since a Will executed while a prior marriage's maintenance or property proceedings are still pending can itself become a point of dispute in those proceedings. Coordinating the timing of a new Will with the status of any ongoing matrimonial litigation, rather than executing it in isolation, avoids handing an estranged former spouse an additional argument to make.

India's succession statutes were written around the assumption that people inherit through marriage and blood, and that assumption has not changed even as the way many couples actually live has moved well past it. A live-in partner gains real protection while the relationship is alive, through the Domestic Violence Act and maintenance law, but almost none of that protection survives the partner's death.

A properly drafted, carefully witnessed Will remains the one instrument that can close that gap for a partner's self-acquired assets, and it is not a complicated document to get right with proper advice. The couples who come to regret not having one are, almost without exception, the couples who assumed they had more time than they did.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a live-in partner automatically inherit property if the other partner dies without a Will?

No. Under the Hindu Succession Act, the Indian Succession Act and Muslim personal law alike, a live-in partner is not recognised as a spouse or heir, so intestate succession passes the entire estate to the deceased's legally recognised heirs, and the surviving partner receives nothing as of right.

Can a live-in partner claim maintenance like a spouse?

Only if the relationship meets the Supreme Court's test in D. Velusamy v. D. Patchaiammal (2010) 10 SCC 469 for a relationship in the nature of marriage, and even then, maintenance runs against the living partner's income under the Domestic Violence Act or Section 144 BNSS. It is not a claim against the deceased's estate and does not function as an inheritance right after death.

Can I leave my property to my live-in partner in my Will?

Yes, for self-acquired property. Indian law recognises broad testamentary freedom to bequeath self-acquired assets to anyone, including a live-in partner. The Will should be carefully drafted and witnessed, since a bequest outside the ordinary family circle is more likely to face a challenge from disappointed relatives.

Can children born of a live-in relationship inherit property?

Courts have held that children of a long, stable live-in relationship benefit from a presumption of marriage, following Tulsa v. Durghatiya (2008) 4 SCC 520. The Supreme Court's 2022 ruling in Kattukandi Edathil Krishnan v. Kattukandi Edathil Valsan and its 2023 ruling in Revanasiddappa v. Mallikarjun have since extended this to a share in ancestral or coparcenary property too, moving past the older view that confined such children to self-acquired property alone. The position remains fact-specific, and families with ancestral property should take specific advice.

Is naming my partner as a bank or insurance nominee enough to protect them?

No. A nominee generally holds the asset as a trustee for the deceased's legal heirs rather than as its owner, so a live-in partner named only as nominee, without being named as a beneficiary in a Will, can still be required to hand the asset over to the legal heirs.

Is there a limit on how much a Muslim testator can leave to a live-in partner by Will?

Yes. Under Muslim personal law, a bequest to someone who is not already an heir is generally capped at one-third of the net estate unless the heirs consent after the testator's death to a larger share, so a Muslim testator wanting to leave more needs either that consent or a separate lifetime transfer structured with specific advice.

If my family is willing to give my live-in partner a share after I die without a Will, is that enough?

It can work, through a gift, release deed or family settlement, but it depends entirely on the heirs' voluntary cooperation, which is often exactly what is missing where a family disapproved of the relationship. It also carries its own stamp duty, registration and potential gift tax consequences, and should not be relied upon as a substitute for a Will.

Does the Domestic Violence Act give a live-in partner a right to inherit the home after the other partner dies?

No. The Domestic Violence Act protects a partner's right to reside in the shared household and claim maintenance while the relationship and the respondent partner are alive. It does not allocate the deceased's estate after death, and any post-death claim to the home has to rest on a Will or on an independently provable financial contribution to the property.
