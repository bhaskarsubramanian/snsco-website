Using a Power of Attorney From Abroad for Indian Property and Court Matters: The Complete Process

How an NRI executes, authenticates, stamps and registers a power of attorney abroad, and what it can and cannot do once it reaches a sale deed, a mortgage or a courtroom in India.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 30 October 2026 · Law stated as on 29 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An NRI who cannot be present in India for every signature usually ends up relying on a power of attorney, and the document fails more often from a defect in execution, stamping or scope than from anything a court later decides on the merits. This piece sets out the full chain, from the desk abroad to the sub-registrar's office in India, and the specific limits the Supreme Court has placed on what such a document can do.

Key points

  • A power of attorney is only a grant of agency; the Supreme Court in Suraj Lamp held it cannot by itself transfer title to immovable property.
  • Execution abroad requires either notarisation followed by apostille (Hague Convention countries) or attestation by an Indian consular or diplomatic officer (non-Convention countries); the two tracks are not interchangeable.
  • A power of attorney executed outside India must be stamped within three months of its first receipt in India under Section 18 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899, and the duty payable depends on the State where it will be used, not where it was signed.
  • Registration under Section 32 and 33 of the Registration Act, 1908 governs who may present a document for registration on the principal's behalf, and is distinct from, and in addition to, stamping.
  • A power of attorney holder's evidence is restricted: under Janki Vashdeo Bhojwani v IndusInd Bank, the holder can depose only to what they personally did under the power, not to facts within the principal's own knowledge.
  • Agency created by a power of attorney ordinarily ends on the principal's death under Section 201 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, subject to the narrow exception in Section 202 for an agency coupled with interest.
  • Using a power of attorney to effect what is in substance a sale, without a registered conveyance, does not pass title; it only attracts the limited protection of Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, if even that applies.
  • State practice varies sharply on stamp duty, on whether a power of attorney for property must itself be registered, and on how long a sub-registrar will accept a foreign-executed document after it reaches India; always check the destination State's schedule before travelling or executing abroad.

What a power of attorney actually is, and is not

A power of attorney is an instrument by which one person, the principal or donor, authorises another, the agent or attorney, to act on the principal's behalf. In Indian law it is governed principally by Chapter X of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (the law of agency), by the Powers of Attorney Act, 1882, and, where immovable property or registration is involved, by the Registration Act, 1908 and the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 or the corresponding State stamp legislation. None of these statutes treats a power of attorney as a conveyance. It creates a relationship of agency; it does not by itself move any right, title or interest in property from one person to another.

This distinction became the subject of a deliberate and often-cited correction by the Supreme Court in Suraj Lamp and Industries Pvt. Ltd. v State of Haryana, (2012) 1 SCC 656 (judgment delivered 11 October 2011, R.V. Raveendran, A.K. Patnaik and H.L. Gokhale JJ). Real estate markets in several cities had developed a practice of transferring land through a General Power of Attorney coupled with an unregistered agreement to sell and a will, usually to avoid stamp duty and registration charges on a formal sale deed. The Court held that this 'GPA sale' or 'SA/GPA/Will' route does not convey title and does not create any interest in immovable property enforceable against the whole world. A power of attorney, the Court said, is the creation of an agency, and nothing more; it empowers the agent to act for the principal, including to execute a registered sale deed if the power says so, but the power itself is not a deed of title. The only protection such an arrangement can claim is the limited, defensive protection against the principal's own dispossession claim under Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, available to a transferee in possession under a contract for sale who has performed their part, and that protection does not create ownership.

A later three-judge bench reaffirmed this and rejected the argument that Suraj Lamp should apply only prospectively, holding that no title passes under an unregistered agreement to sell or an unregistered general power of attorney, whenever executed. The practical message for an NRI principal is simple: a power of attorney is the vehicle by which your attorney executes the registered documents that actually transfer or encumber property on your behalf (the sale deed, the mortgage deed, the lease deed), not a substitute for those documents.

It follows that the first drafting question is never 'what can I achieve with a power of attorney' but 'what registered instrument will actually achieve what I want, and does my power of attorney give my attorney the authority to execute that instrument in my name'. A power that recites authority to 'manage and deal with' a property is commonly read narrowly by registering authorities and by courts; a power intended to support a sale or mortgage should say so expressly, identify the property, and, where possible, be read alongside the sale agreement or facility documents it is meant to implement.

General power and special power: choosing the right instrument

Indian practice and the Powers of Attorney Act, 1882 recognise no rigid statutory distinction between a 'general' and a 'special' power of attorney, but the distinction is functionally important and is drawn consistently by registering authorities, banks and courts. A general power of attorney (GPA) authorises the agent to act across a range of matters, often broadly described, such as managing all the principal's property, operating bank accounts, representing the principal before government authorities, and executing documents generally. A special power of attorney (SPA) is confined to one transaction or a defined set of acts, for example to execute a specific sale deed for a named property, to represent the principal in a named court proceeding, or to receive possession of a specific flat from a builder.

For NRIs, the SPA is usually the safer instrument where the underlying act is a transfer of property or a significant financial transaction, for three practical reasons. First, a narrowly drawn SPA limits the agent's room to exceed authority and reduces the principal's exposure if the agent acts in bad faith; the dishonest exercise of a GPA by a relative or associate, including sale of property to third parties without the principal's knowledge, is one of the most frequently litigated NRI property disputes. Second, sub-registrars and banks are more willing to act on an SPA that squarely covers the transaction in front of them, since it removes any argument about whether a general authorisation extends that far. Third, because stamp duty in several States (discussed below) is charged at conveyance rates specifically where the power authorises sale or transfer of immovable property for consideration, a carefully scoped SPA can sometimes avoid uncertainty about which schedule entry applies, though it will not reduce duty below what the transaction itself would attract.

Where a GPA is unavoidable, for instance because the principal needs the agent to handle an open-ended set of affairs over a period of absence from India, the safer course is to execute it with as much specificity as the circumstances allow, to register it where that is practicable, to keep its term short and renewable, and to retain the power to revoke it on notice. A GPA that authorises the agent to sell any and all immovable property of the principal, without naming the property or the purpose, is both a conveyancing risk for the person dealing with the agent and a fraud risk for the principal.

Execution abroad: the notary-and-apostille track

Where the power of attorney is signed outside India, Section 85 read with the general scheme of the Registration Act and settled practice before Indian sub-registrars require that the execution be authenticated before it can be acted upon in India, because the registering officer or the bank or the court cannot otherwise verify the signature of a person who signed outside the officer's jurisdiction. Two distinct authentication routes exist, and the correct one depends entirely on whether the country where the NRI is executing the document is a party to the Hague Convention of 5 October 1961 Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents (the Apostille Convention).

India has been a party to the Apostille Convention since 2005, and the Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) is India's designated competent authority for issuing apostilles on Indian public documents and, by reciprocal application, for recognising apostilles issued by other member countries without further legalisation. Where the NRI is resident in, or executing the document in, a Convention country (the list runs to well over a hundred countries, including common NRI destinations such as the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Singapore and, since December 2022, Saudi Arabia), the practical sequence is: the principal signs the power of attorney before a local notary public, who notarises the signature; the notarised document is then submitted to the designated apostille authority of that country (in the United States, typically the Secretary of State of the State where the notary is commissioned); the apostille is affixed as a certificate, and no further attestation by an Indian embassy or consulate is required before the document is used in India. The United Arab Emirates is commonly, and wrongly, assumed to be a Convention member; as of the date of this article it is not on the Hague Conference's contracting-party list, so documents executed in the UAE still require consular attestation, not an apostille. The current, authoritative list of contracting States should always be checked against the Hague Conference (HCCH) status table or the MEA's apostille page before relying on any secondary summary, since the list is added to from time to time.

Execution abroad: the consular attestation track

Where the country of execution is not a party to the Apostille Convention, the applicable route is attestation by an Indian diplomatic or consular officer at the Indian embassy, high commission or consulate having jurisdiction over that place. This route is governed by the Diplomatic and Consular Officers (Oaths and Fees) Act, 1948, which empowers Indian diplomatic and consular officers abroad to administer oaths, take affidavits and perform notarial acts, including attesting the execution of a power of attorney, that would otherwise require a notary public in India. Section 3(2) of that Act gives the resulting document a specific evidentiary benefit: once it bears the seal and signature of the diplomatic or consular officer, it is admissible in evidence without further proof of that seal or signature, or of the officer's official character.

In practice, the principal attends the Indian mission in person (most missions do not permit attestation by post or without the executant's physical presence), signs the power of attorney in the presence of the consular officer, who verifies identity (typically against the Indian passport or OCI card) and either attests the signature or, in some missions, certifies the document itself. Some missions, depending on local regulation, instead require or permit local notarisation followed by attestation by the mission (sometimes called 'legalisation' or 'consularisation'), particularly in jurisdictions such as parts of Germany where the consulate's practice is only to verify the signature rather than certify the content. Applicants should check the specific requirements published by the relevant Indian mission, since procedure, fees (set under the Diplomatic and Consular Officers (Fees) Rules, 1949, as amended) and required supporting documents (passport copy, OCI or visa status, proof of the property or purpose) differ mission to mission.

A power of attorney attested by a consular officer, or apostilled under the Convention track, is not yet usable in India for a property transaction. It must still go through stamping and, where the governing provision requires it, registration or at least presentation under Sections 32 and 33 of the Registration Act, both discussed below. The attestation or apostille answers only the question of whether the signature is authentic; it does not answer the separate questions of what stamp duty is payable and where, or whether the document itself needs registration.

Stamping: the three-month rule and why the clock matters

Section 18 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 deals specifically with instruments, including powers of attorney, executed outside India. It requires that such an instrument, if it relates to property situate in India or to a matter or thing done or to be done in India, and is not already duly stamped, be stamped within three months after it has first been received in India. The three-month period runs not from the date of execution abroad but from the date of first receipt in India, which matters because a document can sit unopened or in transit for weeks without the clock starting.

Two consequences follow from missing the window, and they are not the same consequence. First, under Section 18 read with the related provisions on impounding, an instrument produced in evidence or before a registering or other public officer after the three-month period, without having been stamped, can be impounded, and duty together with a penalty becomes payable before it can be acted upon; the three-month bar operates as a safe-harbour for timely stamping, not as an absolute cut-off after which the document becomes incapable of being stamped at all. The Supreme Court has held, in the context of Section 18 read with Section 32, that once a document is impounded the three-month limit in Section 18 is not itself a bar to collecting duty and penalty; stamping after the window remains possible but becomes more expensive and exposes the document, until stamped, to being treated as inadmissible in evidence. The Andhra Pradesh High Court has likewise held that a sub-registrar faced with a foreign-executed GPA presented more than three months after receipt cannot simply refuse it outright; the correct course is to impound it and refer it for adjudication of duty and penalty, not to reject the document.

Second, and separately from any penalty, an instrument that is not duly stamped at all cannot ordinarily be acted upon, produced in evidence, or registered, until the deficiency is cured. For an NRI, the practical lesson is to treat the date the courier or the principal's representative actually receives the document in India, not the date of signing abroad, as the operative date, to retain evidence of that receipt date (a courier tracking record or a dated acknowledgement is useful precisely because disputes over when the three months started are common), and to present the document for stamping promptly rather than waiting until it is needed for a specific transaction.

The duty itself, and the authority before whom the document must be presented for adjudication, is governed by the Stamp Act or the State stamp legislation of the State where the power of attorney is to be used, not the State, if any, connected with its execution. This produces real variation. Under the Maharashtra Stamp Act, a power of attorney authorising the sale or transfer of immovable property for consideration, or without consideration to someone other than a close relative, or in favour of a promoter or developer, is charged at the same ad valorem rate as a conveyance on the market value of the property; a power given without consideration to specified close relatives attracts a flat concessional duty. Other States set flat fees for general powers unconnected with a transfer of property, which can be a few hundred rupees, while a property-transfer power can run into the same bracket as a sale deed. Because the figures and the schedule article numbers change with amendments, and because practice offices (such as banks that supply standard power of attorney formats) sometimes quote outdated figures, the correct course is always to check the current stamp schedule of the destination State, or to have the document adjudicated by the Collector of Stamps for that State, before relying on a quoted rate.

Registration: Sections 32, 33, 18 and 19 of the Registration Act

Registration under the Registration Act, 1908 raises a question distinct from stamping, and the two are frequently and wrongly conflated. Section 17 of that Act lists the documents that must compulsorily be registered, principally instruments that themselves create, declare, assign, limit or extinguish an interest in immovable property of value above a threshold, and a decree or order affecting such property. A power of attorney that does no more than authorise an agent to act is not, by itself, within Section 17's compulsory-registration list in most circumstances, because it does not itself transfer an interest. Where the power of attorney is coupled with an irrevocable authority tied to a transfer, or where a State amendment (as in Maharashtra, under the Registration (Maharashtra Amendment) Act, 2010, which inserted a specific requirement for certain irrevocable powers of attorney relating to transfer of property) brings it within the compulsory category, registration of the power itself becomes mandatory and non-registration can affect its validity.

Separately, Sections 32 and 33 of the Registration Act govern who may present a document, such as a sale deed the attorney is executing on the principal's behalf, for registration. Section 32 requires that every document be presented for registration either by a person executing or claiming under it, or by the representative or assign of such a person, or by the agent of such a person, provided the agent is duly authorised by a power of attorney executed and authenticated in the manner Section 33 prescribes. Section 33 then specifies which powers of attorney qualify for this purpose: if the principal resides in a part of India where the Act is in force, the power must be executed before and authenticated by the Registrar or Sub-Registrar within whose district or sub-district the principal resides; if the principal resides outside India, the power must be executed before and authenticated by a Notary Public, any court, judge, magistrate, an Indian consul or vice-consul, or a representative of the Central Government, which is precisely the consular-attestation or notary-and-apostille route described above.

The Supreme Court has clarified the limited scope of Section 33 in Rajni Tandon v Dulal Ranjan Ghosh Dastidar, holding that the Section 33 authentication requirement is aimed at the situation where a third-party agent, someone other than the executant of the underlying document, presents that document for registration; it does not control the separate case where the very person who signed the document as attorney for the principal (having already executed it under the authority of the power) then presents it themselves. Getting the distinction right matters in practice: an NRI's power of attorney that will be used only so that the attorney can sign a sale deed and then lodge it for registration personally sits differently, for Section 33 purposes, from a power used so that a separate agent can walk a document the NRI signed abroad into the registration office. Pending litigation, including a reference before the Kerala High Court, continues to test the boundary between Section 17 compulsory registration and Sections 32-33 presentation authority for powers of attorney, which is one of the points on which State High Courts have not spoken with one voice, so a document's precise purpose should be matched carefully to the correct provision before it is relied upon.

Using the power of attorney for a sale

Once validly executed, authenticated, stamped and, where required, registered or presentable under Sections 32 and 33, a power of attorney lets the attorney execute a registered sale deed in the principal's name, provided the power expressly or clearly by implication authorises sale of the specific property. The registering sub-registrar will examine the power to confirm it covers the transaction, check the identity of the attorney, and satisfy themselves that the power has not been revoked (discussed below) and that the principal, where alive, has not died, since death automatically terminates most agency. In practice, registering offices increasingly cross-check the power against any available online registration records and ask for the principal's identity documents even though the principal is not personally present, given the history of fraudulent GPA sales that prompted Suraj Lamp in the first place.

A buyer's advocate conducting due diligence on a sale by an NRI's attorney should, at minimum, verify: that the power of attorney is the original or a certified copy, not merely a photocopy claimed to be true; that it is validly stamped for the State of the property and, if the three-month window under Section 18 was missed, that duty and penalty have in fact been paid on adjudication; that it has not been revoked, by checking for any registered revocation deed and, where feasible, by direct confirmation with the principal; that the principal is alive and of sound mind as of the date of execution of the sale deed; and that the power's recitals actually extend to sale, and not merely to management, collection of rent, or representation before authorities. A sale executed by an attorney whose power does not extend to sale, or whose power has lapsed through revocation or the principal's death, conveys nothing, however properly it is otherwise stamped and registered, because the underlying authority is itself absent.

Tax consequences also travel with the principal, not the attorney. Capital gains on the sale accrue to the NRI principal, and TDS under Section 195 of the Income-tax Act (and, with effect from 1 April 2026, the corresponding provisions of the Income-tax Act, 2025, which restates and renumbers much of the 1961 Act's machinery; the TDS obligation on payments to non-residents continues under the new Act's scheme, and buyers should confirm the then-current section number against the Central Board of Direct Taxes' notifications rather than relying on either the old or the new number in isolation) applies to the buyer regardless of whether payment or possession is handled through the attorney. The attorney signing the deed does not change who is taxed.

Using the power of attorney for a mortgage

Lenders financing a purchase, or taking security over an NRI's existing property for a facility extended to the NRI or to a related borrower, will accept a mortgage executed by an attorney only where the power expressly authorises creating a mortgage or charge over the specific property, and even then most banks, as a matter of internal policy rather than strict legal requirement, prefer the mortgage deed and the underlying facility documents to be executed by the principal personally wherever practicable, reserving the power-of-attorney route for cases where personal execution genuinely cannot be arranged. Disputes before Debt Recovery Tribunals have turned on exactly this point: where a borrower's defence is that the attorney (often a family member) was authorised only to stand as guarantor, or to manage the property, and not to encumber it by mortgage, the tribunal must construe the power's actual wording, and a mortgage created beyond the power's scope does not bind the principal.

Equitable mortgage by deposit of title deeds, long used because it avoided registration, has become less reliable where the title deeds are deposited by an attorney rather than the owner personally, and recent High Court decisions have required banks to show that the underlying mortgage creation complies with registration requirements where the facts (such as a contemporaneous letter confirming the mortgage) indicate the transaction is, in substance, one requiring a registered instrument. Where a mortgage is to be enforced later under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 (SARFAESI), a defect in how the mortgage was created, including an attorney acting outside the scope of the power, is one of the few grounds that can genuinely derail a Section 13(2) or Section 14 proceeding, since SARFAESI's summary machinery presumes a validly created security interest. NRIs granting a mortgage-enabling power of attorney should therefore insist on language that specifically names the lender, the facility, and the property, rather than a generic authority to 'deal with' the property, both to protect themselves against misuse and to give the lender the certainty it needs to rely on the document.

Using the power of attorney in litigation: Order III CPC and its limits

Order III of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 governs appearance by agents and pleaders. Rule 1 permits a party to appear in court, apply to court, or act in court either in person, through a 'recognised agent', or through a pleader (advocate) appearing on the party's behalf, except where the court directs personal appearance. Rule 2 defines 'recognised agents' to include, among others, persons holding a power of attorney authorising them to make and do such appearances, applications and acts on behalf of the party. For an NRI unable to be physically present for every procedural step of an Indian suit, a litigation-specific power of attorney, naming the proceeding or at least the subject matter, allows a trusted attorney to file applications, receive notices, and generally act in the litigation.

The authority Order III grants is narrower than it first appears, and the gap trips up unwary litigants. Courts have consistently held that a recognised agent under Order III, Rules 1 and 2 may appear, apply and act, but has no right to plead, meaning no right to address arguments to the court on the merits or to examine and cross-examine witnesses; that right belongs to a pleader under Order III, Rule 4, which means a licensed advocate. A power of attorney holder is therefore not a substitute for engaging counsel, however broad the power's wording; at most it lets the holder instruct counsel, sign pleadings where the power so authorises, and handle procedural steps that do not involve addressing the court.

A second and sharper limit concerns giving evidence. The Supreme Court in Janki Vashdeo Bhojwani v IndusInd Bank Ltd., (2005) 2 SCC 217, held that where a power of attorney holder is examined as a witness, the holder's evidence is confined to acts done by the holder personally under the power; the holder cannot depose to facts that lie within the principal's personal knowledge, and particularly cannot depose, in place of the principal, to matters on which the principal alone is competent to be cross-examined. The Supreme Court in Man Kaur v Hartar Singh Sangha, 2010 INSC 672, approved and applied this restriction. The practical consequence for NRI litigants is significant: sending an attorney to testify about the NRI's own knowledge, intentions or dealings, rather than appearing personally or giving evidence through a properly commissioned route, is likely to be treated as no evidence at all on those points, and can weaken rather than help the NRI's case if the principal's personal account was genuinely needed.

Where the principal's own evidence is unavoidable but personal appearance before the trial court is not practicable, the more reliable route is a commission for recording evidence under Order XXVI of the CPC, increasingly executed by video conference. Indian High Courts, following the Supreme Court's recognition in State of Maharashtra v Dr Praful B Desai that video-conferencing evidence satisfies the requirement that evidence be recorded in the presence of the court, have appointed commissioners to record the evidence of witnesses, including NRI parties, located abroad, subject to the party arranging suitable equipment and satisfying the court about the identity of the person testifying and the integrity of the link. This is a genuinely different tool from a power of attorney: it lets the principal testify personally, remotely, rather than delegating testimony to an agent who cannot speak to the principal's own knowledge.

Revocation and the death of the principal

A power of attorney, being a species of agency, is in principle revocable by the principal at any time, by Section 203 of the Indian Contract Act, subject to the agent's right to compensation for premature revocation of an agreed engagement under Section 205, and subject to the narrow exception carved out by Section 202 for an agency coupled with the agent's own interest in the subject matter, which the principal cannot revoke to the agent's prejudice in that interest. In practice, revocation of a power concerning immovable property should be executed as a formal deed of revocation, ideally registered, and, critically, actual notice of revocation should be given to the former agent and to any third party, such as a bank or a prospective buyer, who might otherwise deal with the former agent in good faith. Section 208 of the Contract Act protects a third party who deals with the agent without notice of the termination; an NRI principal who revokes a power but fails to notify the bank or the sub-registrar's records risks a dealing by the former attorney still binding the principal under that protection.

Separately, and this is frequently overlooked, the agency created by a power of attorney ordinarily terminates automatically on the death of the principal, under Section 201 of the Contract Act, regardless of whether the power describes itself as irrevocable, unless the narrow Section 202 exception for an agency coupled with interest genuinely applies and survives the death (a question the courts have not treated as settled in every fact pattern). The Madras High Court, considering a power executed by multiple principals, has held that the death of one joint principal does not automatically terminate the whole instrument where it was not coupled with interest, leaving the outcome dependent on the facts of each case. The practical rule for an NRI family is that once the principal dies, any further exercise of the power by the erstwhile attorney, whether to sell, mortgage or otherwise deal with the deceased's property, is void as against the estate, and the estate's title passes instead through succession, by will or intestacy, which is the subject of a separate process entirely (probate, letters of administration or a succession certificate, discussed in our earlier article on NRI succession).

Banks, buyers and sub-registrars dealing with an attorney therefore have a genuine practical problem: there is no central public register that reliably confirms, in real time, that a principal is alive and that a given power has not been revoked. Prudent practice is to require a recent, dated life-and-non-revocation confirmation from the principal (sometimes through the Indian mission abroad) before relying on an older power of attorney for a significant transaction, and for principals to keep the duration of any outstanding power as short as the purpose allows.

A practical step-by-step sequence

For a sale, mortgage or litigation-specific power of attorney to be executed abroad for use in India, the order of steps that avoids the most common pitfalls runs as follows. First, decide the precise act the attorney needs to perform, and draft the power to cover exactly that act, naming the property, the transaction, or the proceeding, rather than drafting broadly for convenience. Second, identify whether the country of execution is a Hague Apostille Convention member; if yes, have the principal sign before a local notary and then obtain an apostille from the competent authority of that country; if no, attend the Indian embassy, high commission or consulate with jurisdiction and have the signature attested or the document notarially certified by the consular officer, checking that mission's specific published requirements first. Third, dispatch the authenticated document to India promptly, and record the date it is actually received in India, since that date starts the Section 18 Stamp Act clock. Fourth, within three months of that receipt, present the document for stamping, or for adjudication of duty if there is any doubt about the applicable schedule entry, before the Collector of Stamps or the relevant authority for the State where the power will be used; if the three months are missed, present it for impounding and pay the assessed duty and penalty rather than attempting to use an unstamped document. Fifth, where the power is to be used so that the attorney can execute a registrable document (a sale deed, for example), confirm whether the power itself needs registration under Section 17 as locally amended, and, separately, confirm that it satisfies Sections 32 and 33 for the attorney to present documents for registration. Sixth, retain certified copies, keep the principal reachable to confirm authenticity if the registering authority or a counterparty seeks confirmation, and formally revoke and give notice of revocation the moment the power is no longer needed.

For a litigation power, the sequence is similar through authentication, but stamping requirements for a purely litigation-related power (not touching property) are typically lighter, often a flat, modest duty rather than an ad valorem conveyance rate; the power should be filed with the relevant court at the first appearance, together with proof of its authentication, and the engaged advocate should confirm whether the specific court or tribunal (civil court, consumer forum, RERA authority, or a tribunal with its own procedural rules) has any additional format requirement for powers executed abroad.

What remains unsettled

Several points genuinely vary by State or remain contested in the reported decisions, and should not be treated as settled nationally. Whether a power of attorney relating to immovable property is itself compulsorily registrable, as opposed to merely needing Section 33 authentication for presentation purposes, depends on State amendments to Section 17 (Maharashtra's 2010 amendment being the clearest example) and is the subject of pending litigation in at least one High Court on the relationship between Sections 17, 32 and 33. Stamp duty rates and the schedule entries that apply to a power of attorney for sale, as opposed to a general power, differ across States, change periodically, and are not always consistently described in secondary sources, so the current State notification should be checked in every transaction rather than relied upon from an earlier matter. The exact survival of an agency coupled with interest beyond the principal's death is not fully settled across all fact patterns. And the renumbering of the Income-tax Act provisions relevant to a sale by an NRI (TDS, residency tests) under the Income-tax Act, 2025, effective 1 April 2026, means that practitioners should confirm the then-current section numbers against CBDT notifications for any transaction closing on or after that date, rather than assuming either the old or the new numbering applies uniformly through the transition.

A power of attorney executed abroad is not a single document with a single rule; it is the product of several independent legal requirements, authentication, stamping and, where relevant, registration, each governed by a different statute and each capable of defeating the transaction if it is missed. The Suraj Lamp line of authority also means that even a flawless power of attorney only ever authorises someone else to sign the document that actually transfers or encumbers property; it is never itself that document.

The practical discipline that avoids most disputes is narrow drafting matched to the actual act required, careful attention to the three-month stamping clock from the date of receipt in India rather than the date of signing, and prompt, recorded revocation and notice whenever a power is no longer needed. Where litigation or testimony is involved, a power of attorney gets an NRI's procedural steps done, but it does not let an attorney give evidence the principal alone can give.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a power of attorney by itself transfer ownership of my property in India to someone else?

No. The Supreme Court in Suraj Lamp and Industries v State of Haryana held that a power of attorney only creates an agency; it does not convey title to immovable property. Title passes only through a registered conveyance, such as a sale deed, that the attorney may be authorised to execute on your behalf, or through succession on death. A so-called GPA sale, without a registered deed, does not make the recipient the owner.

I live in the United States. Do I need to go to the Indian consulate, or is a notary enough?

Because the United States is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, you can have the power of attorney notarised locally and then apostilled by the Secretary of State (or equivalent authority) of the State where the notary is commissioned. You do not need to attend the Indian consulate for this route. If you are in a country that is not a Convention member, such as the UAE, you must instead have the document attested at the Indian embassy or consulate.

From what date does the three-month stamping period under Section 18 of the Stamp Act run?

From the date the power of attorney is first received in India, not the date it was signed abroad. Keep dated proof of receipt, such as a courier tracking record, since disputes over whether the three months were missed turn on this date. If the window is missed, the document can still be stamped after being impounded and after duty and penalty are assessed; it is not permanently unusable, but it becomes more expensive and cannot be relied upon until cured.

Can my attorney give evidence in my place if I cannot attend an Indian court hearing?

Only to a limited extent. Under Janki Vashdeo Bhojwani v IndusInd Bank, an attorney can testify about what they personally did under the power, but not about facts within your own personal knowledge, and particularly not on matters where you alone can be cross-examined. If your own testimony is genuinely needed, applying for a commission to record your evidence by video conference under Order XXVI CPC is usually the more reliable route.

Does my power of attorney still work if I die while it is in force?

No. Under Section 201 of the Indian Contract Act, agency, including a power of attorney, ordinarily ends automatically on the principal's death, regardless of whether the document calls itself irrevocable, unless the narrow exception for an agency coupled with the agent's own interest applies and is found to survive on the facts. After death, dealing with the deceased's property requires succession, not the power of attorney.

Can my attorney use the power of attorney to mortgage my property to a bank?

Only if the power expressly, or clearly by necessary implication, authorises creating a mortgage or charge over that specific property. Banks and tribunals examine the actual wording; a power to 'manage' or to act as guarantor has been held not to extend to mortgaging the property. Draft the power to name the lender, the facility and the property if a mortgage is the intended use.

Can a power of attorney holder argue my case in court?

No. Under Order III of the CPC, a recognised agent holding a power of attorney can appear, apply and act procedurally on your behalf, but has no right to plead, meaning no right to argue the case or examine witnesses; that is reserved for a pleader, which means an advocate on record. You still need to engage counsel.

Does my power of attorney need to be registered, or is stamping enough?

Stamping and registration are separate requirements. Most general powers of attorney do not themselves require compulsory registration under Section 17 of the Registration Act, though some States, Maharashtra among them, have amended Section 17 to require registration of certain irrevocable powers tied to property transfer. Separately, Sections 32 and 33 govern whether your attorney's authentication is sufficient for presenting other documents, like a sale deed, for registration. Check both questions for your specific State and transaction.
