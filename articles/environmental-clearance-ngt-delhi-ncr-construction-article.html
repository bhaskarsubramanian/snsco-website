Environmental Clearance and the NGT: What a Delhi NCR Builder or Developer Needs to Get Right

When a construction or township project in Delhi, Noida or Greater Noida needs environmental clearance, how a challenge moves through the National Green Tribunal, and how the GRAP construction ban actually works through the year

By Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 24 November 2026 · Law stated as on 23 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A developer assembling a residential project in Noida, a township proposal in Greater Noida, or a commercial tower in Delhi routinely treats environmental clearance as one line item among many approvals to chase, alongside building plan sanction, fire clearance and the usual municipal sign-offs. That treatment understates what the clearance actually is: a separate legal gate under its own notification, enforceable by its own tribunal, with its own appeal timelines, and, in Delhi NCR specifically, overlaid every winter by a construction ban that has nothing to do with the project's own compliance record and everything to do with the region's air quality on any given day.

Key points

  • Under the Environment Impact Assessment Notification, 2006, a building and construction project with built-up area above 20,000 square metres needs environmental clearance as a Category B project, assessed by the State Level Environment Impact Assessment Authority rather than the central Ministry.
  • A township or area development project crosses into environmental clearance territory where it covers more than 50 hectares of land or has more than 1,50,000 square metres of built-up area, whichever threshold is reached first.
  • An appeal against a grant or refusal of environmental clearance goes to the National Green Tribunal under Section 16 of the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, within thirty days of the order, extendable by the Tribunal for a further period of up to sixty days on sufficient cause, for a combined outer limit most benches treat as a hard ninety days.
  • The Supreme Court held in Madhya Pradesh High Court Advocates Bar Association v. Union of India, 2022 SCC OnLine SC 639, that the National Green Tribunal Act does not oust a High Court's writ jurisdiction under Articles 226 and 227, and separately upheld the constitutionality of a direct appeal from the Tribunal to the Supreme Court under Section 22.
  • The Commission for Air Quality Management imposes a construction and demolition ban across the whole of the National Capital Region, including Noida, Greater Noida, Ghaziabad and Faridabad, once air quality crosses the thresholds for Stage III and Stage IV of the Graded Response Action Plan, regardless of any individual project's own environmental compliance record.
  • A developer should map a project's layout against the applicable thresholds parcel by parcel rather than treat the whole site as a single category, since even one structure crossing the built-up area or land-area threshold can bring the entire project within the clearance requirement.
  • A buyer, lender or investor acquiring an interest in a Delhi NCR project that has already been built should verify that environmental clearance was actually obtained before construction began, since a structure built without the required clearance remains exposed to enforcement action regardless of how long it has stood or how many times it has since changed hands.

Why environmental clearance is a separate gate, not a formality folded into building approval

The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 empowers the Central Government to require prior clearance for specified categories of project before construction can lawfully begin, and the Environment Impact Assessment Notification, 2006, issued under that Act, is the instrument that actually lists which categories of project need that clearance and sets out the procedure for obtaining it. For a Delhi NCR developer, the practical consequence is that environmental clearance operates on top of, not instead of, every other approval a construction project needs; a project can have a sanctioned building plan from the Noida Authority or the Municipal Corporation of Delhi, a fire safety certificate, and every other local approval in place, and still be unlawful to construct if the environmental clearance threshold applies and the clearance itself was never obtained.

This matters because environmental clearance is enforced through a different mechanism than a building-plan violation. Where unauthorised construction without the required building sanction typically triggers action from the local municipal or development authority, construction without a required environmental clearance exposes the developer to action under the Environment (Protection) Act itself, and to challenge before the National Green Tribunal, a specialised body with its own procedure and its own, often more technically exacting, standard of scrutiny than a municipal enforcement wing applies to an ordinary building-plan violation.

The distinction also matters for who can bring a challenge and when. A building-plan violation is typically pursued by the municipal or development authority itself, or by a neighbour with a direct property interest, through the ordinary channels of municipal enforcement or a civil suit. An environmental clearance violation can be brought before the National Green Tribunal by a considerably wider range of parties, residents of the surrounding area, environmental organisations, or any person aggrieved within the Tribunal's broad original jurisdiction over substantial questions relating to the environment, which means a project's exposure on the environmental side is not limited to the specific neighbours or authorities who might contest a building-plan irregularity, and a developer should expect that a wider circle of potential challengers exists for this category of non-compliance than for an ordinary municipal violation.

The thresholds that actually trigger clearance for a construction or township project

The Schedule to the EIA Notification, 2006 places building and construction projects, and separately, townships and area development projects, within specific entries that fix when clearance becomes mandatory. A building and construction project crosses into clearance territory once its built-up area exceeds 20,000 square metres, and projects within a defined upper band below 1,50,000 square metres of built-up area are treated as Category B projects, meaning the clearance is granted at the state level rather than by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change directly. A township or area development project is treated differently and more broadly: it needs clearance where it covers more than 50 hectares of land, or has more than 1,50,000 square metres of built-up area, whichever condition is actually met first, which means a lower-density township spread across a large land parcel can trigger the requirement purely on area even where its built-up area alone would not have.

Category B projects are further divided into B1 and B2, with B1 projects generally required to go through a full Environmental Impact Assessment report and, for most categories, a public hearing, while B2 projects proceed without that full assessment. Building and construction projects specifically are treated as an exception within this B1/B2 framework: they are not required to go through public consultation even where otherwise categorised as B1, reflecting a 2014 amendment notification that eased the process for this specific category of project, though a developer should confirm the current, consolidated text of the notification for the project's specific category rather than assume a particular exemption still applies unchanged, since the notification has been amended repeatedly since 2006.

A Category B project, whether a building and construction project or a township, is examined by the State Level Expert Appraisal Committee and approved by the State Level Environment Impact Assessment Authority, rather than going through the Ministry's own central-level committee, which is the process a developer in Delhi NCR will ordinarily encounter, since very few residential or commercial projects in the region reach the scale that would place them in Category A, which is reserved for larger projects and for proposals within specific ecologically sensitive areas.

Mapping a project's actual footprint before assuming it falls outside the clearance requirement

A common and costly mistake in project planning is treating a large development as a collection of separately built structures, each individually below the 20,000 square metre built-up area threshold, and concluding on that basis that no structure within the project needs clearance. The Ministry's guidance and the Tribunal's own practice have not generally accepted this kind of segmentation where the structures form part of a single, integrated development, a residential complex with multiple towers developed under one layout plan and one set of approvals, for instance, is assessed on its aggregate built-up area rather than tower by tower, and a developer who structures phased construction specifically to keep each phase below the threshold risks the entire project being treated as a single proposal requiring clearance regardless of how the phases are formally divided on paper.

For a mixed-use project combining residential towers, commercial space and a township-style layout, the safer approach is to calculate the aggregate built-up area and land area for the development as a whole at the planning stage, well before applying for building plan sanction, and to treat the environmental clearance application as a parallel track running alongside, not after, the building-plan approval process. A developer who waits until building plan sanction is secured before even checking whether environmental clearance applies risks discovering the requirement only once construction financing and marketing have already begun, at which point the delay in obtaining clearance becomes considerably more expensive than if it had been planned for from the outset.

How the clearance application actually moves through the state authority

An application for environmental clearance is filed through the Ministry's PARIVESH online portal, and for a Category B project in Delhi NCR this means the application is routed to the Delhi, Uttar Pradesh or Haryana State Level Environment Impact Assessment Authority depending on where the project sits, since Noida and Greater Noida fall within Uttar Pradesh's authority while a Delhi project falls within the separate authority constituted for the National Capital Territory. The application is first examined by the State Level Expert Appraisal Committee, which can raise queries, ask for additional survey data, traffic and drainage studies, or a revised layout, before the application is placed before the full State Level Environment Impact Assessment Authority for a final decision.

A practical point developers in the region have run into is that a state authority can itself become non-functional for a period between reconstitution cycles, when the term of the existing members expires before a fresh authority is notified, leaving pending applications without a forum to decide them until the new authority is constituted. Where this has happened in other states, the national-level authority has in at least one instance directed the state's own environment department to continue processing applications in the interim as custodian of the governing environmental plans for the area, which means a developer facing an unexplained, extended delay should specifically check whether the relevant state authority is currently constituted and sitting before assuming the delay reflects something about the application's own merits.

Challenging a clearance, or a refusal, before the National Green Tribunal

A person aggrieved by the grant of an environmental clearance, by a condition attached to it, or by its refusal, can appeal to the National Green Tribunal under Section 16 of the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010. The limitation period is thirty days from the date the order is communicated, and the proviso to Section 16 lets the Tribunal condone a further delay of up to sixty days where the appellant shows sufficient cause for missing the original thirty days, for a combined outer limit of ninety days that benches have generally treated as a hard cap rather than a guideline, declining to go beyond it even where a creditable explanation is offered for the additional delay. The Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in Talli Gram Panchayat v. Union of India addressed when this limitation clock actually starts, holding that the period runs from the earliest date on which any of the relevant duty bearers actually communicates the order, rather than from some later or more convenient date a party might prefer to treat as the starting point.

Separately from this appellate jurisdiction, Section 14 of the National Green Tribunal Act gives the Tribunal original jurisdiction over civil cases involving a substantial question relating to the environment, including enforcement of a legal right relating to the environment, which is a broader jurisdiction than the Section 16 appeal route and is the basis on which third parties, residents, environmental groups, or neighbouring landowners, frequently bring a challenge against ongoing construction activity even where no formal appeal against a specific clearance order is involved.

A developer who has obtained clearance should treat the ninety-day window following communication of the order as a real, finite period of litigation exposure rather than an open-ended risk, and should keep precise records of when and how the clearance was actually published or communicated, since the starting point for the limitation clock is itself frequently a contested issue in these appeals, particularly where a project is challenged by a third party who claims not to have had earlier notice of the clearance.

Does going to the NGT shut out the High Court? The Supreme Court says no

A question that has recurred in environmental litigation is whether the National Green Tribunal Act's specialised jurisdiction under Sections 14 and 22 displaces a High Court's ordinary writ jurisdiction over the same subject matter, since a party dissatisfied with how a Tribunal matter is proceeding might prefer the broader remedies a writ petition can offer. The Supreme Court resolved this in Madhya Pradesh High Court Advocates Bar Association v. Union of India, 2022 SCC OnLine SC 639, decided on 18 May 2022 by a bench of Justices K.M. Joseph and Hrishikesh Roy, holding that Sections 14 and 22 of the National Green Tribunal Act do not oust a High Court's writ jurisdiction under Articles 226 and 227 of the Constitution, since that jurisdiction forms part of the Constitution's basic structure and cannot be excluded by an ordinary statute, however specialised the tribunal it establishes.

The same decision separately upheld the constitutionality of Section 22 of the Act, which provides a direct appeal from a Tribunal decision to the Supreme Court within ninety days, on grounds available under Section 100 of the Code of Civil Procedure, rejecting a challenge that had argued this route was constitutionally impermissible; the Court made clear, however, that because the appeal is confined to the grounds available under Section 100 CPC, the Supreme Court does not function as an ordinary first appellate court simply re-examining the facts when exercising this specific appellate jurisdiction. For a Delhi NCR developer facing adverse Tribunal proceedings, the practical upshot is that a writ petition before the Delhi High Court or the Allahabad High Court remains available in appropriate circumstances even where Tribunal proceedings are ongoing or have concluded, though a party should not assume a writ petition is simply a second attempt at the same arguments the Tribunal has already rejected, since a High Court exercising writ jurisdiction applies its own, generally narrower, standard of review to a specialised tribunal's findings on technical environmental questions.

The GRAP construction ban: a separate, recurring risk that has nothing to do with a project's own compliance

Even a project that has obtained every required clearance and complies fully with its conditions faces a separate, recurring source of construction disruption in Delhi NCR: the Graded Response Action Plan construction and demolition ban imposed by the Commission for Air Quality Management in National Capital Region and Adjoining Areas, the statutory body constituted under its own 2021 Act to coordinate air-quality action across the region. Once air quality in the region crosses the threshold for Stage III of the plan, a ban takes effect on non-essential construction and demolition activity across the whole of the National Capital Region, not merely within Delhi itself, and this ban has been confirmed to extend specifically to Noida, Greater Noida, Ghaziabad, Gurugram and Faridabad, meaning a project in Gautam Buddh Nagar is subject to exactly the same ban as a project within Delhi's own municipal limits. At Stage IV, the ban extends further to cover linear public infrastructure projects such as highways, roads and flyovers, with specific exemptions carved out for categories such as defence-related projects and metro rail construction.

This ban has operated, in substance, as an annual feature of the construction calendar in the region since at least 2017, with courts and the Commission imposing restrictions each year through the October to January period as air quality deteriorates, and developers in the industry have estimated that even a single month of enforced suspension can delay a project's overall delivery timeline by two to three months once remobilisation of labour and materials, and the knock-on effect on dependent construction sequences, are taken into account. Industry bodies have repeatedly sought exemptions for RERA-registered projects on the basis that they serve a public interest in timely delivery to homebuyers, without these exemptions being uniformly granted, and the Supreme Court has separately directed that construction workers affected by these shutdowns be compensated for the period of enforced suspension, a cost that in practice falls on the developer or contractor responsible for the site.

A developer planning a construction schedule in Delhi NCR should build the realistic likelihood of a multi-week Stage III or Stage IV suspension into the project's own timeline from the outset, particularly for any construction activity planned for the October through January window, rather than treating the ban as an unforeseeable force majeure event each time it recurs, since its annual recurrence over the past several years makes it a predictable, budgetable risk rather than a genuine surprise. Construction contracts for Delhi NCR projects should also specifically address how a GRAP-driven suspension is treated for purposes of extension of time and cost escalation, rather than relying on a general force majeure clause that may not have been drafted with this specific, recurring regional risk in mind.

A developer selling units off-plan during this period should also factor the predictable annual disruption into the completion timeline disclosed to buyers and into any RERA-registered project's own stated completion date, since a delay attributable to a GRAP suspension does not necessarily excuse a developer from the consequences of missing a committed completion date under the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 simply because the suspension itself originated from a regulatory direction rather than the developer's own default; whether and how far a GRAP-driven delay can be treated as an excusable event under a specific project's RERA registration and allotment agreements is a question that should be addressed explicitly in the project's own documentation rather than assumed to be covered by a general reference to government action or force majeure.

The Yamuna floodplain: a zone where clearance is not even the right question to ask

A distinct and more absolute restriction applies to land within the Yamuna floodplain running through Delhi, where the question is not whether environmental clearance can be obtained but whether construction is permitted at all. The Master Plan for Delhi designates the floodplain as the O Zone, within which construction is not allowed and ownership of property is itself restricted, a position the National Green Tribunal reinforced in its 2015 ruling generally referred to as the Maili Se Nirmal Yamuna judgment, which prohibited construction activity within the demarcated floodplain outright rather than merely regulating it through a clearance process.

This restriction has been tested and reaffirmed repeatedly in the years since, including by the Supreme Court in January 2024, when the Court declined a proposal to construct residential barracks for police personnel on the floodplain, upholding the National Green Tribunal's own earlier rejection of the same project, and the National Green Tribunal has continued active enforcement proceedings into 2025 and 2026 over encroachment and construction on the floodplain that has not been removed despite earlier directions, including a February 2026 direction requiring the Delhi government to re-examine and expedite the ground-level demarcation of the floodplain boundary itself.

For a developer, buyer or lender anywhere near the Yamuna's Delhi stretch, the practical lesson is that proximity to the river deserves a specific check against the demarcated floodplain boundary before any other due diligence is undertaken, since no environmental clearance process can cure a location that falls within the O Zone, and a project or purchase premised on an assumption that clearance can eventually be obtained for floodplain land is working from a mistaken premise entirely. Where the precise, currently demarcated boundary of the floodplain for a specific parcel is not already a matter of public record, it should be confirmed with the Delhi Development Authority or the relevant irrigation and flood control authority before any transaction proceeds, rather than estimated from the river's visible course, since the legally demarcated floodplain does not necessarily track the river's current physical boundary in every stretch.

Building without clearance: how the compensation for violation is actually calculated

A developer who proceeds with construction without obtaining a required environmental clearance, or who continues building after a stop-work direction has been issued, faces exposure under the polluter pays principle that Indian environmental jurisprudence has applied consistently for decades, but the specific method for calculating how much that exposure amounts to has itself been a live question. The Supreme Court addressed this directly in Rhythm County v. Satish Sanjay Hegde, a decision dated 30 January 2026, which arose from construction carried out without the required environmental clearance and in defiance of a stop-work direction the Maharashtra Pollution Control Board had issued. The Court upheld the Tribunal's power to use the project's cost or turnover as a relevant metric for calculating the environmental compensation payable, treating this as a legitimate exercise of discretion rather than an arbitrary or disproportionate basis for assessment.

The ruling also confirmed that continuing construction activity despite an existing stop-work order can itself justify an enhanced, deterrent component in the damages awarded, over and above whatever restorative compensation is assessed to address the actual environmental harm caused, on the reasoning that a developer who knowingly defies a stop-work direction has engaged in conduct the compensation framework is entitled to specifically discourage, not merely conduct that happens to have caused environmental harm. For a Delhi NCR developer, the practical significance of this ruling is that the financial exposure for building without clearance, or for continuing after being told to stop, is not a fixed, predictable penalty but one that can scale with the project's own value, which means a larger, higher-value project faces correspondingly larger exposure for the same underlying violation, and a developer weighing whether to continue construction while a clearance application or a Tribunal challenge remains pending should treat that calculation, not merely the prospect of an adverse final order, as part of the immediate cost of proceeding without resolution.

A related practical point is that a stop-work direction, once issued, should be treated as binding immediately rather than as a position to be argued against while work continues in parallel, since the Rhythm County reasoning specifically ties the enhanced, deterrent component of compensation to continued defiance of such a direction rather than to the underlying absence of clearance alone. A developer who believes a stop-work order was wrongly issued has the appeal and writ remedies already discussed available to challenge it, but pursuing those remedies while simultaneously continuing construction on the ground is precisely the combination of conduct this ruling treats as inviting the most severe financial consequence, and a far more defensible course is to halt work pending the outcome of whatever challenge is filed, even where the developer considers the underlying order mistaken.

Two approvals developers often overlook alongside environmental clearance

Environmental clearance is not the only pollution-related approval a Delhi NCR construction project typically needs. Separately from clearance under the EIA Notification, a project is generally also expected to obtain Consent to Establish, and later Consent to Operate, from the relevant State Pollution Control Board under the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 and the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981, which is the Delhi Pollution Control Committee for a Delhi project and the Uttar Pradesh Pollution Control Board for a Noida or Greater Noida project. Construction sites have specifically been required, as a condition of this consent, to take dust-control measures such as wind-breaking enclosures around the site, and enforcement action, including direction to halt construction until the consent is actually obtained, has been taken against sites proceeding without it. A Ministry notification dated 12 November 2024 is reported to exempt industrial plants that already hold prior environmental clearance under the EIA Notification from separately needing consent under the Water and Air Acts, though whether and how far this specific exemption extends to building and construction projects, as distinct from industrial plants, was not independently confirmed in this research and should be checked against the current notification and the relevant Pollution Control Board's own current practice before a developer assumes the separate consent requirement no longer applies.

Environmental clearance also intersects with a project's registration under the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016. Clearance is commonly listed among the approvals and documents a promoter must disclose when registering a project with the state Real Estate Regulatory Authority, and a promoter is required to publish, as part of the ongoing disclosure obligations that follow registration, the status of approvals that remain pending, particularly those required before a completion or occupancy certificate can be obtained. Whether a project's RERA registration can proceed while environmental clearance remains pending, or whether clearance must actually be in hand before registration is granted, is a question this research found treated differently in practice across different state authorities rather than settled by a single uniform rule, and a developer should confirm the specific sequencing the relevant state RERA authority, Uttar Pradesh RERA for a Noida project or Delhi's own authority for a Delhi project, actually requires before assuming registration and clearance can simply proceed on two independent, unrelated timelines.

Clearance is not a one-time event: ongoing monitoring and the risk of suspension

Obtaining environmental clearance is the start of a continuing compliance relationship with the issuing authority, not the end of the project's environmental obligations. A clearance order typically comes with a detailed set of conditions covering dust and noise control during construction, management of construction and demolition waste, rainwater harvesting and groundwater recharge measures, landscaping and green-cover requirements, and sewage and solid-waste management once the project becomes operational, and the clearance itself usually requires periodic compliance reports to be submitted to the issuing authority, generally every six months, documenting how these conditions are actually being met on site.

A developer that treats these conditions as paperwork to be filed rather than obligations to be actively implemented exposes the project to a risk that goes beyond the environmental compensation framework discussed above: the issuing authority retains the power to suspend or even revoke a clearance where conditions are not being met, and a construction site operating under a suspended clearance is, for practical purposes, in the same position as one that never obtained clearance at all, exposed to stop-work action and to the same kind of compensation assessment the Supreme Court addressed in Rhythm County. Large residential developments in Delhi NCR have faced exactly this kind of scrutiny where neighbouring residents or resident welfare associations have approached the National Green Tribunal alleging that construction-phase dust and water-management conditions were not being followed, and a developer's compliance record on these recurring, relatively routine conditions often matters as much to how a Tribunal views the project as the more fundamental question of whether clearance was obtained in the first place.

A practical safeguard is to assign specific internal responsibility, not left informally to the site engineer alongside other duties, for tracking each condition in the clearance order against an actual compliance calendar, and to retain the underlying records, water usage logs, waste disposal manifests, dust monitoring readings, in a form that can be produced quickly if a compliance report is due or if a challenge is filed, rather than reconstructed only once a Tribunal notice has actually arrived and the relevant period has already passed. A lender financing the project should likewise treat periodic confirmation of this ongoing compliance, not merely the original grant of clearance, as part of its continuing monitoring of the security, for the reasons already discussed in the context of acquiring an interest in a completed project, building a review of the most recent six-monthly compliance report into whatever periodic inspection or disbursement-linked monitoring the financing arrangement already provides for, rather than treating that report as paperwork the borrower alone needs to worry about.

Due diligence for a buyer, lender or investor acquiring an interest in a Delhi NCR project

A buyer purchasing units in a completed or under-construction project, or a lender taking security over a Delhi NCR development, should treat confirmation that environmental clearance was actually obtained, and obtained before construction began rather than after the fact, as a standard part of due diligence alongside the more familiar checks on title and building-plan sanction. A structure built without a required environmental clearance remains exposed to enforcement action, potentially including demolition, regardless of how long it has stood, how many units have since been sold, or how many times the property has changed hands, and an innocent purchaser or lender inherits that underlying exposure along with the property itself.

The clearance documentation itself, including the specific conditions attached to it, should also be reviewed rather than merely confirming that a clearance exists, since environmental clearances typically carry ongoing compliance conditions, periodic monitoring and reporting obligations, conditions on effluent and waste management, landscaping and green-cover requirements, that continue through the life of the project and, in many cases, into its operational phase after construction is complete, well beyond the point at which the original developer may have exited the project through a sale of individual units or the entire development. A lender financing a Delhi NCR project should condition disbursement on confirmation that these ongoing conditions are actually being complied with, not merely that the clearance was originally granted, since a clearance obtained correctly at the outset does not protect a lender's security if the project later falls out of compliance with its own conditions and faces enforcement action as a result, including the kind of compensation exposure described in the preceding section on the Rhythm County ruling.

Finally, a buyer or investor should specifically ask whether any Tribunal proceeding, pending or concluded, has ever been brought against the specific project, rather than relying solely on the developer's own representations, since a past or ongoing National Green Tribunal matter, even one the developer considers resolved in its favour, can carry continuing compliance obligations of the kind described above, and can also signal a project with a higher-than-average litigation and regulatory risk profile going forward, particularly where the matter involved findings on the adequacy of the original environmental impact assessment itself. Searching the National Green Tribunal's own case records and recent reported decisions for the project's name, and for the names of the specific developer entity and its group companies, is a simple, low-cost step that should be a standard part of this diligence rather than an optional extra reserved only for the very largest and highest-value transactions anywhere in the portfolio.

Environmental clearance for a Delhi NCR construction or township project is not a formality that tracks the pace of building-plan approval; it is a separate statutory gate, triggered by specific, calculable thresholds on built-up area and land area, enforced through a specialised tribunal with its own appeal timelines, and layered, every winter, with a region-wide construction ban that applies regardless of any individual project's own compliance record. A developer who maps these thresholds against the project's actual footprint at the planning stage, rather than discovering them midway through construction financing, avoids the most expensive version of this risk.

For a buyer, lender or investor coming to a Delhi NCR project after the fact, the clearance question deserves the same diligence as title and building-plan sanction, not less, because the consequences of getting it wrong, demolition exposure that survives a change of ownership, and ongoing compliance obligations that outlast the original developer's involvement, do not diminish simply because the structure has already been built and sold. Treating environmental clearance as a parallel, independent track from the outset, rather than a line item to be ticked off once other approvals are secured, remains the more defensible way to plan a project, or to evaluate one, in this region, and the same discipline applies equally to the recurring, seasonal GRAP risk and to the ongoing six-monthly compliance obligations that follow clearance long after construction itself has finished.

Frequently Asked Questions

What built-up area triggers environmental clearance for a building project in Noida or Delhi?

Under the EIA Notification, 2006, a building and construction project needs environmental clearance once its built-up area exceeds 20,000 square metres, assessed as a Category B project by the relevant state-level authority rather than the central Ministry. A township or area development project separately needs clearance where it covers more than 50 hectares of land or exceeds 1,50,000 square metres of built-up area. Several towers forming part of a single, integrated development under one layout are generally assessed on their aggregate built-up area, so phasing construction specifically to keep each phase below the threshold risks the entire project being treated as requiring clearance regardless of the formal phase division.

How long does a person have to appeal an environmental clearance to the NGT?

Section 16 of the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 sets a thirty-day limitation period from communication of the order, extendable by the Tribunal for a further period of up to sixty days on sufficient cause, for a combined outer limit of ninety days that most benches treat as a hard cap.

Does an NGT challenge prevent a developer from also approaching the High Court?

No. The Supreme Court held in Madhya Pradesh High Court Advocates Bar Association v. Union of India, 2022 SCC OnLine SC 639, that the National Green Tribunal Act does not oust a High Court's writ jurisdiction under Articles 226 and 227, so a writ petition remains available in appropriate circumstances alongside or after Tribunal proceedings.

Does the Delhi NCR construction ban apply to projects in Noida and Greater Noida?

Yes. The Graded Response Action Plan construction and demolition ban imposed by the Commission for Air Quality Management applies across the whole National Capital Region once the relevant air-quality stage is triggered, and this has been confirmed to cover Noida, Greater Noida, Ghaziabad, Gurugram and Faridabad, not Delhi alone.

Is a RERA-registered project exempt from the GRAP construction ban?

Not automatically. Industry bodies have repeatedly sought an exemption for RERA-registered projects on the ground that they serve a public interest in timely delivery to homebuyers, but this has not resulted in a uniform exemption being granted, and a developer should not assume RERA registration alone protects a project from a GRAP-driven suspension.

What happens if a structure was built without the required environmental clearance?

It remains exposed to enforcement action, including potential demolition, regardless of how long it has stood or how many times it has changed hands. A buyer or lender acquiring an interest in such a structure inherits that exposure, which is why confirming clearance was obtained before construction began is a standard part of due diligence for any Delhi NCR project.

Can environmental clearance be obtained for construction on the Yamuna floodplain in Delhi?

No. The Yamuna floodplain is designated the O Zone under the Master Plan for Delhi, where construction is not permitted at all and clearance is not the relevant mechanism. The National Green Tribunal's 2015 Maili Se Nirmal Yamuna judgment and subsequent enforcement, including a Supreme Court ruling in January 2024, have consistently upheld this prohibition.

How is the penalty calculated for construction without environmental clearance?

The Supreme Court held in Rhythm County v. Satish Sanjay Hegde, decided 30 January 2026, that the National Green Tribunal can use a project's cost or turnover as a relevant metric for calculating environmental compensation, and that continuing construction despite a stop-work order can justify an enhanced, deterrent component in addition to restorative damages for the actual environmental harm.
