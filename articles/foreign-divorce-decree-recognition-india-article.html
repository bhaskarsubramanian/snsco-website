When a Foreign Divorce Decree Does and Does Not Bind an Indian Court: The Law After the 2026 Rulings

Two Supreme Court decisions in 2026, one allowing a divorce and one refusing to stop one, show exactly how far a decree from abroad travels into an Indian courtroom and where it stops dead.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 30 October 2026 · Law stated as on 29 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A divorce decree stamped by a court in New Jersey, London or Dubai does not, by that fact alone, end a marriage for any Indian purpose. Whether it does depends on a fifty-year-old provision of the Code of Civil Procedure and a test the Supreme Court restated as recently as January 2026, and a related ruling in September 2026 shows how hard it now is to stop the foreign case in the first place.

Key points

  • A foreign divorce decree is not automatically valid in India; Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 decides whether an Indian court treats it as conclusive.
  • The controlling test, from the Supreme Court's 1991 ruling in Y. Narasimha Rao, asks whether the foreign court's jurisdiction and the ground of divorce both match the matrimonial law that actually governs the marriage.
  • In January 2026 the Supreme Court applied that test to refuse an American decree granted on irretrievable breakdown, a ground the Hindu Marriage Act does not recognise, and then used its own Article 142 power to grant the divorce itself.
  • Trying to stop a spouse's divorce petition abroad through an Indian anti-suit injunction got harder in September 2026: the Supreme Court held that the fear of a later unenforceable decree is not, by itself, a reason to restrain early-stage foreign proceedings.
  • Remarrying in India on the strength of a decree that fails the Section 13 test risks a bigamy charge under Section 82 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, since in law the first marriage never ended.
  • Custody taken or retained abroad is difficult to reverse through Indian process because India still has not joined the 1980 Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction.
  • Where the other spouse disputes a foreign decree, or never genuinely contested it abroad, a declaratory suit before the Family Court having jurisdiction is usually the only way to convert doubt into a binding Indian record.
  • Parliament has still not enacted the private international law statute the Supreme Court asked for in 1994, so the whole area continues to run on case law rather than a codified rule.

Why "Is It Valid?" Is the Wrong First Question

Every few months a client arrives with a certified copy of a decree absolute from an English county court, or a final judgment of dissolution from a county court in California, or a decree from a personal status court in Dubai, and asks a version of the same question: is my marriage over in India now? The honest answer is that India has no rule anywhere close to the full faith and credit that American states owe each other's judgments, and no general treaty of mutual recognition with the United Kingdom, the Gulf states or most of the countries where Indian-origin families now live. What India has instead is Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, a single provision written for ordinary civil judgments that the courts have had to stretch, over a century, to cover the one category of foreign judgment that actually reaches most Indian families: the divorce decree.

That stretching matters because a decree's standing in India is never an abstract question. It decides whether a person who remarries in Delhi or Mumbai is committing bigamy. It decides whether a maintenance claim filed in an Indian court can be defeated on the ground that the marriage already ended abroad. It decides whether a share in ancestral property, or a claim to a deceased spouse's estate, survives or falls. None of these questions wait for a convenient moment; they surface in bail applications, in succession proceedings, in passport and visa paperwork, often years after the foreign court closed its file. Getting the Section 13 analysis right before any of those moments, rather than after, is the entire point of this article.

The provision itself is short. It says a foreign judgment is conclusive between the same parties, on a matter the foreign court directly decided, except in six situations. Divorce decrees are judgments like any other for this purpose, with one complication: a decree dissolving a marriage is treated as a judgment in rem, good against the world and not just the two litigants, which is precisely why Indian courts have insisted on checking it more carefully rather than less.

Section 13's Six Gates

Section 13 lists its exceptions as clauses (a) to (f), and a foreign judgment fails to bind an Indian court if any one of them applies. The first asks whether the foreign court was a court of competent jurisdiction, meaning competent not merely by its own domestic law but also in the private-international-law sense that Indian courts apply when deciding whose courts may pronounce on a marriage at all. The second asks whether the foreign court actually decided the case on its merits, as opposed to, say, dismissing it for default or disposing of it on a technical point without engaging the substance of the claim.

The third exception catches a decree that proceeds on an incorrect view of international law, or that refuses to apply the law of India in a case where Indian law was the one that ought to have governed. This is the clause that does most of the work in matrimonial cases, because it is the hook on which the Supreme Court later built the requirement that the foreign court's grounds for divorce must match the personal law actually governing the marriage. The fourth exception denies effect to a decree obtained in proceedings that offended natural justice, most commonly where a party was not given proper notice or a genuine opportunity to be heard.

Fraud supplies the fifth exception: a decree procured by fraud, whether fraud on the foreign court or fraud as to the facts establishing its jurisdiction, is not conclusive, and this is the ground the Supreme Court relied on in the earliest of the leading cases. The sixth and final exception denies effect to a foreign judgment that sustains a claim founded on a breach of any law in force in India. Section 14 of the same Code adds a procedural point that favours the decree-holder at the first stage: a certified copy of a foreign judgment is presumed to have been pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction, so the burden of showing that one of the six exceptions applies falls on the party resisting recognition, not on the one relying on the decree.

Running alongside Section 13 is a provision from the law of evidence that explains why a matrimonial decree is treated differently from, say, a foreign money decree. A final judgment of a competent court in matrimonial jurisdiction is relevant and, broadly, conclusive proof of the legal status it creates, which is the statutory basis for calling such a decree a judgment in rem. The same provision, however, lets a party show that the court which passed it was not competent, or that the judgment was obtained by fraud or collusion, which is exactly the opening Section 13 already provides. The two provisions were read together long before either the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam or the amended Code existed, and nothing in the recognition analysis itself has changed with the 2023 recodification of the evidence law; what has changed is only the numbering of the provision, which this article has not independently verified against the Gazette text and does not state here.

Satya v. Teja Singh: Fraud on Jurisdiction, Not Just Fraud on the Facts

The foundational case is Satya v. Teja Singh, decided by the Supreme Court in 1974 and reported at (1975) 1 SCC 120. The parties had married in India in 1955. The husband went to the United States, and some years later obtained a decree of divorce from a court in Nevada. The wife, who had never been to Nevada and had no connection with it beyond her husband's temporary presence there, challenged the decree when he later relied on it in India.

The Supreme Court refused to give the Nevada decree any effect. It held that jurisdiction, for purposes of recognising a foreign matrimonial decree, is not simply whatever the foreign court said it had; an Indian court is entitled to look behind the decree and ask whether the facts said to confer jurisdiction, in that case a claimed domicile in Nevada, were genuine. The husband had gone to Nevada only long enough to satisfy its residence requirement for jurisdiction and had no intention of making it his home. That, the Court held, was a fraud on the jurisdiction of the foreign court itself, not merely a factual dispute about the marriage, and a decree obtained that way could not bind an Indian court whatever its formal recitals said.

Two points from Satya v. Teja Singh still do the heavy lifting in every later case. First, even a judgment in rem, good against the world, remains open to challenge on the ground that the court delivering it was not competent, or that it was obtained by fraud or collusion. Second, the question of what makes a foreign court competent for recognition purposes is answered by Indian private international law, not by the foreign court's own procedural rules. A Nevada court can be entirely correct about Nevada law and still be found, by an Indian court, to have lacked the jurisdiction that Indian law requires before it will treat the resulting decree as conclusive.

The 1991 Test: Y. Narasimha Rao and Its Three Narrow Exceptions

Satya v. Teja Singh dealt with an extreme case of invented jurisdiction. The harder, more common case is the foreign court that genuinely had jurisdiction under its own law, heard both sides, and granted a divorce on a ground that is simply unavailable under the law governing the Indian marriage, most often some version of no-fault or irretrievable breakdown. The Supreme Court addressed that problem directly in Y. Narasimha Rao v. Y. Venkata Lakshmi, (1991) 3 SCC 451, a case where a husband had obtained a divorce in a Missouri court after representing a residence there that the Court found to be, in substance, only a technical compliance with the state's ninety-day filing requirement.

The test the Court laid down has outlived every later attempt to narrow or expand it. A foreign matrimonial judgment is recognised in India only where the jurisdiction assumed by the foreign court, as well as the ground on which the relief is granted, is in accordance with the matrimonial law under which the parties are married. Where the parties married under the Hindu Marriage Act, in other words, the foreign court's basis for hearing the case and its reason for granting the divorce both have to track what the Hindu Marriage Act itself would recognise, and a decree that satisfies only one of the two still fails.

The Court then carved out three situations in which a decree can still be recognised despite this mismatch. The first is where the proceedings are instituted in, and the relief is granted by, the forum in which the respondent is domiciled or habitually and permanently resides, and the relief is granted on a ground available under the matrimonial law. The second is where the respondent voluntarily and effectively submits to the jurisdiction of the forum and contests the claim on a ground available under the matrimonial law. The third is where the respondent consents to the grant of relief even though the jurisdiction of the forum is not in accordance with the matrimonial law. Outside these three categories, a decree that rests on a ground the governing personal law does not recognise is not conclusive in India, whatever the foreign court's own law says about its validity there.

Candour about the reasoning is worth setting out, because it explains why the exceptions are kept narrow. Divorces obtained abroad were already common by 1991 and the problem was only going to grow, so some workable, predictable rule was needed rather than a case-by-case inquiry into every foreign court's procedure. The three exceptions are the price of that predictability: they are narrow by design, and later courts have resisted suggestions to read them loosely.

What Counts as Genuine Submission, and What Does Not

The second exception, voluntary and effective submission, generates most of the litigation, because almost every respondent in a contested foreign divorce files something. The question the courts actually ask is whether that filing amounted to engaging the merits or was instead a limited appearance aimed only at contesting jurisdiction. A written statement filed under protest, confined to disputing the foreign court's jurisdiction and not engaging the substance of the claim, has repeatedly been held not to amount to submission at all. The same is true where a party is shown to have had no realistic opportunity to defend, for instance because immigration status prevented travel to the forum.

The Delhi High Court's approach in Harmeeta Singh v. Rajat Taneja illustrates the point from the other direction. The wife there had no spouse visa for the United States and therefore had no real occasion to appear and contest the husband's divorce petition filed there; she instead pursued a maintenance claim in India under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, and the High Court restrained the husband from pressing the foreign proceedings further. The case is routinely cited for the proposition that a bare procedural capacity to respond, without a genuine practical opportunity to do so, is not submission in the Narasimha Rao sense.

The same reasoning has been applied by High Courts to decrees from courts outside the common-law world. Where both parties were Hindus domiciled in India and the only connection to a foreign personal-status court was a period of residence there, at least one High Court has held the foreign decree not binding precisely because the jurisdictional basis did not match the parties' matrimonial law, reinforcing that the Narasimha Rao test applies with equal force regardless of which country's court granted the decree. The common thread across these rulings is that courts look past the label a party puts on their own conduct abroad and ask, as a question of fact, whether there was real engagement with the merits on a recognised ground.

January 2026: Kishorekumar Mohan Kale and the Limits of a Supreme Court Cure

The clearest recent application of the whole framework came on 15 January 2026, when the Supreme Court decided Kishorekumar Mohan Kale v. Kashmira Kale, Civil Appeal No. 1342 of 2013, a bench of Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta. The husband held a decree of divorce from a court in the United States, granted on the ground of irretrievable breakdown of the marriage. Irretrievable breakdown is not a ground available under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, which governed the parties' marriage; the Act's grounds remain the specific fault and status grounds in its Section 13, together with mutual consent under Section 13B, and irretrievable breakdown is available only as a basis for the Supreme Court's own Article 142 power, not as a ground any party can plead as a matter of right before a trial court or High Court.

The husband's participation in the American proceedings consisted of contesting jurisdiction by a written submission sent by post, without any further appearance or engagement with the merits. Applying Narasimha Rao, the Supreme Court held that this did not amount to a voluntary and effective submission to the foreign court's jurisdiction, and that the decree, resting on a ground the governing matrimonial law did not recognise and lacking any qualifying submission, failed the Section 13 test and could not be treated as conclusive in India.

What makes the ruling instructive rather than merely a restatement is what the Court did next. Rather than leave the parties married in the eyes of Indian law after eighteen years of separation with no realistic prospect of reconciliation, the Supreme Court exercised its power under Article 142 of the Constitution to dissolve the marriage itself on the ground of irretrievable breakdown. That power is the Supreme Court's alone; it is not available to a Family Court, a High Court, or any other forum, and it is typically exercised only in a matter that has actually reached the Supreme Court on appeal, after the ordinary channels have been exhausted. A litigant cannot plan around the expectation of an Article 142 rescue. For everyone short of the Supreme Court itself, a foreign decree that fails the Narasimha Rao test leaves the marriage legally subsisting in India until a competent Indian court dissolves it through the ordinary statutory grounds, or until the parties qualify for a fresh mutual-consent decree, or until their case is one of the small number the Supreme Court itself agrees to hear.

Which Personal Law the Decree Has to Match

The Narasimha Rao test asks whether the foreign decree's ground matches the matrimonial law governing the marriage, which makes identifying that governing law the first practical step in any case. For a marriage solemnised under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, the comparison is against Section 13 of that Act: adultery, cruelty, desertion for a continuous period, conversion to another religion, incurable unsoundness of mind, virulent and incurable leprosy as it stood before later amendment, venereal disease in a communicable form, renunciation of the world, and presumption of death after seven years' disappearance, together with mutual consent under Section 13B. Because irretrievable breakdown is not on this list as a ground any ordinary court can grant, a no-fault decree from a foreign jurisdiction applying its own no-fault statute will almost always fail the ground-matching limb of the test unless one of the three Narasimha Rao exceptions independently rescues it.

For Christian spouses, the comparison runs against the grounds in the Indian Divorce Act, 1869, as repeatedly amended, and Indian courts have granted relief under that Act's own grounds without regard to whether the foreign country connected to the marriage would recognise the same grounds, which cuts both ways when the comparison runs the other direction on an incoming foreign decree. For marriages solemnised under the Special Marriage Act, 1954, the position is less settled, since that Act contains no provision at all addressing recognition of a foreign decree touching a marriage registered under it; courts have had to extend the Narasimha Rao reasoning by analogy rather than apply a dedicated statutory text.

This patchwork is not an oversight that has gone unnoticed. As far back as 1994, in Neerja Saraph v. Jayant V. Saraph, (1994) 6 SCC 461, the Supreme Court itself observed that recognition of foreign matrimonial decrees was a hard question of private international law and suggested that Parliament consider legislation along the lines of the United Kingdom's statute on reciprocal enforcement of foreign judgments, together with compulsory registration of marriages involving a non-resident Indian spouse. Three decades on, no such dedicated statute exists, and the entire structure described in this article continues to rest on judge-made doctrine built around a single provision of a procedural code never written with marriage in mind.

Do You Need an Indian Court to Say So? The Declaratory Suit

A decree that plainly satisfies Narasimha Rao, typically a genuine mutual-consent foreign divorce where both spouses actively participated and the ground is one Indian law would also recognise, does not legally require a separate Indian proceeding before either spouse can rely on it. As a matter of prudence, however, many practitioners still recommend obtaining an Indian court's declaration, because registrars of marriage, passport authorities, and the other spouse's future lawyers will often want a document from an Indian forum rather than relying on their own reading of a foreign judgment.

Where the decree is genuinely doubtful, whether because it was ex parte, because the ground does not match the governing personal law, or because the other spouse disputes its validity outright, a separate Indian proceeding stops being optional. The forum for that proceeding is the Family Court, not an ordinary civil court. Section 7 of the Family Courts Act, 1984, read with its Explanation, places a suit or proceeding for a declaration as to the validity of a marriage or as to the matrimonial status of a person within the exclusive jurisdiction of the Family Court, and Section 8 correspondingly excludes civil courts from that jurisdiction wherever a Family Court has been established. The Supreme Court confirmed in Balram Yadav v. Fulmaniya Yadav, AIR 2016 SC 2161, that this exclusive jurisdiction extends equally to a negative declaration, meaning a suit asking the court to declare that a marriage still subsists or that a purported divorce has no effect, and not just to a suit seeking an affirmative declaration of validity.

The practical sequence, where recognition is genuinely contested, is therefore to file in the Family Court having jurisdiction, typically where the marriage was solemnised, where the parties last resided together, or where the respondent currently resides, seeking a declaration on the Section 13 CPC question directly. That proceeding lets an Indian court test the foreign decree against the Narasimha Rao framework with both sides present and with the benefit of evidence about how the foreign proceedings were actually conducted, which is precisely the inquiry a later criminal court or succession court is ill-equipped to undertake as a side issue in some other case.

Stopping the Foreign Case Before It Starts: Anti-Suit Injunctions After September 2026

A spouse who learns that the other side has filed, or is about to file, for divorce abroad sometimes wants to stop that case rather than wait to fight recognition later. The tool for that is an anti-suit injunction, an order of an Indian court restraining a person within its personal jurisdiction from pursuing or continuing proceedings in a foreign court. The Supreme Court confirmed in Dinesh Singh Thakur v. Sonal Thakur, decided on 17 April 2018, that Indian courts do have this power over a party subject to their personal jurisdiction, but held that it must be exercised sparingly rather than routinely, because it necessarily intrudes on the authority of a foreign court over its own proceedings. On the facts of that case, where a wife had filed for divorce abroad on a ground unavailable under the Hindu Marriage Act, the Court declined to grant the injunction, reasoning that the mere availability of a ground Indian law does not recognise did not by itself show the foreign proceedings were oppressive or vexatious.

That reluctance hardened further in Mihir Prakash Bajoria v. Vidushi Jain Bajoria, 2026 INSC 1034, decided on 22 September 2026 by a bench of Justices S.V.N. Bhatti and N.V. Anjaria. The husband there had obtained an ex parte anti-suit injunction from a trial court in Alipore, West Bengal, on 1 November 2025, restraining the wife from continuing a divorce petition she had filed in the United Kingdom, where the couple had in fact been living. The Calcutta High Court set that injunction aside on 15 December 2025, and the Supreme Court dismissed the husband's further appeal.

The Court treated the twin considerations for any anti-suit injunction as whether the foreign proceedings are oppressive or vexatious and whether refusing the injunction would cause irreparable harm to the party seeking it, and held that an injunction is ordinarily unwarranted while the foreign proceedings remain at an early, unresolved stage. It rejected the argument that the Indian court should intervene merely because the eventual UK decree might later fail the Section 13 CPC test in India; that risk, the Court held, is speculative at the injunction stage and does not by itself make the foreign forum oppressive. It also found the United Kingdom to be the more appropriate forum on the facts, since that is where the family had actually lived, and left both parties free to challenge the eventual UK order, including on recognition grounds, once it was actually passed.

Read together, the two rulings leave a narrow and now narrower path for stopping a foreign matrimonial case through an Indian injunction. A party has to show something closer to genuine oppression, such as a forum chosen purely to disadvantage the other spouse procedurally or a case that will foreclose an Indian spouse's ability to be heard at all, rather than simply pointing to a ground of divorce the foreign court will apply that Indian law would not. Waiting for the foreign decree and then contesting its recognition under Section 13 CPC, rather than trying to prevent the foreign case altogether, is now the more realistic strategy in most cases, however much it defers the point at which the matter is actually resolved.

The Remarriage Trap

The most serious practical consequence of getting this analysis wrong falls on the spouse who remarries in India in reliance on a foreign decree that later turns out not to satisfy Section 13. Section 82(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, which carries forward the former Section 494 of the Indian Penal Code, punishes a person who marries again during the lifetime of a spouse, where that marriage is void by reason of its taking place during the life of such spouse, with imprisonment of up to seven years together with a fine. The elements of the offence, as the Supreme Court set out in Pashaura Singh v. State of Punjab, (2010) 11 SCC 749, are a subsisting first marriage, a second marriage during the first spouse's lifetime, and the absence of a valid decree or other lawful ground ending the first marriage before the second one took place.

If a foreign decree fails the Narasimha Rao test, the legal position is not that the first marriage ended and the foreign court merely lacked authority to say so; it is that, for Indian purposes, no divorce occurred at all. A person who then remarries in India is, in the eyes of Indian criminal law, marrying while still married, exposed to prosecution on a complaint by the aggrieved spouse or a specified relative, with no limitation period barring the complaint however many years have passed. This is a different situation from one where an Indian court's own decree is later set aside on appeal; the Supreme Court held in Krishna Gopal Divedi v. Prabha Divedi, (2002) 10 SCC 216, that a person who remarries while a validly passed ex parte Indian decree still stands cannot be prosecuted for bigamy merely because that decree is later reversed, since the second marriage was lawful when contracted. A foreign decree that never satisfied Section 13 in the first place never had that lawful status to begin with, which is precisely why the distinction matters and why remarrying on a doubtful foreign decree, without first securing an Indian declaration, carries a risk that remarrying on a domestic decree later overturned does not.

Custody and Maintenance When the Decree Does Not Travel

A foreign court's custody or maintenance orders raise a related but distinct problem, because India has still not acceded to the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, 1980, despite the Law Commission having recommended accession and despite periodic legislative proposals that have not resulted in either accession or a dedicated domestic statute on the subject. The practical consequence is that a foreign custody order is not automatically binding on an Indian court; Indian courts treat the welfare of the child as the controlling standard in any proceeding brought here, and will weigh a foreign order as one relevant factor rather than as a conclusive determination, which can work for or against a parent depending on which side of the order they stand. A parent anticipating or facing an inter-country custody dispute needs a separate, focused assessment of that question, which this publication has covered at length elsewhere.

Maintenance presents a narrower but still real gap. The Maintenance Orders Enforcement Act, 1921, as amended, allows the Central Government to notify a foreign country as a reciprocating territory, after which a maintenance order from an Indian court can be registered and enforced there, and vice versa, but this mechanism only operates for countries actually notified under it, and most of the countries where Indian families have settled have no such notification in force. Where no reciprocal arrangement exists, the practical route for a spouse owed maintenance is almost always a fresh domestic claim before an Indian court, whether under the applicable personal law, under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita's maintenance provisions, or under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, rather than an attempt to enforce the foreign maintenance order directly. This mirrors the custody position in one respect: the safer course in both cases is treating the Indian proceeding as the operative one and the foreign order as supporting evidence, not as a shortcut past Indian process.

What Still Has No Settled Answer

Several questions in this area remain genuinely open rather than merely under-litigated. Exactly how much engagement with a foreign proceeding counts as the voluntary and effective submission that Narasimha Rao requires has never been reduced to a bright-line rule; a written statement filed only to contest jurisdiction fails, as Kale confirmed again in January 2026, but the courts have not said how much more a respondent must do, whether appearing through counsel without personally attending is sufficient, or whether participating in a settlement conference abroad counts as contesting the claim on the merits.

The Special Marriage Act's silence on recognition of foreign decrees touching marriages registered under it is a second genuine gap, addressed so far only by analogy to Hindu Marriage Act cases rather than by any ruling squarely on that Act's own text. Third, the call Neerja Saraph made in 1994 for a dedicated statute on recognition of foreign matrimonial judgments and compulsory registration of marriages involving an NRI spouse remains unanswered more than thirty years later, leaving the whole field to be worked out case by case rather than under a codified rule that could give both spouses certainty before, rather than after, a foreign court is approached.

Finally, nothing in the record through October 2026 suggests any imminent change in India's position on the 1980 Hague Convention, despite the gap this leaves in cross-border custody and abduction cases; any claim that accession is imminent should be treated with caution until an official notification or a specific legislative bill actually appears.

A Practical Order of Steps

The first step, before anything else, is identifying the law that actually governed the marriage: the Hindu Marriage Act, the Indian Divorce Act for Christian spouses, the Special Marriage Act for a civil or inter-faith marriage, or another personal law, since every later question is measured against that Act's own grounds and not against some general notion of fairness. The second step is comparing the ground on which the foreign decree was actually granted against that Act's list of recognised grounds, reading the foreign judgment itself rather than relying on a summary, since courts have repeatedly found that decrees described loosely as being for "mutual consent" or "breakdown" sometimes rest, on a close reading, on a different stated ground.

A third and equally necessary step is working out exactly how the respondent participated in the foreign proceeding: whether there was a genuine appearance and contest on the merits, a limited appearance to protest jurisdiction only, or no participation at all, since this is what decides whether any of the three Narasimha Rao exceptions can apply despite a ground mismatch. Where the decree clears both the ground and the participation tests, it can generally be relied upon, though obtaining an Indian declaration remains the safer course before remarriage, before dealing with jointly held property, or before any filing where the other spouse might later dispute the decree's effect.

Where the decree fails either test and the other spouse disputes its effect, the realistic options are a declaratory suit in the Family Court having jurisdiction to settle the Section 13 question directly, or fresh matrimonial proceedings in India on a ground the governing Act actually recognises, bearing in mind that an Article 142 solution of the kind granted in Kale is available only if the matter independently reaches the Supreme Court and is not a planning assumption anyone can rely on in advance. A spouse who anticipates an undesired foreign filing and wants to prevent it altogether should move quickly, since Bajoria shows the window for an anti-suit injunction narrows further the longer the foreign case has run, and should expect to show real oppression or vexatiousness rather than a bare mismatch of grounds. Finally, custody and maintenance questions linked to the same marriage should be raised in Indian proceedings in parallel rather than left to ride on whatever the foreign court eventually orders, since neither a custody order nor a maintenance order from abroad binds an Indian court automatically.

The law in this area has not changed its basic shape since 1991: a foreign divorce decree binds an Indian court only when the foreign forum's jurisdiction and the ground it applied both track the personal law actually governing the marriage, subject to three narrow exceptions for genuine domicile, genuine submission, or genuine consent. What 2026 added was confirmation, from two separate Supreme Court benches within nine months of each other, that this test is being applied with the same rigour at both ends of the problem: strictly against a decree that fails it, however many years the parties have been apart, and just as strictly against attempts to use an Indian injunction to head off an undesired foreign case before it has even reached a final order.

For a family weighing a foreign filing, or holding a foreign decree they are not certain will hold up, the practical lesson is to run the Narasimha Rao analysis early rather than discover its answer in a bigamy complaint, a maintenance defence, or a succession dispute years later. The marriage either ended, as a matter of Indian law, or it did not, and nothing about a foreign court's own confidence in its judgment changes which of those two is true.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a divorce decree from the US, UK or Gulf automatically valid in India?

No. Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 governs whether an Indian court treats any foreign judgment, including a divorce decree, as conclusive. The Supreme Court's test from Y. Narasimha Rao v. Y. Venkata Lakshmi (1991) asks whether the foreign court's jurisdiction and the ground of divorce both match the matrimonial law governing the marriage. A decree that fails this test, and does not fall within one of three narrow exceptions, has no effect in India even though it is perfectly valid where it was granted.

Does it matter if both spouses agreed to the foreign divorce?

It matters a great deal, but only if the agreed ground is one the governing Indian personal law also recognises, or if the respondent's consent independently satisfies the third Narasimha Rao exception. A genuine mutual-consent foreign decree, with both parties actively participating, is generally safer to rely on than an ex parte decree, but a decree granted only on a no-fault or irretrievable-breakdown ground can still fail the ground-matching limb of the test even where both spouses wanted the divorce.

Can I remarry in India right after getting a divorce abroad?

Only safely if the decree clearly satisfies Section 13 CPC and the Narasimha Rao test. If it does not, the first marriage is treated in India as still subsisting, and remarrying exposes the person to prosecution for bigamy under Section 82 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, with no limitation period on the complaint. Obtaining an Indian declaration of the decree's validity before remarrying removes this risk.

What did the Supreme Court decide in the January 2026 Kale case?

In Kishorekumar Mohan Kale v. Kashmira Kale, decided 15 January 2026, the Court held that a US decree granted on irretrievable breakdown, a ground the Hindu Marriage Act does not recognise, did not bind the parties in India because the husband had only contested the US court's jurisdiction by post without further participation. The Court then used its own Article 142 power to grant the couple a divorce directly, a remedy available only to the Supreme Court itself and only once a matter reaches it.

Can I get an Indian court to stop my spouse's divorce case abroad?

Indian courts can grant an anti-suit injunction against a person within their personal jurisdiction, but the Supreme Court held in September 2026, in Mihir Prakash Bajoria v. Vidushi Jain Bajoria, that this is an exceptional remedy requiring real oppression or vexatiousness and a risk of irreparable harm, not merely the possibility that the eventual foreign decree will fail India's recognition test. An injunction is particularly unlikely while the foreign case is still at an early stage.

Does a foreign court's custody order bind an Indian court?

No, not automatically. India has not joined the 1980 Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, and Indian courts treat the welfare of the child as the controlling standard in any proceeding brought here, weighing a foreign custody order as a relevant factor rather than as something that must be followed. A parent involved in a cross-border custody dispute should pursue the Indian proceeding as the operative one rather than relying on the foreign order alone.

Do I need to go to an Indian court to confirm my foreign divorce, or can I just rely on the decree?

If the decree clearly passes the Narasimha Rao test, no separate Indian proceeding is legally required, though many practitioners still recommend a precautionary declaration. If the decree is doubtful or disputed by the other spouse, a declaratory suit before the Family Court having jurisdiction, which has exclusive authority over questions of marital status under Section 7 of the Family Courts Act, 1984, is the practical way to obtain a binding answer.

Is India likely to join the Hague Convention on Child Abduction soon?

Nothing in the public record through October 2026 points to an imminent accession. The Law Commission has recommended it and earlier draft bills have been discussed, but no legislation or official notification has followed, and the government's past stated position has been against joining without a supporting domestic law. Any claim that accession is close should be treated with caution until an official step is actually taken.
