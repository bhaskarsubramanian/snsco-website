Succession Under Dayabhaga Law: What Still Makes Bengali Hindu Families Different

Why West Bengal never had a Mitakshara coparcenary, what the 2005 amendment actually changed there, and probate before the Calcutta High Court today

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 6 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Most writing on Hindu succession in India is written as though the Mitakshara school, with its coparcenary, its birthright by survivorship, and the 2005 amendment that gave daughters an equal share in that coparcenary, is simply the law. For a Bengali Hindu family, and for any family whose property or succession is governed by the Dayabhaga school historically followed in West Bengal, that account is incomplete in ways that matter in practice, in how a father's property actually passes on his death, in what a 2005 amendment drafted for Mitakshara families changes or does not change for a Dayabhaga one, and in a probate requirement before the Calcutta High Court that was, until very recently, stricter than almost anywhere else in India and has just been removed.

Key points

  • Under Dayabhaga law, historically followed in West Bengal, a son acquires no right by birth in his father's property; the father remains absolute owner during his lifetime, and the son's right arises only on the father's death, by inheritance, not by survivorship as under Mitakshara law.
  • Because Dayabhaga never recognised survivorship, a Dayabhaga family's joint or ancestral property does not pass automatically to surviving male members on a death the way Mitakshara coparcenary property did before 2005; it has always devolved by succession under the ordinary heirship rules, which is why the Hindu Succession Act, 1956's general succession provisions, not its coparcenary provisions, have always been the operative ones for Dayabhaga families.
  • The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 rewrote Section 6 of the 1956 Act to give daughters an equal share in Mitakshara coparcenary property by birth; because Dayabhaga recognises no coparcenary of that kind, the 2005 amendment's central change has limited direct application to a Dayabhaga family's joint property, though the Act's general succession rules, which already applied to such families, are unaffected by this distinction.
  • Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925, which made probate or letters of administration compulsory for a Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain will falling within the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of the Calcutta, Madras or Bombay High Courts, has been omitted with effect from 20 December 2025 by the Repealing and Amending Act, 2025; probate for such a will is no longer a compulsory precondition to an executor or legatee enforcing rights under it, though obtaining probate remains advisable for practical reasons discussed below.
  • The West Bengal Thika Tenancy (Acquisition and Regulation) Act, 2001 and its predecessor create a separate statutory tenancy and a dedicated Thika Controller for a recognised category of tenanted land in Kolkata, and courts have held that neither the Thika Controller nor the Kolkata Municipal Corporation has jurisdiction to decide a genuine dispute about who holds title; that question belongs to a civil court.
  • A partition suit remains the standard route to resolve fragmented co-ownership among multiple Dayabhaga heirs where family arrangement fails, and is filed before the ordinary civil court or, within the Calcutta High Court's original civil jurisdiction, on the original side, subject to the court's pecuniary jurisdiction.

A school of law that most succession writing simply skips

India has two principal schools of classical Hindu law governing joint family property and succession, Mitakshara and Dayabhaga. Mitakshara applies across most of the country. Dayabhaga applies in West Bengal and, with local variations, in Assam and parts of Odisha. Almost every general explanation of Hindu succession written for a national audience, including coverage of the landmark cases on coparcenary and the 2005 amendment, describes the Mitakshara position and treats it as the default. For a Bengali Hindu family, that default is not the law that actually governs how a father's flat in south Kolkata, or a family's ancestral house in a mofussil district, passes to his children.

This matters for reasons well beyond academic classification. Whether a son has any claim to property while his father is alive, whether that property can be attached by a son's creditors during the father's lifetime, whether siblings become automatic joint owners on a father's death or must each establish a specific inherited share, and how a 2005 amendment aimed at Mitakshara coparcenary interacts with a Dayabhaga family's joint property, all turn on getting the underlying school right. Getting it wrong, assuming a Bengali Hindu family's ancestral house works the same way a Mitakshara coparcenary in Uttar Pradesh or Maharashtra would, is a common and avoidable source of error in advice given to such families and in pleadings drafted for them.

This article sets out where Dayabhaga genuinely differs from Mitakshara, what the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 and its 2005 amendment actually changed and did not change for a Dayabhaga family, the probate position before the Calcutta High Court following a major 2025 reform, and the partition and title problems that recur in practice for Bengali Hindu families holding property jointly. It builds on, without repeating, this firm's general articles on intestate succession under Hindu law and on title and possession disputes, which readers should consult for the rules that apply uniformly across both schools.

A practical complication worth flagging at the outset is that which school governs a given family is not always as obvious as geography suggests. Dayabhaga has historically governed Bengali Hindu families settled in West Bengal and, with local variation, families in Assam and parts of Odisha, but a family that migrated out of Bengal generations ago, or a family that settled in Kolkata from elsewhere in India and never adopted Bengali custom, can end up disputing, as a genuine question of fact, which school actually governs its property. This is seldom the central issue in a straightforward estate, but it surfaces often enough in contested partition and succession litigation involving families with a mixed or migrated history that it should be checked rather than assumed from surname, mother tongue or current address.

The core difference: no right by birth, because there is no survivorship

Under Mitakshara law, a son acquires an interest in ancestral, coparcenary property the moment he is born. This is traditionally described as an unobstructed heritage, unobstructed because it vests without anything else needing to happen, and it is why Mitakshara coparcenary property is said to pass by survivorship: when a coparcener dies, his interest does not descend to his heirs in the ordinary sense but accrues to the surviving coparceners, because they already held an interest alongside him from the moment of their own birth.

Dayabhaga rejects this starting point entirely. Under Dayabhaga, a son has no interest whatsoever in his father's property, ancestral or otherwise, while the father is alive. The father is the absolute owner of the property during his lifetime and may deal with it, sell it, gift it or otherwise dispose of it, without needing his sons' consent, in a way a Mitakshara father holding coparcenary property could not do as freely with his sons' birthright share. The sons' claim arises for the first time on the father's death, and it arises by inheritance, succession to a deceased owner's estate, not by survivorship between co-owners who already held their shares.

This single difference explains almost everything else that follows. A Dayabhaga family's so-called joint family property, property that has descended through the family and is held jointly by several relatives, is joint only in the sense that several people have inherited defined, though often unpartitioned, shares in it following one or more deaths. It has never been joint in the Mitakshara sense of several people holding an interest from birth in property that automatically redistributes itself among survivors each time one of them dies. Dayabhaga has only ever recognised one mode by which an interest in family property passes from one generation to the next: succession on death, governed, since 1956, by the Hindu Succession Act's general rules rather than by any coparcenary-specific provision.

Daughters, widows, and what changed in 1956

Because Dayabhaga never tied inheritance to survivorship among male coparceners in the Mitakshara sense, its classical texts recognised, even before 1956, a wider circle of heirs than Mitakshara did in the absence of sons, including daughters and widows inheriting in defined circumstances where no closer male heir existed, though in a more limited and conditional form than the equal treatment the law provides today. Mitakshara succession before 1956, by contrast, operated on a strict survivorship basis among male coparceners that left daughters and widows with little beyond a right of maintenance in most situations where a son or other male coparcener survived.

The Hindu Succession Act, 1956 replaced both classical positions with a single statutory scheme of succession applicable to all Hindus regardless of school, Mitakshara or Dayabhaga alike, for property that is not coparcenary property. Section 8 of the Act lays down the order of succession for a Hindu male dying intestate, starting with Class I heirs, which includes sons, daughters, the widow and the mother in equal measure for property falling within the section. Because Dayabhaga property was never governed by a survivorship rule to begin with, Section 8's general succession scheme was, from 1956 onward, already the operative rule for the overwhelming bulk of what a Dayabhaga family would call its ancestral or joint property, there being no separate survivorship-based coparcenary carve-out comparable to the one Section 6 then created for Mitakshara families.

This is the point most general explanations of the 1956 Act miss when they are written with only the Mitakshara position in view. For a Mitakshara family, the 1956 Act, as originally enacted, still preserved the father's coparcenary interest passing by survivorship rather than under the Act's general succession rules, which is precisely what left daughters excluded from an equal share in that property until the 2005 amendment intervened. For a Dayabhaga family, there was no comparable survivorship carve-out to preserve. A Dayabhaga daughter inheriting under Section 8 in 1956 was, in that specific sense, already closer to the position a Mitakshara daughter would not reach until the 2005 amendment, some fifty years later.

What the 2005 amendment changed, and did not change, for a Dayabhaga family

The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 rewrote Section 6 of the 1956 Act to make a daughter a coparcener by birth in Mitakshara coparcenary property, on the same footing as a son, and to provide that on a death after the amendment's commencement, 9 September 2005, the deceased coparcener's interest devolves by testamentary or intestate succession rather than by survivorship, with the coparcenary property deemed to have been notionally partitioned immediately before the death for the purpose of working out that interest. This is, rightly, treated as a landmark reform, and it is the reform most commonly cited when the subject of Hindu daughters' property rights comes up.

Section 6, both before and after the 2005 amendment, is a provision about Mitakshara coparcenary property specifically. Dayabhaga law does not recognise a coparcenary of the kind Section 6 addresses, because, as explained above, Dayabhaga never allowed an interest in family property to vest by birth or to pass by survivorship in the first place. The practical consequence is that the 2005 amendment's central mechanism, converting a son-only birthright into a son-and-daughter birthright in coparcenary property, has no direct application to a family whose joint property was never held on a coparcenary, survivorship basis to begin with. There is nothing of that kind in a Dayabhaga family's holdings for the amendment to operate on.

This should not be read to mean the 2005 amendment is irrelevant to West Bengal, or that Dayabhaga daughters are somehow worse off. The opposite is closer to the truth: because Dayabhaga succession, as explained above, was already succession-based rather than survivorship-based under Section 8 from 1956 onward, a Dayabhaga daughter's position relative to her brothers in inherited property had already been on a materially more equal footing for decades before 2005, in a way the 2005 amendment was specifically needed to achieve for Mitakshara families. The amendment's practical significance for a Bengali Hindu family therefore tends to arise less from any change to the family's own Dayabhaga-governed property and more where the family also holds property, through marriage, business association or otherwise, that happens to be governed by Mitakshara law, or in mixed families whose members have migrated between regions and whose property is debated between the two schools on the facts. Which school in fact governs a particular family's property, including whether a family has, through long settlement outside Bengal or otherwise, come to be treated as governed by Mitakshara custom, is occasionally a genuinely contested question of fact and should not be assumed from the family's surname or current address alone.

Probate before the Calcutta High Court: a rule that just changed

Until very recently, West Bengal, together with the areas falling within the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of the Calcutta High Court, sat under a probate rule stricter than almost anywhere else in India. Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 barred an executor or a legatee from establishing, in any court, a right claimed under a will until a competent court had granted probate of that will, or letters of administration with the will annexed, where the section applied. By virtue of Section 57 and the territorial scope the Act attached to it, this compulsory probate requirement extended specifically to wills made by Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains falling within the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of the High Courts at Calcutta, Madras and Bombay, or concerning immovable property situated there, a rule the Supreme Court had confirmed does not extend to a will made, or property situated, outside those territorial limits.

This made probate practice before the Calcutta High Court's original side a routine, almost unavoidable, part of administering a deceased Bengali Hindu's estate wherever the will or the property fell within that jurisdiction, in sharp contrast to most of India, where probate has long been optional for a Hindu will and is sought mainly where a bank, a land registry or a buyer insists on it as a practical matter rather than because the law demands it.

That changed on 20 December 2025. The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 omitted Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act, along with consequential changes to the sections that referred to it, bringing the compulsory-probate position for Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh and Jain wills within the Calcutta, Madras and Bombay original jurisdictions into line with the optional-probate position that has long applied in the rest of the country. As matters stand today, an executor or legatee under such a will is no longer legally required to obtain probate before enforcing rights under it, a reform discussed in more general terms in this firm's existing article on probate no longer being mandatory following the repeal of Section 213.

This does not mean probate has become pointless for a Bengali Hindu family's will. Obtaining probate still produces a court order conclusively establishing the will's validity and the executor's authority, which banks, the land registry, and buyers in a subsequent sale will often still expect as a practical matter even where the law no longer compels it, and which forecloses, far more effectively than an unprobated will can, a later challenge to the will's genuineness by a disappointed relative. Families should expect their advisors to discuss, on the facts of each estate, whether probate remains worth obtaining voluntarily even now that it is not compulsory, rather than assuming the repeal removes any reason to seek it.

How Calcutta High Court probate actually proceeds

Probate and letters of administration applications falling within the Calcutta High Court's jurisdiction are handled on the court's original side under its Testamentary and Intestate jurisdiction, following procedure set out in Chapter XXXV of the Original Side Rules read with the Indian Succession Act, 1925. An application is made by petition, supported by the original will and the death certificate, and the court directs citation, notice to the persons who would otherwise be entitled to the estate, so that anyone with an objection has an opportunity to come forward.

A person who wishes to oppose the grant may lodge a caveat at the Registry on the prescribed form, and must ordinarily follow it with a supporting affidavit disclosing both the caveator's own interest in the estate and the specific grounds of objection, generally within a short period the Rules fix, extendable by the court for sufficient reason. Once a caveat with a proper supporting affidavit is on record, the matter typically proceeds as a contested testamentary suit rather than a routine, unopposed grant, with the dispute tried on evidence much as an ordinary civil suit would be. Where no caveat is lodged, or a lodged caveat is not supported by the required affidavit within time, the Registry can note that position and the grant can proceed on an unopposed basis.

A separate jurisdictional point for a family with property outside West Bengal is that a judge sitting on the Calcutta High Court's original side under the Indian Succession Act does not function, for this purpose, as a general principal civil court of unlimited original jurisdiction; the court's own Rules limit probate to property within the court's jurisdiction, and a family with assets spread across more than one State should expect to coordinate, where necessary, a grant obtained in one jurisdiction being recognised or re-sealed for use in respect of assets situated elsewhere, rather than assuming a single Calcutta grant automatically covers property located outside the court's own jurisdiction.

Partition suits and the fragmented title problem

Because Dayabhaga succession has always proceeded by inheritance to defined, if often unpartitioned, shares rather than by survivorship, a Bengali Hindu family's jointly held property very commonly ends up owned by a large and growing number of co-owners across generations, siblings who each inherited a fractional share from a parent, their children who in turn inherited fractional shares of those fractional shares, and so on, often without anyone having formally divided the physical property by metes and bounds at any point along the way. This is not a defect in Dayabhaga law as such; it is the predictable long-run consequence of a system that allocates defined inheritance shares on every death rather than periodically consolidating ownership through survivorship the way Mitakshara coparcenary historically did until it, too, was converted to a succession-based scheme by the 2005 amendment.

The standard remedy where family arrangement fails is a partition suit, filed by any co-owner against the others, asking the civil court to divide the property by metes and bounds where that is practicable, or to order a sale and division of the proceeds where physical division is not practicable, typically in the case of a single building or a small plot that cannot sensibly be split among many owners. Within Kolkata and the area of the Calcutta High Court's ordinary original civil jurisdiction, such a suit may fall on the court's original side depending on the property's value and location; outside that area, it proceeds before the ordinary civil court having jurisdiction over the property, on the same general principles that govern partition suits anywhere else in India.

A recurring complication specific to older Kolkata properties is thika tenancy. The West Bengal Thika Tenancy (Acquisition and Regulation) Act, 2001, continuing a regime that traces back to the Calcutta Thika Tenancy Act, 1949 and its 1981 successor, creates a distinct statutory tenancy for structures built on another's land under specified conditions, administered through a dedicated Thika Controller rather than through the ordinary civil court or the Kolkata Municipal Corporation. Courts have held that the Thika Controller's jurisdiction is confined to matters the Act itself commits to that forum, and that a genuine dispute over who actually holds title, as opposed to a dispute over thika tenancy status, remains a question for a civil court rather than one the Thika Controller or the municipal corporation can decide. A family untangling co-ownership in an older Kolkata property should establish, before filing anything, whether a thika tenancy question is genuinely in the mix, since that materially affects which forum has jurisdiction over which part of the dispute.

Common title problems in practice

The most frequent title problem this firm encounters in Dayabhaga-governed Kolkata properties is not a dispute about who the heirs are in principle, that is usually clear enough from the family tree, but a chain of unrecorded or informally recorded transfers across generations: a share sold by one sibling to another without a registered deed, a widow's limited interest under pre-1956 law never formally clarified after the 1956 Act converted such interests into full ownership, or a family settlement acted upon for decades without ever being reduced to a registered instrument. Each of these creates a gap between who the family, in practice, treats as the owner of a given portion and who the registered record actually shows, a gap that surfaces, often expensively, the moment a sale, a mortgage or an inheritance requires a clean title to be shown.

A second recurring problem is the mismatch between a mutation or municipal assessment record, which tends to lag behind actual inheritance by years or decades, and the true current ownership under the succession rules described above. As with title disputes generally, a mutation entry is an administrative record for tax and assessment purposes and does not itself establish or defeat title; where a dispute arises, the registered chain of title and the applicable succession rules, not the mutation register, determine who actually owns what share.

For a family facing either problem, the practical starting point is the same: establish the family tree and the dates of each relevant death with supporting documents, identify every transfer in the chain and whether each was properly registered, and treat an informal family understanding about who owns what, however long-standing and however amicable, as something to be formalised through a registered deed or a court-recorded partition rather than left as an understanding. Families that do this while relations remain cordial generally spend a fraction of what the same exercise costs once a dispute has already hardened between heirs who no longer agree.

Wills and testamentary freedom: another consequence of absolute ownership

A further, less-discussed consequence of a Dayabhaga father being the absolute owner of his property during his lifetime is that he has always had wide freedom to dispose of that property by will, exactly as any other absolute owner would, without the complication a Mitakshara father historically faced of having to will away only his own undivided coparcenary interest while his sons' birthright share in the same property remained untouched by the will. A Dayabhaga will covering the testator's property is, in this sense, a more straightforward instrument than a will attempting to deal with Mitakshara coparcenary property often was before 2005, precisely because there was no competing birthright share for the will to run up against.

This does not mean a Dayabhaga will is immune from challenge. The ordinary grounds on which any will can be contested, lack of testamentary capacity, undue influence, suspicious circumstances surrounding execution, or a forged or substituted document, apply equally here, and the removal of compulsory probate under Section 213 makes it, if anything, more important for a family to consider obtaining probate voluntarily where there is any realistic prospect of a dispute among the heirs, since an unprobated will, now valid to found a claim without a grant, is correspondingly easier for a disappointed relative to attack collaterally in some other proceeding without the will's validity having first been conclusively tested.

Where a Dayabhaga family member living or working abroad executes a will dealing with Indian property, the general rules on execution, attestation and registration that apply to any Indian will apply here too, and this firm's article on succession, wills, probate and inheritance for NRIs after the 2025 reform covers that ground, including the practical registration and safekeeping steps an NRI testator should take, in more detail than is repeated here.

Family settlements, and why an oral understanding stops being enough

Given how often Dayabhaga succession leaves a family holding a property in fragmented, multi-generational shares, many families resolve the practical allocation of who uses which portion, or who receives which asset, through an informal family settlement rather than a formal partition suit. Where every affected family member genuinely agrees and the settlement does no more than record an arrangement without itself operating as the instrument that transfers or extinguishes an interest in immovable property, courts have long recognised that a family settlement can be acted upon without registration, on the footing that it is a recognition of existing rights rather than a fresh transfer.

That exception has limits that matter in practice, and families routinely misjudge where those limits sit. A document that does more than record a pre-existing understanding, one that itself creates, declares, assigns or extinguishes a right, title or interest in immovable property worth more than a token amount, falls within Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908 and requires registration in the same way any other instrument affecting immovable property does; an unregistered document of that kind faces the same evidentiary bar under Section 49 discussed in this firm's related article on managing Indian property from abroad. A family that has genuinely settled how a property will be divided, and intends the settlement itself to be the operative document dividing it, should treat registration as the safer course rather than relying on an exception whose boundary is fact-sensitive and has been the subject of considerable litigation in its own right.

The practical lesson for a Bengali Hindu family untangling several generations of Dayabhaga inheritance is to resolve, explicitly and in writing, whether a given family arrangement is meant merely to record shares that already exist under the succession rules, in which case an unregistered memorandum may suffice, or is meant to change who holds what, in which case registration should not be skipped merely because the parties are related and on good terms with each other at the time.

Succession certificates for bank accounts, shares and other moveable assets

Where a Dayabhaga family member dies intestate, or leaves a will that does not need to go through probate, the heirs still often need a separate document to deal with the deceased's bank accounts, shares, fixed deposits and similar moveable assets, since a bank or a company's registrar will rarely release these on the strength of a family tree alone. The relevant instrument is a succession certificate under Part X of the Indian Succession Act, 1925, obtained by petition to the District Judge under Section 372, which must set out the deceased's time and place of death, the relatives entitled to succeed, and a list of the specific debts and investments, bank accounts, shares, insurance claims and the like, for which the certificate is sought.

A succession certificate serves a narrower purpose than probate and operates differently from it. It does not determine title to immovable property at all, and Section 370, as it stood before the 2025 reform, barred a succession certificate from being granted in respect of any debt or security to which Sections 212 or 213 applied. The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 also amended Section 370 itself as a consequential change, removing its cross-reference to the now-omitted Section 213, so the bar as it currently reads should be checked against the amended statutory text rather than assumed to still name Section 213. For the great majority of Dayabhaga family estates consisting mainly of bank balances, shares and similar assets rather than a will requiring probate, the succession certificate route under Section 372 remains the practical mechanism regardless of the recent change to Section 213, and heirs should expect to use it alongside, not instead of, whatever title documentation is needed for any immovable property in the estate.

The certificate, once granted, is effective across India under Section 380 and the holder is both empowered and, under Section 375, protected to deal with the debts and securities it covers, with any genuinely entitled person left with a claim for compensation against the certificate holder rather than against the bank or company that paid out on the strength of the certificate in good faith. This is a point of practical comfort for banks and registrars asked to release funds, and it is also why a bank will generally insist on seeing a certificate rather than releasing funds on the heirs' own representations, however clear the family's account of who is entitled appears to be.

Dayabhaga is not a historical curiosity confined to textbooks on Hindu law. It is the system that, in practice, still shapes how succession actually works for a very large number of Bengali Hindu families, in ways that a generic, Mitakshara-framed account of Indian succession law simply does not capture. A son's lack of any right by birth, a family's joint property having always devolved by inheritance rather than survivorship, the 2005 amendment's limited direct bite on a Dayabhaga family's own holdings, and a probate requirement before the Calcutta High Court that has just been removed after decades in force, are all points where the general rule taught nationally and the rule that actually governs a specific Bengali Hindu family's property can diverge.

None of this is reason for a family to treat its succession planning as settled simply because it is Dayabhaga and therefore, in some loose sense, already more equal than Mitakshara was before 2005. The fragmented, multi-generational co-ownership that Dayabhaga succession tends to produce over time is its own source of disputes, and the recent change to the probate rule changes what is legally required without changing what is practically wise. A family that puts its title chain, its wills and, where several heirs already hold undivided shares, a clear partition or family settlement in order now generally avoids a far more expensive reckoning among the next generation of heirs.

A will, a registered family settlement, or a timely partition suit each cost comparatively little while the people who actually hold the relevant knowledge, who inherited what, when, and on what understanding, are still alive to confirm it. The same questions, left unresolved into a third or fourth generation of heirs who never knew the original facts firsthand, routinely take years of litigation and far greater expense to untangle.

Frequently Asked Questions

My father is still alive. Can I claim a share in our ancestral Kolkata house now, the way a son in a Mitakshara family might?

No. Under Dayabhaga law, which has historically governed Bengali Hindu families, a son acquires no interest in his father's property, ancestral or otherwise, while the father is alive. The father remains absolute owner during his lifetime and can deal with the property as he chooses. Your claim as a son arises only on his death, by inheritance under the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, not by any right existing from birth.

Does the 2005 Hindu Succession Amendment, which gave daughters equal coparcenary rights, apply to our Dayabhaga family's property in West Bengal?

The amendment's specific mechanism rewrote Section 6 of the 1956 Act, which deals with Mitakshara coparcenary property. Dayabhaga does not recognise a coparcenary of that kind, since property was never held by survivorship in the first place, so the amendment has no direct application to that distinction for a Dayabhaga family's own holdings. This does not disadvantage Dayabhaga daughters; their position under the Act's general succession rules, which have applied since 1956, was already closer to equal treatment with sons than a Mitakshara daughter's position was before 2005.

Do I still need probate for my father's will if he lived in Kolkata and we are Hindus?

Not as a strict legal precondition any longer. Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925, which made probate compulsory for such wills within the Calcutta High Court's ordinary original civil jurisdiction, was omitted with effect from 20 December 2025. Probate is no longer legally mandatory before an executor or legatee can enforce rights under the will. It can still be worth obtaining voluntarily, since banks, the land registry and buyers in a later sale often still expect it as a practical matter, and it forecloses challenges to the will far more effectively than an unprobated will does.

Several of us inherited fractional shares in our grandfather's house over three generations. What is the actual remedy to sort this out?

A partition suit, filed by any co-owner against the others, asking the court to divide the property physically where that is practicable or to order a sale and division of the proceeds where it is not. This is the standard route for the fragmented, multi-generational co-ownership that Dayabhaga succession, built on inheritance rather than survivorship, commonly produces over time. Before filing, establish the family tree, the dates of each death in the chain, and whether any earlier transfers between family members were properly registered, since gaps in that chain are usually the real obstacle to a clean resolution.

Our Kolkata property has been described as a thika tenancy. Does that change where we file our dispute?

It can. The West Bengal Thika Tenancy (Acquisition and Regulation) Act, 2001 creates a separate statutory tenancy for qualifying structures, administered by a dedicated Thika Controller rather than the ordinary civil court or the Kolkata Municipal Corporation. Courts have held that neither the Thika Controller nor the Corporation can decide a genuine dispute over who holds title, as distinct from a dispute over thika tenancy status itself; title disputes remain for a civil court. Establish whether thika tenancy status is genuinely in issue before deciding which forum to approach.

A mutation record shows my uncle as the owner of a share my father always said was his. Does the mutation entry settle the question?

No. A mutation entry is an administrative record maintained for revenue and tax assessment purposes and does not by itself create, extinguish or conclusively prove title. Where ownership of a share is genuinely disputed, the registered chain of title and the applicable succession rules determine who actually owns it, not the mutation register, which may simply not have been updated to reflect an inheritance or transfer that in fact took place.

Is Dayabhaga law still relevant today, or has the Hindu Succession Act made the distinction between schools meaningless?

The distinction still matters wherever it governs how a family's joint or ancestral property is treated, principally on the question of whether property passed by survivorship among coparceners, as Mitakshara provided before 2005, or always by inheritance, as Dayabhaga has always provided. For straightforward intestate succession to a Hindu male's separate or self-acquired property, the Hindu Succession Act's general rules under Section 8 apply uniformly regardless of school, so the distinction matters most for joint and ancestral property questions and for the historical analysis of how a family's current holdings came to be structured.
