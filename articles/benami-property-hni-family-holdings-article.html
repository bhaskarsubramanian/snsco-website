Benami Property and HNI Family Holdings: What the Prohibition Act Exempts and Confiscates

A family that puts a flat in a spouse's name, lets a son hold shares as a nominee, or keeps ancestral land in the karta's name assumes none of this is anyone else's business. The Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act disagrees with parts of that assumption, and the law on how far it reaches is, as things stand today, genuinely unsettled.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 15 November 2026 · Law stated as on 15 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Most high-net-worth families hold at least one asset the way their grandparents held it: a flat bought in a wife's name because the husband already had enough property on his own books, agricultural land left in the karta's name on behalf of the whole joint family, or a bank locker and a handful of shares that everyone in the family knows belong to the father even though a son's name sits on the folio. None of this was designed to hide anything from anyone. It is simply how property was arranged before anyone thought to ask whether the arrangement had a name in law. It does. The Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988, as amended with effect from 1 November 2016, gives that arrangement a name, a set of conditions under which it is perfectly lawful, and a set of consequences, confiscation without compensation and prosecution among them, when it is not. The difficult part for most families is that the line between the lawful version and the punishable one turns on documentation most households never thought to keep.

Key points

  • Section 2(9) of the Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988 defines benami transaction broadly, but Section 2(9)(A) carves out four situations many HNI families already rely on without recognising that they are relying on a statutory exemption.
  • Property bought in a spouse's or child's name is not benami only if the consideration is traceable to the purchaser's known, declared sources; the exemption fails the moment the funding trail cannot be shown from bank records, returns or disclosed assets.
  • A karta or HUF member holding property for the benefit of the joint family, and a brother, sister or lineal ascendant or descendant named as a joint owner where the money came from a known source, are separately exempted, each on its own conditions.
  • A benami transaction is liable to confiscation by the government without any compensation under Section 5, and Section 53 carries rigorous imprisonment of one to seven years together with a fine that can run up to 25% of the property's fair market value.
  • The Supreme Court's 2022 ruling in Ganpati Dealcom, which held the 2016 amendment could not reach transactions entered into before 25 October 2016 and struck down part of the unamended Act as unconstitutional, was recalled by the Court itself on 18 October 2024 and sent back for fresh hearing before a different bench.
  • A Supreme Court order reported in November 2025 went further and held that the liberty the 2024 recall order gave to reopen cases already decided on the strength of the 2022 ruling was itself wrongly granted, since a later overruling is not a ground to seek review of an earlier, final order. On 8 May 2026, a two-judge Bench in Manjula and Others v. D.A. Srinivas, 2026 INSC 465, held that the 2016 amendment's confiscation machinery is a civil consequence that can reach pre-2016 transactions even though the Act's criminal provisions remain prospective only, the most recent Supreme Court word on the point while the restored Ganpati Dealcom appeal remains pending before a three-judge Bench.
  • Section 4 of the Act bars a benamidar from suing to recover benami property from the person who actually benefits from it, which means a family member who agreed informally to hold an asset for another often has no civil remedy if the arrangement later turns sour.
  • None of the statutory exemptions protect an arrangement whose real purpose is to defeat a creditor, understate assets to a tax authority, or conceal the true owner from a regulator; the exemption tracks the source of the money and the relationship, not the family's intentions.

How an Ordinary Family Arrangement Becomes a Benami Question

The Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act was passed in 1988 to deal with a narrow mischief: a person financing the purchase of property while putting someone else's name on the title, usually to hide black money, defeat a creditor, or escape a tax liability. For nearly three decades the Act had almost no working machinery, no adjudicating authority, no attachment procedure and a penalty clause that a 1986 report of the Law Commission itself had described as toothless. The Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Amendment Act, 2016, which took effect on 1 November 2016, rebuilt the Act from the definitions up: a sharper description of what counts as a benami transaction, a four-tier enforcement structure borrowed loosely from the money-laundering statute, and a penalty regime with real teeth.

For an ordinary salaried household, the rebuilt Act rarely comes up. For a family that has accumulated property across two or three generations, often buying land or flats in whichever family member's name happened to be convenient at the time, structured a joint family holding through a karta, or routed shares and mutual fund folios through a spouse's or an adult child's name for reasons that had nothing to do with concealment, the rebuilt Act is now a live question whether the family has ever thought about it or not. An income-tax search, a loan default that triggers a creditor's inquiry into the family's assets, or simply a succession dispute between siblings can each put a decades-old holding arrangement in front of an Initiating Officer who has never met the family and has no reason to assume good faith.

The honest starting point for any family in this position is that the Act does not ask why a holding arrangement was set up. It asks two narrower questions: who actually paid for the property, and does the way the title is held fall within one of the specific situations Parliament chose to exempt. A family that can answer both questions comfortably has very little to worry about. A family that has never had to answer them before, because nobody ever asked, is the family this piece is written for.

The Definition in Section 2(9) and Why It Is Wider Than Most People Assume

Section 2(9) of the Act, as it stands after the 2016 amendment, defines a benami transaction in four limbs. The first and most familiar, at sub-clause (A), covers a transaction where property is transferred to, or held by, one person, while the consideration for it has been provided, or is to be provided, by another person, and the property is held for the immediate or future benefit, direct or indirect, of the person who provided the consideration. The remaining three limbs, at sub-clauses (B) to (D), reach transactions carried out or made in a fictitious name, transactions where the owner of the property is not aware of, or denies knowledge of, the ownership, and transactions where the person providing the consideration is not traceable or is fictitious.

It is sub-clause (A) that governs almost every genuine family holding arrangement, and it is sub-clause (A) alone that carries the statutory exceptions this piece spends most of its attention on. The exceptions at the proviso to Section 2(9)(A) do not apply to sub-clauses (B), (C) or (D); an outright fictitious-name transaction or one where the recorded owner denies knowing anything about it gets no benefit of the family exemptions at all, however it is dressed up.

A second feature of the definition that families tend to underestimate is how little it cares about intention at the point of enforcement. The question an Initiating Officer asks is not whether the family meant any harm when the arrangement was set up thirty years ago. It is whether the arrangement, examined today against the statutory text, falls inside or outside an exemption. A family that genuinely never intended to hide anything can still find itself holding a benami asset in the eyes of the Act if it cannot produce the paperwork the exemption requires.

Property in a Spouse's or a Child's Name

The exception families lean on most often is the one covering property held in the name of a spouse or a child, where the consideration for that property has come from known sources of the person providing it. The exemption does not ask the family to prove the arrangement was innocent in some moral sense. It asks for a specific, traceable fact: that the money used to buy the property can be shown to have come from income, savings or assets the purchaser has actually declared, whether in tax returns, audited accounts, or disclosed wealth records, rather than from an unexplained or undisclosed source.

This is where a surprising number of otherwise careful families run into difficulty. A property bought twenty years ago in a wife's name out of her husband's business income is squarely within the exemption if the funding trail from the husband's declared income to the purchase can still be shown. The same property becomes much harder to defend if the original payment was made in cash that was never reflected in anyone's returns, or if the family simply cannot locate the decades-old bank statements and sale consideration proof that would establish the trail today. The exemption is only as strong as the family's own record-keeping, and record-keeping from thirty years ago is rarely as complete as record-keeping done with this provision in mind.

A related point advisers to HNI families raise repeatedly is that the spouse-or-child exemption does not extend, on its own wording, to a daughter-in-law, a son-in-law, or a grandchild. A family that has, for whatever reason, put a significant asset in the name of a daughter-in-law rather than a son or a daughter should not assume the same exemption automatically covers that holding; the relationship the proviso names is narrower than the relationship an Indian family would ordinarily describe as close.

The Karta and the Hindu Undivided Family

Joint family property forms the second limb of the exception. It covers property held by a karta, or by a member of a Hindu undivided family, where the property is held for his or her own benefit or for the benefit of other members of the family, and the consideration for it has come from known sources of the Hindu undivided family. This is the exemption that protects the ordinary pattern of joint family property, agricultural land, an ancestral house, or shares bought with HUF funds, where title sits in the karta's individual name because that is how joint family property has traditionally been recorded, while the beneficial interest is understood by everyone in the family to belong to the HUF as a whole.

The exemption turns on two separate facts that a family should be able to establish independently of each other: first, that the property is genuinely held for the family's benefit rather than the karta's personal benefit alone, and second, that the funds used to acquire it actually belonged to the HUF, typically traceable to an HUF bank account, HUF income already assessed to tax in the HUF's own hands, or ancestral property that has been treated consistently as joint family property across generations. A family that has never formally constituted or operated an HUF account, and has simply treated whatever the patriarch earned as family money without separating it from his personal income, will usually find this exemption harder to establish than a family that has kept the HUF's finances distinct.

Brother, Sister and Lineal Relatives as Joint Owners

Siblings and lineal relatives supply the third limb, and it is the one families most often misread. It covers property held in the name of a brother, sister, or lineal ascendant or descendant, where that person is shown as a joint owner, not the sole recorded owner, and the consideration has come from known sources of the person actually claiming the benefit. The condition of joint ownership is what separates this exception from the spouse-or-child exception discussed above, and families frequently miss it: putting a property entirely in a brother's or a parent's name, with no other family member named as a co-owner on the title or in any document connected with the property, falls outside this particular exemption even if every other condition is satisfied.

A family that wants the protection of this exception, for instance where an elder brother holds a property jointly with a younger sibling who actually paid for it, or where a married daughter is named as a joint owner alongside her father on a property her father financed, needs the joint ownership to appear somewhere in the documentary record connected with the property, not merely in the family's own understanding of who the asset really belongs to. The statutory language, as courts and commentators read it, extends to a person shown as a joint owner in any document relating to the property, which gives some flexibility beyond the title deed itself, but a family should not rely on an undocumented joint-ownership understanding to carry the exemption.

The Fiduciary Exception and Where It Does Not Reach

Fiduciary relationships make up the fourth and final exception, covering a person holding property in a fiduciary capacity, the classic examples being a trustee holding trust property, an executor administering an estate, a partner holding partnership property, or a director holding property on behalf of a company. This exception exists because these relationships are already governed by their own body of law, trust law, the law of estates, partnership law and company law, each of which already fixes the fiduciary's duties and the beneficiary's remedies, so the Benami Act does not need to duplicate that regulation by treating every fiduciary holding as a suspect transaction.

The exception is narrower than families sometimes assume once a trust or a company enters a family's structure. A private family trust genuinely administered by its trustees for the named beneficiaries, with the trust's own funds used for any acquisition, sits comfortably within the fiduciary exception. A structure that is called a trust on paper, but where the named trustee is in practice simply holding an asset for an individual family member who provided the funds and retains full control, risks being read for what it actually is rather than what its documents call it, and the fiduciary label alone will not save it if the underlying arrangement looks, in substance, like an ordinary benami holding dressed in trust language.

What None of the Exemptions Cover

Two points apply across all four exemptions and are worth stating plainly because families sometimes read the exemptions as more forgiving than they are. First, every one of the exemptions requires the consideration to have come from a known source. An arrangement that otherwise fits the shape of an exempted relationship, a property in a son's name, a joint holding with a sister, land in the karta's name, still falls outside the exemption if the family cannot show where the money actually came from, whether because it was never declared, was paid in untraceable cash, or simply predates any record the family has kept. Second, none of the exemptions apply at all to the other three limbs of the definition under sub-clauses (B) to (D): a transaction in a fictitious name, or one where the recorded owner denies any knowledge of owning the property, gets no benefit of the family carve-outs regardless of who the parties are.

A related misconception is that the exemptions are a shield against every kind of scrutiny once the relationship fits. They are not. An exemption under Section 2(9)(A) answers the narrow question of whether the transaction counts as benami under this particular Act. It says nothing about whether the same arrangement might separately raise questions under income tax law if the funding itself was never disclosed, under the Foreign Exchange Management Act if the funds crossed a border without the right route, or under company and insolvency law if the asset was moved to defeat a creditor on the eve of a default. A family's property holding can clear the benami exemption and still be vulnerable on one of these other fronts, and advisers who look only at the benami question without checking the others are giving the family half the picture.

Confiscation Under Section 5 and the Machinery That Gets There

Where a transaction is found to be benami and falls outside every exemption, Section 5 of the Act makes the property liable to confiscation by the Central Government, and confiscation under this Act carries no compensation to either the benamidar whose name is on the title or the beneficial owner who actually paid for it. This is a materially harsher consequence than most civil remedies a family might otherwise associate with a property dispute, where a losing party at least retains the value of what it is found to owe; here the asset itself is simply taken by the state.

Getting to a confiscation order involves a specific sequence that the 2016 amendment built into the Act. An Initiating Officer who has reason to believe a person is a benamidar issues a show-cause notice under Section 24(1) and may provisionally attach the property, first on an interim basis and then, after completing the inquiry, through a final provisional attachment, with the Initiating Officer required to refer the matter to the Adjudicating Authority within fifteen days of a final attachment order. The Adjudicating Authority, after hearing the parties, either confirms the attachment and holds the property to be benami, in which case it stands referred for confiscation, or releases it. An order of the Adjudicating Authority can be appealed to the Appellate Tribunal, which since a 2019 restructuring functions through the same Appellate Tribunal set up under the Smugglers and Foreign Exchange Manipulators (Forfeiture of Property) Act, and from the Tribunal a further appeal lies, on a question of law, to the jurisdictional High Court.

A family whose property has been provisionally attached should treat the fifteen-day and subsequent timelines in this chain as genuinely important, not procedural formality. Tribunals have shown themselves willing to quash an attachment where the sequence was not followed correctly, for instance where the Approving Authority's prior approval was obtained before, rather than after, the Section 24(1) notice was actually issued, which several reported decisions treat as a condition precedent the department cannot get around after the fact. Challenging the sequence, rather than only the merits of whether the transaction is benami, is frequently the more immediate line of defence available to a family facing attachment.

Legal representation at the attachment stage matters more than families sometimes assume, because the Adjudicating Authority's order confirming or releasing an attachment is where the factual record for the entire case gets built. A family that treats the show-cause notice casually, assuming the real fight will happen later before the Appellate Tribunal or the High Court, often finds that the documents and explanations it should have placed on record at the Adjudicating Authority stage are far harder to introduce for the first time on appeal, where the forum is reviewing what was already decided rather than hearing the matter afresh.

Prosecution Under Section 53 and the Separate Penalty for False Information

Confiscation of the property is a civil consequence running alongside a separate criminal one. Section 53 makes entering into a benami transaction, as the benamidar, the beneficial owner, or anyone who abets or induces either of them to enter into it, an offence punishable with rigorous imprisonment for a term of not less than one year, which may extend to seven years, together with a fine that can reach twenty-five per cent of the property's fair market value, fair market value itself being defined under Section 2(16) as the price the property would ordinarily fetch if sold in the open market on the date of the transaction.

A separate and often overlooked provision, Section 54, penalises furnishing false information to any authority under the Act, with its own prison term and fine calculated as a percentage of fair market value, distinct from the Section 53 penalty for the underlying transaction itself. A family responding to an inquiry under this Act should treat every representation made to the Initiating Officer with the same care it would bring to a sworn statement, since an inaccurate or incomplete answer given under pressure to resolve the matter quickly can itself become a separate offence layered on top of whatever the underlying benami question turns out to be.

Both offences under the Act are, under Section 50, non-cognizable and bailable, which gives an accused person meaningful procedural protection compared to some other economic offences, but that protection goes to arrest and bail, not to the underlying exposure to imprisonment and fine on conviction, nor to the separate and independent confiscation of the property itself, which proceeds through the adjudicating machinery regardless of how the criminal prosecution, if one is launched at all, eventually turns out.

The Retrospectivity Question: Ganpati Dealcom, Its Recall, and Where It Stands Today

No part of this area of law has moved more than the question of whether the 2016 amendment can reach transactions entered into before it came into force. On 23 August 2022, a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court in Union of India v. Ganpati Dealcom Pvt. Ltd. held that the 2016 amendment could not be applied to confiscate property acquired in a transaction entered into before 25 October 2016, and went further, declaring Section 3(2) of the amended Act manifestly arbitrary and striking down related provisions of the unamended 1988 Act as unconstitutional. For nearly two years, that ruling was treated as settled law, and a family whose arrangement predated the amendment had a straightforward constitutional defence available to it.

On 18 October 2024, in an order reported as 2024 INSC 799, the Supreme Court recalled its own 2022 judgment on a review petition filed by the Union, holding that the constitutional validity of the provisions could not have been properly adjudicated in the absence of an actual contest between parties on that specific question, and sent the matter back for fresh hearing before a different bench. The effect of a recall, as distinct from a mere review dismissal, is that the 2022 judgment is treated as if it had not been delivered at all for the purpose of the issues it addressed, which means the retrospectivity question the 2022 ruling appeared to have settled is, as of today, open again and awaiting a fresh decision.

The position has moved once more since the recall. A Supreme Court order reported by LiveLaw in November 2025, in a matter involving the Union's attempt to reopen an earlier case that had itself been decided on the strength of the liberty the 2024 recall order granted to revisit matters settled under the now-recalled 2022 ruling, held that a later overruling of a precedent is not, by itself, a ground on which an otherwise final order can be reopened, and found that the 2024 order had been wrong to grant that liberty in the first place. Read together, the 2022 ruling, its 2024 recall, and the 2025 order on the scope of that recall leave the central retrospectivity question pending before the Supreme Court on fresh facts, while narrowing how far parties can use the recall itself to reopen matters that had already become final before 18 October 2024.

The question has moved again since the November 2025 order. On 8 May 2026, a two-judge Bench of the Supreme Court in Manjula and Others v. D.A. Srinivas, 2026 INSC 465, held, in the course of deciding a separate succession and benami dispute, that the 2016 amendment's confiscation and attachment machinery is a civil consequence attached to the property rather than a penal consequence on a person, so it is not barred by Article 20(1) of the Constitution and can reach transactions completed before the amendment; the Bench held the Act's penal provisions, by contrast, remain prospective only. Manjula does not itself decide the restored Ganpati Dealcom appeal, which a three-judge Bench has yet to rehear, and commentary is divided on how far a two-judge ruling on this point binds pending that rehearing, but it is, as of today, the most recent word from the Supreme Court on retrospective confiscation, and it points the other way from the recalled 2022 position. For a family with a pre-2016 arrangement facing a benami proceeding today, the honest advice has therefore shifted: the constitutional argument that looked decisive in 2022 is no longer available as settled law, and the most recent Supreme Court authority on confiscation actually points toward retrospective application, while criminal prosecution for a pre-2016 transaction remains unavailable under either ruling. A defence built on retrospectivity needs to be argued on its merits, taking Manjula into account, before whichever forum hears the matter, rather than cited as something the Supreme Court has already decided either way.

Benami Findings Alongside Other Proceedings: Civil Recovery, SARFAESI and Insolvency

Section 4 of the Act bars a suit, claim or action to enforce any right in respect of property held benami against the person in whose favour the property is held, or against any other person, by or on behalf of a person claiming to be the real owner of that property. In practical terms, this means a family member who let a sibling or an in-law hold an asset informally, with everyone in the family understanding whose money bought it, has no civil suit available to recover it if the benamidar later refuses to hand it back or sells it to a third party; the very arrangement the real owner wants a court to enforce is the arrangement the Act treats as having no legal existence worth protecting. Families planning any informal holding arrangement today should treat Section 4 as the clearest possible warning against relying on an understanding that is not backed by one of the statutory exemptions.

A separate line of difficulty has opened up at the intersection of benami attachment and secured lending. Banks enforcing security under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 have increasingly run into properties that are also the subject of a benami attachment, and tribunals examining these cases have had to work out which claim takes priority where the borrower's other, unencumbered assets would in any event be sufficient to satisfy the bank's claim without touching the attached property. A family business that has mortgaged property to a lender, where that same property later becomes the subject of a benami inquiry involving a different family member's name on the title, should expect the SARFAESI and benami proceedings to run in parallel rather than one simply yielding to the other, and should get both addressed together rather than assuming a bank's security interest automatically takes the property outside the Initiating Officer's reach.

The same overlap arises, less often but with higher stakes, where a corporate debtor's assets are under examination in a corporate insolvency resolution process and a benami attachment surfaces against one of those assets. Resolution professionals and committees of creditors dealing with such an asset need the benami position resolved, or at least clearly understood, before building a resolution plan around that asset, since a plan built on the assumption that the asset belongs cleanly to the corporate debtor can unravel if the asset is later confiscated in benami proceedings that were already pending when the plan was approved.

Benami Risk Inside Succession Planning and Family Settlements

Families doing succession planning, whether through a will, a family settlement, or a private trust, run into the benami question most often at the point of documenting how an asset came to be held in a particular family member's name decades ago. A family settlement that redistributes property among siblings works cleanly when each sibling's existing holding is itself free of any benami taint; it becomes considerably more complicated when one sibling's share includes an asset that was only ever held in that sibling's name because an earlier generation put it there using another family member's money, with no exemption clearly established and no joint ownership documented at the time.

A will or a family settlement cannot cure a benami problem simply by describing the asset as belonging to whoever currently holds title to it. If the underlying transaction falls outside every exemption, the asset remains exposed to confiscation regardless of what the family's own succession documents say about who is meant to inherit it, and a family doing succession planning around an asset with an uncertain holding history should get that history examined and, where possible, regularised, before building a will or a settlement around an assumption of clean title that the family has never actually tested.

Where a family is setting up a private trust for the first time and intends to transfer an existing property into it, the transfer itself needs scrutiny from the benami angle, a separate check from the trust and tax angle advisers usually focus on first. A property that was itself acquired decades ago using one family member's money but held in another's name carries its benami exposure into the trust if it is simply transferred in without first resolving whose asset it legally was to transfer.

A Practical Checklist Before an Inquiry, Not After One

The single most useful thing an HNI family can do in this area, well before any notice arrives, is reconstruct the funding trail for every significant asset that is not held in the name of the person who actually paid for it. This means locating, where they still exist, the bank statements, loan documents, sale deeds and tax returns that connect the money to the person claimed to be the real owner, and being honest with the family's own advisers about any asset where that trail cannot currently be shown, rather than discovering the gap for the first time when an Initiating Officer asks the same question.

Where an existing arrangement does not cleanly fit one of the four statutory exemptions, for instance a property held solely in a sibling's name with no joint ownership on record, or an asset in a daughter-in-law's name that the spouse-or-child exemption does not reach, the family should treat regularising that arrangement, through a properly documented and, where stamp duty and registration requirements apply, properly executed transfer or declaration, as a live priority rather than a problem to deal with only if it ever actually comes up.

Going forward, any new acquisition the family plans to hold in one member's name while another member's money funds it should be documented at the time, not reconstructed years later: a clear record of the source of funds, the relationship between the parties, and, where the exemption requires it, joint ownership reflected in the relevant document, costs very little to put in place when the transaction happens and can be extremely difficult to recreate once the people who arranged it are no longer available to explain it.

What Remains Unsettled

The retrospectivity of the 2016 amendment's confiscation and attachment machinery is less open than it was even a few months ago, though the point is still not finally closed by the bench that will actually decide the restored Ganpati Dealcom appeal. The Supreme Court's 2022 ruling that appeared to settle the point in favour of prospective-only operation was recalled in October 2024, and on 8 May 2026 a two-judge Bench in Manjula and Others v. D.A. Srinivas, 2026 INSC 465, held that confiscation is a civil consequence that can reach pre-2016 transactions, while the Act's criminal provisions remain prospective only. The three-judge Bench still due to rehear the restored Ganpati Dealcom appeal has not yet ruled, so a family relying on Manjula as the final word on confiscation, rather than as the most recent and currently most authoritative word pending that rehearing, is overstating how settled the point actually is; a family assuming the opposite, that confiscation definitely cannot reach a pre-2016 transaction, is simply wrong on the law as it currently stands.

A second, narrower point left open by the sources available for this piece is the precise treatment of a daughter-in-law or son-in-law under the family exemptions, where commentary consistently reads the spouse-or-child exception as not extending to that relationship but no higher court ruling confirming the point directly could be located in this research. A family in that specific position should have the question tested against the statutory text and any available tribunal or High Court decisions directly on point, rather than relying on the general commentary this piece has drawn on.

The Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act does not treat every family holding arrangement with suspicion, and the exemptions for a spouse or child, a karta holding joint family property, a brother or sister as a joint owner, and a genuine fiduciary are wide enough to cover most of what ordinary Indian families actually do with their property. What the exemptions do not forgive is the absence of a traceable funding source and, for the sibling and lineal-relative exemption specifically, the absence of documented joint ownership. A family that has kept neither is exposed to confiscation and prosecution regardless of how innocent the original arrangement actually was, and the honest response to that exposure is to reconstruct the record now, while the people who can explain it are still available, rather than waiting for an Initiating Officer to ask the question first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it illegal to buy property in my spouse's name?

No, provided the money used to buy it can be traced to your own known, declared sources of income. The exemption under Section 2(9)(A) of the Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act covers property bought in a spouse's or child's name on these terms. It fails if the funding cannot be shown to come from a disclosed source.

Can ancestral land held in the karta's name be treated as benami?

Not if it is genuinely held for the benefit of the Hindu undivided family and the consideration came from known HUF sources. This is a separate statutory exemption from the spouse-or-child exemption, and a family should be able to show both the HUF's funding and the family-benefit nature of the holding if the question is ever raised.

Does putting a sibling's name on a property as a joint owner protect it from being called benami?

Yes, if the sibling is shown as a joint owner, not the sole recorded owner, and the money came from the known source of the person claiming to be the real owner. A property held entirely in a brother's or sister's name alone, with no joint ownership documented anywhere, does not get this exemption even if the family understands whose money bought it.

What happens if a property is found to be benami?

It becomes liable to confiscation by the Central Government under Section 5 of the Act, with no compensation to either the person whose name is on the title or the person who actually paid for it. Separately, Section 53 provides for rigorous imprisonment of one to seven years and a fine of up to 25% of the property's fair market value for the benamidar, the beneficial owner, and anyone who abetted the transaction.

Can the 2016 amendment be used against a property transaction from before 2016?

As of today, yes for confiscation, no for prosecution. The Supreme Court held in 2022 that it could not, then recalled that judgment in October 2024 and sent the underlying constitutional question back for fresh hearing before a three-judge Bench that has not yet ruled. In the meantime, a two-judge Bench held on 8 May 2026, in Manjula and Others v. D.A. Srinivas, 2026 INSC 465, that the 2016 amendment's confiscation and attachment machinery is a civil consequence that can reach a pre-2016 transaction, while criminal prosecution under the Act remains available only for transactions after the amendment.

If I let a relative hold an asset in their name on my behalf, can I sue to get it back?

Generally no. Section 4 of the Act bars a suit to enforce any right in benami property against the person holding it, which means an informal arrangement that does not fall within one of the statutory exemptions leaves the real owner without a civil remedy if the arrangement later breaks down.

Does a property held through a family trust face the same benami risk?

A genuine trust, where the trustee holds property for the named beneficiaries under the trust's own documentation and the trust's own funds, falls within the fiduciary exemption. An arrangement that is labelled a trust but functions, in substance, as one person holding an asset for another who provided the money and retains full control does not get the benefit of the label alone.

Is there a time limit for the authorities to act once a benami transaction is suspected?

The Act sets internal timelines for the Initiating Officer and the Adjudicating Authority, including a requirement to refer a final provisional attachment to the Adjudicating Authority within fifteen days. These are procedural safeguards a family facing attachment should check carefully, since several tribunal decisions have quashed attachments where the department did not follow the required sequence.
