NRIs Buying Indian Shares and Mutual Funds After the June 2026 FEMA Reform

The route NRIs have long used to buy listed Indian shares was quietly rebuilt in June 2026: wider eligibility, a doubled individual limit, and a new rupee account sitting at the centre of how the money moves.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An NRI buying shares of a listed Indian company has never been treated the same as a resident buying the same shares on the same exchange. The purchase sits under the Foreign Exchange Management Act rather than ordinary domestic law, it has always needed a specific account and reporting structure behind it, and the ceiling on how much of a company an NRI could collectively hold has moved more than once since the route was first created. The Ministry of Finance moved that ceiling again in June 2026, in a notification that also widened who can use the route at all. An NRI who last checked these rules before mid-2026, or who is relying on a relative's or broker's account from several years ago, is very likely working from a position that is no longer current.

Key points

  • NRIs and OCIs invest in listed Indian shares under Schedule III of the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, a route commonly still called the Portfolio Investment Scheme even though the regulations were recast in 2017 and again in 2019
  • The Ministry of Finance's Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) (Third Amendment) Rules, 2026, notified as S.O. 3030(E) on 12 June 2026, widened Schedule III eligibility from NRIs and OCIs specifically to any individual person resident outside India, and raised the individual investment ceiling from 5% to 10% of a listed company's paid-up capital and the aggregate ceiling for all such individual investors together from 10% to 24%
  • The Reserve Bank of India issued a corresponding amendment to the Foreign Exchange Management (Mode of Payment and Reporting of Non-Debt Instruments) Regulations to align the payment and reporting mechanics with the widened Schedule III route
  • A new designated repatriable rupee account for Schedule III investments sits alongside the existing routing through an NRE, NRO or FCNR(B) account and the long-standing requirement for a permission letter from a bank designated for this purpose
  • Purchases made on a non-repatriable basis, funded from an NRO account, are treated differently under the Non-Debt Instruments Rules and do not count against the Schedule III repatriable-basis ceilings in the same way
  • NRIs may invest in Indian mutual funds on either a repatriable or non-repatriable basis, but a number of asset management companies decline applications from investors resident in the United States or Canada because of the compliance burden the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act places on them, a commercial choice rather than a legal bar
  • A sectoral cap below 24%, where one applies to a particular company's business, continues to override the general aggregate ceiling, and a company can still choose a lower ceiling for its own shares by board and shareholder resolution

A Route That Keeps Being Renamed Without Really Changing Its Purpose

Every NRI who has looked into buying shares on an Indian stock exchange has run into the term Portfolio Investment Scheme, usually shortened to PIS, often alongside a warning that the scheme has been discontinued, replaced, or liberalised, depending on which article or bank brochure is doing the explaining. The underlying reality is less dramatic than the competing claims suggest. The specific route an NRI uses to buy and sell shares of listed Indian companies on a stock exchange has been recast more than once at the level of which regulation contains it, but the basic structure, an NRI investing through a bank account opened for the purpose, subject to a cap on how much of any one company NRIs collectively may hold, has been continuous since the scheme was first introduced decades ago.

The regulation currently governing this route is Schedule III to the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, issued by the Department of Economic Affairs in the Ministry of Finance under the rule-making power the Foreign Exchange Management Act gives the Central Government for non-debt instruments, with the Reserve Bank of India issuing the corresponding regulations on the mode of payment and reporting that make the rule operational in practice. The 2019 Rules themselves replaced an earlier 2017 version, which had itself replaced the original FEMA 20 regulations of 2000, and each of these recastings has, at different points, been reported in the press and in bank communications as the scheme being abolished, simplified or merged into general FDI rules, which is part of why confusion about its current status persists among NRIs who have not had reason to check the position recently.

What has not stayed constant through these recastings is the ceiling on NRI holdings in any one listed company, which the Ministry of Finance has revised more than once, most recently and most significantly in June 2026. An NRI relying on a limit figure learned from an older article, a relative's experience from years ago, or even a bank's own onboarding material that has not been refreshed, risks working from a number that the June 2026 amendment has since overtaken, which is the main reason this article exists.

The June 2026 Amendment: Who Can Invest and How Much

The Ministry of Finance notified the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) (Third Amendment) Rules, 2026 on 12 June 2026, through Notification S.O. 3030(E), with the changes taking effect the same day. The amendment did two distinct things to Schedule III. First, it widened the category of eligible investor: where Schedule III had previously spoken specifically of Non-Resident Indians and Overseas Citizens of India, the amended text now covers any individual person resident outside India, a category that includes NRIs and OCIs but is no longer limited to them, extending the route in principle to a foreign national with no Indian origin at all, investing in their individual capacity.

Second, the amendment raised the investment ceilings that had applied under the route for some years. The individual ceiling, the maximum any single eligible individual investor may hold in one listed Indian company under Schedule III, moved from 5% to 10% of that company's paid-up equity capital. The aggregate ceiling, the maximum all eligible individual investors together may hold in one company under the route, moved from 10% to 24% of the company's paid-up equity capital on a fully diluted basis. Both figures apply across the schedules an individual investor's holding is aggregated under, so an individual's Schedule III holding is counted together with any holding the same individual has under another applicable schedule of the Non-Debt Instruments Rules for the purpose of checking against the 10% individual ceiling.

The Reserve Bank of India followed with its own amendment to the Foreign Exchange Management (Mode of Payment and Reporting of Non-Debt Instruments) Regulations, aligning the payment and reporting framework with the widened rule, since the Ministry of Finance's Non-Debt Instruments Rules set the eligibility and ceilings while the Reserve Bank's own regulations govern how the money actually moves and how the transaction is reported. An NRI's broker and bank will, in practice, be following the Reserve Bank's regulations day to day far more than the underlying Ministry notification, which makes it worth confirming with both the bank and the broker that their own systems and forms have actually been updated to reflect the June 2026 change, since an institution working from an outdated internal process can still apply the old 5% and 10% figures in practice even after the law itself has moved.

A separate point the amendment did not disturb is the position of foreign portfolio investors registered under the Securities and Exchange Board of India's FPI framework, who invest under the entirely different Schedule II of the same Rules rather than Schedule III. An NRI who also happens to be eligible to register as an FPI, which is unusual but not impossible depending on structure, needs to keep the two routes and their separate ceilings distinct rather than assuming the Schedule III changes have any bearing on an FPI-route holding.

Commentary published in the weeks after the notification generally treated the widening of eligibility as the more significant long-term change, since it opens a route previously understood as reserved for people of Indian origin to any individual abroad willing to go through the account-opening and compliance process, while treating the ceiling increase as a more technical adjustment that mainly benefits a smaller number of concentrated or strategic holdings. Both changes nonetheless arrived in the same notification and took effect on the same day, and an NRI reading older material that discusses only one of the two, typically the eligibility widening, since it is the more easily summarised headline, should check that the same source also reflects the ceiling change, rather than assuming an article that got the eligibility point right necessarily also has the current percentage figures correct.

Why the Ceiling Still Matters to an Individual Investor

An individual NRI buying a modest holding in a handful of large, widely held companies is unlikely to come anywhere near even the old 5% individual ceiling, and for most retail NRI investors the ceiling question is, practically speaking, academic. It stops being academic for an NRI or OCI family that has built a genuinely large, concentrated position in a single company, whether through years of accumulation, an inheritance of shares from a resident relative, or a deliberate strategic stake in a smaller-capitalisation company where the free float is thin enough that even a moderately sized holding represents a meaningful percentage of the total.

The aggregate ceiling matters in a different, less visible way: it caps how much of a company all NRI and now, after June 2026, all eligible individual foreign investors together can hold under Schedule III, which means an individual NRI's own ability to keep buying can be constrained by how much other NRIs and eligible individuals have already bought, a figure the individual investor has no direct visibility into and no control over. In practice, the company and its registrar track the aggregate NRI and individual foreign shareholding and the stock exchanges publish this data, so a broker executing a Schedule III purchase order is checking against the current aggregate figure at the time of the trade, and an order can be rejected or only partially executed where the aggregate ceiling for that specific company has already been reached, which is a more common practical constraint for smaller, thinly held companies than the 24% figure might suggest.

A company is also free to set its own lower ceiling for Schedule III and Schedule II investment in its shares, by a resolution of its board followed by a special resolution of its shareholders, and a smaller number of companies, generally those in sectors where the government has set a sectoral cap on foreign investment below the general ceiling, are bound by that sectoral cap regardless of what Schedule III's own default figures say. An NRI considering a significant position in any company should check that specific company's own disclosed foreign shareholding limit, available from the company's periodic filings and from the depositories, rather than assuming the general 10%/24% figures from the June 2026 amendment automatically apply to every listed company without exception.

The Accounts an NRI Actually Needs

The mechanics of a Schedule III purchase have always run through a specific account structure rather than an NRI simply wiring money to a broker the way a resident investor might. An NRI investing on a repatriable basis, meaning the sale proceeds and any dividends can later be sent back abroad without further RBI permission, funds the purchase from an NRE account or an FCNR(B) deposit, and the transaction has conventionally required a permission letter from a bank specifically designated to monitor the individual's Schedule III transactions against the applicable ceilings, together with a linked demat account and a trading account opened for this purpose.

A further account option entered this structure through the June 2026 reform: a designated repatriable rupee account, which some commentary describes as intended specifically for Schedule III transactions and through which sale proceeds can be remitted abroad or credited back to the investor's own account in India, sitting alongside rather than replacing the existing NRE, FCNR(B) and NRO-based routes. Reports describing this account in the weeks following the June 2026 notification differ on exactly how it interacts with the pre-existing permission-letter mechanism, and this article accordingly does not attempt to state a single, settled description of the new account's precise mechanics; an NRI planning a significant Schedule III investment should ask the designated bank directly which account structure it is currently using for new Schedule III business, rather than assuming either the old or the new structure applies without confirming it.

Investment on a non-repatriable basis works differently and is, for many NRIs with no present intention of sending the proceeds abroad, considerably simpler. Funded from an NRO account, a non-repatriable Schedule III purchase is treated under the Non-Debt Instruments Rules broadly on par with a resident's own investment for the purpose of the ceilings that matter to the company, which is why many NRIs who are not focused on repatriation choose to route routine equity investment through an NRO account rather than setting up the fuller repatriable structure, reserving the NRE-funded repatriable route for the specific holdings they do intend to eventually move abroad.

Mutual Funds: A Simpler Route With Its Own Restriction

Units of a domestic mutual fund scheme, by contrast, are not governed by Schedule III of the Non-Debt Instruments Rules in the same way direct equity purchases are, and an NRI investing in a mutual fund does not need the Schedule III permission-letter and designated-bank apparatus described above. An NRI can invest in Indian mutual fund schemes, equity, debt or hybrid, on either a repatriable basis, funding the investment from an NRE or FCNR(B) account, or a non-repatriable basis, funding it from an NRO account, with the asset management company's own Know Your Customer process confirming the investor's NRI status, PAN and the specific bank account the investment and any later redemption will run through.

The genuine, well-documented restriction in this space has nothing to do with Indian law directly and everything to do with the compliance burden the United States' Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act and, to a lesser extent, similar Canadian reporting obligations place on a foreign financial institution that accepts money from a US or Canadian resident. A meaningful number of Indian asset management companies decline to accept fresh investment from NRIs resident in the United States or Canada specifically because the ongoing FATCA reporting and compliance cost of maintaining those accounts outweighs the business the accounts bring in, a commercial decision each fund house makes independently rather than a prohibition SEBI or the RBI has imposed. Which specific fund houses currently accept US- and Canada-resident NRI investors, and under what conditions, changes often enough that this article does not attempt to list them; an NRI resident in either country should check directly with the specific fund house or platform before assuming an application will be accepted.

Where a fund house does accept a US- or Canada-resident NRI, the application commonly still requires additional documentation beyond what other NRIs face, typically an explicit FATCA declaration and, in some cases, a requirement that the transaction be processed offline through physical forms rather than the fund house's ordinary online onboarding, precisely because the compliance obligation the fund house is managing is triggered by accepting the investment at all rather than by the particular channel used to accept it. An NRI used to the fully digital onboarding available to NRIs resident elsewhere should expect a slower, more paperwork-heavy process specifically on account of US or Canadian residency, and should build that expectation into the time taken to actually get invested rather than assuming the process will match what a sibling or friend resident in, say, the UAE or Singapore experienced.

Redemption, Dividends and Where the Money Actually Lands

On redemption of mutual fund units or sale of shares held on a repatriable basis, the proceeds, net of whatever tax the fund house or broker's clearing system withholds at source, are credited to the NRE or FCNR(B) account the original investment was funded from, or, following the June 2026 change, potentially to the new designated repatriable rupee account where that structure is in use, from which the NRI can remit the money abroad without needing a fresh RBI permission for that specific remittance, since the repatriable character was already established at the time of the original investment.

Proceeds from a holding on a non-repatriable basis are credited to the investor's NRO account instead, and moving that money abroad afterward is a separate question governed by the general NRO repatriation rules, including the standard annual ceiling on remittances from NRO account balances, rather than by anything specific to the Schedule III route itself. An NRI who originally invested on a non-repatriable basis and later wants to send the proceeds abroad should not expect the sale itself to have resolved the repatriation question; it has simply moved the money into an NRO account from which the ordinary repatriation rules then apply.

Dividends on Schedule III holdings follow the same repatriable or non-repatriable character as the underlying shares, credited to the corresponding account, and are subject to tax deduction at source at the rate applicable to a non-resident shareholder under the Income-tax Act and any relief available under the investor's country of residence's double taxation avoidance agreement with India, a tax question this article does not attempt to resolve in detail since the applicable rate depends entirely on the specific treaty and the investor's own residency position.

An NRI who changes country of residence after the original investment was made should not assume the tax and FEMA position simply carries over unchanged. The account structure itself, NRE, NRO, FCNR(B) or the new designated repatriable rupee account, depends on NRI status rather than on any one specific country of residence, so moving from one country to another while remaining an NRI generally does not by itself require restructuring the holding. What does need checking on a change of residence is the treaty relief available on dividends and capital gains, since India's double taxation avoidance agreements differ considerably from country to country, and an NRI who moved, for example, from a country with a favourable treaty rate to one with a less favourable rate, or to the United States or Canada where the FATCA considerations discussed earlier also come into play for any fresh mutual fund investment, should revisit both the tax and the mutual-fund-onboarding position rather than assuming the existing arrangement is unaffected by the move.

The Ceiling Before June 2026, and Why 24% Is Not Entirely New

It is worth being precise about what was actually new in June 2026 and what was simply made the default. Before the amendment, the individual ceiling for an NRI or OCI under Schedule III stood at 5% of a company's paid-up equity capital, and the aggregate ceiling for all NRIs and OCIs together stood at 10%, figures that had themselves been the product of earlier revisions going back to the scheme's original design decades before. A company could already raise its own aggregate ceiling above 10%, up to 24% or, with further shareholder approval, up to the applicable sectoral cap for foreign investment in that company's business, by a resolution of its board followed by a special resolution of its shareholders.

What the June 2026 amendment changed is that 24% became the general statutory aggregate ceiling available without a company needing to pass its own resolution to get there, and 10% became the general individual ceiling in the same way, converting what had previously been an opt-in, resolution-dependent enhancement into the default position for every listed company. A company that had already passed a resolution raising its own aggregate ceiling to 24% before June 2026 sees essentially no change in practical headroom from the amendment; a company that had never bothered passing such a resolution, often because its actual NRI shareholding was nowhere near even the old 10% aggregate figure, now has the higher ceiling available by default regardless.

This history matters for an NRI assessing a specific company's available headroom, because a company's own historical special resolution, if one exists, may in principle have set a ceiling different from either the old default or the new one, a bespoke figure between 10% and the applicable sectoral cap that the company's shareholders chose for reasons specific to that company. Where such a resolution exists and has not been formally revisited since June 2026, the question of whether the company's bespoke ceiling or the new statutory default now governs is a point worth confirming with the company's registrar rather than assuming the new general figure automatically supersedes an older, company-specific resolution.

Tax on Gains: What Actually Gets Withheld Before the Money Reaches You

Buying and selling under Schedule III does not change how capital gains on listed Indian shares are taxed; it only governs whether the NRI is permitted to hold and trade the shares in the first place. Gains on listed equity shares and equity-oriented mutual fund units held for more than twelve months are treated as long-term capital gains, taxed under the Income-tax Act at the rate applicable to such gains above the statutory exemption threshold, while gains on a holding of twelve months or less are treated as short-term capital gains and taxed at the separate rate the Act prescribes for that category. These rates and thresholds have been revised by successive Finance Acts over the years, so an NRI should confirm the current figures for the year in which the gain actually arises rather than relying on a rate remembered from an earlier year.

For an NRI specifically, the practical point that matters most day to day is withholding rather than the final rate: a broker or mutual fund house is generally required to deduct tax at source on a non-resident's capital gains at the time of sale or redemption, before the net proceeds are even credited to the NRE, NRO or FCNR(B) account, which means the NRI's own annual tax return is usually reconciling an amount already withheld rather than computing and paying a fresh liability from scratch. Where the actual tax due, after applying any relief available under India's double taxation avoidance agreement with the NRI's country of residence, is lower than what was withheld, the NRI's remedy is to claim a refund through the annual return rather than to expect the broker to withhold a lower figure automatically at the point of sale, since very few brokers apply treaty relief at the withholding stage without the NRI having separately obtained a lower-deduction certificate from the tax authorities in advance.

An NRI who expects a large gain and wants to avoid the cash-flow drag of an over-withheld amount sitting with the tax department until a refund is processed should look into applying for a lower or nil deduction certificate ahead of the transaction, a process that takes time to complete and is accordingly only worth pursuing where the gain in question, and the resulting over-withholding, is large enough to justify the effort well before the sale is actually executed.

Compliance Mistakes That Surface Only on an Audit or a Bank Query

The most common practical mistake an NRI makes with this route has nothing to do with the ceilings at all: it is continuing to operate an ordinary resident demat and trading account, opened while the investor was still resident in India, after moving abroad and becoming an NRI, without converting it to the correct NRI account structure. A resident account is simply not the correct vehicle for an NRI's trading activity under FEMA, and continuing to trade through one after a change in residential status is itself a FEMA contravention, regardless of whether the specific trades would otherwise have been permissible under Schedule III if routed correctly.

A related mistake is treating the account conversion as something to get around to eventually rather than something to do promptly on becoming an NRI, often because the existing resident account is already linked to familiar bank details, a long trading history and a specific broker relationship the investor does not want to disrupt. The conversion itself is a standard process most brokers and depository participants can handle without closing and reopening the account from scratch, and the inconvenience of doing it properly is considerably smaller than the exposure of having a bank or the tax department later notice, often years afterward when a large transaction or an income-tax scrutiny draws attention to the account's history, that trading continued on a resident account well after the investor's status changed.

NRIs who hold shares jointly with a resident family member, commonly a spouse or parent still living in India, where the account itself may be structured correctly for the resident holder but the NRI's own share of any gain, and the NRI's own continuing obligation to route any fresh purchase through a compliant NRI structure, gets overlooked because the account's day-to-day operation is being handled by the resident joint holder. An NRI in this position should periodically confirm, independently of whoever is actually managing the account day to day, that their own FEMA position on that holding is correctly structured, rather than assuming a family member's diligence on the resident side of the account automatically covers the NRI's own compliance position as well.

What an NRI Working From an Old Understanding Should Check Now

An NRI who set up a PIS or Schedule III arrangement some years ago, and has not reviewed it since, should start by confirming with the designated bank whether the account structure actually in use reflects the June 2026 changes, since a bank that has not updated its own internal process could still be operating the arrangement under the pre-amendment 5%/10% ceilings and the older account structure without either the bank or the NRI realising the underlying rule has moved. This matters less for the ceiling itself, since few individual NRIs come close to even the old limits, and matters more for the broader point that an institution several steps behind a regulatory change is a reasonable signal to ask more questions rather than assume everything else about the arrangement is equally current.

A different check applies to an NRI or OCI family holding, or planning to build, a genuinely large position in a single listed company, whether for strategic, promoter-family or simple long-term concentration reasons, should specifically check that company's own current foreign shareholding limit and how much of the aggregate ceiling other NRI and eligible foreign individual investors have already used, rather than assuming the general 10% individual figure is automatically available in full for that particular company. Where the position is large enough to approach either the individual or the company-specific aggregate ceiling, this is a point worth raising with the company's own registrar and with the depository well before placing a large order, since an order that cannot be executed in full against the remaining ceiling headroom is discovered at the worst possible moment if it is only checked at the point of the trade itself.

A foreign national with no Indian origin who has, until now, assumed that Schedule III was simply unavailable to them because it was described everywhere as an NRI and OCI scheme should treat the June 2026 widening as a genuine change worth revisiting, rather than continuing to rely on an outdated understanding that the route was closed to them. The widened eligibility is recent enough, and reported with enough variation in how thoroughly different commentators have covered it, that confirming the current position directly with a bank authorised to handle this business, rather than relying on a general article from before mid-2026, remains the sensible first step before attempting an actual transaction.

The route an NRI uses to buy listed Indian shares has survived three recastings of its governing regulation without losing its essential shape: a specific account, a bank permission mechanism, and a ceiling on how much of any one company NRIs and, now, other eligible foreign individuals can collectively hold. What the June 2026 amendment changed is real and recent enough that an NRI relying on an older understanding of the limits, the eligible investor category, or the account structure should treat that understanding as provisionally out of date until it has been checked against the current rule.

None of this removes the separate, entirely commercial restriction that FATCA compliance costs impose on US- and Canada-resident NRIs seeking to invest in Indian mutual funds, a restriction that sits outside Indian law altogether and has to be checked fund house by fund house rather than resolved by reference to any RBI or SEBI rule. The practical lesson across both the equity and mutual fund routes is the same: confirm the current position with the specific bank, broker or fund house actually handling the transaction, rather than with a general understanding of how the scheme used to work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can NRIs still buy shares of listed Indian companies in 2026?

Yes. NRIs and OCIs invest under Schedule III of the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, a route still commonly called the Portfolio Investment Scheme. A June 2026 amendment widened eligibility beyond NRIs and OCIs to any individual person resident outside India and raised the investment ceilings.

What is the current individual and aggregate limit for NRI shareholding in a listed Indian company?

Following the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) (Third Amendment) Rules, 2026, notified on 12 June 2026, the individual ceiling is 10% of a company's paid-up equity capital, and the aggregate ceiling for all eligible individual investors together is 24%, both up from the earlier 5% and 10%. A company-specific sectoral cap or a lower limit set by the company's own board and shareholders can override these general figures.

Do I need a special bank account to buy Indian shares as an NRI?

Yes. A Schedule III purchase on a repatriable basis is funded from an NRE or FCNR(B) account, routed through a bank designated to monitor the transaction against the applicable ceilings, with a linked demat and trading account. The June 2026 reform added a new designated repatriable rupee account option; a non-repatriable purchase is instead funded from an NRO account.

Can an NRI in the USA or Canada invest in Indian mutual funds?

Legally, yes, but many Indian asset management companies decline such applications because of the compliance burden the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act places on them. This is a commercial choice by individual fund houses, not a legal prohibition, so which fund houses accept US- or Canada-resident NRIs changes and should be checked directly with the fund house.

What happened to the Portfolio Investment Scheme for NRIs?

It was not abolished. The scheme was recast into Schedule III of the Non-Debt Instruments Rules, 2019, which replaced an earlier 2017 version of the same underlying regulations. The core structure, a designated account and a ceiling on collective NRI holding, has continued under each recasting, most recently widened and raised in June 2026.

Can a foreign national who is not an NRI or OCI now invest in Indian listed shares?

The June 2026 amendment extended Schedule III eligibility from NRIs and OCIs specifically to any individual person resident outside India, which in principle includes a foreign national with no Indian origin, subject to the same account structure, permission requirements and ceilings that apply to NRI investors.

Can I repatriate the sale proceeds of Indian shares I bought as an NRI?

Yes, if the shares were bought on a repatriable basis from an NRE or FCNR(B) account, or the new designated repatriable rupee account, since the repatriable character was established at purchase. Shares bought on a non-repatriable basis from an NRO account are credited to NRO on sale and are then subject to the ordinary NRO repatriation limits.
