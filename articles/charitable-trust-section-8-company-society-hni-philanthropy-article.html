Charitable Trust, Section 8 Company or Society: Choosing a Vehicle for Family Philanthropy in India

What actually governs a public charitable trust, why it is not simply the Indian Trusts Act, how the choice between a trust and a Section 8 company affects control and dissolution later, and what changes for every such vehicle's tax registration under the Income-tax Act, 2025

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 24 November 2026 · Law stated as on 23 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family deciding to formalise its giving, after years of writing cheques informally or routing donations through whichever organisation asked, usually reaches for the word 'trust' as if it were the only option and the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 as if it were the statute that governs it. Neither assumption survives contact with how Indian charity law is actually structured. The real choice is between three distinct legal vehicles, each with its own governing statute, its own compliance burden, and its own consequences for how much control the founding family keeps and how easily the vehicle can later be wound up or converted.

Key points

  • The Indian Trusts Act, 1882 governs private trusts and, by its own Section 1, does not apply to public or charitable trusts; a public charitable trust in a state without its own public trusts legislation, including West Bengal and Delhi, is instead governed primarily by the Charitable and Religious Trusts Act, 1920, a distinction families and even some advisors routinely get wrong.
  • A family choosing a vehicle for organised philanthropy is choosing between three structures with meaningfully different statutes: a public charitable trust, a society registered under the Societies Registration Act, 1860 requiring a minimum of seven members, and a Section 8 company under the Companies Act, 2013, which can be formed with as few as two members and directors.
  • A trust generally carries a lighter ongoing compliance burden and suits a family that wants to retain clear, concentrated control through a small group of trustees, while a Section 8 company carries a heavier compliance burden, including statutory audit and regular board and general meetings, but is often regarded by CSR funders and institutional donors as the more credible and more readily fundable structure for a larger operation.
  • A public charitable trust is generally treated as difficult to dissolve once created, since its charitable objects are intended to operate in perpetuity, whereas a Section 8 company can be wound up or have its licence revoked under the Companies Act with comparatively more procedural flexibility, a difference families should weigh before committing to either structure for a philanthropic vehicle whose long-term shape is not yet certain.
  • The Income-tax Act, 2025, in force from 1 April 2026, replaces the familiar Sections 12A and 12AB registration route with Section 332 and replaces Section 80G donor-deduction approval with Section 354, though an entity already holding a valid registration or approval under the old provisions is not required to seek fresh registration merely because the new Act has commenced.
  • The Finance Act, 2025 extended the standard validity period for charitable registration from five years to ten years for trusts and institutions with annual receipts not exceeding Rs 5 crore in each of the two preceding tax years, while a larger entity, and separately the Section 80G donor-approval track on at least one reported reading, remain on a shorter validity cycle, so a family should confirm its own entity's specific validity period rather than assume the longer period applies.
  • A public charitable trust or Section 8 company that will receive donations from NRI relatives, a foreign-based family member, or any other foreign source needs to separately assess its position under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010 before accepting such funds, since FCRA registration or prior permission is a distinct requirement layered on top of, and independent from, the choice of charitable vehicle and its tax registration.

Why the vehicle matters before the first rupee moves

A family that has been giving informally for years, sometimes through a running joint account, sometimes by writing personal cheques to causes a family member cares about, eventually reaches a point where the giving has outgrown that informality: a sizeable one-time gift, a recurring commitment the family wants to keep going across generations, or simply a wish to claim the tax deduction a registered charitable vehicle makes available that an informal gift does not. At that point, the family has to choose a legal structure, and the choice made at this stage shapes, for years afterward, how much control the family keeps, how much it costs to run the vehicle, how credible the vehicle looks to an institutional co-funder, and how hard it will be to wind the vehicle up if the family's circumstances or priorities change.

This article works through that choice for a family or family business in India considering formal philanthropy, specifically the decision between a public charitable trust, a society, and a Section 8 company, and separately addresses the tax registration regime these vehicles now sit inside following the Income-tax Act, 2025. It does not address a private family trust set up to hold and pass down the family's own wealth across generations, a materially different instrument covered in a separate piece on this site, since a private family trust and a public charitable trust, despite sharing the word 'trust,' serve different purposes and sit under different parts of the law.

The confusion between the two is worth dwelling on briefly because it is so common in family conversation. A private family trust, typically created to hold the family's own shares, property or investments and to distribute income or capital among named family beneficiaries according to the settlor's wishes, is a wealth-management and succession-planning instrument, and the family itself, or a defined class of family members, remains the beneficiary. A public charitable trust, by contrast, dedicates its assets to a charitable purpose benefiting the public or a section of the public, and no family member can be a beneficiary of that charitable purpose in the way a family member is a beneficiary of a private family trust; a family member can serve as a trustee administering the charitable trust, but cannot receive its charitable assets as a personal beneficiary without defeating the very public character that qualifies the trust for its tax treatment. Families occasionally approach a private family trust as though it were a vehicle for philanthropy, or a public charitable trust as though it could still benefit family members directly; neither works, and the two should be kept conceptually and documentarily separate from the outset even where the same family sets up both instruments around the same time.

Three vehicles, three statutes

A public charitable trust is created by a trust deed under which the settlor transfers property to trustees to be held and applied for charitable purposes. A society is formed by a minimum of seven persons who subscribe to a memorandum of association under the Societies Registration Act, 1860, and is typically governed by a managing committee elected from among its members, a structure historically favoured by educational institutions, professional bodies and membership-based charitable organisations rather than by a single family's own philanthropic vehicle. A Section 8 company is a company incorporated under the Companies Act, 2013 for charitable objects, promoting commerce, art, science, sports, education, research, social welfare, religion, charity, protection of environment or similar objects, which applies its profits toward those objects and is prohibited from paying dividends to its members; it can be formed by as few as two persons as a private company limited by guarantee or shares, which makes it, of the three structures, the one most easily set up and controlled by a single family without needing to recruit outside members.

Each of these three statutes, the Societies Registration Act, 1860, the Companies Act, 2013, and whichever statute actually governs public charitable trusts in the relevant state, operates independently of the others, and a family's choice between them is a choice of which statute's governance, reporting and dissolution rules will apply to the vehicle for as long as it exists. None of the three statutes determines the vehicle's tax treatment on its own; tax registration under the Income-tax Act is a separate step required regardless of which of the three structures the family chooses, addressed later in this article.

A fourth variant worth naming, though it is less often the right choice for a dedicated family vehicle, is registering an existing public charitable trust or society as a Section 8 company's trustee or member rather than converting the underlying vehicle itself; families sometimes pursue a layered structure of this kind where an operating Section 8 company carries out the charitable activity day to day while a trust holds a specific endowed corpus intended to fund that activity in perpetuity, keeping the perpetual-corpus function and the operating-activity function in separate legal shells. This layered approach adds its own compliance overhead, since it means running two registered entities rather than one, and is generally worth the added complexity only once the family's philanthropic activity has grown large enough that separating the endowment from the day-to-day operating vehicle produces a genuine governance or risk-management benefit, rather than being the default starting structure for a family just beginning to formalise its giving.

The Indian Trusts Act does not govern a public charitable trust, and the point is not academic

A common and consequential misunderstanding is that the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 is the statute governing a public charitable trust. It is not. Section 1 of that Act confines its own application to private trusts, and a public charitable or religious trust falls outside it. For a state that has not enacted its own public trusts legislation, including West Bengal and Delhi, the relevant central statute is instead the Charitable and Religious Trusts Act, 1920, a comparatively old and procedurally thin piece of legislation primarily concerned with enabling a trustee to obtain the court's directions on the trust's management and with registering particulars of the trust, rather than providing the kind of detailed, administrator-backed regulatory regime found in states that have enacted their own public trusts law.

Maharashtra and Gujarat are the clearest example of that latter model: both states have their own Public Trusts Acts, administered by a Charity Commissioner's office with its own registration, accounting, change-of-trustee approval and enforcement machinery specific to public trusts. A family setting up a public charitable trust in West Bengal or Delhi is not operating under an equivalent Charity Commissioner regime, and should not assume the oversight, registration formalities, or approval requirements that apply to a Maharashtra or Gujarat public trust apply in the same form to a trust created in a state governed instead by the 1920 Act. This state-by-state variation is one of the clearest ways the choice of vehicle and the choice of state intersect, and a family with philanthropic interests or assets spanning more than one state should have this checked specifically for each state in which it intends to hold trust property or carry out charitable activity, rather than assuming a single, uniform national regime for public charitable trusts.

The practical consequence of operating under the 1920 Act rather than a Maharashtra or Gujarat-style Public Trusts Act shows up most clearly in what oversight the trustees can expect, and cannot expect, from the state itself. A Maharashtra public trust registers with, and reports periodically to, the Charity Commissioner's office, which maintains its own public register of trusts, approves certain changes of trustee, and has statutory powers to inquire into a trust's management. A trust created in West Bengal or Delhi under the 1920 Act's lighter framework does not sit inside an equivalent, actively supervising administrative body, which in one sense reduces the trust's regulatory burden but in another sense means the trustees themselves carry a correspondingly larger share of the responsibility for the trust's own governance discipline, since there is less external institutional oversight filling that role by default. Families used to hearing about the Maharashtra Charity Commissioner's role in that state's charitable sector should not assume a materially similar institutional backstop exists for a trust created under the 1920 Act.

The society route, and why it is less often the right fit for a single family

A society's minimum membership requirement of seven persons, and its governance through an elected managing committee rather than through trustees or directors the founding family appoints directly, make it a structure better suited to a membership-based organisation, an alumni association, a professional body, or an educational institution intended to have a broader base of stakeholders than a single family, than to a family's own philanthropic vehicle intended to remain under that family's continuing direction. A family can, in principle, use a society by recruiting seven family members or close associates as the required members, but doing so does not change the structure's fundamentally membership-governed character, and control can in principle shift away from the founding family if the managing committee's composition changes over time in ways the family did not fully anticipate when the society was formed.

For this reason, a society is comparatively rarely the vehicle a family chooses for its own dedicated philanthropic entity, and is more commonly encountered where the charitable activity itself, running a school, a professional association, or a membership-based welfare body, naturally calls for a broader membership base than a single family can or should supply on its own. A family weighing the society route specifically because an existing, well-regarded charitable society in its sector already uses that structure should still separately assess whether a trust or Section 8 company would serve the family's own control and succession preferences better, rather than choosing a society purely because it is the conventional structure in that particular charitable sector, since the structure a well-known peer institution happens to use reflects that institution's own history and stakeholder base, not necessarily what best fits a single family's own particular priorities and circumstances.

A society also tends to involve a somewhat different registration and reporting relationship with the state registrar of societies than either a trust or a Section 8 company has with its own regulator, including periodic filing of the list of managing committee members and, in several states, a requirement to file annual accounts with the registrar to keep the registration current. A family evaluating a society primarily because it plans to run a school, a hospital, or another large operating institution, where a broader base of governing members genuinely reflects the institution's own stakeholders, teachers, alumni, or local community figures, rather than purely the founding family, should weigh this reporting relationship as part of the ordinary cost of the society structure, not as an unusual burden specific to one state's practice.

The Section 8 company: heavier compliance, often greater institutional credibility

A Section 8 company sits under the Companies Act, 2013 and carries the compliance obligations that follow from that: a statutory audit regardless of size, board meetings held at the frequency the Act requires, annual filings with the Registrar of Companies, and the general governance discipline the Companies Act imposes on any company, tempered in some respects by exemptions the Ministry of Corporate Affairs has granted Section 8 companies specifically, such as relaxed requirements around company secretary appointment at smaller scale. This is a materially heavier ongoing administrative burden than either a trust or, in most cases, a society typically carries, and a family should budget for the recurring cost of company secretarial and audit support before choosing this route.

The compensating advantage, reported consistently across philanthropy and compliance advisory commentary, is credibility with institutional funders. A corporate CSR department disbursing funds under its Companies Act CSR obligation, or a larger grant-making foundation evaluating a partner organisation, will often regard a Section 8 company's governance structure, its board, its statutory audit trail, its filings on public record with the Registrar of Companies, as a more verifiable and more institutionally familiar counterpart than a trust's comparatively lighter public disclosure regime. A family building a philanthropic vehicle that it expects, over time, to co-fund larger projects alongside corporate CSR money or institutional grants should weigh this credibility consideration seriously against the heavier compliance cost, rather than defaulting to a trust purely because a trust is simpler to set up at the outset.

A Section 8 company's compliance load also comes with a genuine corresponding benefit beyond pure institutional perception: the discipline the Companies Act imposes tends to catch governance problems earlier than a trust's lighter framework typically does. A statutory audit conducted annually, board minutes recording decisions and dissenting views, and filings on public record that a professional advisor, a prospective institutional funder, or even a family member who was not closely involved in day-to-day management can review, together create a paper trail that makes it harder for a philanthropic vehicle's governance to drift quietly off course over several years without anyone noticing until a dispute or a funding application forces the question. A family that has seen a relative's or associate's charitable trust run into difficulty specifically because no one outside a small circle of trustees was tracking its finances closely enough should weigh this governance-discipline benefit of the Section 8 company structure as a real, not merely cosmetic, advantage, separate from the institutional-credibility point already discussed.

Control and succession: where a trust usually keeps the advantage for a single family

A trust deed lets the settlor specify, with considerable precision, who the trustees are, how successor trustees are appointed, and what powers the trustees hold, all fixed at the point the trust is created and amendable thereafter only to the extent the trust deed itself, or the general law, permits. For a family wanting to keep a philanthropic vehicle under the continuing direction of a defined group, typically the founder and a small number of family members or trusted advisors, a trust's structure maps more directly onto that intention than either a society's elected-membership model or a Section 8 company's board-and-shareholder model, even though the family can, with careful drafting of the company's articles of association, replicate a good deal of that control within a Section 8 company as well.

The practical difference tends to show up less in the founding generation, when the family's own members naturally occupy every trustee or director seat regardless of structure, and more as the vehicle passes to a second or third generation, or as the family brings in independent trustees or directors to add credibility or expertise. A trust deed that specifies succession by direct family nomination preserves the founding family's control more durably across that transition than a Section 8 company structure that, absent carefully drafted articles reserving nomination rights to the family, could in principle see its board composition drift toward independent directors over successive appointment cycles. Families that care deeply about multi-generational control of the vehicle's direction should have this succession question drafted explicitly into the founding documents, whichever structure is chosen, rather than assuming either structure protects family control by default.

A related, frequently overlooked drafting point concerns what happens if a family member who is also a trustee or director becomes incapacitated, falls into a serious dispute with other family members, or simply wants to step back from active involvement while the family's broader philanthropic commitment continues. A trust deed or a Section 8 company's articles that specify only how a trustee or director is appointed, without addressing removal, resignation, or a mechanism for resolving a deadlock between family members who disagree on the vehicle's direction, leaves the vehicle exposed to exactly the kind of internal dispute that, in a family business context, would normally be addressed through a detailed shareholders' agreement. A philanthropic vehicle deserves the same drafting attention to these governance contingencies as the family's commercial holding structures receive, even though the amounts involved are often smaller and the family's instinct is to treat the charitable vehicle's documentation as a lower priority than the business's own constitutional documents.

Dissolution and amendment: the trust's comparative rigidity

A public charitable trust, once validly created, is generally treated in Indian law as intended to operate in perpetuity for its stated charitable objects, and dissolving it, or substantially altering those objects, is reported consistently across professional commentary as considerably harder than winding up a Section 8 company, precisely because a charitable trust's assets are regarded as dedicated to the public charitable purpose rather than remaining within the settlor's or trustees' discretion to redirect or recall. A family that creates a public charitable trust should understand, before signing the trust deed, that unwinding the structure later, if family circumstances change, if the chosen charitable objects no longer reflect the family's priorities, or if the family simply wants to consolidate its philanthropy into a different vehicle, is not a straightforward administrative step.

A Section 8 company, by contrast, can be wound up or have its Section 8 licence revoked under the Companies Act's own winding-up and licence-revocation provisions, a process that, while not casual or instant, follows company law's comparatively more developed and more procedurally defined exit routes than a public charitable trust's own dissolution rules allow for. A family genuinely uncertain whether its philanthropic commitment and chosen objects will remain stable over the vehicle's intended lifespan, as opposed to a family confident the vehicle should exist, largely unchanged, across multiple generations, should weigh this asymmetry specifically, since it is often a more durable practical difference between the two structures than the compliance-cost difference discussed above, which can be managed with professional support regardless of which structure is chosen.

There is also a middle path worth naming for a family unsure whether its commitment will prove durable: starting smaller and more provisionally, for instance by channelling the family's giving for a few years through an established, well-run public charitable trust or Section 8 company already operating in the family's chosen cause area, rather than incorporating a dedicated vehicle immediately. This lets the family test both its own sustained appetite for formal philanthropy and its preferred cause area's practical demands before committing to the more permanent structure, and specifically before committing to a public charitable trust's comparatively rigid dissolution profile, which is the structure most likely to leave a family that changes its mind within a few years with an asset it finds genuinely difficult to redirect or unwind.

The tax registration regime in transition: from Sections 12A/12AB to the Income-tax Act, 2025

Whichever of the three vehicles a family chooses, none of them is tax-exempt, or capable of giving its donors a tax deduction, by virtue of its chosen legal form alone; separate registration under the income tax law is required, and that registration regime has itself just changed. Under the Income-tax Act, 1961, this ran through Section 12A and, from 2021, the restructured Section 12AB for the entity's own tax-exempt registration, and Section 80G(5) for the separate approval that lets a donor claim a deduction for a gift to the entity. The Income-tax Act, 2025, which came into force on 1 April 2026, replaces Section 12A and 12AB with Section 332 for the entity's own registration, and replaces Section 80G(5) with Section 354 for the donor-deduction approval, carrying over substantially the same underlying policy rather than changing the basic requirement that a charitable entity register and that donors to it be separately approved for deduction purposes.

An entity that already holds a valid registration under Section 12AB, or an approval under Section 80G(5), as on 1 April 2026 is not required to obtain fresh registration or approval merely because the new Act has commenced; the existing registration or approval is treated as continuing under the new Act's equivalent provisions for whatever period of validity it already carries. This matters directly to the vehicle-choice decision addressed throughout this article, because it means a family setting up a new trust, society or Section 8 company today applies for registration and approval under the new Act's own Sections 332 and 354 from the outset, using the procedural forms and conditions the new Act and its rules prescribe, rather than under the now-superseded 1961 Act provisions a family setting up an equivalent vehicle before April 2026 would have used.

Validity periods and the renewal trap a family should not walk into

Registration validity is not indefinite, and this is the point at which families most commonly lose exemption status, not through any substantive non-compliance but through missing a renewal window. The Finance Act, 2025 extended the standard validity period for Section 12AB registration from five years to ten years specifically for trusts and institutions whose aggregate annual receipts did not exceed Rs 5 crore in each of the two tax years preceding the tax year in which the registration or renewal is granted; a larger entity remains on a shorter validity cycle under the general rule. Reported commentary on the position is not fully consistent on whether the Section 80G donor-approval track received the same ten-year extension or remains confined to a five-year cycle regardless of the entity's size, and a family's own advisor should confirm the specific validity period that applies to its entity's registration and its separate 80G approval rather than assume the two run on identical cycles.

The practical renewal trap is one families and even some professional advisors have already encountered under the outdated regime: an entity's 2021-22 registration, running for a fixed five-year period, carried a renewal deadline, calculated as six months before expiry, that fell due by the end of September 2025, and an entity that missed that specific window lost its exemption from the following tax year regardless of how compliant its charitable activity otherwise was. A family setting up a new vehicle today should calendar the registration's actual expiry date from the outset, build the renewal application into the vehicle's recurring compliance calendar well before the deadline rather than relying on being reminded closer to the date, and treat the registration's validity period as a governance matter the trustees or board track actively rather than a one-time administrative step completed at formation and then forgotten.

A related procedural point worth flagging is that registration and donor-approval renewal under the new Act is reported to run through Form 105, used for both the entity's own Section 332 registration and the donor-deduction approval under Section 354, which simplifies what was previously, under the old Act, a somewhat more fragmented set of forms across the two tracks. A family's compliance advisor filing a renewal today should confirm which specific form and procedure the jurisdictional tax authority is currently accepting, since a regime this recently transitioned can carry transitional inconsistencies between what the Act and Rules formally prescribe and what a given assessing officer's office is, in practice, equipped to process smoothly in the months immediately following a major procedural change of this kind.

FCRA: the cross-border giving question, addressed here only in outline

A family vehicle that expects to receive contributions from an NRI relative abroad, from a family member who has taken foreign citizenship, or from any other source situated outside India, needs to separately assess whether that contribution counts as a 'foreign contribution' under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010, and if so, whether the vehicle needs FCRA registration or prior permission before it can lawfully accept the funds. This is a distinct statutory question from everything discussed above: a vehicle can be a perfectly validly constituted public charitable trust or Section 8 company, fully registered under the Income-tax Act's own provisions, and still be acting unlawfully if it accepts a qualifying foreign contribution without the FCRA clearance the Act requires.

This article does not attempt the fuller FCRA analysis the question deserves, including the specific and sometimes counter-intuitive rules on when a remittance from an NRI relative does or does not count as a foreign contribution, a question addressed in a separate guide on this site to which a family expecting cross-border donations should turn before accepting the first such gift. The point to take from this article is narrower and procedural: FCRA clearance, where it is required, is a separate approval layered on top of the vehicle-choice decision and the Income-tax Act registration discussed above, obtained independently of both, and a family that has structured its philanthropic vehicle correctly and registered it correctly under the Income-tax Act has not, by that fact alone, also cleared the FCRA question.

A related timing point is worth flagging even within this outline-level treatment. FCRA registration itself carries eligibility conditions, including, in most cases, a minimum period of the entity's own prior existence and demonstrated charitable activity, which a newly formed vehicle will not yet satisfy. A family that already knows, at the time it sets up the vehicle, that it expects early and recurring contributions from relatives abroad should raise the FCRA question with its advisors at that formation stage, and plan around the separate prior-permission route for initial foreign gifts while the vehicle builds the track record FCRA registration itself requires, rather than treating FCRA as a question to revisit only once the first foreign remittance is already being arranged, since the prior-permission route itself takes time to process and does not lend itself to a last-minute request once a relative abroad is ready to send the money.

A practical framework for choosing between the three

A family or family business weighing this decision is better served working through a short, deliberate sequence of questions than reaching directly for whichever structure a friend or advisor used for an unrelated vehicle. First, how important is keeping the vehicle under the founding family's direct, continuing control across generations, as against building a broader governance base from the outset; a trust generally serves the former better, a society the latter, and a Section 8 company can be drafted toward either depending on how its articles allocate board nomination rights. Second, how large and how institutionally funded does the family expect the vehicle's activity to become; a vehicle that will largely run on the family's own giving can usually tolerate a trust's lighter compliance burden, while a vehicle expected to attract corporate CSR money or institutional grants at scale should weigh the Section 8 company's greater institutional credibility against its heavier compliance cost.

Third, how confident is the family that the vehicle's charitable objects and its intended lifespan will remain stable, as against genuine uncertainty about whether the family's priorities or structure might change materially in the years ahead; a family in the latter position should weigh the practical difficulty of dissolving or substantially amending a public charitable trust against a Section 8 company's comparatively more developed winding-up and licence-revocation routes before committing to the trust structure specifically because it was simpler to set up. Finally, whichever structure the family chooses, the choice of vehicle is only the first of two decisions; the second, equally consequential decision is getting the Income-tax Act registration and, if relevant, the FCRA position right from the outset and keeping both on an active compliance calendar for as long as the vehicle exists, since neither the trust deed, the society's memorandum, nor the Section 8 company's incorporation certificate does that work on its own. A family that treats this framework as a single decision made once at formation, rather than as an ongoing discipline the vehicle's governing body revisits periodically as the family's circumstances, the vehicle's scale, and the surrounding regulatory framework all continue to change, is the family most likely to find itself, years later, holding a structure that no longer fits its actual needs and that proves harder to unwind than it was to set up.

The word 'trust' carries enough everyday weight in family conversation about philanthropy that families often skip past the actual choice of legal vehicle, and skip past the fact that the statute governing a public charitable trust is not the one most people instinctively reach for. A public charitable trust, a society and a Section 8 company each sit under a different statute, each strikes a different balance between the founding family's continuing control and the vehicle's institutional credibility, and each carries a different practical difficulty if the family later wants to dissolve or substantially change the vehicle's direction. None of these differences is right or wrong in the abstract; they are trade-offs a family should make deliberately, against its own priorities for control, scale and permanence, rather than inherit by default from whichever structure a well-known philanthropic family or corporate foundation happens to use.

Getting the vehicle right is only half the task. The Income-tax Act, 2025's transition from the familiar Section 12AB and Section 80G framework to Sections 332 and 354 changes the procedural detail of registration without changing the underlying discipline every such vehicle needs: register correctly at formation, track the registration's actual validity period rather than assume it runs indefinitely, and separately clear the FCRA question before accepting the first contribution from abroad. A family that gets the vehicle-choice decision right and then lets the registration lapse through an administrative oversight has solved only the more visible half of the problem. The families that get this right treat the philanthropic vehicle's compliance calendar with the same seriousness they bring to the family business's own statutory filings, rather than as a secondary obligation that can wait until the trustees or directors happen to notice a deadline approaching.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 govern a public charitable trust in India?

No. Section 1 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 confines the Act's application to private trusts, and a public charitable or religious trust falls outside it. In a state without its own public trusts legislation, including West Bengal and Delhi, a public charitable trust is instead governed primarily by the Charitable and Religious Trusts Act, 1920. States such as Maharashtra and Gujarat have enacted their own Public Trusts Acts with a Charity Commissioner's office, which apply instead of the 1920 Act within those states.

What is the real difference in compliance burden between a charitable trust and a Section 8 company?

A public charitable trust generally carries a lighter ongoing compliance load, without the statutory audit, board-meeting frequency and Registrar of Companies filing obligations a Section 8 company carries under the Companies Act, 2013. The trade-off is that a Section 8 company's heavier compliance and public filing record are often regarded by corporate CSR departments and institutional grant-makers as more credible and more verifiable, which can matter for a vehicle expecting to attract larger, institutionally funded support.

Can a public charitable trust be dissolved or converted into a different structure later?

Generally, no, not easily. A public charitable trust is treated as intended to operate in perpetuity for its stated charitable objects, and dissolving it or substantially changing those objects is reported consistently as considerably harder than winding up a Section 8 company, since the trust's assets are regarded as dedicated to the public purpose rather than remaining within the trustees' discretion to redirect. A family uncertain whether its chosen objects and structure will remain stable over time should weigh this rigidity before choosing a trust specifically for that reason.

What happened to Section 12A and Section 12AB registration under the new Income-tax Act, 2025?

The Income-tax Act, 2025, in force from 1 April 2026, replaces Section 12A and 12AB registration with registration under Section 332, and replaces Section 80G(5) donor-deduction approval with approval under Section 354. An entity already holding valid registration or approval under the old provisions as on 1 April 2026 is not required to seek fresh registration or approval merely because the new Act has commenced; the existing registration continues for whatever period of validity it already carries.

How long does charitable registration now last before it needs renewal?

The Finance Act, 2025 extended the standard validity period from five years to ten years for a trust or institution whose aggregate annual receipts did not exceed Rs 5 crore in each of the two preceding tax years; a larger entity remains on the shorter cycle. Commentary is not fully consistent on whether the separate Section 80G donor-approval track received the same extension, so a family's advisor should confirm the specific validity period for both the entity's registration and its 80G approval rather than assume they run on identical cycles.

Does a Section 8 company need more members or directors than a trust needs trustees?

Not necessarily more than a trust, but more formally structured. A Section 8 company can be incorporated with as few as two members and directors as a private company, comparable to or fewer than the trustees a family might appoint to a trust. A society, by contrast, requires a minimum of seven members under the Societies Registration Act, 1860, which is why a society is less often chosen for a single family's own dedicated philanthropic vehicle.

Does receiving a donation from an NRI relative trigger FCRA registration for a charitable trust or Section 8 company?

It can, depending on the specific facts, including whether the relative has retained Indian citizenship or taken a foreign one and how the contribution is characterised. This is a separate question from the choice of charitable vehicle and from Income-tax Act registration, assessed under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010, and a vehicle expecting donations from abroad should have this checked specifically before accepting such a contribution, since accepting a qualifying foreign contribution without the required FCRA clearance is unlawful regardless of how properly the vehicle is otherwise constituted and registered.
