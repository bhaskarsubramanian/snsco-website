100% FDI in Indian Insurance After the 2025 Reform: What Changed and What a Foreign Insurer Still Has to Do

The headline number moved from 74% to 100% in a matter of months. The conditions attached to that number, and the pieces of the reform that are enabling powers rather than finished rules, matter just as much to an entity actually planning to use it.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 18 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

India's insurance sector has raised its foreign investment ceiling three times since private entry began in 2000, from 26% to 49% in 2015, to 74% in 2021, and now, after the Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025 and the Indian Insurance Companies (Foreign Investment) Amendment Rules, 2025 that followed it in December 2025, to 100% under the automatic route. This piece sets out what the 2025 reform actually changed, which parts of it remain an enabling power waiting for a later notification rather than a working rule today, which single governance condition survived from the old regime, and what a foreign insurer, reinsurer or intermediary actually has to do to use the new ceiling rather than simply read about it.

Key points

  • The Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025 received presidential assent in December 2025, and the Indian Insurance Companies (Foreign Investment) Amendment Rules, 2025, notified around 30 December 2025, raised the automatic-route foreign investment ceiling in an Indian insurance company from 74% to 100%, subject to verification by the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India.
  • The widely reported removal of the old requirement that a majority of directors and key management personnel be resident Indian citizens is real, but one governance condition survived the final rules: at least one among the Chief Executive Officer, the managing director and the chairperson of the board has to be a resident Indian citizen.
  • The Act's 'composite licence' idea, letting a single insurer write more than one class of insurance business under one licence, is not yet a working licence category; the Act only inserted an enabling power letting the Central Government, in consultation with the IRDAI, notify a class of insurance business, and the actual composite licensing framework still depends on a further notification and IRDAI regulations that have not, on the available record, been issued yet.
  • Life Insurance Corporation of India's own foreign investment ceiling, reported at 20% under the automatic route, was not raised by this reform and continues to sit well below the ceiling that now applies to every other Indian insurer.
  • A separate amendment to Section 32A of the Insurance Act, 1938, restricting common directors or officers across an insurer and a banking or other financial company, was reported as not yet brought into force following representations from the industry, so an investor should check the current, specific notification position on Section 32A rather than assume it already applies in the form the Act's text describes.
  • Reaching 100% ownership of an Indian insurance company is a shareholding question answered by the 2025 reform; it does not by itself change the separate licensing conditions the IRDAI applies to the company itself, including minimum capital, solvency margin and fit-and-proper requirements that apply regardless of who owns the company's shares.

From 26% to 100%: Why This Change Looks Bigger Than the Last Two

India opened its insurance sector to private and foreign capital in 2000 with a foreign ownership ceiling of 26%, a figure that held for fifteen years before Parliament raised it to 49% in 2015. The 2021 increase to 74% came with a specific governance condition attached: a foreign-controlled insurer above the old 49% threshold had to keep a majority of its directors and key management persons as resident Indian citizens, and had to meet profit-retention conditions before repatriating dividends, a structure meant to reassure Parliament that higher foreign ownership would not mean the company's actual control moved entirely offshore.

The Union Budget for 2025-26 announced a move to 100%, and unlike the earlier two increases, which took months or years to translate from announcement into an operative rule, this one moved comparatively fast. The Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Insurance for All, Protection for All) (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025 passed both Houses of Parliament in mid-December 2025 and received presidential assent on 20 December 2025, and the Indian Insurance Companies (Foreign Investment) Amendment Rules, 2025, the instrument that actually carries the new ceiling into force, followed within the same fortnight, reported as notified around 30 December 2025 under notification number G.S.R. 928(E).

What makes this increase different in substance, not just in speed, is what the final rules actually dropped relative to 2021. The 2021 regime's defining feature, a board majority of resident Indian directors as the price of higher foreign ownership, did not survive into the 2025 rules in that form. A foreign parent evaluating this reform should treat the jump from 74% to 100% and the removal of the board-majority condition as two separate, both significant, changes rather than treating the headline percentage as the whole story.

The policy reasoning behind the pace of this particular change, reported consistently across commentary on the reform, centres on India's own insurance penetration figures, which have lagged behind the government's stated ambitions for the sector, and on the view that earlier, more cautious increases in 2015 and 2021 had not by themselves drawn in the scale of fresh foreign capital the sector's capital-intensive growth plans need. Adoption after the 2021 increase to 74% was reported as comparatively muted, with average foreign holding in private life insurers still sitting well below even that older ceiling as of late 2024, a data point the government's own case for moving straight to 100%, rather than to some intermediate figure, appears to have leaned on.

What the 2025 Act Actually Changed in the Insurance Act, 1938

The Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha Act works primarily by amending the Insurance Act, 1938 and the IRDAI Act, 1999, rather than by creating a wholly new statute, and several of its changes are more about regulatory housekeeping than about the foreign investment ceiling that has attracted most of the attention. Reported changes include a reworking of how an insurer's transfer of business or amalgamation under Section 35 of the Insurance Act is processed, the removal of a fixed three-year tenure for intermediary registration under a new sub-provision at Section 42D, and an increase in the maximum monetary penalty IRDAI can impose for non-compliance, reported as rising from Rs 1 crore to Rs 10 crore, alongside a new power for IRDAI to order disgorgement of wrongful gains.

The Act also widened the definition the IRDAI Act uses for 'class of insurance business', extending it, according to commentary on the enacted text, beyond the traditional life, general and health categories to cover reinsurance and any further class the Central Government chooses to notify in consultation with the Authority. This single definitional change is the statutory basis for the composite licensing idea discussed in the next section, and it is worth understanding on its own terms, as an enabling definition rather than a licence anyone can apply for today.

For an entity assessing this reform purely for its foreign investment implications, the practical point is that the 100% ceiling itself was not inserted by amending the Insurance Act's ownership provisions directly; it was carried into force through the separate Foreign Investment Rules discussed below, made under the foreign exchange framework rather than under the Insurance Act itself. The Act's own amendments matter mainly for how they widen what an insurer, once owned, can actually be licensed to do.

Composite Licensing: an Enabling Power, Not Yet a Licence

A composite licence, letting a single insurer sell life, general and health products under one authorisation instead of needing separate licensed entities for each, was one of the most discussed proposals in the run-up to the 2025 Act, and reporting on what the Bill would and would not contain shifted more than once as the legislation moved through Parliament. The version that was finally enacted does not itself create a composite licence category that an insurer can apply for. What it does is empower the Central Government, acting in consultation with the IRDAI, to notify a class of insurance business, and commentary on the enacted text is explicit that this language, while it lays the groundwork for a composite regime, is not itself drafted as a composite licensing provision.

This distinction matters for any foreign insurer weighing whether to enter India as a single composite entity rather than through the traditional separate life, general and health company structure. On the facts available, that option is not yet open; it depends on a further Central Government notification specifying the relevant class or classes of business, followed by IRDAI regulations actually implementing a composite licensing framework, neither of which has been confirmed as issued on the sources available for this piece. An investor planning an India entry around a composite structure should build in the real possibility that this framework arrives later than the ownership reform did, and should not structure a transaction on the assumption that composite licensing is already operative.

Until that further notification and the accompanying regulations appear, the practical legal position for a new entrant is unchanged from the pre-2025 position: a life insurance business, a general insurance business and a health insurance business each still generally require separate licensed entities, and 100% foreign ownership applies to each of those entities individually rather than to a single composite vehicle writing all three.

The Foreign Investment Rules: What the December 2025 Amendment Actually Removed

The instrument that actually raises the ownership ceiling is the Indian Insurance Companies (Foreign Investment) Amendment Rules, 2025, amending the Indian Insurance Companies (Foreign Investment) Rules, 2015. The amended rules permit aggregate foreign investment, including investment by foreign portfolio investors, in the equity shares of an Indian insurance company up to 100% of its paid-up equity capital under the automatic route, meaning no prior government approval is required for the investment itself, subject to the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India's own verification of the investment and the investor.

The draft version of these rules, published for comment in 2025, had proposed removing essentially every ownership-linked governance condition tied to the old 74% regime, including the requirement that a majority of directors and key management personnel be resident Indian citizens and the profit-retention condition on repatriating dividends that applied above 49% foreign ownership. Multiple law firm summaries of the rules as finally notified confirm that the majority-resident-director requirement was in fact dropped, along with the profit-retention condition, which is the single change that most materially distinguishes the 2025 regime from 2021's.

The rules as notified also removed certain other sub-clauses of the earlier rules entirely, reported in some commentary as omitting specific clauses of Rule 9(3) along with an entire Rule 4A, though the precise operative text of these omissions should be checked against the gazette notification itself before being relied on for a specific transaction, since secondary summaries of exactly which sub-clauses were omitted were not fully consistent with each other in the sources available.

The One Condition That Survived: Resident Indian Leadership

Removing the board-majority requirement did not mean removing every governance condition. The final rules kept a narrower version: at least one among the Chief Executive Officer, the managing director and the chairperson of the company's board has to be a resident Indian citizen. This is a materially lighter requirement than a majority of the board and key management personnel, and it gives a wholly foreign-owned Indian insurer considerably more flexibility in how it staffs its leadership team than the 2021 regime allowed, while still keeping one point of accountable, India-resident leadership in place.

A separate, related requirement reported in commentary on the rules concerns insurance intermediaries with majority foreign ownership, which are required to be incorporated as limited companies under the Companies Act, 2013, and, similarly to the insurer-level requirement, to keep at least one among the chairperson, managing director, CEO or principal officer as a resident Indian citizen. A foreign insurance broker or corporate agent structuring its Indian entity around the new ownership ceiling should treat this intermediary-specific condition as a separate compliance point from the insurer-level condition discussed above, since the two apply to different categories of regulated entity under the IRDAI's framework.

The resident-leadership condition also interacts with IRDAI's own fit-and-proper assessment of individual directors and key managerial personnel, an assessment the 2025 reform did not remove or soften in any respect reported in the sources reviewed for this piece. A wholly foreign-owned insurer still has to put forward individuals, whether for the resident leadership role specifically or for the rest of its board and management, who clear IRDAI's existing scrutiny on integrity, track record and relevant experience, so the governance simplification this reform brought should not be read as a general easing of IRDAI's appetite for scrutinising who actually runs a licensed insurer, only as a narrowing of how many of those individuals have to be resident Indian citizens specifically.

For a foreign group planning its own board and management structure for an Indian insurance subsidiary, the practical task this condition creates is straightforward to state and genuinely important to plan for early: identifying, before the investment closes rather than after, which specific individual will hold the CEO, managing director or chairperson role as a resident Indian citizen, and building that person's appointment into the transaction timeline alongside the capital infusion itself.

LIC and the Carve-Outs This Reform Did Not Touch

The 100% ceiling applies to Indian insurance companies generally, but it did not extend to Life Insurance Corporation of India, the state-owned life insurer that remains India's largest by a wide margin. Reporting on the reform describes LIC's own foreign investment ceiling as continuing to sit at 20% under the automatic route, unaffected by the broader sectoral increase, a reminder that 'the insurance sector' in Indian regulatory practice is not always treated as a single uniform category even where a reform's language appears to apply across the board.

Foreign portfolio investment into listed Indian insurers continues to be governed by its own separate framework, under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) Rules, 2019 and SEBI's FPI regulations, rather than by the insurance-specific rules discussed in this piece, and an investor planning to build an insurance exposure through portfolio flows rather than a direct strategic stake should look to that separate framework rather than assuming the 100% insurance-specific ceiling automatically governs portfolio investment in the same way.

Reinsurance intermediaries and insurance marketing firms sit in a similar position to the carve-outs above, in that the 2025 reform's headline ownership change does not, on the sources reviewed for this piece, describe any specific recalibration of the separate licensing categories IRDAI maintains for these narrower classes of regulated entity. A foreign group operating, or planning to operate, through one of these narrower categories should check IRDAI's current, category-specific regulations directly rather than assume the general 100% figure this piece describes for insurers and intermediaries necessarily carries across unchanged to every adjacent category IRDAI separately licenses.

Section 32A and the Common-Directorship Question Still Unresolved

One part of the 2025 Act has had a noticeably more uncertain path into force than the headline ownership change. Section 32A of the Insurance Act, 1938, as amended, would restrict an insurer from appointing a director or officer who simultaneously holds a director or officer position in a banking company or certain other financial entities, addressing a long-standing concern about conflicts of interest where the same individuals sit across an insurer's and a bank's leadership. Reported commentary on the Act's rollout states that this particular amendment has not been brought into force, specifically because of representations the industry made to the IRDAI after the Act was passed.

A foreign group that already has, or is planning to build, common board representation across an Indian banking affiliate and an Indian insurance affiliate should treat Section 32A as a live compliance question rather than a settled one, checking the current notification status of this specific provision before assuming either that it already restricts common directorships, or that it never will. Because the sources available describe only that the provision has not yet been brought into force, not whether or when it eventually will be, this is a point worth tracking rather than resolving definitively today.

Entry Routes for a Foreign Insurer Today

For a foreign insurer, reinsurer or broker actually planning to use the new ceiling, the available entry routes are, in substance, the same ones that existed before the reform, just without the old ownership cap limiting how much of the resulting entity the foreign party can hold. Setting up a new Indian insurance company from scratch remains possible but is, as it always has been, a capital- and time-intensive route, requiring the IRDAI's own licensing process, a minimum capital requirement, and the regulator's assessment of promoters' and directors' fitness, none of which the 2025 reform changed.

Acquiring the existing Indian joint venture partner's stake in an insurer that already holds an IRDAI licence is, for most foreign groups already present in India through a joint venture structure, the more immediately relevant route, and it is this route the reform most directly affects: a foreign partner that previously could not go beyond 74% without the Indian partner retaining some stake can now, subject to IRDAI's verification of the transaction and the resident-leadership condition discussed above, acquire the entire remaining stake and run the company as a wholly owned subsidiary.

A fourth, less immediately obvious route is relevant to a foreign group that already holds a minority stake below even the old 74% ceiling, through an earlier, more conservative investment made years before this reform was contemplated. For that group, the question is less about whether it can now go to 100% in principle, which it generally can, and more about whether the original joint venture or shareholders' agreement, often drafted a decade or more ago around a much lower ceiling, actually gives it a contractual right to increase its stake without the Indian partner's further consent, a point that depends entirely on how that specific agreement was drafted rather than on anything the 2025 reform itself changed.

Operating purely as an insurance intermediary, a broker, corporate agent or third-party administrator, rather than as a risk-carrying insurer, remains a distinct and generally lighter-touch route for a foreign entity whose primary interest is distribution or claims administration rather than underwriting risk on its own balance sheet, subject to the intermediary-specific resident-leadership and incorporation conditions noted earlier.

Reinsurance Branches: a Separate Route the 2025 Reform Did Not Change

A foreign reinsurer weighing an India strategy should not assume the 2025 ownership reform is the only route into the market, or even the most relevant one for a pure reinsurance business. Foreign reinsurers other than Lloyd's syndicates have, since 2015, been able to operate directly in India through a registered branch office under the IRDAI (Registration and Operations of Branch Offices of Foreign Reinsurers other than Lloyd's) Regulations, 2015, a structure that sidesteps the equity-ownership framework discussed throughout this piece entirely, because a branch has no separate shareholding to speak of in the first place. Lloyd's syndicates operate under their own, separate 2016 regulations rather than the 2015 regulations applicable to other foreign reinsurers.

Both regimes gate entry through a net owned funds test rather than an ownership percentage: the foreign reinsurer applying for a branch licence has to certify net owned funds, broadly paid-up equity capital, free reserves and securities premium, of at least Rs 5,000 crore as per its last audited balance sheet, calculated under the relevant regulation and, for the Lloyd's route, under a differently worded but broadly similar sub-regulation, and has to separately infuse a minimum capital of Rs 100 crore into the Indian branch itself once registered. A reinsurer operating this way remains under an ongoing obligation to report any erosion of more than 5% in its net owned funds to IRDAI, a continuing compliance point distinct from anything the 2025 ownership reform introduced.

IRDAI has reportedly circulated an exposure draft proposing to consolidate the separate Lloyd's and non-Lloyd's branch regimes into a single set of regulations with a revised net owned funds definition, though the sources available for this piece do not confirm whether that consolidation has since been finalised, and a reinsurer planning a branch entry should check IRDAI's current regulations directly rather than assume either the 2015 or 2016 regime, as described here, remains unamended by the time it actually applies.

What a Foreign Insurer Actually Has to Do Next

An entity planning to rely on the new ceiling should treat the 100% figure as the end point of a sequence rather than a single step it can complete on its own. The sequence begins with confirming, as a transaction-specific question rather than a general assumption, the current, fully notified text of the Foreign Investment Rules and whatever consolidated FDI policy position DPIIT has issued operationalising the 100% automatic route, since the exact date this became fully operative is reported differently across different commentary and should be confirmed against the current official notification rather than any single secondary source, including this one.

The next step is identifying the individual who will satisfy the resident Indian leadership condition, as either CEO, managing director or chairperson, and building that appointment, with whatever IRDAI fit-and-proper clearance it requires, into the transaction timetable rather than treating it as an afterthought once the capital has already moved. IRDAI's own verification of the investment, a step the automatic route does not dispense with even though it removes the need for prior government approval, should be factored in as a genuine review stage with its own timeline, not a formality that follows automatically once the shares have changed hands.

Finally, an entity whose plans depend specifically on a composite licence rather than on separately licensed life, general or health entities should build real contingency into its timeline for the possibility that the further notification and IRDAI regulations a composite framework needs have not yet been issued, treating that piece of the reform as aspirational until the specific notification confirming it actually appears.

A group operating across more than one of the routes this piece has described, say a foreign insurer that already holds equity in an Indian life insurance company and separately runs a reinsurance branch through its group reinsurance arm, should map each regulatory relationship separately rather than assuming a single compliance review covers the group's entire Indian footprint. The equity ownership rules, the resident-leadership condition, the branch net owned funds test and the Section 32A question are each administered under different regulatory instruments, and IRDAI's engagement with the group on one does not substitute for separate engagement on the others.

Buying Out the Indian Joint Venture Partner: What Diligence Actually Changes

For the large number of foreign insurers already present in India through a joint venture structure built around the old 49% or 74% ceilings, the most immediately usable consequence of the 2025 reform is the ability to buy out the Indian partner entirely rather than negotiate around a residual minimum stake that regulation, not commercial preference, used to require someone else to hold. That shift changes the shape of the diligence exercise, not just the final shareholding number. Where a foreign partner previously diligenced a target mainly to confirm the value of the incremental stake it was acquiring, buying out the remaining balance now means diligencing the company as a whole, as if for a full acquisition, because once the Indian partner is gone there is no longer a second shareholder whose own governance rights and information access have been quietly supplementing the foreign partner's own oversight of the business.

Specific points worth particular attention in this diligence include the insurer's actual solvency margin position measured against the regulatory minimum, since IRDAI's own approval of the ownership change will look closely at this regardless of what the buyer's own modelling shows; any related-party arrangements the Indian partner's own group had in place with the insurer, distribution agreements, outsourced services, office premises, which may need to be renegotiated or unwound once that partner is no longer a shareholder; and the status of any existing shareholders' agreement provisions, exit mechanics, valuation formulas, non-compete undertakings, that were drafted around the old ownership ceiling and may not translate cleanly to a full buyout without being renegotiated.

A buyer should also check whether the Indian partner's exit itself triggers any separate approval or disclosure obligation, under the insurer's own licence conditions, under the Competition Act where the transaction meets the relevant thresholds, or under securities law where either party is a listed company, treating the ownership change as a transaction requiring its own full regulatory clearance map rather than assuming the 2025 reform's removal of the ownership cap also removed every other approval that would otherwise apply to a change of this kind.

Tax Considerations a Wholly Owned Structure Does Not Avoid

Moving to full ownership changes who receives an Indian insurer's returns, not the tax framework those returns are taxed under. Dividends an Indian insurer pays to its foreign parent are taxed in the hands of the recipient, with the Indian company required to withhold tax at source under Section 195 of the Income Tax Act, at the rate the Act specifies or, where lower, the rate available under India's double taxation avoidance agreement with the parent's home jurisdiction, subject to the parent actually obtaining and furnishing a tax residency certificate and meeting whatever beneficial-ownership conditions the specific treaty requires. A foreign group should not assume that reaching 100% ownership itself changes this withholding position; it changes who the dividend goes to, not the rate at which it is taxed on the way out.

An eventual sale of shares in an unlisted Indian insurance company, whether a partial sale down from 100% or a full exit, is taxed as a capital gain under the Income Tax Act, with the applicable rate and holding-period classification turning on the ordinary rules for unlisted shares rather than anything specific to insurance, and again subject to whatever relief the seller's relevant tax treaty provides, an analysis that should be run on the specific facts of the exit rather than assumed from this reform alone. A foreign group planning its eventual exit strategy at the same time it plans its entry, rather than treating exit tax planning as a question for some later year, is generally better placed to structure the original investment, including the choice of holding jurisdiction, in a way that keeps its later options open.

None of this tax analysis is specific to the 2025 reform; it is the ordinary Indian cross-border tax framework that applied before the ownership ceiling changed and continues to apply after it. The point worth taking from this section is narrower and easy to miss amid the excitement about the new ceiling: a wholly owned Indian insurance subsidiary is not a tax-free or tax-simplified structure merely because no Indian partner is left to share in the economics, and a foreign group should run its usual cross-border tax diligence on a 100% structure with the same rigour it would apply to a 74% one.

Repatriation, Exit and the Separate FEMA Questions

Raising the ownership ceiling answers the question of how much of an Indian insurer a foreign entity can hold. It does not, by itself, answer the separate question of how that entity eventually takes its returns, or its capital, back out of India, which continues to be governed by the general foreign exchange framework under FEMA rather than by anything specific to the 2025 insurance reform. Dividends remain remittable subject to applicable withholding tax and the general FEMA reporting requirements that apply to any foreign-owned Indian company, and the removal of the old profit-retention condition specific to insurance above 49% foreign ownership means an insurer no longer faces that particular, sector-specific restriction, though it remains subject to the ordinary company law and tax conditions on dividend declaration that apply to any Indian company.

An eventual exit, whether a partial sale down from 100% or a full sale of the Indian insurance business, continues to require IRDAI's approval of the transferee under the same fit-and-proper and resident-leadership framework discussed above, and a foreign investor should plan an exit route with the same regulatory lead time in mind that the original entry required, rather than assuming that having entered under the simplified 2025 rules guarantees an equally simple path back out. Where the buyer on the way out is itself a foreign entity, the same 100% ceiling and the same resident-leadership and IRDAI verification conditions apply to it in turn, so a sale to another foreign strategic investor is not a route around the conditions this piece has described, only a transfer of who has to satisfy them next, and a seller should factor that buyer-side review timeline into its own exit planning rather than treating the sale as complete once a price has been agreed between the two private parties.

A foreign investor should also keep in view that FEMA's reporting obligations, the filing of the relevant forms reporting the issue or transfer of shares to a non-resident within the prescribed timeline, apply to the 100% investment in exactly the same way they applied to any smaller foreign investment before this reform, and a lapse on this reporting side, rather than anything to do with the insurance-specific ownership rules, remains one of the more common, avoidable compliance failures in cross-border Indian transactions generally. Treating the FEMA reporting timeline as a separate workstream from the IRDAI approval process, rather than assuming one automatically covers the other, avoids a gap that has tripped up otherwise well-advised transactions in other sectors and has no reason to behave differently in insurance.

What Remains Unsettled

Several pieces of this reform are better described as in motion than as settled. The exact date on which the 100% automatic route became fully operative is reported inconsistently across commentary, with some sources pointing to a DPIIT press note in early February 2026 and at least one further NDI Rules amendment reported months later in mid-2026, a sequence that suggests the operative framework kept being refined well after the headline Act and the December 2025 rules were first reported as the reform's complete package. A transaction relying on the 100% ceiling should be structured against the current, specifically confirmed notification position rather than against the state of the law as it was first announced.

The composite licensing framework and the Section 32A common-directorship restriction are each, for different reasons, enabling provisions awaiting further action rather than rules currently doing the full work their supporters announced, and a foreign insurer, broker or group with cross-sector banking and insurance interests in India should track both specifically rather than assuming either has already taken effect in the form originally described when the Act was passed.

The move from 74% to 100% foreign ownership in Indian insurance, and the parallel removal of the old board-majority and profit-retention conditions, is a genuine liberalisation rather than a cosmetic one, and it changes what a foreign group can realistically do with an existing or new Indian insurance platform. It is not, however, a single switch that was flipped once in December 2025 and has worked identically ever since; the resident-leadership condition survived in a narrower form, LIC sits outside the reform entirely, and the composite licensing idea that generated much of the initial commentary remains an enabling power rather than a working licence category on the facts available.

A foreign insurer, reinsurer or broker evaluating this reform should treat the headline percentage as the opening fact of the analysis, not its conclusion, and should confirm the current, specific notification position on each of the open points this piece has flagged, the composite licensing framework, the Section 32A timeline, and the exact date the automatic route became fully operative, before building a transaction timetable around any of them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a foreign insurer now own 100% of an Indian insurance company?

Yes, under the automatic route, following the Indian Insurance Companies (Foreign Investment) Amendment Rules, 2025, which raised the ceiling from 74% to 100%, subject to IRDAI's verification of the investment and the investor. Life Insurance Corporation of India is a specific exception, with its own foreign investment ceiling reported at 20%.

Does the board still need a majority of resident Indian directors?

No, that requirement, which applied above 49% foreign ownership under the 2021 regime, was removed by the final 2025 rules. One narrower condition survived: at least one among the CEO, the managing director and the board chairperson must be a resident Indian citizen.

Can a foreign insurer get a single composite licence to sell life, general and health insurance together?

Not yet, as a working licence category. The 2025 Act only inserted an enabling power letting the Central Government, in consultation with IRDAI, notify a class of insurance business; the actual composite licensing framework still needs a further notification and IRDAI regulations that have not been confirmed as issued.

Is there still a condition on retaining profits before repatriating dividends?

The profit-retention condition that applied to insurers with foreign ownership above 49% under the 2021 regime was removed by the final 2025 rules, according to multiple law firm summaries of the notified text. Ordinary company law and tax rules on dividend declaration and withholding continue to apply regardless.

What about an insurance broker or corporate agent rather than a risk-carrying insurer?

A majority foreign-owned insurance intermediary is required to be incorporated as a limited company under the Companies Act, 2013, and to keep at least one among its chairperson, managing director, CEO or principal officer as a resident Indian citizen, a condition reported as separate from, though similar to, the insurer-level requirement.

Does this reform remove the restriction on common directors across a bank and an insurer in the same group?

Not currently in practice. Section 32A of the Insurance Act, 1938, as amended by the 2025 Act, would restrict such common directorships, but reported commentary states this specific provision has not been brought into force, following representations the industry made to IRDAI, so the current, confirmed notification position should be checked before assuming the restriction already applies.

Does a foreign reinsurer have to use the equity ownership route at all?

No. A foreign reinsurer other than a Lloyd's syndicate can operate directly in India through a registered branch under the IRDAI (Registration and Operations of Branch Offices of Foreign Reinsurers other than Lloyd's) Regulations, 2015, subject to a net owned funds test reported at Rs 5,000 crore and a minimum branch capital infusion reported at Rs 100 crore, a route that does not involve the equity ownership rules discussed throughout this piece at all. Lloyd's syndicates use a separate, broadly similar 2016 regime.

When exactly did 100% FDI in insurance become available?

The Act received presidential assent on 20 December 2025, and the Foreign Investment Rules carrying the 100% ceiling were notified around 30 December 2025. Commentary differs on the exact date the automatic route became fully operative in practice, with reports pointing to a DPIIT notification in early February 2026 and a further NDI Rules amendment reported months later, so the current official notification should be checked for a specific transaction.
