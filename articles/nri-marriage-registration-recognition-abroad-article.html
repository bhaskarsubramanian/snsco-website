Marriage, Registration and Recognition for NRIs: What the Law in India Actually Requires

A marriage that is complete in the eyes of an Indian family and a marriage that an American, British or Canadian immigration officer will accept as proven are not always the same document. This article sets out what Indian law requires to register a marriage, when a marriage solemnised abroad needs anything further in India, what the three largest NRI destination countries actually look for, and what changes once a marriage breaks down or a second marriage is contemplated.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 5 November 2026 · Law stated as on 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An NRI couple's marriage is tested against more documentary standards than most Indian couples ever encounter: an Indian personal-law requirement, a civil registration rule that varies by state, and then a foreign consulate's own checklist that was never written with an Indian wedding in mind. This article works through registration under the Hindu Marriage Act and the Special Marriage Act, marriages performed or registered abroad under the Foreign Marriage Act, what the US, UK and Canada actually accept as proof of marriage, and what the law does about a second marriage or a deserted spouse.

Key points

  • A Hindu, Buddhist, Jain or Sikh marriage solemnised with the proper rites is valid in India whether or not it is registered, since Section 8(5) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 makes clear that registration affects evidence of the marriage, not its validity; the registration certificate becomes essential only when a foreign authority, not an Indian one, is asked to recognise the marriage.
  • The Special Marriage Act's thirty-day public notice and objection procedure under Sections 6 and 7 has been held directory rather than mandatory by the Allahabad High Court in Safia Sultan v. State of U.P. (2021), meaning parties can ask in writing that the notice not be published, but practice varies by Marriage Officer and the question remains the subject of pending litigation before the Supreme Court.
  • A marriage solemnised abroad under foreign law is generally recognised in India without any further step, but registering it under Section 17 of the Foreign Marriage Act, 1969 produces an Indian-law certificate that Indian banks, passport offices and registrars can accept without independently assessing a foreign marriage law, which most are neither equipped nor willing to do.
  • The United States, the United Kingdom and Canada each look for a government-issued civil registration certificate of an Indian marriage, not a religious record alone; a pandit's certificate, Nikahnama or church document without civil registration is treated as insufficient by all three, however strong the relationship evidence otherwise is.
  • An apostille from the Ministry of External Affairs is generally required for an Indian-issued marriage certificate to be used in a Hague Convention country such as the United States, but it cannot be used to apostille a certificate issued abroad; a foreign-issued certificate needs an apostille from the country where it was issued, a distinction that trips up couples who send documents to the wrong authority.
  • A second marriage contracted while an earlier marriage subsists is void under Hindu personal law and exposes the person who contracted it to prosecution under Section 82 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (up to seven years, rising to ten where the first marriage was concealed); relying on a foreign divorce decree that is not recognised under Indian law to remarry carries the same risk.
  • A deserted spouse can obtain a maintenance order under Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 from an Indian court even where the other spouse lives abroad; the foreign location affects how the order is enforced, not whether the court has the power to make it.

Why an NRI couple cannot treat marriage registration as optional paperwork

A couple married in a gurdwara in Jalandhar or a temple in Kanpur usually does not think about a registration certificate on the day itself. The priest performs the rites, the families exchange gifts, and everyone present treats the ceremony as the marriage. Under most personal laws this is broadly correct. A Hindu marriage solemnised with the proper rites is a valid marriage whether or not it is ever registered, and the Supreme Court has said so more than once. For a couple who will spend their married life in India, the absence of a registration certificate is rarely more than an inconvenience.

An NRI couple does not get that luxury. The certificate nobody thought about at the wedding becomes, within a year or two, the document a US consulate wants before it will issue a spousal visa, the proof a UK Home Office caseworker insists on before treating cohabitation evidence as meaningful, or the paper an Indian passport office asks for before it will add a spouse's name to a passport. None of these authorities were present at the ceremony. All of them work from documents, and the document they recognise is a registration certificate issued by an authority empowered by Indian law, not a priest's blessing or a wedding video.

The difficulty is compounded by timing. Couples who marry in India before one of them moves abroad often register the marriage only when a visa application forces the issue, sometimes years after the wedding and sometimes after the relationship has already begun to strain. Registering late is possible under most state rules, but it usually means more paperwork, not less: affidavits explaining the delay, witnesses who can still be located, and in some states a magistrate's order. Registering close to the time of the wedding is the sensible course; registering late is a fallback that works but costs more in time and difficulty than it would have cost at the outset.

Two statutes, two very different procedures

An Indian marriage can be registered under one of two frameworks, and which one applies depends on how the marriage was solemnised, not on where the parties live. A marriage performed according to Hindu, Buddhist, Jain or Sikh rites is governed by the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, whose Section 8 does not itself prescribe a registration procedure but authorises each State Government to make its own rules. The result is a separate procedure in nearly every state: Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, West Bengal, Odisha and Karnataka, among others, each have their own Hindu Marriage Registration Rules, with their own forms, timelines and fees, and the state of solemnisation, not the state of residence abroad, generally decides which rules apply. Sub-section (5) of Section 8 makes clear that a marriage's validity does not depend on registration; an unregistered Hindu marriage remains a marriage, and the certificate is evidence of it, not a condition for it.

A marriage between parties who do not share a common personal law, or who simply prefer a civil ceremony, is instead solemnised and registered under the Special Marriage Act, 1954. Unlike the Hindu Marriage Act, the Special Marriage Act creates the marriage through registration itself; there is no separate religious ceremony that stands on its own. Section 15 of the same Act also allows a marriage already solemnised under another law, including a Hindu, Muslim, Christian or Parsi marriage, to be registered under the Special Marriage Act instead, a route some NRI couples use deliberately because a Special Marriage Act certificate is a single, nationally uniform document rather than a state-specific one.

For an NRI couple deciding which route to use, the practical difference is this: a Hindu Marriage Act certificate depends on state rules that an immigration officer abroad has probably never seen before and may ask the applicant to explain, while a Special Marriage Act certificate follows one statute throughout India and tends to be more immediately recognisable to a foreign caseworker comparing it against a checklist. Neither certificate is inherently better evidence of a valid marriage. The choice is really about which procedure is easier to complete correctly, and about whether the couple is willing to go through the Special Marriage Act's notice procedure, which is where the next complication begins.

The Special Marriage Act's notice requirement: what the law says and what courts have done with it

Sections 5 to 7 of the Special Marriage Act, 1954 set out a procedure that looks straightforward on the page. Either party gives notice of the intended marriage to the Marriage Officer of the district where at least one of them has resided for not less than thirty days. The officer publishes the notice, keeps it open to public objection for thirty days, and any person can object to the marriage on specified grounds such as a subsisting prior marriage or want of age. If no objection is upheld, the marriage can proceed once the thirty days have run.

This notice-and-objection procedure has become one of the most litigated features of the Act, for a simple reason: publishing a couple's names, addresses and intention to marry in a public notice has been used by disapproving families to locate and pressure interfaith or inter-caste couples before the marriage takes place, sometimes with violent results. The Allahabad High Court addressed this directly in Safia Sultan v. State of U.P., decided in January 2021, holding that compulsory publication interferes with a couple's fundamental rights to liberty and privacy, and that Sections 6 and 7 should be read as directory rather than mandatory. On that reading, parties can ask the Marriage Officer, in writing, not to publish the notice or invite objections, and the officer should accommodate that request rather than publish automatically.

Many Marriage Officers across the country have followed this approach, but it has not been adopted uniformly, and the Supreme Court has not finally settled the point. A challenge to the constitutionality of the notice and objection provisions remains pending before the Delhi High Court, after the Supreme Court in December 2024 declined a plea to transfer that challenge to itself, and the plain text of Sections 6 and 7 has not been amended to make the Safia Sultan position the statutory rule. An NRI couple planning to marry under the Special Marriage Act, particularly during a short visit to India timed around work leave, should raise the privacy point with the Marriage Officer at the outset and ask in writing whether publication can be dispensed with, rather than assuming either that publication is unavoidable or that it will automatically be skipped. Officers in different districts, and even different officers in the same district, have taken different views on this, and it is not an area where a couple should discover the office's practice for the first time at the counter.

One further point matters for an NRI timeline. Section 14 sets a three-month window from the date of the notice given under Section 5 within which the marriage must be solemnised; if the marriage is not solemnised within that window, the notice and the proceedings arising from it lapse and a fresh notice is required. For a couple whose foreign-based partner is in India for a limited period, missing that window because of a delayed objection process or a documentation gap can mean the entire notice procedure has to be repeated on a future visit, a cost worth planning around rather than discovering afterward.

Marrying before an Indian Marriage Officer abroad

An Indian citizen does not have to return to India to marry under Indian law. The Foreign Marriage Act, 1969 lets a Marriage Officer attached to an Indian diplomatic or consular mission abroad solemnise a marriage under Section 14, provided at least one of the parties is an Indian citizen and the marriage is permitted under the personal law or custom applicable to at least one of them. The procedure broadly mirrors the Special Marriage Act's notice-and-objection structure, including a notice period during which objections can be raised, though the specific forms, fees and local requirements vary from mission to mission. The first step for any couple going this route is to check the website of the specific embassy or consulate that will handle the marriage, not a general guide, for the current document checklist.

A marriage solemnised this way is registered by the Marriage Officer at the time, and the certificate issued is recognised in India in the same way a Special Marriage Act certificate is, since the Foreign Marriage Act is modelled closely on it. This route suits a couple who are both already abroad, or where the Indian partner cannot easily travel to India for the ceremony, but it still requires planning around the mission's own notice period and appointment availability, which can run into several weeks depending on the post.

Registering a marriage already solemnised abroad under foreign law

The more common situation for an NRI couple is the reverse: the marriage has already taken place abroad, under the law of the country where the couple lives, perhaps at a registry office in London, a courthouse in New Jersey, or before a civil official in Toronto. Section 17 of the Foreign Marriage Act allows this marriage to be registered with the Indian Marriage Officer at the relevant Indian mission, so long as the officer is satisfied that the marriage was validly solemnised under the law of that foreign country and that at least one party is an Indian citizen. Sub-section (6) of Section 17 provides that, once registered, the marriage is deemed to have been solemnised under the Foreign Marriage Act from the date of registration, giving the couple an Indian-law marriage certificate referencing their foreign ceremony rather than requiring them to marry a second time in India.

Registration under Section 17 is not compulsory for the marriage to be recognised in India as a matter of private international law; a marriage validly performed abroad under the law of that place is ordinarily recognised in India under general conflict-of-laws principles even without this registration. What registration adds is a document that an Indian authority, a bank, a passport office, a property registrar, a court, can accept at face value without being asked to independently assess the validity of a foreign marriage law, which is not something most Indian officials are equipped or willing to do on their own. For an NRI couple who intend to use their marriage for any Indian-law purpose, holding both the foreign marriage certificate and an Indian Section 17 registration, or at minimum an equivalent translation and apostille of the foreign certificate, removes an entire category of friction later.

Where the marriage was performed abroad but neither party registers it under Section 17, the couple should at minimum obtain a certified, apostilled or legalised copy of the foreign marriage certificate, translated into English if it is not already, since this is the document Indian authorities will eventually ask to see. An apostille from the country where the marriage took place, not an Indian MEA apostille, is usually required for a foreign-issued certificate, and the requirements differ meaningfully from the apostille the MEA applies to an Indian-issued certificate for use abroad, a distinction that catches out more than a few couples who apostille the wrong document.

A ceremony without civil registration: the gap that surfaces years later

Not every marriage that an Indian family treats as complete meets the civil registration standard that a foreign government will demand. A pandit's certificate, a Nikahnama solemnised by a qazi, an Arya Samaj marriage certificate, or a church record, each of these can be a perfectly valid religious marriage under the relevant personal law, and in India that is usually sufficient. A foreign consulate reviewing a spousal visa application is typically looking for something more specific: a government-issued civil registration certificate under the Hindu Marriage Act, the Special Marriage Act, or the equivalent personal-law registration framework, with the religious ceremony record treated as supporting evidence rather than the primary document.

This gap rarely surfaces at the wedding. It surfaces when the visa application is filed, sometimes years later, and the couple discovers that the only document they have is a religious certificate the consulate's checklist does not recognise on its own. The fix, registering the marriage under the applicable state's Hindu Marriage Registration Rules after the fact, is available in nearly every state, but it requires the couple to establish, usually by affidavit and sometimes before a magistrate, that the marriage did in fact take place on the date claimed, with witnesses who were present and are still available to confirm it. The earlier a couple does this, the easier that evidentiary burden is to meet; waiting a decade makes it progressively harder to find witnesses or satisfy an increasingly cautious registrar.

A related trap catches couples who married under one personal law but later want the uniformity of a Special Marriage Act certificate, perhaps because one spouse's foreign visa checklist specifically names it. Section 15 permits this conversion, but only on proof that the original marriage was valid, which again means affidavits and, where the registrar asks for it, witnesses. Starting this process while the people who attended the original ceremony are still reachable saves a second round of difficulty on top of the first.

What the United States actually wants to see for an I-130 petition

A US citizen or lawful permanent resident filing Form I-130 to sponsor a spouse, or a spouse filing for adjustment or an immigrant visa on that basis, needs to establish that a legally valid marriage exists, and USCIS and the relevant consular post look to the law of the place where the marriage was performed to decide that question. For a marriage performed in India, this generally means a government-issued registration certificate, not a religious record alone. Where the certificate was issued by an Indian state authority, an apostille from India's Ministry of External Affairs is typically needed before the document will be accepted for use in the United States, since India is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention and the US accepts apostilled documents from member countries without further consular legalisation.

Where the marriage took place in the United States itself, the certificate comes from the state or county where it was registered, typically from the Secretary of State's office in that state, and does not go through the Indian MEA apostille process at all. Couples sometimes confuse the two apostille pathways and send an Indian-issued certificate to the wrong authority, or try to have a US-issued certificate apostilled in India, which generally cannot be done since an apostille can only be issued by the authority of the country where the document originated. Getting the right certificate to the right apostille authority, and allowing for the processing time involved, which can run to several weeks depending on the state's own verification steps, is worth building into the timeline well before a visa interview is scheduled.

Beyond the certificate itself, USCIS and consular officers also assess whether the marriage is bona fide, meaning entered into for a genuine marital relationship rather than primarily to obtain an immigration benefit. Evidence of a joint life, shared finances, correspondence, travel together, family recognition of the marriage, matters here independently of whether the paperwork is in order, and a couple with a technically perfect certificate but little else to show can still face additional scrutiny or a request for further evidence.

UK spouse visa: proving a marriage is valid, not just that a ceremony happened

The UK's spouse visa route asks a similar underlying question in different words: is this a legally valid marriage or civil partnership, and is the relationship genuine and subsisting. For an Indian marriage, the Home Office's starting expectation is an official state-issued marriage certificate under the Hindu Marriage Act, the Special Marriage Act, or the relevant personal-law framework, with a certified English translation if the original is in a regional language. A religious document alone, a pandit's certificate, a Nikahnama without civil registration, or a church record without accompanying civil registration, is treated as insufficient on its own to establish the legal validity the Home Office is looking for, even though the same document might be entirely adequate for most purposes within India.

Beyond the certificate, UK caseworkers give close attention to relationship evidence: cohabitation records, joint financial commitments, communication history, and in some cases a prior period of knowing each other that makes an arranged or introduced marriage credible rather than suspicious on its face. Applicants should check the current Home Office guidance directly on English language and financial requirements, since these change periodically and are not always reflected accurately in commercial immigration guides, several of which disagree with each other on points like whether a police clearance certificate is required, a disagreement that suggests at least some of that commentary has not kept pace with current practice.

Canada spousal sponsorship: registration and the genuineness inquiry

Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada similarly expects a registered marriage certificate, not merely evidence that a ceremony or solemnisation occurred, and has stated that a record of solemnisation or a marriage licence alone is not accepted as proof of marriage for sponsorship purposes. Where the Indian marriage certificate is in Hindi or another regional language, a certified translation is required, and some applicants are also asked to apostille the certificate, though practice on this point is not entirely uniform across visa offices, so the specific checklist for the processing office handling the file should be followed rather than assumed.

IRCC's genuineness assessment goes further than most other jurisdictions' in the volume of relationship evidence it typically expects: photographs from shared events over time, records of communication, evidence of financial interdependence, and a credible account of how the relationship developed, particularly where the couple married relatively quickly after meeting, since officers are alert to marriages of convenience and will probe the relationship history in an interview if anything in the file seems thin. Where a couple cannot produce a formal marriage certificate at all, Canada's common-law partner route offers an alternative, but it requires documented evidence of twelve months of continuous cohabitation, an evidentiary exercise of its own rather than simply a fallback that avoids the marriage certificate question.

Second marriages, a subsisting first marriage, and bigamy exposure

Indian personal law generally does not permit a second marriage while an earlier one subsists. Under the Hindu Marriage Act, a marriage solemnised while either party already has a living spouse is void, and this is not a technicality cured by simply not telling anyone about the first marriage. The criminal consequence sits alongside the civil one: what used to be prosecuted under Section 494 of the Indian Penal Code is now charged under Section 82 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, which carries imprisonment of up to seven years with a fine, rising to up to ten years where the second marriage was concealed from the new spouse, broadly preserving the structure of the old Sections 494 and 495 of the IPC.

An NRI context adds two complications worth flagging specifically. A person who obtains a foreign divorce that is not recognised as valid under Indian law, because, for instance, an Indian court never had jurisdiction to grant it or the other spouse was not properly served, can find that the first marriage is treated in India as still subsisting even though a foreign court's decree says otherwise. Marrying again on the strength of that foreign decree can then expose the person to a bigamy charge in India regardless of their good-faith belief that they were free to remarry. The question of when a foreign divorce decree does and does not bind an Indian court deserves its own treatment rather than a summary here, and is covered in full elsewhere.

The Supreme Court has also held that a person cannot evade the bigamy provisions by converting to a religion that permits polygamy, such as Islam, purely to contract a second marriage while the first Hindu marriage remains legally undissolved; the conversion does not retroactively validate a second marriage entered into in bad faith for that purpose. Courts have drawn a distinction between the spouses in some reported cases: a person who marries someone whose earlier marriage is still subsisting, without knowing that fact, has in some instances been held not liable for the offence, while the person who concealed the subsisting marriage remains liable, though this line of authority is fact-specific and should not be read as a general defence available whenever a party claims ignorance.

When the marriage breaks down from a distance: desertion, maintenance and the limits of what a deserted spouse can do

Marriage registration, apostilles and visa paperwork assume the relationship is intact. A meaningful share of NRI marriage disputes involve a spouse, often the one who married into the family and remained in India while the other spouse works abroad, being deserted, left without maintenance, or unable to trace the other spouse's current location and income. The maintenance remedy that existed under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure now sits under Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, in substantially the same form, letting a magistrate order a husband to maintain a wife, and in some circumstances a wife to maintain a husband, who is unable to maintain themselves. A deserted spouse is not barred from this remedy merely because the other spouse now lives abroad; Indian courts have jurisdiction over maintenance petitions where the applicant resides, and the fact that the respondent is overseas affects enforcement, not the court's power to pass an order.

Section 144's disentitling provision cuts the other way too, and matters for completeness: a spouse shown to be living in adultery, or who refuses without sufficient reason to live with the other spouse, can be denied maintenance under sub-section (4), though courts have read living in adultery narrowly, generally requiring continuous and repeated conduct rather than an isolated lapse, and have been equally cautious about accepting a bare refusal-to-cohabit argument where the refusal has a genuine basis such as cruelty. Where maintenance has been ordered and the paying spouse is abroad with assets or income there, enforcing that order across borders raises a separate set of questions, including which countries recognise and enforce Indian maintenance orders and through what mechanism, questions this article does not repeat since they are addressed in full elsewhere.

Two further situations recur often enough in NRI matrimonial disputes to flag briefly, both of which have dedicated treatments the reader should turn to for the full picture rather than relying on a summary here: a look-out circular issued against a spouse attempting to leave India while matrimonial or criminal proceedings are pending, and the question of whether and how a foreign divorce decree is recognised by an Indian court, which determines whether either spouse is actually free to remarry and whether ancillary orders such as alimony made abroad can be enforced in India at all.

A short checklist before and after the ceremony

Before the wedding: decide whether the marriage will be solemnised under the Hindu Marriage Act or equivalent personal law, or under the Special Marriage Act, and if the latter, raise the notice-publication question with the Marriage Officer in writing at the outset rather than assuming either outcome. If either party will need the marriage recognised abroad within the following year or two for a visa or sponsorship application, register the marriage at the time rather than treating registration as something to arrange later. If the marriage takes place abroad, keep the original foreign certificate, obtain any apostille from the country of solemnisation promptly, and consider registering it with the nearest Indian mission under Section 17 of the Foreign Marriage Act if an Indian-law certificate would be useful.

After the wedding: before applying for a US, UK or Canadian spousal visa, check the current, specific document checklist published by that country's own immigration authority rather than a general guide, since apostille and translation requirements, and the weight given to relationship evidence, change and vary by jurisdiction in ways no single summary can capture. If either spouse was previously married, resolve and document the status of that earlier marriage, including whether a foreign divorce decree is recognised in India, before relying on it to remarry. If the relationship is already in difficulty, treat registration and documentation as protective steps in their own right; a spouse who has none of the paperwork this article describes is, in practice, starting any later maintenance, custody or property dispute from a weaker position than one who does.

None of what registration under the Hindu Marriage Act, the Special Marriage Act or the Foreign Marriage Act actually requires is difficult in isolation. What makes it complicated for an NRI couple specifically is that three or four different legal systems, Indian personal law, Indian civil registration rules, the destination country's immigration rules, and sometimes a third country's apostille process, all have to line up correctly before the paperwork does what the couple needs it to do.

The couples who avoid trouble later are usually the ones who treat the registration certificate as part of the wedding itself rather than an afterthought, who get the right apostille on the right document the first time, and who are honest early about anything in either spouse's marital history that could complicate recognition later. The couples who run into difficulty are rarely the ones with a genuinely invalid marriage; they are far more often the ones who assumed a religious ceremony and a wedding video would be treated by a consulate officer the same way it was treated by their own families, and who found out otherwise at the worst possible time.

Frequently Asked Questions

We married in a temple and have only the priest's certificate and wedding photographs. Is our marriage valid in India?

Yes, for most purposes within India a Hindu marriage solemnised with the proper rites is valid whether or not it is registered; Section 8(5) of the Hindu Marriage Act says registration does not affect the marriage's validity. The gap appears when you need the marriage recognised by a foreign authority, which typically wants a government-issued registration certificate rather than a religious record, so register the marriage under your state's Hindu Marriage Registration Rules if you expect to need it abroad.

Can we marry under the Special Marriage Act without the thirty-day public notice being published?

Several courts, most notably the Allahabad High Court in Safia Sultan v. State of U.P. (2021), have held that publication under Sections 6 and 7 is directory and can be dispensed with if the parties ask the Marriage Officer in writing. Not every Marriage Officer follows this, and the point remains the subject of pending litigation, so raise the request explicitly and in writing with the specific officer handling your file rather than assuming the outcome.

We got married at a courthouse in the US. Do we need to register the marriage again in India?

Not necessarily for the marriage to be recognised in India, since a marriage validly performed under the law of the place where it occurred is generally recognised here. What you may want, for convenience in dealing with Indian authorities later, is to register it under Section 17 of the Foreign Marriage Act at the nearest Indian mission, which gives you an Indian-law certificate referencing the US marriage, or at minimum keep a properly apostilled and translated copy of the US certificate.

The US consulate rejected my spouse's visa documents because our marriage certificate was only from the Arya Samaj. What do we do?

An Arya Samaj certificate is a religious document, and most consulates, including the US, generally want a government-issued civil registration certificate instead or in addition. Register the marriage under the Hindu Marriage Registration Rules of the state where it was solemnised, which is available even some years after the ceremony with supporting affidavits and witnesses, then apostille the resulting certificate through the Ministry of External Affairs before resubmitting it.

My husband is abroad and has stopped sending money. Can I get a maintenance order even though he lives overseas?

Yes. An Indian magistrate's court where you reside has jurisdiction to pass a maintenance order under Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita even though your husband lives abroad; his location affects how the order is enforced against his foreign income or assets, not whether the court can make the order in the first place.

Can my husband avoid a bigamy charge by converting to Islam before marrying a second time?

No. The Supreme Court has held that a Hindu husband who converts purely to contract a second marriage while his first marriage remains legally undissolved is still guilty of bigamy under the applicable provision, now Section 82 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita; the conversion does not retroactively validate the second marriage.

I got a divorce decree from a UK court. Can I remarry in India on the strength of that decree?

Only if that decree is recognised under Indian law, which depends on questions like whether the foreign court had proper jurisdiction over the marriage and whether you were given a fair opportunity to contest the proceedings. A decree that is not recognised in India leaves your earlier marriage subsisting here, and remarrying on the strength of an unrecognised foreign decree can expose you to a bigamy charge regardless of your belief that you were free to remarry.

Does a Canadian or UK caseworker accept our pandit's wedding certificate if we also have years of joint bank statements and photographs?

Relationship evidence like joint finances and photographs supports the genuineness of the marriage but does not substitute for proof that the marriage is legally valid. Both Canada and the UK expect a government-issued registration certificate, or, for Canada specifically, twelve months of documented cohabitation under the common-law partner route if no such certificate exists, so the pandit's certificate alone is unlikely to satisfy either requirement even with strong relationship evidence behind it.
