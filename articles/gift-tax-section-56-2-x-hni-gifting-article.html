Tax on Gifts in India: The Relative Exemption, Clubbing and HNI Gifting Mistakes

A gift between family members is routinely treated as tax-free in India, but the exemption has a precise boundary, and a family that gifts outside it, or inside it without reading the clubbing rules, can end up with a tax demand on money that already changed hands years ago

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

India abolished its Gift Tax Act in 1998 and the common belief that followed, that gifts between Indians carry no tax consequence at all, has never been accurate. Since 2004, and in its present form since 2017, the Income-tax Act taxes a gift as the recipient's income unless a specific exemption applies, and the exemption for gifts between relatives, while generous, defines 'relative' far more narrowly than most families assume.

Key points

  • A gift of money exceeding Rs 50,000 in a year, or immovable or movable property whose value crosses the same threshold, is taxable as the recipient's income under the Income-tax Act unless an exemption applies; the position carries over largely unchanged from Section 56(2)(x) of the 1961 Act into Section 92(2)(m) of the Income-tax Act, 2025, now in force.
  • The 'relative' exemption covers a defined, closed list: spouse; brother or sister; brother or sister of the spouse; brother or sister of either parent; any lineal ascendant or descendant of the individual; any lineal ascendant or descendant of the spouse; and the spouse of any person in that list. A cousin, a friend, or an in-law outside that list is not a 'relative' for this purpose, however close the relationship feels.
  • Gifts received on marriage, by inheritance or under a will, in contemplation of death, from a local authority, or from a registered charitable trust or institution are separately exempt regardless of who gives them, and these exemptions often rescue a gift that the relative test alone would not cover.
  • A tax-free gift under the relative exemption is not necessarily free of further tax consequence: income later earned from the gifted asset can be clubbed back into the giver's own income under Section 64 when the gift runs between spouses or to a minor child, even though the gift itself attracted no tax at the point of transfer.
  • A gift of immovable property is tested against its stamp duty value, not the figure written into the gift deed, and a gift of unlisted shares or other movable property is tested against a fair market value computed under prescribed valuation rules, both of which routinely produce a tax exposure that the parties never anticipated when the gift deed was drafted.
  • Gifting an asset does not reset its tax cost for the recipient: under Section 49(1), the recipient inherits the giver's original cost of acquisition and holding period, so the tax deferred rather than eliminated resurfaces, often years later, when the recipient eventually sells the asset.
  • A gift to one's own Hindu Undivided Family, and a gift routed through a family trust, each carry their own clubbing and valuation traps that are frequently missed in family wealth planning, and each needs to be checked against the current law separately from an ordinary gift between two individuals.

The abolition that never actually happened

The Gift Tax Act, 1958 was repealed with effect from October 1998, and for roughly six years after that repeal, India genuinely had no statute taxing gifts as such. That six-year gap is the source of a belief that has outlived its own cause: a great many people in India, including some who should know better, still describe gifts between family members as untouched by tax law altogether.

That stopped being true from 1 September 2004, when Parliament inserted a new clause into Section 56(2) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 bringing certain gifts of money back into the tax net as income from other sources. The provision was broadened substantially in 2009 and again in 2017, when it took its most recent shape as clause (x), extending the tax to gifts of immovable property and other movable property and not merely money. A family relying on advice or assumptions formed before 2017, or worse, before 2004, is relying on a version of the law that no longer exists.

The law has moved again since. The Income-tax Act, 2025 came into force on 1 April 2026, replacing the 1961 Act for income earned from that date, and multiple tax publications report that the gift provision now sits at Section 92(2)(m) of the new Act rather than Section 56(2)(x), with the substance of the rule carried forward largely unchanged. A gift made today, in the financial year 2026-27, is governed by the new numbering; a gift made before 1 April 2026 and still under assessment is governed by the old one. The two provisions should be read as continuous for practical purposes, but a notice or a return that cites the wrong one is worth flagging rather than ignoring.

The basic rule: what counts as a taxable gift

The provision, under either numbering, works by exception. The general rule taxes three categories of receipt without consideration in the hands of an individual or a Hindu Undivided Family: any sum of money exceeding Rs 50,000 in aggregate during the year; any immovable property whose stamp duty value exceeds Rs 50,000, received without consideration, or received for a consideration that falls short of the stamp duty value by more than the prescribed margin; and any other specified movable property (shares, jewellery, artwork, bullion and similar items named in the provision) whose fair market value, again tested the same way, crosses Rs 50,000.

Two features of this structure catch people out regularly. First, the Rs 50,000 figure is a threshold for the entire category in the year, not a per-gift or per-giver allowance; a person who receives four separate cash gifts of Rs 20,000 each from four different non-relatives in the same year has crossed the threshold and the whole amount, not merely the excess over Rs 50,000, becomes taxable. Second, the test for property is value-based rather than document-based: a sale deed that records a price lower than the stamp duty value by more than the permitted margin is treated, for this purpose, as if the shortfall itself were a gift, even where the parties genuinely intended and executed a sale rather than a gift.

Where a taxable gift exists, it is taxed as income from other sources in the hands of the recipient, at that recipient's own slab rate, in the year of receipt. There is no separate gift tax rate and no exemption limit beyond the Rs 50,000 threshold itself once that threshold is crossed; the entire value becomes part of the recipient's taxable income for the year, which can push a recipient into a materially higher bracket than their regular income alone would have.

The relative exemption: who is actually on the list

The single exemption families rely on most is also the one most often misapplied, because the word 'relative' in the Income-tax Act carries a specific, closed definition for this purpose rather than its everyday meaning. The Explanation to the provision, confirmed on the Income Tax Department's own published guidance, lists a relative of an individual as: the spouse; a brother or sister; a brother or sister of the spouse; a brother or sister of either parent; any lineal ascendant or descendant of the individual; any lineal ascendant or descendant of the spouse; and the spouse of any person described in those preceding categories.

Read carefully, this list does include a brother-in-law or sister-in-law (through either the individual's own sibling's spouse or the spouse's sibling), a father-in-law or mother-in-law (as a lineal ascendant of the spouse), and grandparents, grandchildren and great-grandchildren on both sides (as lineal ascendants or descendants, with no generational limit stated in the provision itself). It does not include a cousin of any degree, an aunt or uncle's spouse beyond what the list captures, a friend however close, or a nephew or niece who is the child of a sibling rather than the sibling in person, because the provision moves up and down the direct line and across to siblings and their spouses, but does not reach sideways into a sibling's own children.

For a Hindu Undivided Family, the test is different and, in one sense, simpler: a gift received by an HUF from any of its own members is exempt, regardless of how that particular member would be classified under the individual's relative list. A gift the HUF receives from someone who is not a member at all, including a relative of the karta who happens not to belong to that particular HUF, is tested under the ordinary rules exactly as if the HUF were an individual stranger to that person.

The exemptions that have nothing to do with being a relative

Families fixate on the relative test because it is the exemption most people have heard of, but the provision carries several other, independent exemptions that frequently do more practical work. A gift received on the occasion of the individual's marriage is exempt without regard to who gives it, covering gifts from friends, colleagues and distant relatives who fall outside the relative list entirely, provided the gift can genuinely be tied to the marriage itself rather than to some other occasion that happens to fall near the wedding date.

Property received under a will or by way of inheritance, or in contemplation of the giver's death, is exempt outright, which is why a bequest from an aunt, uncle or family friend who is not a 'relative' under the Explanation still escapes tax entirely when it passes through a will or intestate succession rather than as a lifetime gift. A gift from a local authority, or from a fund, foundation, university, educational institution, hospital, medical institution, trust or other institution registered under Sections 12AA or 12AB (or covered by specific notifications), is also exempt, which matters to a family receiving a scholarship, a medical relief payment or a grant from a registered charitable body.

One category that families frequently assume is covered but is not: a gift from an employer to an employee does not fall within this provision's exemptions at all, because it is not treated as a gift under this head in the first place. Any benefit an employer confers on an employee beyond agreed salary is ordinarily taxed as a perquisite under the salary head, a different set of rules with its own valuation methods, and routing such a payment through gift-deed language does not move it into the gift provision or its exemptions.

Immovable property: why the deed's own figure rarely matters

A gift of land or a flat tests differently from a gift of money, and this is where many family transactions generate an unexpected tax bill years after the deed was registered. The provision measures a gift of immovable property against its stamp duty value, the value the state government's stamp authority would assign for registration purposes, not the consideration (if any) actually mentioned in the deed and not the fair market value a valuer might independently arrive at.

Where the gift is made for no consideration at all, as most family gift deeds are, the entire stamp duty value is treated as the taxable amount, subject to the relative and other exemptions already described. Where some consideration does pass, as happens in family transfers structured as a sale at a nominal or below-market price rather than as an outright gift, the shortfall between the stamp duty value and the actual consideration is taxed if that shortfall exceeds the higher of Rs 50,000 or the prescribed percentage margin, a trap that catches transfers the parties never thought of as gifts at all.

Tribunal rulings have treated the stamp duty value as a rebuttable figure rather than an unchallengeable one: where a recipient genuinely disputes that the stamp duty value overstates the property's fair market value, the Assessing Officer is required to refer the valuation to the District Valuation Officer rather than simply adopting the stamp authority's figure. This gives a recipient a real route to contest an inflated valuation, but it is a route that has to be invoked at the assessment stage with supporting material, not something that corrects itself automatically because the stamp duty value looks obviously high to the family involved.

Shares and other movable property: valuation disputes are the norm, not the exception

Gifts of listed shares are comparatively straightforward to value, since market price on the date of transfer is readily available and rarely contested. Gifts of unlisted shares, the kind that dominate family-business succession planning, are a different matter entirely, because their fair market value has to be computed under Rule 11UA of the Income-tax Rules using a prescribed method (commonly the net asset value method, though the discounted cash flow method is also permitted in defined circumstances), and that computation is exactly the kind of exercise competent valuers can legitimately disagree about.

A family transferring shares of a closely held company between relatives, as part of succession planning for a family business, therefore faces a double valuation exposure even where the relative exemption applies and no tax is payable on the transfer itself in the end: first, whether the computed fair market value is accurate enough to survive scrutiny if the transaction is ever examined, and second, whether the cost basis the recipient will carry forward for a future sale has been correctly recorded at the point of the gift, a point addressed in the next section.

Jewellery, bullion, paintings, sculptures and other named categories of movable property are valued, for a gift received without consideration, at fair market value determined under the same Rule 11UA framework, generally by reference to a registered valuer's report where market comparables are not straightforward. Families gifting these categories of asset between relatives face no tax exposure on the gift itself under the relative exemption, but should still retain a contemporaneous valuation, because the absence of one becomes a problem only when it is most needed: at a later date, when the asset is sold and the cost basis is challenged, or when the family is asked to establish that the original gift was genuinely exempt rather than merely asserted to be.

Clubbing under Section 64: the tax-free gift that is not the end of the story

The relative exemption answers only one question: is the transfer itself taxed as the recipient's income. It says nothing about what happens afterwards to the income the gifted asset goes on to generate, and that second question is governed by an entirely separate set of anti-avoidance rules in Section 64 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, commonly referred to as the clubbing provisions.

Where an individual transfers an asset to a spouse, directly or indirectly, otherwise than for adequate consideration, any income that asset generates thereafter, rent, interest, dividends and the like, is clubbed with and taxed in the hands of the transferor, not the spouse who nominally received the gift, year after year for as long as the asset is held. The gift of the asset itself may be entirely exempt under the relative exemption described above; the income the asset earns afterwards is a different matter, assessed separately, and clubbing applies to it regardless of the gift's own tax-free character.

The same principle applies, with its own specific sub-rule, to a gift to a minor child: income earned by a minor from an asset gifted by a parent is clubbed with the income of whichever parent has the higher total income, subject to a modest annual exemption of Rs 1,500 per child under the clubbing provision, and this clubbing continues until the child attains majority. It does not apply to income the child earns through their own skill, talent or manual work, a narrow carve-out that matters far less to most families than the general rule.

A small number of situations escape clubbing even on a transfer between spouses: a transfer made for adequate consideration, a transfer made as part of an agreement to live apart, and a transfer made before the marriage took place are each recognised exceptions. Families planning a gift between spouses with the specific aim of shifting future income into the lower-earning spouse's hands should treat Section 64 as the first thing to check, not an afterthought, because the plan frequently fails entirely once clubbing is applied, leaving the family with the administrative burden of the gift and none of the intended tax benefit.

A gift to one's own HUF: a route families use and a trap that comes with it

A gift by an individual member into their own Hindu Undivided Family is a transaction Indian families have used for generations, often to pool resources for a joint family purpose or simply because the HUF is treated as the natural vehicle for inherited or jointly held wealth. As already noted, a gift received by an HUF from its own member is exempt under the relative-equivalent rule for HUFs specifically, so the transfer itself ordinarily carries no tax cost to the HUF as recipient.

The clubbing provisions reach this transaction from the other direction, however. Section 64(2) specifically addresses the case of an individual who is a member of an HUF converting or transferring their own separate property into the common pool of the family, whether by actual transfer or by the more informal route of simply treating separate property as joint family property, and the income from that property, once in the HUF's hands, continues to be included in the individual's own total income rather than being treated purely as the HUF's income going forward, subject to the specific proportionate rules the sub-section lays down for what happens on a later partition of the family.

This is a frequently overlooked consequence for families using HUF contributions as a wealth-pooling or tax-planning device: a member who contributes a significant personal asset into the family HUF, expecting the income from that asset to be taxed going forward as the HUF's income at the HUF's own rates and slabs, discovers instead that a meaningful share of it continues to be assessed in their own individual hands under Section 64(2), which can undercut the very reason the contribution was made in the first place.

Cost basis after a gift: the tax deferred is not the tax eliminated

A gift that is entirely tax-free at the point of transfer, under the relative exemption or any other exemption described above, still carries a tax consequence forward to the day the recipient eventually sells the asset. Section 49(1) of the Income-tax Act provides that where a capital asset was acquired by the taxpayer under a gift (or certain other specified modes, including inheritance and a family partition), the cost of acquisition for computing capital gains on a future sale is deemed to be the cost to the previous owner who actually paid for or first acquired the asset, not the value at which the gift itself was recorded, and the previous owner's holding period is tacked on to the recipient's own holding period for determining whether the eventual gain is long-term or short-term.

This single rule is the reason a family gift of appreciated property, land bought decades ago at a modest price and gifted today at its present market value, does not actually eliminate the capital gains tax that has been building up over those decades; it simply transfers the liability for that accumulated gain to whichever family member eventually sells the property, computed on the original, much lower, historical cost rather than on the value at the date of the gift.

Families planning a gift of appreciated property or shares for succession purposes should treat this carried-forward cost basis as central to the plan, not as a technical footnote. A gift that looks, on paper, like it has moved an asset out of the family's taxable estate at no cost has, in reality, only postponed the tax and handed the eventual bill, calculated on the original cost, to the next generation, a consequence that is often discovered only when that generation comes to sell and finds the tax exposure far larger than the current market value of the asset alone would suggest.

Where this goes wrong in practice: a cautionary pattern from recent reporting

A case reported in the financial press in late 2025 illustrates how quickly a family gift that everyone involved believed was fully exempt can turn into a substantial, unanticipated tax demand. The reported facts describe a family gift of roughly Rs 1.3 crore that the family treated as exempt on the strength of the relative relationship between the parties, only for the Income Tax Department to reassess the position and raise a demand of roughly Rs 70 lakh. The reported ground for the demand was not that the relationship fell outside the relative definition, since a brother-in-law is squarely within the Explanation's list, but a dispute over documentation, reportedly because no gift deed had been executed, even though the law does not actually require a gift deed once the relationship and the money trail are established. The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal has since been reported to have deleted the addition in full, restoring the taxpayer's assessed income and tax liability to their original, undisputed figures.

The pattern that case illustrates recurs across the kind of disputes tribunals have been deciding regularly, though the recurring failure is usually evidentiary rather than definitional: a family that can show the relationship and the money trail, but never executed a gift deed or kept clear records of the transfer, finds itself defending the exemption from scratch once the Department examines the return, often years after the gift itself and well after the paperwork that would have settled the question quickly could still have been put together.

The lesson this kind of case teaches is not that family gifts are unsafe; the overwhelming majority of gifts between genuinely qualifying relatives remain exactly as exempt as families expect. The lesson is that a gift deed and a clear banking trail should be put in place at the time of the gift, not reconstructed after a demand notice arrives, because even a gift to an unquestionably qualifying relative can otherwise be disputed and has to be defended, at cost and delay, all the way to appeal before the exemption is confirmed.

Cross-border gifts: a brief word on the FEMA layer

A gift involving an NRI, whether an NRI giving to a resident relative in India or a resident giving to an NRI relative abroad, sits under this same income tax framework, with an additional layer of foreign exchange law that domestic gifts do not carry. Receiving a gift of money or property from an NRI relative in India is generally permitted without special approval, and the income tax relative exemption applies in exactly the same way regardless of where the giver is resident, provided the relationship falls within the Explanation's list.

Remitting a gift out of India to a relative abroad is a different question, governed by the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, which caps the amount a resident individual can remit abroad for gifts and other permitted purposes in a financial year, currently subject to a ceiling that is periodically revised and should be confirmed against the Reserve Bank of India's current circular before any significant remittance is planned, rather than assumed from an earlier year's figure. This FEMA dimension operates entirely independently of the income tax exemption discussed through this piece; a gift can be completely exempt from income tax under the relative exemption and still be blocked, delayed or restricted at the point of remittance by a FEMA ceiling that has nothing to do with the Income-tax Act at all.

A practical checklist before the gift deed is signed

Before executing any significant family gift, four questions are worth working through in sequence rather than assuming the answer to any of them. First, does the specific relationship between giver and recipient actually appear on the Explanation's list, checked word by word rather than by general impression, and if it does not, does one of the other exemptions (marriage, inheritance, a registered institution) apply instead. Second, if the gift is of immovable property, what is its current stamp duty value, obtained from the relevant sub-registrar's office or an equivalent reliable source, rather than an estimate based on the last transaction in the family or the neighbourhood some years ago.

Third, if the gift is between spouses or to a minor child, has the clubbing position under Section 64 been worked out separately from the gift's own tax treatment, including whether the intended tax benefit of the gift (shifting future income to a lower bracket) will actually survive clubbing or will simply be clubbed straight back to the giver. Fourth, has the cost of acquisition and holding period the recipient will carry forward under Section 49(1) been documented at the time of the gift, with supporting records of the original owner's purchase cost and date, so that a sale decades later does not become a scramble to reconstruct historical figures from incomplete family memory.

None of these four steps requires treating a family gift as an adversarial transaction between the parties. They are simply the minimum factual groundwork the statute itself demands before anyone, including the family's own accountant, can say with confidence that a particular gift is exempt, how much of its future income will be clubbed, and what tax the next generation will eventually owe when the asset is finally sold.

The relative exemption under the Income-tax Act remains, for the great majority of Indian families, a reliable and genuinely generous route for moving wealth within the family without an immediate tax cost. Its reliability depends entirely on checking the actual, closed list of qualifying relationships against the specific relationship in question, rather than against what feels, in ordinary family language, like a close enough connection to qualify.

Even where the relative exemption squarely applies, a gift is rarely the end of the tax analysis. Clubbing can tax the asset's future income back to the giver, and the carried-forward cost basis under Section 49(1) means the capital gains tax that has accumulated on an appreciated asset has been postponed to the next generation's eventual sale rather than erased by the gift itself. A family that works through both of these consequences before the gift deed is signed, rather than after a tax notice arrives, is the family that actually gets the benefit the gift was meant to deliver.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a gift of money between parents and children taxable in India?

No. A parent and child relationship falls within the 'relative' definition (as a lineal ascendant and descendant of each other), so money, immovable property or other specified movable property gifted between a parent and child is exempt from tax under the relative exemption, regardless of the amount involved.

Can a gift to a cousin, nephew or close friend be tax-free?

Not under the relative exemption, because a cousin, a sibling's child (as distinct from the sibling in person) and a friend each fall outside the Explanation's closed list. Such a gift can still be exempt if it is received on the occasion of the recipient's marriage, under a will or inheritance, or from certain exempt categories of giver, but not on the basis of the relationship alone, and if none of those other exemptions applies, the gift is taxable once it exceeds Rs 50,000 in the year.

Does gifting property to a spouse actually save tax on future rental income?

Usually not. While the gift itself is exempt between spouses, Section 64 of the Income-tax Act clubs the rental or other income the gifted property earns back into the income of the spouse who made the gift, for as long as the property is held, unless the transfer was for adequate consideration or one of the other narrow exceptions applies.

What value does the tax department use for a gifted flat or plot of land?

The stamp duty value assigned by the state's stamp authority for registration purposes, not the figure recorded in the gift deed and not an independent market valuation. A recipient who genuinely disputes that this value overstates fair market value can ask the Assessing Officer to refer the question to the District Valuation Officer.

If a gift is tax-free, does the recipient pay any tax when they later sell the asset?

Often yes, and sometimes a substantial amount. Under Section 49(1), the recipient's cost of acquisition for a gifted asset is the original owner's cost, not the asset's value at the time of the gift, and the original owner's holding period is added to the recipient's own. A later sale is therefore taxed on the full gain built up since the asset was first acquired, often decades earlier at a much lower cost, not merely the gain since the gift.

Is a gift received from an employer tax-free like a gift from a relative?

No. A gift or benefit from an employer to an employee is not assessed under the gift provision at all; it is ordinarily taxed as a perquisite under the salary head, with its own valuation rules, regardless of how the payment is documented.

Does the Income-tax Act, 2025 change how gifts are taxed?

Multiple tax publications report that the Income-tax Act, 2025, in force from 1 April 2026, carries the gift provision forward from Section 56(2)(x) of the 1961 Act into Section 92(2)(m) of the new Act with the underlying taxation principle unchanged. Gifts made before 1 April 2026 remain governed by the 1961 Act's numbering for any pending assessment.

Can an NRI receive a gift from a resident relative in India without any FEMA issue?

Generally yes, since receiving a gift from a relative is a permitted transaction, and the income tax relative exemption applies the same way whether the giver is resident or non-resident. Remitting a gift out of India to a relative abroad is a separate question governed by the Liberalised Remittance Scheme's annual ceiling, which should be checked against the Reserve Bank of India's current circular before the remittance is made.
