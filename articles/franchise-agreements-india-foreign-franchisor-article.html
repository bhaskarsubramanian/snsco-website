Franchise Agreements in India: Protecting a Foreign Franchisor's Brand, Royalty and Termination Rights

India has no franchise statute at all, which means a foreign brand entering the Indian market gets exactly the protection its own contract gives it, and nothing more.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 19 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign brand looking to franchise into India will not find a franchise law to read, because there isn't one. No statute requires a franchisor to disclose anything before a franchisee signs, sets a cooling-off period, or gives a terminated franchisee a right to compensation for the goodwill they built. The entire relationship rests on the contract itself, read against the Trade Marks Act, the Contract Act and the Competition Act, which makes the drafting of that one document considerably more consequential in India than it would be in a jurisdiction with a dedicated franchise code behind it.

Key points

  • India has no franchise-specific statute; the relationship is governed entirely by the Indian Contract Act, 1872, the Trade Marks Act, 1999, the Competition Act, 2002 and FEMA, with no mandatory disclosure document, no cooling-off period, and no statutory right to compensation on termination
  • A franchisor that fails to disclose material facts about the business opportunity still risks a misrepresentation or fraud claim under the Contract Act even without a dedicated disclosure statute, which is why voluntary disclosure remains the safer practice despite not being legally required
  • Recording the franchise trademark licence as a registered user under Sections 48 and 49 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 gives the franchisor a stronger non-use defence and a clearer enforcement position than relying on an unrecorded, written-consent licence alone
  • A franchisor that does not actually exercise quality control over how its mark is used risks a finding of naked licensing, which can expose the mark to a cancellation action under Section 57 on the ground that it no longer distinguishes the franchisor's goods or services
  • Royalty and lump-sum technology transfer and trademark fees moved to the automatic route under FEMA following Press Note 8 of 2009, removing the earlier caps, though the payment still has to be made on an arm's length basis and reported under the standard current account rules
  • A post-termination non-compete restraining a former franchisee is judged under Section 27 of the Contract Act, and the Supreme Court's reasoning in Gujarat Bottling Co. v. Coca-Cola Co. that a restraint coextensive with the agreement's term is not a restraint of trade does not automatically extend to a restraint operating after the agreement has ended
  • A franchisor's right to stop a terminated franchisee from continuing to use its marks, signage and operating manuals is well established in recent Indian case law, including a February 2026 Delhi High Court order in the Moti Mahal dispute, and is generally easier to enforce than a post-termination non-compete
  • Resale price maintenance imposed on franchisees, fixing or effectively fixing the price at which they must sell, is presumed anti-competitive under Section 3(4) of the Competition Act, 2002, while exclusive territory and minimum purchase clauses are assessed under a rule-of-reason test rather than presumed unlawful

Why India Has No Franchise Law, and What Fills the Gap

Unlike the United States, where the Federal Trade Commission's Franchise Rule mandates a detailed disclosure document before any franchise sale, or Australia, which has its own statutory Franchising Code of Conduct, India has never enacted a dedicated franchise law. A parliamentary standing committee has, at various points, recommended sector-specific protection, including for automobile dealers who function much like franchisees, but no general franchise statute has followed from that recommendation, and no current legislative proposal for one was found in the research for this article.

What governs a franchise relationship in India instead is a patchwork of general law: the Indian Contract Act, 1872 for the agreement itself and any misrepresentation in forming it, the Trade Marks Act, 1999 for the licence to use the franchisor's mark, the Competition Act, 2002 for restraints the agreement places on the franchisee's commercial freedom, FEMA for payments flowing abroad, and ordinary civil procedure for enforcing whatever the contract says. None of these statutes was written with franchising specifically in mind, which means a franchisor cannot assume any of the protections a franchise-specific law would typically supply, a minimum notice period, a right to disclosure, a floor on what the agreement must say, exist here by default.

The practical consequence is that the franchise agreement itself has to do all the work a dedicated statute would otherwise do elsewhere. A foreign franchisor accustomed to a jurisdiction where the law fills in gaps left by a thin contract should not assume the same gaps get filled the same way in India; where the agreement is silent or ambiguous, an Indian court applies ordinary contract interpretation principles rather than a franchise-specific default rule, because no such default rule exists.

This gap is sometimes described, in industry commentary rather than by any government body, as leaving foreign franchisors unusually exposed when something goes wrong, since there is no regulator whose job is specifically to police franchise relationships, no franchise-specific limitation period, and no mandatory minimum notice period before termination unless the parties themselves agreed to one. The flip side of that same gap is a genuine freedom to draft exactly the structure a particular business needs, a short notice period where the franchisor wants to be able to exit a underperforming relationship quickly, a longer one where the franchisor wants franchisees to feel secure enough to invest heavily in a location, without a statutory floor forcing either choice. A foreign franchisor that treats the absence of regulation purely as a risk, rather than also as a drafting opportunity, is leaving value on the table that a more deliberate approach to the contract would otherwise capture.

No Disclosure Document Is Required, But Misrepresentation Risk Still Bites

A franchisor entering the United States is required to hand a prospective franchisee a Franchise Disclosure Document covering twenty-three specified categories of information, including litigation history, financial statements and a list of existing franchisees, at least fourteen days before any agreement is signed or any payment is taken. Nothing comparable exists under Indian law. A franchisor is free, as far as any disclosure statute is concerned, to provide as little or as much information as it chooses before a franchisee signs, and no statutory cooling-off period gives the franchisee a window to walk away after signing either.

This absence of a disclosure mandate is not the same as an absence of risk. A franchisor that makes representations about likely revenue, footfall, or the performance of existing outlets that turn out to be false or substantially exaggerated remains exposed to a claim for misrepresentation or fraud under the general provisions of the Contract Act, and potentially to a claim of unfair trade practice depending on how the franchisee is characterised for the purpose of consumer protection law. The absence of a specific disclosure statute removes the floor a franchisor must meet, not the ceiling on what a franchisee can later argue was misrepresented.

The practical lesson for a foreign franchisor used to drafting against a disclosure statute's checklist is to keep doing something similar voluntarily, a written pre-contract disclosure covering the unit economics, the franchisor's own litigation history in India if any, and the realistic basis for any revenue projection, not because Indian law requires it, but because the absence of a required format makes it easier, not harder, for a disappointed franchisee to later claim they were told something that was not true and that nothing on paper now contradicts them. A disclosure document a franchisor was never required to produce is also a document that, once produced and signed off on by the franchisee, becomes useful evidence that whatever was actually said matched what was later put in writing.

The same logic extends to how a franchisor's Indian master franchisee or area developer, where one is used instead of dealing with each outlet franchisee directly, is permitted to pitch the opportunity to prospective sub-franchisees. A master franchisee under commercial pressure to fill territories quickly has every incentive to overstate the opportunity, and a foreign franchisor whose own brand name ends up attached to whatever the master franchisee actually said, even where the foreign franchisor never saw or approved the specific pitch, can find itself named in a misrepresentation claim it had no direct hand in making. Reviewing and approving the actual sales materials a master franchisee uses, rather than leaving that entirely to the master franchisee's own judgment, closes a gap many foreign franchisors only discover once a sub-franchisee dispute is already under way.

Registered User or Permitted User: Getting the Trademark Licence Right

A franchise is, at its legal core, a trademark licence wrapped around an operating system, and the Trade Marks Act, 1999 gives a franchisor two distinct routes to license its mark to a franchisee. The first, under Sections 48 and 49, is registration as a "registered user", which requires a joint application to the Trade Marks Registry supported by the written licence agreement, following the procedure under Rule 86 of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017. The second, recognised under Section 2(1)(r)(ii), is a lighter "permitted user" arrangement based on written consent alone, without any filing at the Registry.

The registered user route carries real advantages a franchisor entering India at scale should not overlook. Under Section 48(2), use of the mark by a registered user counts as use by the franchisor itself for the purpose of defending the registration against a non-use cancellation action under Section 47, which matters considerably where the franchisor's own direct use of the mark in India is limited and most of the visible use in the market comes through franchisees. A registered user also has standing of its own under Section 52 to bring an infringement action, which an unrecorded permitted user generally does not have under Section 53, a meaningful difference where a franchisee is best placed, being the one actually present in the local market, to spot and act against a counterfeit or infringing use early.

Against this, registration does require filing the licence agreement with the Registry, which some franchisors are reluctant to do because the agreement becomes part of a record the Registry holds, and the process adds a procedural step before the arrangement is formally complete. A franchisor running a small number of outlets through a single trusted operator may reasonably choose the lighter permitted-user route; a franchisor planning a network of outlets across multiple Indian cities, where it genuinely needs each local franchisee to be able to act against infringement without waiting for the franchisor's own, slower involvement from abroad, is generally better served registering the licence properly. Rule 90 of the 2017 Rules is also worth flagging on this point: registering a licence with the Trade Marks Registry does not by itself authorise sending royalty money out of India, which is a separate question answered by FEMA, addressed later in this article.

A related choice many foreign franchisors face is whether to license the mark directly to each Indian franchisee or to route the licence through a single Indian master franchisee, who in turn sub-licenses to the outlet-level operators. Where a master franchisee structure is used, the registered user filing, if the franchisor chooses that route, generally needs to reflect the actual chain of licensing rather than treating the foreign franchisor and the ultimate outlet operator as though they had a direct relationship the paperwork does not actually show. Getting this chain of title wrong on the trademark side rarely surfaces as a problem while the relationship is working well, but becomes a real complication exactly when it matters most, in an infringement action or a dispute over who actually has standing to enforce the mark against a third party, which is reason enough to have the registered user filings reviewed against the actual commercial structure rather than against a template that assumes a simpler, direct relationship.

Naked Licensing: Losing Control of the Mark by Failing to Police It

Indian trademark law does not use the American term "naked licensing", but the underlying concept applies all the same: a trademark owner who licenses its mark without exercising meaningful quality control over how the licensee actually uses it risks the mark losing the very distinctiveness that justified its registration in the first place, since the mark no longer reliably signals that the goods or services it appears on actually meet the franchisor's own standard. Where that happens, the mark becomes vulnerable to a rectification or cancellation action under Section 57 of the Trade Marks Act, on the ground that it is wrongly remaining on the register.

The Competition Appellate Tribunal addressed the underlying principle in the Schott Glass India dispute (arising from a complaint by Kapoor Glass, later upheld by the Supreme Court on appeal in May 2025, Civil Appeal No. 5843 of 2014), holding that a quality-control clause on paper is not enough; a licensor has to actually enforce it for the arrangement to be treated as a genuine licence rather than what common-law doctrine calls a bare, or naked, licence. The point that matters for a franchise specifically is that a contractual quality control clause on paper is necessary but not sufficient; what protects the mark is the franchisor actually exercising the inspection, approval and enforcement rights the agreement gives it, not merely having those rights sit unused in a clause nobody acts on.

A long-running dispute over the TAFE and Massey Ferguson marks in India illustrates the stakes, with one side having argued that decades of inadequate supervision over how the mark was used amounted to an effective abandonment of meaningful control. A foreign franchisor that treats its Indian quality control rights as boilerplate, rather than as an operational commitment to actually inspect outlets, review marketing materials, and enforce standards consistently across its Indian network, is taking on exactly this risk, and the risk compounds the longer inadequate supervision continues, since a cancellation action typically has to show a sustained pattern rather than a single lapse.

A franchisor that genuinely cannot maintain hands-on supervision across every Indian outlet, which describes most foreign franchisors running any network of meaningful size, should treat delegation to a properly empowered master franchisee or regional manager as the practical answer, rather than treating the absence of direct foreign oversight as itself a weakness. What the naked licensing doctrine actually requires is that someone with real authority is exercising quality control consistently, not that the franchisor's own foreign head office personally inspects every outlet; a master franchisee contractually obliged to run, and actually running, a documented inspection and compliance programme on the franchisor's behalf satisfies the underlying requirement just as well as direct foreign supervision would, provided the franchisor can show, if the question is ever tested, that the programme was real rather than nominal.

Royalty and Lump-Sum Fees: What the 2009 Liberalisation Actually Freed Up

Before December 2009, a foreign franchisor charging an Indian franchisee a technology transfer fee, a trademark or brand-name royalty, faced statutory caps under the foreign investment policy then in force, commonly cited as a lump-sum ceiling of USD 2 million and royalty ceilings in the range of 1 to 2% of domestic sales and a higher percentage of exports, depending on which category of payment was involved. Press Note 8 of 2009, issued by the then Department of Industrial Policy and Promotion with effect from 16 December 2009, removed these caps entirely and moved royalty and lump-sum technology transfer and trademark payments to the automatic route, meaning no prior government or Reserve Bank approval is required merely because the ceiling would otherwise have been exceeded.

This liberalisation did not remove every constraint on what a foreign franchisor can actually be paid. The payment still has to be made on an arm's length basis, consistent with transfer pricing principles that apply regardless of the FEMA position, and it remains subject to the ordinary current account transaction rules under the Foreign Exchange Management (Current Account Transactions) Rules, 2000, along with the withholding tax and treaty considerations this site's existing guide on royalty and fees for technical services addresses in detail. Whether a particular franchise fee is itself treated as royalty for trademark use, as a technology transfer fee, or as ordinary consideration for services, which determines exactly how the FEMA and tax rules apply to it, depends on how the specific payment is actually structured and described in the agreement, a classification question the research for this article could not resolve in the abstract and that should be confirmed for the specific fee structure a franchisor intends to use.

A practical point many foreign franchisors miss is that the 2009 liberalisation addresses whether approval is required, not whether the payment is automatically allowed to leave India without any further compliance step. An authorised dealer bank processing the outward remittance will still expect the standard documentation, a certificate confirming applicable tax has been withheld, and confirmation the payment matches what the underlying agreement actually provides for, before releasing the funds, which means the operational compliance burden around a franchise royalty has not disappeared merely because the regulatory ceiling has.

Franchise fee structures also commonly combine several different payment streams in one agreement, a one-time upfront franchise fee for the right to open the first outlet, a continuing royalty calculated as a percentage of gross or net sales, and sometimes a separate marketing or advertising fund contribution the franchisee pays into a pool the franchisor controls. Each of these streams can, in principle, attract a different tax and FEMA characterisation, and a franchisor that lumps them together under a single generic label in the agreement, rather than itemising them separately, makes the authorised dealer bank's and the tax authority's job of confirming the correct treatment considerably harder, which in practice tends to slow down the remittance rather than speed it up. Itemising each payment stream separately in the agreement, with its own basis for calculation clearly stated, is a small drafting discipline that pays for itself the first time a remittance has to be processed, and it also gives the franchisor a cleaner answer if an Indian tax assessment later questions why a particular outward payment was treated the way it was.

Non-Compete Clauses During and After the Franchise Term

A franchise agreement typically restrains the franchisee from dealing in competing brands or operating a competing business, both while the franchise is running and for some period after it ends, and Indian law treats these two restraints quite differently under Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, which voids any agreement in restraint of trade subject to narrow statutory exceptions. The Supreme Court's 1995 decision in Gujarat Bottling Co. Ltd. v. Coca-Cola Co., reported at (1995) 5 SCC 545, upheld a clause barring the bottler from dealing in competing beverage brands for the duration of its bottling agreement with Coca-Cola, reasoning that a restraint operating only during the subsistence of the agreement, rather than after it ends, falls outside what Section 27 treats as an unlawful restraint of trade, since it promotes rather than restricts trade by ensuring the franchisee's full commercial attention goes to the brand it has actually taken on.

That reasoning is specifically tied to the restraint's timing, co-extensive with the agreement rather than surviving it, and does not, on its own terms, validate an equivalent restraint that continues to bind the franchisee after the franchise agreement has ended. A post-termination non-compete, restraining a former franchisee from operating a competing business for some period after the relationship ends, sits in considerably more uncertain territory under Section 27, closer to the employment non-compete cases this site's existing guide on non-compete and garden leave clauses addresses, than to Gujarat Bottling's own facts.

Franchisors drafting for the Indian market should treat the during-term restraint as the one Gujarat Bottling actually supports with confidence, and should not assume the same confidence automatically extends to a post-termination restraint of the kind many foreign franchise templates include as a matter of course. Where the franchisor's real concern is less about the former franchisee competing in the abstract and more about that franchisee trading on the goodwill, systems and confidential operating knowledge the franchise relationship gave them access to, a narrower confidentiality obligation and a de-branding requirement, addressed in the next section, tend to be considerably easier to enforce in India than a broad post-termination non-compete framed purely as a restraint on competing.

A foreign franchise template imported wholesale from a jurisdiction with a more generous approach to post-termination restraints, the United States in particular, often includes a non-compete running for one or two years across a defined radius around the former outlet, a formula that reads as standard there but sits on genuinely uncertain footing once transplanted into an Indian contract without adjustment. Rather than deleting the clause outright, which gives up a protection that may still have some persuasive value even where its enforceability is doubtful, a franchisor is often better served pairing a shorter, more defensible restraint with the stronger confidentiality and non-solicitation obligations, covering the franchisor's customer data, supplier relationships and training materials specifically, that Indian courts have shown more willingness to enforce than a broad restraint on competing as such.

De-Branding on Termination: The Easier Right to Enforce

Where a post-termination non-compete sits on uncertain ground, a franchisor's right to stop a terminated franchisee from continuing to use its marks, signage, decor and operating manuals rests on considerably firmer footing, because that right is simply an application of ordinary trademark and copyright infringement law rather than a restraint-of-trade question under Section 27 at all. Once a licence to use a mark ends, whether by expiry or termination, continued use of that mark by the former licensee is infringement in the ordinary sense, not a separate contractual non-compete question.

The Delhi High Court's order in Moti Mahal Legendary Hospitality v. M/S Sant Foods, in CS(COMM) 189/2026 and reported on 7 March 2026, illustrates the point directly: once the franchise agreement was terminated, the Court held, on a prima facie view, that the former franchisee had no continuing right to run a restaurant under the Moti Mahal name, and restrained further use of the mark. Separate, more recent orders in related Moti Mahal disputes, including an ex parte interim injunction reported in May 2026 against a different former associate, show the franchisor continuing to actively enforce this position across its network rather than treating a single favourable order as sufficient protection on its own, consistent with the point made earlier about naked licensing requiring sustained, active enforcement rather than a one-time court order.

A franchise agreement's termination clause should accordingly spell out, in operational detail rather than general language, exactly what the franchisee has to do once the agreement ends: removing branded signage within a specified and genuinely enforceable window, returning or destroying operating manuals and confidential materials, ceasing use of franchisor-specific software and point-of-sale systems, and allowing the franchisor or its representative to inspect the former outlet to confirm compliance. A franchisor that has this kind of concrete de-branding obligation in its contract, rather than a bare statement that the licence ends on termination, has a considerably easier case to bring if a former franchisee drags its feet, precisely because the court is being asked to enforce a specific, already-agreed obligation rather than to work out from first principles what de-branding should require.

The urgency of acting quickly after termination is worth building into the franchisor's own internal process, separate from what the contract says. Every week a terminated franchisee continues trading under the franchisor's name is a week in which customers may form the impression that the outlet is still part of the network, which does lasting reputational damage that an eventual injunction, however firmly worded once it is finally granted, cannot fully undo after the fact. A franchisor that has already drafted the specific de-branding obligations this section describes, and that moves to enforce them through an urgent interim application the moment a termination is not complied with, rather than waiting to see whether the former franchisee complies voluntarily, is in a considerably stronger position than one that treats enforcement as a last resort to be considered only once informal requests have clearly failed.

Competition Law: Resale Price Maintenance and Exclusive Territory Clauses

A franchise agreement routinely includes terms that would, outside a franchise context, draw competition law scrutiny: a requirement that the franchisee sell only within an assigned territory, a requirement to source inputs exclusively from the franchisor or an approved supplier, and sometimes a cap or floor on the price the franchisee can charge the public. Section 3(4) of the Competition Act, 2002 treats these as vertical agreements, covering tie-in arrangements, exclusive supply, exclusive distribution, refusal to deal and resale price maintenance, and prohibits them only where they cause or are likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition, applying what the Competition Commission of India describes as a rule-of-reason analysis rather than an automatic prohibition.

Resale price maintenance is the category that has drawn the clearest adverse findings. The Competition Commission's first finding on this point, against Hyundai Motor India in 2017, concerned a discount control mechanism that effectively fixed the minimum price dealers could offer, which the Commission treated as unlawful price fixing from below. A more recent assessment of a different manufacturer's dealership agreement found no contravention where dealers were capped only from selling above a maximum recommended price, with no floor on how far below that price they could go, illustrating that the line the Commission actually draws is between a cap, generally permissible, and a floor, generally not.

Exclusive territory and exclusive sourcing clauses, by contrast, are not treated as presumptively unlawful at all, and the Commission has cleared a considerable number of exclusive distribution and exclusive supply arrangements on the basis that exclusivity, on its own, can serve entirely legitimate commercial purposes, protecting a franchisee's investment in building out a territory being one of them, rather than being inherently aimed at reducing competition. A foreign franchisor can generally draft exclusive territory protection for its franchisees with reasonable confidence, provided the exclusivity is not paired with a price floor or another restraint that tips the overall arrangement into the kind of effect the Commission has actually found objectionable in past cases.

A franchisor that wants franchisees to source key inputs exclusively from the franchisor itself, rather than from any approved supplier, should be able to explain the commercial reason for that requirement in terms the Commission's rule-of-reason analysis actually recognises, consistency of a proprietary recipe or formulation, protection of trade secrets embedded in a specific input, or quality assurance that a third-party supplier genuinely could not match, rather than leaving the requirement to look like a simple additional revenue stream for the franchisor dressed up as a quality measure. A requirement with a clearly articulated, genuine commercial rationale is considerably better placed to survive scrutiny than the same requirement defended only after the fact, once a disgruntled franchisee or a competitor has already raised the question with the Commission.

A Drafting Checklist for a Foreign Franchisor Entering India

Several points are worth settling before a foreign franchisor signs its first Indian franchisee, given how much weight the contract alone has to carry in the absence of a franchise statute. First, prepare a voluntary disclosure document covering unit economics and any revenue representations, even though none is legally required, specifically to reduce later misrepresentation exposure rather than to comply with a rule that does not exist. Second, decide between the registered user and permitted user route for the trademark licence based on the scale of the planned Indian network, registering where the franchisor needs its Indian franchisees to be able to act against infringement independently.

Third, build an actual quality control programme, not merely a quality control clause, with a realistic inspection and enforcement cadence the franchisor's Indian team or appointed master franchisee will genuinely follow, since the clause alone does nothing to prevent a naked licensing finding if nobody acts on it. Fourth, draft the termination and de-branding obligations in specific, operational language, a defined removal window, a defined inventory and materials return process, an inspection right, rather than a general statement that the licence ends on termination, since that specificity is what makes the obligation enforceable quickly rather than argued about from scratch after the relationship has already soured.

Fifth, treat the royalty and fee structure as a question to resolve with both Indian counsel and a tax adviser together, confirming how the specific payment will be characterised for FEMA and withholding tax purposes before the agreement is finalised, rather than assuming the 2009 liberalisation answers every question the structure raises. Sixth, where the agreement includes territorial exclusivity, sourcing restrictions or any pricing guidance to franchisees, have it reviewed against the Competition Act's vertical agreement framework specifically for any clause that could be read as fixing a minimum resale price, the one category of restraint the Competition Commission has shown it will act on without much hesitation.

What Remains Unsettled

Three points deserve a caveat rather than a confident statement. Whether a post-termination non-compete in a franchise agreement, as distinct from the during-term restraint Gujarat Bottling actually addressed, can survive a Section 27 challenge in India has not been settled by a reported decision found in the research for this article, and a franchisor relying on such a clause should treat it as considerably weaker than the during-term restraint rather than assume the same reasoning simply carries over. Whether a particular franchise fee structure is properly characterised as royalty, as a technology transfer fee, or as consideration for services for FEMA and tax purposes depends on the specific drafting and was not resolved in general terms by this research; it needs confirmation for the specific structure involved. And while no draft Indian franchise statute or disclosure law was found to be currently pending, the recommendation of a parliamentary standing committee for sector-specific franchise protection for automobile dealers signals that at least some pressure for statutory change exists, and a foreign franchisor planning a long-term Indian presence should not assume the current, purely contract-driven regime is necessarily permanent. A franchisor building a network it expects to still be running in a decade is better served checking this position again periodically, rather than treating today's purely contractual regime as a fixed backdrop that will still look the same when the agreement eventually comes up for renewal.

A foreign brand entering India through franchising is not stepping into a lightly regulated version of the franchise law it knows from home; it is stepping into a jurisdiction with no franchise law at all, where the contract it signs is doing work that a dedicated statute would otherwise do for it. That is not necessarily a worse position, since it gives the franchisor considerably more freedom to draft exactly the protections it needs rather than working within a statutory template designed for a generic franchise relationship, but it is a position that punishes a thin or carelessly adapted contract far more severely than a jurisdiction with a statutory floor underneath it would.

The protections that actually hold up in India, a properly recorded trademark licence, quality control a franchisor genuinely exercises rather than merely papers over, a specific and operational de-branding obligation, and a royalty structure checked against both FEMA and tax treatment before it is finalised, are not exotic requirements. They are the ordinary discipline of careful contract drafting, made considerably more consequential by the fact that nothing in Indian statute law is standing behind the franchisor if the contract itself falls short.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a franchise law in India that a foreign franchisor needs to comply with?

No. India has no dedicated franchise statute, no mandatory disclosure document, and no statutory cooling-off period. The relationship is governed by the Indian Contract Act, 1872, the Trade Marks Act, 1999, the Competition Act, 2002 and FEMA, with the franchise agreement itself carrying most of the weight a dedicated statute would otherwise bear.

Does a foreign franchisor have to give a franchisee a disclosure document before signing?

No statute requires it, unlike the United States' Franchise Disclosure Document regime. A franchisor that makes false or exaggerated representations about the business opportunity still risks a misrepresentation or fraud claim under the Contract Act, which is why voluntary disclosure remains good practice even though it is not legally mandatory.

Should a foreign franchisor register its trademark licence as a registered user in India?

It depends on the scale of the planned network. Registration under Sections 48 and 49 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 gives the franchisor a stronger non-use defence and lets the franchisee bring its own infringement actions, which matters more for a multi-city network than for a single trusted operator relying on a simpler written-consent licence.

Can a franchisor lose its trademark rights in India by not supervising its franchisees properly?

Yes, through what is called naked licensing elsewhere. A franchisor that licenses its mark without actually exercising quality control over how it is used risks a cancellation action under Section 57 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999, on the ground that the mark no longer reliably distinguishes the franchisor's goods or services.

Can a franchise agreement stop a terminated franchisee from running a competing business?

A restraint operating only during the franchise term is well supported by the Supreme Court's ruling in Gujarat Bottling Co. v. Coca-Cola Co. A restraint continuing after termination is on considerably less certain ground under Section 27 of the Contract Act, and a franchisor should not assume it will be enforced as readily as a during-term restraint.

Can a franchisor force a terminated franchisee to stop using its brand name and signage?

Yes, and this is generally easier to enforce than a post-termination non-compete, since it rests on ordinary trademark infringement law rather than restraint-of-trade principles. Indian courts, including the Delhi High Court in a March 2026 order involving the Moti Mahal brand, have restrained former franchisees from continuing to use a franchisor's marks after termination.

Can a franchisor tell its franchisees what price to charge customers?

Setting or effectively fixing a minimum resale price is presumed anti-competitive under Section 3(4) of the Competition Act, 2002, and the Competition Commission has found this unlawful in at least one reported case. Capping the maximum price a franchisee can charge, without setting a floor, has been treated differently and has not drawn the same adverse finding.
