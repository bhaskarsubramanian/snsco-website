The Forum Map for Civil and Commercial Disputes in Kolkata

Why a Kolkata business cannot simply file 'in the High Court' or 'in the City Civil Court': how the Original Side, the City Civil Court, the Commercial Division and the Alipore courts actually divide the work.

By Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A Kolkata trader owed forty lakh rupees by a counterparty in Park Street has at least three courts to choose between before a single page is drafted, and getting that choice wrong costs months rather than days. Kolkata runs a civil forum structure found nowhere else in eastern India: a High Court with two separate jurisdictions sitting under one roof, a City Civil Court with its own pecuniary ceiling, a Commercial Division layered on top of both since 2016, and a set of district courts at Alipore and elsewhere that take over the moment a dispute steps outside the old town limits.

Key points

  • The Calcutta High Court's Original Side and Appellate Side are not simply two benches of the same court; the Original Side exercises a separate ordinary original civil jurisdiction confined to the old town of Calcutta under the 1865 Letters Patent, while the Appellate Side hears appeals, writs and other work from across the state.
  • The City Civil Court, Calcutta takes most civil suits arising within the town limits up to its pecuniary ceiling, raised in stages and now reported at one crore rupees after a 2013 West Bengal amendment; above that figure, a suit generally has to go to the High Court's Original Side instead.
  • A commercial dispute of the specified value under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 is heard by the Commercial Division, whether that sits within the High Court or within the City Civil Court, and West Bengal's own notifications on where that line falls have been read differently by different benches; a litigant should confirm the current figure before filing rather than relying on any single number.
  • Section 12A pre-institution mediation is mandatory for a commercial suit that does not genuinely need urgent interim relief, and the Calcutta High Court applies the same Supreme Court tests on this point as every other commercial court in the country.
  • Once a Commercial Division is constituted, it alone decides arbitration matters connected with a commercial dispute, including Section 9, Section 11, Section 34 and Section 37 applications, even when the party originally filed on the ordinary Original Side.
  • Naming Kolkata as the venue in an arbitration clause is not the same as naming it the seat, and a party that gets this wrong can lose its chosen forum entirely, as the Supreme Court's 2022 ruling in Ravi Ranjan Developers shows.
  • A business with property, a factory or a registered office outside the old town limits, in large parts of South or North 24 Parganas, falls outside the City Civil Court's reach altogether and answers instead to the district judge at Alipore or Barasat.
  • The appeal clock differs sharply by route: ninety days for an ordinary first appeal, sixty days for a commercial appeal under Section 13(1A), and a separate route again for an intra-court appeal from a single judge of the Original Side.

A city with more forums than it first appears to have

Most Indian cities present a litigant with a straightforward choice: a district court below a certain value, a High Court above it, and a single appellate ladder running upward from whichever one the suit started in. Kolkata does not work that way. The Calcutta High Court is one of three chartered High Courts that still carries the civil jurisdiction it was given in 1862, which means it functions as two courts under one name: an Original Side that tries civil suits directly, and an Appellate Side that hears appeals, writ petitions and other work referred to it from across West Bengal. Sitting beside both of these is the City Civil Court, Calcutta, a separate court created by state legislation in 1953 specifically to take the bulk of ordinary civil litigation off the High Court's Original Side. Since 2016 a fourth layer has been added on top of all of this: a Commercial Division, constituted inside both the High Court and the City Civil Court, that takes over any dispute meeting the definition of a commercial dispute under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015.

For a business owner this is not an academic point about court administration. Filing a suit in the wrong one of these forums in Kolkata does not always produce a quick transfer order; it can produce an objection to jurisdiction that sits on the record for years, a defendant who argues the entire suit is a nullity, or a plaintiff who discovers at the Section 12A mediation stage that the dispute should never have gone to the forum it was sent to. A trader, a landlord, a lender or a contractor operating in or around Kolkata needs a working map of which of these four forums actually has the case before a single document is drafted, not after a defendant's lawyer has pointed out the problem.

This piece sets out that map as it stands today: the historical split between the Original and Appellate Sides of the High Court, the City Civil Court's place beneath it, the Commercial Division's reach over both, the district courts of the 24 Parganas that take over once a dispute steps outside Kolkata's old town limits, and the arbitration and execution questions that follow once a forum has actually decided the case. Where the exact rupee figures that separate these forums are reported differently by different sources, that is said plainly rather than papered over, because getting the figure wrong is exactly the kind of mistake that costs a Kolkata litigant real time.

Original Side and Appellate Side: two courts wearing one name

The Calcutta High Court's ordinary original civil jurisdiction traces back to Clause 12 of the Letters Patent of 1865, the charter instrument that, along with its 1862 predecessor, created the High Court and continues to define a part of its working structure even today. Clause 12 does two things that matter to a Kolkata litigant. First, it confines the High Court's power to try a civil suit directly, rather than on appeal, to matters connected with the old town of Calcutta: a suit for land can only be filed there if the land lies within that area, and a suit of any other kind can be filed there only if the defendant resides or carries on business within the same limits, or if at least part of the cause of action arose there and the court grants leave to sue on that basis. Second, because that jurisdiction is tied to a defined geographic area rather than to the whole of West Bengal, every other kind of judicial business reaching the High Court, meaning appeals from subordinate courts across the state, writ petitions under Article 226 of the Constitution, and references from tribunals, falls instead to what the Court's own rules call the Appellate Side.

The area to which Clause 12 jurisdiction is confined is often described as bounded broadly by Circular Road, a small but commercially significant part of the city that includes much of the business district around Dalhousie Square and the old mercantile quarter. The precise boundary rests on the Calcutta High Court (Jurisdictional Limits) Act, 1919, which gave statutory form to the limits originally fixed under the Letters Patent; a litigant who needs the boundary resolved for a specific property or place of business should check the Act's schedule and the High Court's current practice directions rather than relying on the rough description given here, since the 1919 Act's text is the primary source on the point and secondary commentary on the exact line is not always consistent.

The practical consequence for a filing decision is that a case must be marked for the correct side from the outset, and the High Court's own Original Side Rules set out how that marking is to be done: broadly, where the respondents or the record in question sit within the Original Side's territorial reach, the matter is listed there, and everything else goes to the Appellate Side. Courts have held that a suit or writ petition filed on the wrong side is not simply a formality to be waived; one reported decision held a writ petition not maintainable on the Appellate Side where the entire cause of action fell within the Original Side's territorial jurisdiction, though the Court has in other instances recognised an inherent power to permit conversion from one side to the other rather than dismissing the proceeding outright. A Kolkata-based business and its counsel should treat the choice of side as a jurisdictional question to settle before drafting begins, not as paperwork to be corrected later if challenged.

The City Civil Court: Kolkata's main civil forum below the ceiling

Before 1953, every ordinary civil suit arising within the town of Calcutta, whatever its value, had nowhere to go but the High Court's Original Side, a position that became increasingly unworkable as the city's population and commercial activity grew through the twentieth century. The West Bengal legislature answered this by passing the Calcutta City Civil Court Act, 1953, which created a new court with its own cadre of judges and gave it jurisdiction over civil suits up to a defined pecuniary ceiling; the court itself began functioning on 23 February 1957. The effect was to split what had been a single jurisdiction into two tiers: suits within the ceiling go to the City Civil Court, and suits above it, or suits of a kind the Act does not cover, remain with the High Court's Original Side.

That ceiling has moved more than once since 1953. Secondary accounts of the Act's history describe an original pecuniary limit as low as one lakh rupees, raised to ten lakh rupees by a later amendment, a figure that a number of older reported judgments still reflect. The most recent change on record is the West Bengal Act 18 of 2013, dated 10 October 2013, which raised the City Civil Court's pecuniary jurisdiction under Section 5(2) of the 1953 Act from ten lakh rupees to one crore rupees; the bill's own statement of objects and reasons gives exactly this purpose. A business valuing a claim at, say, sixty lakh rupees should today expect to file in the City Civil Court rather than the High Court's Original Side, while a claim comfortably above one crore rupees sits with the Original Side instead, subject always to confirming that no further amendment has moved the figure since 2013.

This ceiling applies to the ordinary run of civil suits: money claims, suits for specific performance, suits touching immovable property within the town limits, and the like. It does not by itself answer whether a dispute is also a commercial dispute for the purposes of the Commercial Courts Act, which is a separate question addressed below and can send a suit to a Commercial Division sitting inside either the City Civil Court or the High Court, depending on value, regardless of where the ordinary civil ceiling would otherwise have placed it.

The Commercial Division: a cross-cutting layer over both courts

The Commercial Courts Act, 2015 did not replace the structure described above; it cut across it. Where a dispute falls within one of the closed categories listed in Section 2(1)(c) of the Act, covering ordinary mercantile transactions, construction and infrastructure contracts, intellectual property disputes, joint venture and shareholder disputes, and a defined list of similar categories, and meets the specified value, the dispute is heard by a Commercial Division rather than by whichever ordinary civil forum would otherwise have had it. West Bengal constituted a Commercial Division within the Calcutta High Court by a notification dated 16 July 2016, and the City Civil Court has its own commercial jurisdiction as well, so that a commercial dispute of modest value can land in a Commercial Division sitting inside the City Civil Court rather than inside the High Court itself. Reported Calcutta High Court decisions describe the two as holding concurrent jurisdiction over a defined band of commercial disputes, with the High Court's Commercial Division taking disputes above a further threshold.

Pinning down that further threshold is less uncertain than it may first appear. The Commercial Courts Act's 2018 amendment lowered the national floor for a commercial dispute's specified value from one crore rupees to three lakh rupees, and left state governments free to fix the actual figure for their own commercial courts anywhere above that floor. West Bengal's notification dated 20 March 2020, issued under Section 3(1A) of the Act, set a three-tier structure that Calcutta High Court rulings, including a 2024 decision applying it to a claim of about sixty-seven lakh rupees and later rulings in 2026, have applied consistently since: the City Civil Court's Commercial Division has exclusive jurisdiction below ten lakh rupees, the City Civil Court and the High Court's Commercial Division share concurrent jurisdiction between ten lakh rupees and one crore rupees, and the High Court's Commercial Division has exclusive jurisdiction above one crore rupees. A further Calcutta High Court notification dated 11 November 2025 is cited in later rulings on this point, so a litigant should still confirm against the High Court's current notification that this banding has not since been revised before relying on it in a filing.

West Bengal has also notified commercial courts outside Kolkata itself, at Siliguri, Asansol, Alipore and Rajarhat among other locations, each exercising commercial jurisdiction within its own territorial limits at the level below the High Court's Commercial Division. A business whose registered office or principal place of business sits in one of these districts, rather than within the town of Calcutta, should expect its commercial dispute to be filed before the local commercial court rather than assuming Kolkata's City Civil Court or High Court has concurrent reach over it; territorial jurisdiction under the ordinary rules of the Code of Civil Procedure continues to decide which of these courts is the right one even after the commercial layer is added.

One point is settled regardless of where the specified-value line falls: once a Commercial Division has been constituted, it does not share arbitration-related business with the ordinary civil benches sitting alongside it. A 2026 Calcutta High Court ruling held plainly that non-commercial benches cease to have jurisdiction over commercial arbitration matters once a Commercial Division exists, a point developed further in the section on arbitration below.

Section 12A mediation: the same national rule, applied at Kolkata's own mediation centre

A commercial suit of the specified value cannot be filed in Kolkata, any more than anywhere else in India, without first attempting pre-institution mediation under Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, unless the plaint genuinely contemplates urgent interim relief. The Supreme Court settled the mandatory character of this requirement in Patil Automation Private Limited v. Rakheja Engineers Private Limited, (2022) 10 SCC 1, holding that a suit filed in breach of Section 12A is liable to be rejected under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, with that consequence applied prospectively to suits filed on or after 20 August 2022. The Calcutta High Court's Commercial Division, like every other commercial court in the country, applies this rule as a threshold question before the suit proceeds any further.

The urgency exception has been read narrowly since the Supreme Court's decision in Yamini Manohar v. T.K.D. Keerthi, reported as 2023 LiveLaw (SC) 906, which rejected the idea that a bare prayer for interim relief in the plaint is enough to avoid mediation. The Court must examine the pleadings themselves to see whether the case genuinely needs urgent protection, something closer to a threatened sale of specific assets or an imminent limitation bar than the generalised urgency that attends most disputes between an unpaid creditor and a reluctant debtor. A Kolkata plaintiff tempted to dress up an ordinary payment claim as urgent risks the same outcome courts elsewhere in India have reached: being sent back to mediation, with the months already spent drafting the plaint largely wasted.

The mediation itself runs through the authority constituted for the purpose under the rules framed for West Bengal, typically staffed through the state's legal services authority machinery, and the Calcutta High Court maintains its own Mediation Centre that handles court-referred mediation more broadly. A plaintiff should plan for the mediation process to take up to three months from the date of application, extendable by two further months with both sides' consent, and should use that window to finish preparing the suit itself rather than treating mediation as a pause in the real work.

Choosing where to file: territorial jurisdiction and the paperwork that follows

Once a business has worked out which tier, City Civil Court, Original Side, or a Commercial Division sitting within either, the next question is territorial jurisdiction, which continues to run on the ordinary rules in Sections 15 to 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure read together with any jurisdiction clause in the contract. A suit ordinarily lies where the defendant resides or carries on business, or where the cause of action, wholly or in part, arose. For a Kolkata business dealing with a counterparty elsewhere in India, this usually means the suit can still be filed in Kolkata if the contract was performed there, payment was due there, or the defendant has a place of business there, even if the defendant's registered office sits outside West Bengal.

Contracts drafted for Kolkata counterparties frequently name 'the courts at Kolkata' or 'the courts at Calcutta' without specifying which of the four forums described above is meant, and that vagueness becomes a live dispute only once litigation is actually needed. A clause naming a court that would not independently have jurisdiction under the Code cannot confer jurisdiction on it merely by agreement, so parties drafting fresh contracts do better to state expressly whether a commercial dispute of the specified value is meant to go to the Commercial Division of the High Court or of the City Civil Court, and to use the City Civil Court's and the High Court's formal names rather than the informal shorthand of 'Kolkata courts' that invites argument later.

Valuation of the suit deserves separate attention from valuation of the underlying claim, because it decides which of the forums described above actually has the case. The specified value for a commercial dispute is ordinarily the amount claimed for a money suit, but a claim touching immovable property or seeking specific performance can require a distinct valuation exercise under the applicable court fees legislation. A plaintiff who undervalues a suit to save on court fees, or who overlooks that a claim crosses the City Civil Court's one crore rupee ceiling, risks a defendant raising a jurisdictional objection that sends the matter to a different forum after months have already passed, effectively restarting the clock on mediation and on the case management process described in the next section.

Once filed: case management, disclosure and summary judgment for commercial suits

A commercial suit filed in Kolkata, in either the Commercial Division of the High Court or of the City Civil Court, proceeds under the amended Code of Civil Procedure provisions introduced specifically for commercial suits nationally, and these do not vary from state to state. Order XI requires both sides to disclose relevant documents early and with specificity, and a party that fails to disclose a document it later wants to rely on faces real difficulty introducing it afterward. A defendant has thirty days from service of summons to file a written statement, extendable to a maximum of one hundred and twenty days only on payment of costs, after which the right to file a written statement is forfeited altogether; this is considerably tighter than the regime most litigants are used to from ordinary civil practice outside the Commercial Courts Act.

The case management hearing under Order XV-A is meant to be held within four weeks of the affidavits of admission and denial being filed, and at that hearing the court frames issues, fixes a timetable for evidence, and sets dates for arguments, with the stated aim of closing arguments within six months of the first such hearing. Whether a given bench in Kolkata holds strictly to this timetable varies in practice, and the better approach for a party is to treat the statutory timeline as the standard to insist on at each hearing rather than something that enforces itself without active prompting from counsel.

Order XIII-A lets a party apply for summary judgment, without a full trial, once summons has been served but before issues have been framed, where the opposing party has no real prospect of succeeding and there is no other compelling reason for a trial. The Supreme Court encouraged commercial courts generally to decide such applications rather than deferring every dispute to trial out of habit, in Reliance Eminent Trading and Commercial Private Limited v. Delhi Development Authority, 2026 INSC 436, particularly where the dispute turns on a point of law or contractual construction rather than genuinely contested facts. A Kolkata defendant facing such an application needs a response that goes beyond bare denial, identifying specifically which facts remain genuinely contested rather than relying on the general argument that the claim should go to trial as a matter of course.

Arbitration seated in Kolkata: which bench actually decides it

A business that chooses Kolkata as the seat of arbitration in its contract needs to know, separately from the civil forum question above, which bench of the Calcutta High Court actually hears its Section 9, Section 11, Section 34 and Section 37 applications. The settled answer since the Commercial Division's constitution is that these go to the Commercial Division rather than to an ordinary civil bench of the Original Side, even where the party originally filed the application on the Original Side without routing it there directly. A 2024 Calcutta High Court ruling held that a Section 11(5) application filed on the Original Side still had to be decided by the Commercial Division, reasoning that Section 10(2) of the Commercial Courts Act uses the word 'shall' and is accordingly mandatory, and a 2026 ruling went further, holding that non-commercial benches cease to have jurisdiction over commercial arbitration matters entirely once the Commercial Division exists. A Section 34 challenge decided by the wrong bench has been set aside on this basis by a Division Bench in 2025, underlining that getting the bench right is not a mere formality that a later court will overlook.

Naming Kolkata in the arbitration clause is not by itself enough to fix the seat, and this distinction has tripped up more than one party before the Calcutta High Court. In Ravi Ranjan Developers Private Limited v. Aditya Kumar Chatterjee, decided by the Supreme Court in 2022, the Court set aside an arbitrator appointment made by the Calcutta High Court because neither the cause of action nor the seat of arbitration was actually connected to Kolkata; the clause had referred to Kolkata only as a venue where the tribunal might sit, not as the juridical seat, and the Supreme Court held that a Section 11(6) application has to go to the High Court exercising supervisory jurisdiction under Section 2(1)(e) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, over the court that would otherwise have jurisdiction, which was not the Calcutta High Court on the facts of that case. A Kolkata business drafting an arbitration clause should state expressly that Kolkata is the seat, and not merely the venue or the city where hearings are expected to be convenient, if it wants the Calcutta High Court to have supervisory jurisdiction when a dispute arises.

On the separate question of how an arbitrator is actually appointed once a Section 11 application is properly before the Calcutta High Court, the position nationally, and in Kolkata along with it, is still governed by the pre-2019 version of Section 11(6A), which confines the court to examining the existence of the arbitration agreement rather than its validity on the merits. The Supreme Court held in Mayavati Trading Private Limited v. Pradyuat Deb Burman, (2019) 8 SCC 714, that the 2019 amendment's substitution of this provision, which would have shifted appointments to arbitral institutions designated by the Chief Justice, never actually came into force because the notification needed to bring it into effect was never issued, so courts continue to apply the earlier, narrower test. Parties in Kolkata negotiating an arbitration clause should not assume an institutional-appointment regime is already operating in practice; ad hoc appointment by the High Court itself remains the ordinary route unless and until that notification is issued.

Court-annexed mediation in Kolkata, through the Calcutta High Court Mediation Centre, is a separate process from arbitration and should not be confused with it; the Centre handles mediation referred by the court in pending litigation, including the Section 12A pre-institution mediation described earlier, rather than functioning as an arbitral institution administering arbitration proceedings.

When the dispute sits outside Kolkata's town limits: Alipore and the district courts

Not every business with a Kolkata address actually falls within the forums described so far. The City Civil Court's jurisdiction, and the High Court's Original Side jurisdiction under Clause 12, both depend on the dispute being connected to the old town of Calcutta, roughly the area bounded by Circular Road and the historic municipal limits; large parts of what is commonly thought of as Kolkata today, including substantial stretches of the southern and northern fringes of the metropolitan area, fall instead within South 24 Parganas or North 24 Parganas district for civil court purposes. A factory in Budge Budge, a warehouse in Baruipur, or an office whose registered address sits in a part of the metropolitan area outside the old town limits answers to the district judge and the subordinate civil judges of South 24 Parganas, sitting at Alipore, rather than to the City Civil Court.

Alipore functions as the district and sessions headquarters for South 24 Parganas, with a cadre of subordinate judges holding jurisdiction across the district subject to the pecuniary and territorial limits fixed for each court by the usual notifications under the Bengal, Agra and Assam Civil Courts Act, 1887 and related West Bengal rules. A business dealing with a counterparty or a property in this part of the metropolitan area, or in the equivalent district courts for North 24 Parganas sitting at Barasat, should not assume that Kolkata's City Civil Court or High Court Original Side has jurisdiction simply because the dispute is commonly described as a Kolkata matter; the actual location of the property, the defendant's place of business, or the place where the cause of action arose decides the forum, exactly as it would for any other district in the state.

Commercial disputes arising within these districts are not left without a commercial forum of their own. As noted above, West Bengal has notified a commercial court at Alipore itself, exercising the same Commercial Courts Act jurisdiction over qualifying disputes within South 24 Parganas that the Commercial Division exercises within Kolkata's town limits, so a business in this part of the metropolitan area need not look to central Kolkata at all once it has confirmed that its dispute is, in fact, a commercial dispute of the specified value.

Appeals: three different clocks depending on the route

A first appeal from a City Civil Court decree, where the court is treated as a principal civil court of original jurisdiction for appellate purposes, lies to the Calcutta High Court under Section 96 of the Code of Civil Procedure, and carries the ordinary ninety-day limitation period available under the Limitation Act, 1963 for a regular first appeal, unless the dispute is a commercial one, in which case the shorter commercial appeal period described below applies instead. An appeal from a decree of a district judge or subordinate judge at Alipore or Barasat follows the same ordinary route to the High Court's Appellate Side.

A decision of a single judge sitting on the High Court's Original Side follows a different route again: an intra-court appeal, commonly called a Letters Patent Appeal, lies to a Division Bench of the same High Court under Clause 15 of the Letters Patent, rather than directly to the Supreme Court or to an external appellate forum. This internal appeal structure is a distinctive feature of the chartered High Courts, including Calcutta, and does not exist in the same form for High Courts that lack original civil jurisdiction.

For a commercial dispute, Section 13 of the Commercial Courts Act displaces both of the routes above with its own scheme: an appeal from a Commercial Court below the level of a District Judge lies to the Commercial Appellate Division of the High Court, and an appeal from the Commercial Division of the High Court itself, whether that Commercial Division sits within the City Civil Court or within the High Court's own structure, likewise lies to the Commercial Appellate Division of the same High Court, not to any outside forum. Section 13(1A) fixes the limitation period for this appeal at sixty days from the date of the judgment or order, markedly shorter than the ninety days available for an ordinary first appeal. The Supreme Court has confirmed that Section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963 can still extend this period in a genuine case of sufficient cause, following a three-judge bench's 2021 correction of its own earlier ruling in N.V. International v. State of Assam, but has been equally clear that condonation beyond sixty days is the exception granted for a short delay with a bona fide explanation, not a routine extension a litigant can count on. A Kolkata litigant who misses the sixty-day window should start preparing a day-by-day account of the delay immediately, rather than waiting until the appeal itself is ready to file, since the explanation for the delay matters as much as the appeal's merits at the condonation stage.

Beyond the Commercial Appellate Division, the only further recourse is a special leave petition to the Supreme Court under Article 136 of the Constitution, which is discretionary rather than a matter of right and is granted sparingly, typically where the case raises a substantial question of law or a manifest failure of justice rather than simply a disagreement with how the facts were assessed below.

Getting paid: executing a decree or award once the forum has ruled

Winning a suit or arbitration in Kolkata is not the end of the matter if the losing party does not pay voluntarily. A money decree, whether from the City Civil Court, the High Court's Original Side, or a Commercial Division sitting within either, is executed under Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, which lets the decree-holder attach and sell the judgment debtor's movable or immovable property, attach bank accounts, or in limited circumstances seek the debtor's arrest and detention where the debtor has the means to pay and wilfully refuses. An arbitral award is executed in the same way, as if it were a decree of the court, once the time for a Section 34 challenge has passed or any such challenge has failed, under Section 36 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996.

Where the judgment debtor's assets sit outside the executing court's own territorial reach, perhaps a bank account in Delhi or a factory in another state, the decree-holder applies under Section 39 of the Code for the Kolkata court that passed the decree to transfer it for execution to the court within whose limits those assets are situated. This is routine practice rather than a fresh suit, but it still takes real time, and a decree-holder in Kolkata is better served by identifying the judgment debtor's attachable assets well before the decree is actually passed, through disclosure obtained during the suit or independent inquiry, rather than starting that search only once execution proceedings are filed.

A judgment debtor is not without recourse at the execution stage itself. Objections under Section 47 of the Code, and a challenge to a decree obtained by fraud or without jurisdiction, can still be raised even after the decree has become final on the merits, and a decree-holder should expect execution to take meaningful time even after winning outright, particularly where the judgment debtor is determined to resist payment through every procedural avenue the Code allows.

A practical sequence for a Kolkata business

A business contemplating litigation in Kolkata does better working through a defined sequence than rushing to draft a plaint. The first step is confirming where the dispute is actually located for jurisdictional purposes: within the old town limits governed by Clause 12 of the Letters Patent and the City Civil Court Act, or within South or North 24 Parganas district where Alipore or Barasat takes over instead. The second step is valuing the claim carefully against the City Civil Court's one crore rupee ceiling and, separately, against whichever specified-value figure currently applies to commercial disputes, since both figures decide the forum and getting either one wrong risks a jurisdictional objection months into the case.

The third step, for a dispute that qualifies as commercial, is deciding honestly whether the facts support a genuine claim of urgency before attempting to skip Section 12A mediation; a party that overstates urgency and is caught out by the Yamini Manohar test loses more time than one that completes mediation in parallel with preparing the suit. The fourth is assembling the documentary record with the Order XI disclosure obligations already in mind, since a party that is disciplined about disclosure from the outset is also better placed to bring or resist a summary judgment application later.

The fifth step, specific to any dispute touching arbitration, is checking the contract's wording on seat and venue before assuming the Calcutta High Court has supervisory jurisdiction; a clause that names Kolkata only as a convenient hearing location, rather than the seat, can leave a party appealing to the wrong court entirely, as the Ravi Ranjan Developers case shows. The sixth is calendaring the correct appeal clock from the day any adverse order is passed, sixty days for a commercial appeal and ninety for an ordinary one, since the gap between these two periods catches out litigants who assume the longer period applies by default.

A seventh and often neglected step is keeping a clear, dated internal record of the dispute as it develops, since Kolkata's courts, like commercial courts elsewhere, expect parties to plead specific dates and specific documents rather than general narratives of a business relationship gone wrong. A business that can hand its lawyer a clean record of correspondence, invoices and demands has a genuine head start at every stage described in this piece, from the choice of forum through mediation, case management and, if it comes to that, execution of the final decree.

Where the position remains unsettled

Three points deserve a flag rather than a firm answer. The first is whether West Bengal's 20 March 2020 notification under Section 3(1A), which Calcutta High Court rulings have applied as giving the City Civil Court's Commercial Division exclusive jurisdiction below ten lakh rupees, concurrent jurisdiction with the High Court's Commercial Division between ten lakh rupees and one crore rupees, and the High Court's Commercial Division exclusive jurisdiction above one crore rupees, remains the operative position after a further notification dated 11 November 2025. A Kolkata litigant with a claim in this range should get the current gazette notification checked directly before relying on this banding.

The second is the precise geographic boundary of the 'town of Calcutta' for the purposes of Clause 12 jurisdiction and the City Civil Court Act. The 1919 Act fixing those limits is the primary source, and while the boundary is commonly described as running along Circular Road, a business with a property or office close to that boundary should have the point checked against the Act's schedule rather than against the general description given in this piece.

The third is the extent to which the district commercial courts at Siliguri, Asansol, Alipore and Rajarhat share a single pecuniary structure with each other and with the Kolkata forums; the sources available for this piece confirm that these courts exist and exercise commercial jurisdiction within their own districts, but do not reliably confirm a specific rupee figure for each, and that figure is accordingly left out rather than stated on the strength of a single source.

Kolkata's civil forum structure rewards a litigant who checks the map before filing rather than one who assumes the city works the way most other Indian commercial centres do. The split between the Original and Appellate Sides of the High Court, the City Civil Court's place beneath it, the Commercial Division's reach across both, and the district courts waiting just outside the old town limits are not formalities to clear quickly on the way to the real dispute; getting any one of them wrong is itself capable of becoming the dispute.

The clearest practical lesson is that Kolkata's forum questions are, more often than in most Indian cities, questions of degree rather than of kind: a difference of a few lakh rupees in valuation, or a few hundred metres in the location of a defendant's office, can shift a case from one court to an entirely different one with its own procedure and its own appeal clock. Building that checking step into a dispute strategy from the outset remains the most reliable way to use Kolkata's layered court system rather than be caught out by it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Calcutta High Court's Original Side a different court from its Appellate Side?

They sit within the same High Court but exercise different jurisdictions. The Original Side tries civil suits directly, under a jurisdiction confined to the old town of Calcutta by Clause 12 of the 1865 Letters Patent, while the Appellate Side hears appeals, writ petitions and other work from across West Bengal. A case filed on the wrong side can face a maintainability objection, so the correct side has to be identified before filing.

What is the pecuniary limit of the City Civil Court, Calcutta?

It is reported at one crore rupees following a West Bengal amendment dated 10 October 2013 that raised it from ten lakh rupees. A suit valued above this figure, arising within the town of Calcutta, ordinarily goes to the High Court's Original Side instead, subject to confirming no further change has been made to the figure since.

How does a business know if its Kolkata dispute counts as a 'commercial dispute'?

It has to fall within one of the closed categories listed in Section 2(1)(c) of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, such as ordinary mercantile transactions, construction contracts, intellectual property disputes or shareholder disputes, and meet the specified value that applies in West Bengal. West Bengal's notification of 20 March 2020 places this at ten lakh rupees: the City Civil Court's Commercial Division has exclusive jurisdiction below that figure, both courts share concurrent jurisdiction between ten lakh rupees and one crore rupees, and the High Court's Commercial Division has exclusive jurisdiction above one crore rupees, subject to confirming that a later notification, including one dated 11 November 2025, has not revised this banding.

Can a Kolkata commercial suit skip Section 12A mediation if it is urgent?

Only if the plaint itself genuinely shows a specific and immediate need for interim protection, not a general claim of urgency. Following the Supreme Court's ruling in Yamini Manohar v. T.K.D. Keerthi, the Calcutta High Court, like commercial courts elsewhere, examines the pleadings closely and sends the parties back to mediation if the urgency claim looks like a device to avoid Section 12A.

If an arbitration clause names Kolkata, does the Calcutta High Court automatically have jurisdiction over it?

Not necessarily. The Supreme Court's 2022 decision in Ravi Ranjan Developers Private Limited v. Aditya Kumar Chatterjee held that naming Kolkata only as a venue, rather than as the juridical seat, does not confer jurisdiction on the Calcutta High Court. A clause should state expressly that Kolkata is the seat of arbitration if that is what the parties intend.

Which bench of the Calcutta High Court decides a Section 9 or Section 34 application connected to a commercial dispute?

The Commercial Division, once one has been constituted, rather than an ordinary civil bench of the Original Side. Calcutta High Court rulings in 2024, 2025 and 2026 have held that this applies even where the application was originally filed on the Original Side, and that non-commercial benches lose jurisdiction over such matters once the Commercial Division exists.

My business and the other side are both based in what is commonly called Kolkata. Does the City Civil Court always have jurisdiction?

Not always. The City Civil Court's reach, like the High Court Original Side's, is confined to the old town of Calcutta's limits. A business whose property, office or counterparty sits in the wider metropolitan area outside those limits, in South or North 24 Parganas district, falls instead within the jurisdiction of the district courts at Alipore or Barasat.

How long does a business have to appeal an adverse order from a Kolkata commercial court?

Sixty days from the date of the judgment or order, under Section 13(1A) of the Commercial Courts Act, compared with ninety days for an ordinary first appeal that is not a commercial dispute. Condonation of delay beyond sixty days is possible under Section 5 of the Limitation Act but is treated as an exception for a short, bona fide delay rather than a routine extension.
