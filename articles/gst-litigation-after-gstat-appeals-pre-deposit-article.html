GST Litigation After the GST Appellate Tribunal: Show Cause Notices, Pre-Deposit, ITC Denial and the New Appeal Map

With the GST Appellate Tribunal now hearing cases, the route from a show cause notice to final resolution has changed more than most assessees realise.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 24 October 2026 · Law stated as on 21 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

For the first eight years of the GST regime, a disputed demand that survived the first appeal usually went straight to a writ court, because the Tribunal the statute promised did not exist. That changed through late 2025 and into 2026, as the GST Appellate Tribunal was notified, staffed and opened for filing. The result is a more structured and more procedurally exacting path, with pre-deposit obligations at two separate stages, a backlog filing deadline that has already passed, and High Courts now routinely sending taxpayers back to the statutory ladder they had previously bypassed.

Key points

  • The GST Appellate Tribunal became operational in phases from September 2025, with its Principal Bench in New Delhi and State Benches now sitting at over forty locations.
  • A taxpayer typically faces pre-deposit twice: ten per cent of the disputed tax to appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals) under Section 107, and a further ten per cent, capped at twenty crore rupees each for CGST and SGST, to appeal to the Tribunal under Section 112.
  • The special backlog window for appeals against orders communicated before 1 April 2026 closed on 30 June 2026; appeals against later orders now run on the standard three-month limitation period.
  • Delhi High Court rulings in Gaurav Jain and Rohit Garg hold that the right of appeal, and the pre-deposit that attaches to it, vests on the date the show cause notice issues, so the October 2025 pre-deposit amendment does not reach appeals from earlier notices.
  • The Supreme Court has upheld Section 16(2)(c), denying input tax credit where the supplier has not paid tax, but at least one High Court has read the provision down for a bona fide purchaser, leaving the scope of that protection unsettled.
  • High Courts are now generally declining writ petitions once GSTAT is functional, reserving writ relief for a genuinely non-functional forum, a jurisdictional defect, or a clear breach of natural justice.
  • The Supreme Court has held that a Section 69 arrest order must be communicated to the person before arrest, giving an accused a real opportunity to seek anticipatory bail first.

From writ-heavy to tribunal-first: the shift GSTAT brings

The structure the CGST Act always promised was a three-tier appeal ladder: a first appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals) under Section 107, a second appeal to the GST Appellate Tribunal under Section 112, and a final appeal confined to substantial questions of law to the jurisdictional High Court under Section 117. For years after the Act came into force in 2017, the middle rung simply did not exist. GSTAT was not constituted, and a taxpayer whose appeal failed before the Commissioner had, in practice, only a writ petition under Article 226 to turn to, regardless of whether the dispute actually raised a constitutional or jurisdictional question suited to writ jurisdiction.

That gap closed through the second half of 2025 and into 2026. The Tribunal was formally launched on 24 September 2025, began its first phase of adjudicatory sittings on 16 February 2026 with the Principal Bench at New Delhi, and now operates through more than thirty State Benches sitting at over forty locations across the country. Justice (Retired) Sanjaya Kumar Mishra was appointed its first President. By March 2026 the e-filing portal was live, and by April 2026 over a thousand appeals had reportedly been filed online.

The consequence for a litigator is that the writ petition, which had become the default second-level remedy almost by necessity, is no longer the obvious next step once a first appeal fails. High Courts across the country are now, with increasing consistency, directing taxpayers back to the statutory Tribunal, and the sections that follow trace what that means at each stage, from the show cause notice through to a possible appeal to the High Court on a point of law.

The demand stage: Sections 73, 74 and the unified Section 74A

For tax periods up to FY 2023-24, a demand for unpaid, short-paid or erroneously refunded tax, or for wrongly availed input tax credit, proceeds under one of two provisions depending on whether fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression is alleged. Section 73 covers the ordinary case without any such allegation. The order must generally issue within three years of the due date for filing the annual return for the relevant year, and the show cause notice itself must issue at least three months before that three-year deadline. Section 74 covers cases involving fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts, carries a five-year window for the order, and requires the notice at least six months ahead of that deadline. The penalty consequences differ sharply: a non-fraud demand under Section 73 that is paid promptly can attract a materially lower penalty than a fraud finding under Section 74, which is part of why a taxpayer receiving a notice should immediately check which provision it invokes and whether the underlying facts actually support a fraud allegation.

The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 introduced Section 74A with effect from 1 November 2024, applicable to tax periods from FY 2024-25 onward. It folds the fraud and non-fraud scenarios into a single provision: a show cause notice must issue within forty-two months of the relevant due date, and the order must follow within twelve months of the notice, extendable by a further six months where reasons are recorded. The penalty rate under Section 74A still depends on whether fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression is found, even though both scenarios now share one procedural track. For periods up to FY 2023-24, Sections 73 and 74 continue to apply exactly as before; Section 74A has not displaced them retrospectively.

This dual regime, old-period demands under Sections 73 or 74 and new-period demands under Section 74A, means a tax team handling a multi-year audit or investigation frequently has to track two separate limitation clocks for what feels, commercially, like a single continuing dispute. Getting the applicable section and its corresponding deadline right for each year under scrutiny is often the first and most consequential check before any argument on the merits is even reached.

Show cause notice defects and the natural justice safeguards taxpayers can invoke

A disproportionate share of GST litigation at every level, from the first reply to the department through to the High Court, turns not on whether tax is actually due but on whether the show cause notice and the order that followed it complied with basic procedural safeguards. A notice that fails to disclose the specific grounds relied on, that is issued by an officer without territorial or subject-matter jurisdiction over the taxpayer, or that is followed by an order passed without considering the taxpayer's reply at all, is vulnerable on natural justice grounds independent of the substantive tax position.

Two recurring fact patterns illustrate this well. The Calcutta High Court has repeatedly set aside registration cancellations and other adverse orders where the officer who conducted the hearing and the officer who issued the final decision were different people, treating this mismatch as a failure of the hearing requirement itself rather than a mere irregularity. In one such matter, the Court set aside a rejection of a revocation application because the department had relied on a field inspection report that was never supplied to the taxpayer, compounding the defect with a separate complaint that the taxpayer's consultant had been unavailable to respond to the underlying show cause notice in time.

A notice issued to an entity that no longer legally exists raises a different, more fundamental defect. The Supreme Court refused to revive a demand of roughly three hundred and sixty-three crore rupees against Vodafone Idea, holding that a GST notice cannot be issued to an entity that has ceased to exist following a merger, since there is simply no addressee in law capable of responding to or being bound by it. A taxpayer facing a notice addressed to a predecessor entity, a dissolved partnership, or a company that has since merged into another should treat this as a threshold jurisdictional objection to be raised immediately, rather than folding it into a reply on the merits.

First appeal: Commissioner (Appeals) under Section 107

An appeal against an adverse order goes first to the Commissioner (Appeals), filed in Form APL-01 within three months of the order being communicated, with the appellate authority empowered to condone a further delay of up to one month on sufficient cause shown; several commentators treat this combined four-month window as a hard outer limit, with no residual power to condone delay beyond it. The appellant must pay the admitted tax in full, together with ten per cent of the remaining disputed tax, under Section 107(6), with that ten per cent computed on the tax amount alone and excluding interest, penalty and fee from the base. Once the pre-deposit is made, recovery proceedings for the balance disputed amount are treated as stayed for the duration of the appeal.

The ceiling on this pre-deposit was reduced from twenty-five crore rupees to twenty crore rupees, applied separately to central and state tax, with effect from 1 November 2024, following the GST Council's recommendation at its fifty-third meeting. The GST Council has since also recommended a separate forty crore rupee ceiling specifically for appeals that involve only a penalty with no accompanying tax demand, intended to soften the burden on penalty-only disputes, though whether this recommendation has actually been enacted into the statute could not be confirmed from the sources available and should be checked against the current notified position before being relied on.

A string of 2026 Madras High Court decisions has pushed back against departmental attempts to demand a higher pre-deposit than the statute allows, holding that a requirement of twenty-five per cent, where the statutory figure is ten per cent under Section 107, cannot stand. Taken together with the Calcutta High Court's insistence on consistent officer identity between hearing and decision, the first appellate stage has become, in 2026, considerably more exacting on the department's own procedural compliance than it was treated as being in the regime's earlier years.

The pre-deposit retrospectivity dispute: Gaurav Jain, Rohit Garg and the vested right of appeal

An amendment effective 1 October 2025 substituted the proviso to Section 107(6), extending the ten per cent pre-deposit requirement to appeals against orders that impose only a penalty, with no accompanying tax demand, a category that had not previously attracted any pre-deposit at all. The amendment's reach into disputes that originated before it took effect became the subject of a significant Delhi High Court line of authority through 2026.

In Gaurav Jain v. Joint Commissioner (Appeals-II), CGST Delhi Zone, the Delhi High Court held that the right of appeal is a substantive, vested right that attaches to a dispute at its very commencement, which in a tax adjudication corresponds to the date the show cause notice issues, not the date the final order is passed. Applying that principle in Rohit Garg v. Union of India, a Division Bench held that the amended pre-deposit requirement under the substituted proviso to Section 107(6) cannot be applied to appeals arising from adjudication proceedings that began with a show cause notice issued before 1 October 2025, and that appeals should not be rejected merely because the order-in-original itself happened to be passed after that date. The Court declined, however, to interfere by writ with the underlying demand itself in that batch of petitions, tied to a common investigation spanning more than six hundred entities, holding that the challenges did not disclose the kind of patent lack of jurisdiction that would justify bypassing the statutory Section 107 appeal altogether.

A September 2026 batch of writs extended the same Gaurav Jain reasoning to penalty-only appeals pending at both the first appellate stage and before GSTAT itself, suggesting the vested-right principle is being treated as a general rule for pre-deposit amendments rather than confined to the specific October 2025 change. One distinct issue was left open in Rohit Garg: whether Section 122 penalties can be imposed on non-taxable persons at all is, per that ruling, pending before the Supreme Court and was not finally decided.

GSTAT at last: benches, backlog deadline and where things stand today

Notification S.O. 4220(E), dated 17 September 2025, fixed the limitation framework for the Tribunal's opening phase. For any order communicated to the appellant before 1 April 2026, the appeal had to be filed before GSTAT by 30 June 2026, a single backlog deadline covering what had, by then, become several years of appeals that had nowhere statutory to go. That date has now passed. For orders communicated on or after 1 April 2026, the standard limitation period under Section 112, three months from communication of the order, applies in the ordinary way, with the Tribunal empowered to condone a further delay of up to three months under Section 112(6) on sufficient cause.

A separate date, 31 December 2026, is sometimes mentioned alongside the backlog deadline, but commentary is clear that this is not a further extension of the filing window itself; it marks only the date up to which a relaxed scrutiny and defect-management process on the e-filing portal continues to apply to appeals that were filed within the proper window.

Electronic filing on the GSTAT portal is now the exclusive route; manual filing is no longer available, and a separate appeal is required for each aggrieved person, with joint appeals not permitted under the Tribunal's Procedure Rules, 2025, notified on 24 April 2025. Early commentary in late 2025 noted that the portal was not yet fully functional and recorded very few filings in its first weeks, but by the first quarter of 2026 the Tribunal was reporting over a thousand appeals filed and had begun its substantive hearings. Taxpayers with a pending or anticipated GST dispute should treat the backlog deadline as closed and should calculate their own limitation from whichever date their order was actually communicated, rather than assuming any residual grace period remains available.

Appeal to GSTAT under Section 112: pre-deposit, e-filing and an unresolved ambiguity

An appeal to GSTAT under Section 112 carries its own pre-deposit obligation, separate from and in addition to the ten per cent already paid to reach the Commissioner (Appeals). Section 112(8) requires the appellant to pay the full admitted amount, plus a further ten per cent of the remaining disputed tax, in addition to the amount already paid under Section 107(6), with the tax component of this second deposit capped at twenty crore rupees. Commentary is divided on whether this cap applies once in total or separately to the central and state components of the tax, with some sources describing a combined forty crore rupee ceiling in the way the first-appeal cap operates, though the statutory text itself appears to speak of a single cap at the Tribunal stage rather than two parallel ones; this is a point worth confirming in the specific appeal rather than assuming either reading.

A more basic ambiguity concerns the total cumulative burden by the time an appeal reaches the Tribunal. One line of commentary treats the combined pre-deposit, ten per cent at the first appeal plus a further ten per cent at the Tribunal, as reaching twenty per cent of the disputed tax in total by the time GSTAT hears the matter. Another reads the Tribunal-stage ten per cent as genuinely additional on top of whatever was paid before, which produces the same twenty per cent outcome in most cases but is framed as an additive mechanism rather than a cumulative percentage, and at least one detailed analysis has flagged the phrase in addition to in Section 112(8) as creating real interpretational uncertainty about edge cases, such as where the first-appeal pre-deposit was itself calculated on a different base. This is not yet a settled question and deserves specific calculation, rather than an assumed round figure, in any given matter.

Separately, the GSTAT Help Centre's own published guidance was reported, as recently as mid-2026, to still refer to a twenty per cent pre-deposit figure in at least one place, in apparent tension with the ten per cent stated in Section 112(8) itself. Practitioners have been advised to keep a screenshot of whatever figure the portal displays when filing and to raise a formal portal incident if there is a discrepancy, since a dispute over the deposit amount at the filing stage can otherwise delay or jeopardise the appeal itself.

Input tax credit denial under Section 16(2)(c): the Bhandari Scrap Traders ruling and a High Court divide

Section 16(2)(c) conditions a buyer's entitlement to input tax credit on the supplier having actually paid the tax charged on the supply to the Government, a condition that places the burden of a supplier's default squarely on the buyer who paid for the goods or services in good faith and holds a valid invoice. This provision has generated some of the most contested GST litigation of the past two years precisely because a buyer has, in practice, no reliable mechanism to verify in real time whether its supplier has in fact remitted the tax collected from it.

The Supreme Court addressed the provision's validity in Bhandari Scrap Traders v. Union of India, dismissing the special leave petition by an order dated 24 July 2026 and holding that Section 16(2)(c) is constitutionally valid and does not need to be read down, distinguishing the GST credit mechanism from the earlier VAT framework under which some High Courts had taken a more buyer-protective view. The Court referred to the interplay between Sections 41 and 73 or 74 and Rule 37A, and reiterated, without directing it, that the Government should consider building a real-time mechanism to verify a supplier's tax payment, a point the Gujarat High Court had already made in its own May 2026 decision in Maruti Enterprise, which the Supreme Court's order affirmed.

A conflicting signal comes from the Tripura High Court's decision in Sahil Enterprises, delivered on 6 January 2026, which read Section 16(2)(c) down to protect a bona fide purchasing dealer, reasoning that there is no mechanism available to such a dealer to verify whether its supplier has actually paid the tax, and describing the burden the provision places on an innocent buyer as excessive. Commentary differs on how far the Supreme Court's later Bhandari Scrap Traders order actually disturbs Sahil Enterprises; one reading treats the Tripura decision as simply not addressed by the Supreme Court's order, while a separate and less reliable account claims the Supreme Court itself confined Section 16(2)(c) to cases of fraud or collusion, a claim that conflicts with the clearer reporting of the order upholding the provision without such a qualification. Given this conflict, a buyer facing an ITC denial on this ground should treat the point as unsettled rather than resolved, and should look closely at whether its own facts, particularly any evidence of genuine payment to the supplier and absence of any collusion, bring it within whatever protective reasoning a bona fide purchaser can still invoke pending clearer appellate guidance.

A practical remedy exists alongside the litigation. Section 41(2), in force since 1 October 2022, read with Rule 37A, requires a recipient to reverse previously claimed credit where the supplier has not paid the tax, with interest, but allows the recipient to re-avail that credit once the supplier eventually makes the payment. For a buyer caught in a genuine, non-collusive supplier default, pursuing reversal and re-availment under this mechanism, while also preserving the Section 16(2)(c) constitutional argument in case of a future favourable ruling, is often a more reliable short-term path than litigating the underlying denial to a conclusion that, as matters stand, could go either way depending on the forum.

Rule 86A blocking and the reversal-and-re-availment mechanism

Separate from a final denial of credit under Section 16(2)(c), Rule 86A allows a tax officer who has reason to believe that credit in the electronic ledger has been fraudulently availed or is otherwise ineligible to block the taxpayer's ability to use that credit, without first issuing a show cause notice or giving a hearing. This is a provisional, investigative tool rather than a final adjudication, and courts have accordingly scrutinised it on narrower but no less important grounds: whether the officer genuinely held reasons to believe, whether those reasons were recorded, and whether the block has outlasted the one-year outer limit the rule itself imposes.

High Court decisions on Rule 86A have consistently required that the reasons supporting a block be recorded and be capable of surviving judicial scrutiny, rather than being a formulaic recital, and have treated the one-year limit as a genuine outer boundary rather than a renewable administrative convenience. A taxpayer whose credit has been blocked under Rule 86A for longer than a year, or where the department cannot produce a contemporaneous record of its reasons, has a reasonably strong basis to challenge the block directly, often without needing to wait for whatever final adjudication the department may eventually bring under Section 73, 74 or 74A.

The distinction between a Rule 86A block and a final Section 16(2)(c) denial matters for strategy as much as for doctrine. A block is reversible on its own narrower procedural terms and does not require the taxpayer to engage with the underlying question of whether the supplier actually paid the tax; a final denial does. Conflating the two, by arguing the merits of supplier non-payment in response to what is only a provisional block, risks giving the department more to work with than the narrower procedural challenge would have required.

Writ jurisdiction after GSTAT: when the alternate remedy still yields

The general pattern across 2026 High Court decisions is a reversion to the ordinary alternate remedy doctrine the GST regime had, for practical reasons, been unable to apply fully while GSTAT remained unconstituted. The Orissa High Court disposed of a writ petition once GSTAT had been constituted, holding it would not be proper to keep a writ pending when the designated statutory forum could adjudicate the dispute, and in a later 2026 matter went further, holding that the pre-deposit requirement under Section 112(8) cannot be sidestepped by filing a writ petition once the Tribunal is functional, and directing the petitioner to make the pre-deposit as a condition of proceeding. The Allahabad High Court has taken the same view, dismissing a writ once GSTAT's members had actually been appointed and relying on earlier Supreme Court authority on the alternate remedy principle generally.

The exception courts continue to recognise is a genuinely non-functional forum. An earlier Orissa High Court ruling had accepted that a writ court can be approached where the statutory appellate forum is not functional, since a citizen should not be left without any remedy at all, though that same court went on to dismiss the writ once it noted that GSTAT had since become functional in the interim. The Madhya Pradesh High Court, dismissing a writ against a registration cancellation, took a similar approach while also noting that an alternate remedy is not an absolute bar to a writ petition; in that case, the combination of the petitioner's own missing documentation and the availability of the Tribunal route justified declining to entertain the writ.

For a litigator deciding where to file, the working position through 2026 is that a writ should be reserved for cases presenting a genuine jurisdictional defect, such as a notice issued to a non-existent entity along the lines of the Vodafone Idea ruling, a clear and demonstrable breach of natural justice such as the officer-mismatch pattern the Calcutta High Court has repeatedly flagged, or a forum that is not actually functional for the taxpayer's own State or category of dispute. Where none of these applies and GSTAT is open for filing, the statutory route, pre-deposit and all, is now the expected path, and a writ filed without addressing why the statutory alternative is inadequate risks summary dismissal.

Registration cancellation, revocation and recurring disputes

A cancelled GST registration can be revoked on application under Section 30 read with Rule 23, filed before the Proper Officer, but a cluster of 2026 Calcutta High Court decisions illustrates how often the cancellation or the rejection of a revocation application is itself procedurally flawed. In S.K.M. Timber Private Limited v. Superintendent of Central Tax, the Court treated a cancellation as unsustainable where the officer who conducted the hearing and the officer who actually decided the matter were different people, a defect the Court treated as going to the heart of what a hearing is meant to achieve rather than as a mere irregularity capable of being cured after the fact. In a related matter, the Court set aside a rejection of a revocation application where the department relied on a field inspection report that was never supplied to the taxpayer, compounded by the taxpayer's own difficulty in responding to the underlying notice because its consultant was unavailable at the relevant time.

Other Calcutta decisions have gone the other way on the facts while reinforcing the same procedural themes: one set aside a cancellation for lack of proof of property ownership at the registered address, finding the underlying ground itself unsustainable on the record, while another, describing cancellation as counter-productive to the revenue's own interest where the taxpayer was willing to clear outstanding dues, ordered revival of the registration on payment of all dues rather than leaving the business deregistered and therefore unable to generate further revenue for recovery.

The common thread across these disputes is that a registration cancellation, unlike a tax demand, often destroys the taxpayer's ability to continue operating at all while the underlying dispute is litigated, which gives procedural defects in the cancellation process outsized practical importance. A taxpayer facing cancellation should scrutinise, as a first step, whether the officer who heard the matter is the same officer who decided it, whether any report or document relied on in the order was actually furnished before the decision, and whether a genuine opportunity to respond, rather than a formal but practically unusable one, was given.

Arrest, summons and the Sunil Biyani safeguard

Section 69 of the CGST Act allows the Commissioner to authorise the arrest of a person where there are reasons to believe that specified serious offences, generally involving larger amounts of tax evasion or fraudulent credit, have been committed. The Supreme Court's decision in Union of India v. Sunil Biyani, reported at 2026 INSC 849, addressed what happens between the Commissioner recording those reasons and the actual arrest. The Court held that an arrest order under Section 69, resting as it does on the Commissioner's recorded reasons to believe, must be communicated to the person concerned before any arrest is carried out, so that the individual has a genuine opportunity to challenge the order or to seek anticipatory bail before being taken into custody rather than only after the fact. The Court permitted service of the order through modes including the email address and mobile number furnished under Rule 8 of the CGST Rules, among other lawful means, and set aside an interim week-long protection from arrest that the Bombay High Court had granted, reasoning that such interim protection cannot survive once the substantive bail application it was tied to has itself been dismissed.

The case arose from a Directorate General of GST Intelligence investigation into alleged fake input tax credit and circular invoicing, in which the individual concerned had already been summoned three times under Section 70 for questioning before the arrest question arose. The ruling does not create a general immunity for a purchaser who holds invoices; commentary following the decision has been careful to note that producing invoices alone does not insulate a buyer from scrutiny where the department's case goes beyond a simple documentary dispute. What the ruling does establish is a procedural safeguard at the point of arrest itself, applicable regardless of how strong or weak the underlying tax case eventually turns out to be.

For a taxpayer or individual under investigation, the practical implication is that a Section 70 summons, repeated or otherwise, is not itself a sign that an arrest is imminent without further notice, but once reasons to believe have actually been recorded and an arrest order passed, that order must now reach the person before they can be arrested on it, which in turn opens a window, however brief, to approach a court for anticipatory bail that the earlier practice of unannounced arrest had effectively foreclosed.

Appeal to the High Court under Section 117, and a route from notice to Tribunal in outline

A party aggrieved by a GSTAT order may appeal to the jurisdictional High Court under Section 117, but only where the High Court itself is satisfied that the appeal involves a substantial question of law; the High Court does not re-examine the facts the Tribunal has already found. The appeal must be filed within one hundred and eighty days of the order being received, though the High Court may condone a later filing on sufficient cause shown. Once the Court identifies a substantial question of law, it must frame that question explicitly, and the appeal is then heard confined to that question, save that the Court may, for recorded reasons, take up an additional substantial question it considers also arises. Where the framed question requires it, the Court may decide an issue the Tribunal failed to decide or decided incorrectly, and the statute requires a bench, rather than a single judge, to hear the appeal.

Pulling the preceding sections into a single sequence: on receiving a show cause notice, a taxpayer should first confirm which provision, Section 73, 74 or 74A, actually governs the relevant tax period, since the applicable limitation and penalty consequences differ; a reply should address every ground the notice raises and should flag immediately any defect in the officer's jurisdiction or in the identity of the addressee, along the lines the Vodafone Idea ruling illustrates, rather than waiting to raise it later. If an adverse order follows, the first appeal under Section 107 must be filed within three months, accompanied by the ten per cent pre-deposit computed on the tax component alone, with a careful check of whether the October 2025 amendment to the pre-deposit proviso even applies, given the Gaurav Jain and Rohit Garg line of authority on when the right of appeal actually vests. Where the first appeal also fails, the route now runs to GSTAT under Section 112 within three months, with the Tribunal's further pre-deposit calculated and the e-filing portal's own stated figure checked and recorded at the time of filing, rather than assumed from general commentary. Only once the Tribunal has ruled does Section 117 come into play, and only then on a genuine question of law rather than a bid to reopen findings of fact. A writ petition, under this sequence, is reserved for the narrower circumstances described earlier, not treated as an alternative at any stage where the statutory ladder is actually open and functioning.

What remains unsettled

Several points central to this area are genuinely open rather than merely untested in a given State. The scope of Section 16(2)(c) for a bona fide purchaser remains divided between the Supreme Court's general upholding of the provision in Bhandari Scrap Traders and the Tripura High Court's reading down of it in Sahil Enterprises for an innocent buyer, with commentary disagreeing on how far, if at all, the Supreme Court's order actually disturbs the Tripura position; a buyer facing an ITC denial on this ground should not assume either outcome until the conflict is resolved at the appellate level. The cumulative or additive character of the pre-deposit by the time a dispute reaches GSTAT, and the precise application of the twenty crore rupee cap under Section 112(8), are both reported inconsistently across professional commentary and are worth confirming against the GSTAT portal's own figures and, where in doubt, against the statutory text directly in any specific matter.

Whether the recommended forty crore rupee ceiling for penalty-only first appeals has actually been enacted, rather than merely recommended by the GST Council, could not be confirmed and should be checked before being relied on. The question whether Section 122 penalties can be imposed on a non-taxable person at all was expressly left open in the Rohit Garg line of decisions pending a Supreme Court ruling. And although GSTAT is now functional at the Principal Bench and across its State Benches, the volume of backlog appeals filed by the 30 June 2026 deadline means the pace at which the Tribunal actually clears that backlog, as opposed to simply having opened its doors to receive it, remains to be seen and will likely shape how quickly the writ-to-tribunal shift described in this article is fully felt on the ground.

The practical effect of GSTAT's arrival is less a change in the substance of GST law than a change in where and how a taxpayer is expected to argue it. Disputes that would, two years ago, have gone directly to a High Court by writ, often on arguments dressed up to look constitutional or jurisdictional because no other forum existed, are now expected to travel the statutory ladder, pre-deposit and all, with writ relief reserved for the narrower set of cases that genuinely call for it.

That shift rewards a different kind of preparation. A taxpayer who treats the show cause notice reply, the first appeal and a prospective Tribunal filing as three linked stages of a single record, rather than as separate fires to put out as they arise, is in a materially stronger position than one who waits for each stage to force a reaction. With the backlog deadline now behind the system and ordinary limitation periods back in force, that discipline, more than any single substantive argument, is likely to decide how most GST disputes play out from here.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the GST Appellate Tribunal actually functioning now, or is it still being set up?

GSTAT is functioning. It was formally launched in September 2025, began its first phase of hearings on 16 February 2026 with the Principal Bench at New Delhi, and now operates through over thirty State Benches sitting at more than forty locations, with over a thousand appeals reported as filed online by early 2026. A taxpayer can no longer assume a writ petition is the only available route once a first appeal fails.

I missed the 30 June 2026 deadline for filing a backlog GST appeal. What happens now?

That deadline applied specifically to orders communicated before 1 April 2026. If your order falls in that category and you missed 30 June 2026, the Tribunal retains a limited power under Section 112(6) to condone a further delay of up to three months on sufficient cause, but this is discretionary and not guaranteed. For orders communicated on or after 1 April 2026, the standard three-month limitation period from communication of the order applies in the ordinary way, with the same three-month condonation power available.

How much pre-deposit do I actually need to pay to get my GST appeal heard?

Generally, the first appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals) requires ten per cent of the disputed tax under Section 107(6), and a second appeal to GSTAT requires a further ten per cent under Section 112(8), capped at twenty crore rupees for the tax component, in addition to what was already paid at the first stage. Commentary differs on whether this produces a combined twenty per cent by the time the Tribunal hears the matter or should be read differently in specific cases, so the exact figure should be confirmed against the current portal calculation and, where in doubt, the statutory text, before filing.

Does the new 10% pre-deposit rule for penalty-only appeals apply to a show cause notice issued before October 2025?

According to the Delhi High Court's rulings in Gaurav Jain and Rohit Garg, no. The Court held that the right of appeal, and the pre-deposit obligation that comes with it, vests on the date the show cause notice is issued, not the date the final order is passed, so the amended pre-deposit requirement effective 1 October 2025 does not reach appeals arising from notices issued before that date, even if the order itself was passed afterward.

Can my input tax credit be denied just because my supplier did not pay the tax I paid them?

The Supreme Court has upheld Section 16(2)(c), which conditions your credit on the supplier actually having paid the tax to the Government, and dismissed a challenge to its validity in Bhandari Scrap Traders. At least one High Court, in Tripura, has taken a more protective view for a genuinely bona fide purchaser who had no way to verify the supplier's compliance, so the law is not uniformly settled. Separately, Rule 41(2) read with Rule 37A lets you reverse the credit with interest and re-avail it once your supplier eventually pays, which is often a faster practical remedy than litigating the point to a final answer.

Can I still file a writ petition instead of appealing to GSTAT?

Generally, no, once GSTAT is functional for your dispute. High Courts have been consistently relegating taxpayers to the statutory appeal route and declining to let a writ petition bypass the pre-deposit requirement. A writ remains available in narrower situations, such as where the Tribunal is genuinely not functional for your category of case, where the notice was issued without jurisdiction, such as to an entity that no longer legally exists, or where there has been a clear breach of natural justice, for instance where different officers conducted the hearing and passed the order.

What happens if my GST registration is cancelled?

You can apply for revocation under Section 30 read with Rule 23 before the Proper Officer. Courts, particularly the Calcutta High Court, have repeatedly set aside cancellations or rejections of revocation applications where the hearing and the decision were conducted by different officers, or where documents relied on in the order were never shared with the taxpayer beforehand, so these procedural points are worth examining closely before assuming the cancellation must simply be accepted or separately challenged on the merits.

Can I be arrested under GST law without any warning?

The Supreme Court's 2026 ruling in Union of India v. Sunil Biyani held that an arrest order under Section 69 must be communicated to the person before the arrest is carried out, so that the person has a genuine opportunity to seek anticipatory bail or otherwise challenge the order first. This does not mean an arrest cannot happen, and it does not shield someone merely because they hold invoices if the department's case goes beyond a documentary dispute, but it does require the order itself to reach the person before, rather than only after, they are taken into custody.
