Home Loans and Loans Against Property for NRIs: The FEMA Rules Lenders Actually Apply

Who can lend to an NRI or OCI buying or already holding property in India, what currency and quantum conditions apply, how the loan can be repaid from abroad, and what happens if it is not repaid

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 14 November 2026 · Law stated as on 14 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An NRI buying a flat in Noida or a house in Kolkata usually assumes the loan will work the same way it would for a resident sibling applying at the same branch. It mostly does, but FEMA overlays a separate set of conditions on who may lend, what currency the loan must be in, how it may be repaid, and who may stand as co-applicant or guarantor. A loan already running against a property the NRI holds, taken for a purpose other than buying it, sits inside the same regulatory framework. This article sets out that framework as it stands today, and what changes once a payment is missed.

Key points

  • A rupee housing loan or loan against property can be extended to an NRI or OCI cardholder only by an authorised dealer bank or a housing finance company operating under Reserve Bank of India conditions; the loan itself must be denominated in rupees, never in a foreign currency, and its quantum, margin money and repayment period must be on par with what the same lender would extend to a resident borrower for a comparable loan.
  • The loan proceeds are paid to the seller, builder or, for a loan against property, disbursed for the stated purpose, directly through the Indian banking system; they cannot be credited to the borrower's NRE or FCNR(B) account, which keeps the transaction inside the rupee lending framework rather than letting it operate as a foreign-currency remittance.
  • Repayment can be made by inward remittance through normal banking channels, or by debit to the NRI's NRE, NRO or FCNR(B) account, and lenders also permit repayment of the EMI directly from a resident relative's own bank account, provided the relative pays from that account and not in cash.
  • Because an NRI applicant is often not physically present for the full life cycle of the loan, from sanction through registration to periodic renewals, a power of attorney in favour of a resident in India, and in many cases a resident or NRI guarantor, are standard conditions rather than exceptions.
  • Default on an NRI home loan or loan against property is enforced exactly as it would be for a resident borrower: the lender's security interest and its remedies under the SARFAESI Act, 2002 do not turn on the borrower's residential status, and the borrower's NRI status is not, on its own, a defence or a reason for the lender to proceed differently.
  • A loan against property taken by an NRI sits inside the same FEMA borrowing framework as a purchase loan, but the eligible end-uses and the documentation a lender will accept vary considerably between institutions, and should be confirmed with the specific lender before the NRI treats the property as a source of rupee liquidity.

A question distinct from whether an NRI can buy property at all

Most NRIs already know, in broad outline, that they can buy residential and commercial property in India and that the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 permits this subject to conditions that do not apply to agricultural land, plantation property or farmhouses. What is less well understood is that financing that purchase, or later borrowing against a property already owned, runs through a separate and more specific set of FEMA conditions governing who may lend rupees to a non-resident, in what form, and how the debt may be serviced from outside India.

These conditions sit in the Foreign Exchange Management (Borrowing and Lending) Regulations, 2018, notified by the Reserve Bank of India as FEMA 3(R)/2018-RB, which replaced the framework that had operated since 2000. The 2018 regulations consolidate the rules on who in India may lend to, and borrow from, a person resident outside India, and the provisions touching rupee loans to NRIs and OCI cardholders for housing purposes sit within this consolidated framework, read together with the Reserve Bank's master direction on borrowing and lending transactions between residents and non-residents.

This article deals with two related situations: an NRI or OCI cardholder taking a fresh loan to buy residential property in India, and an NRI who already owns property in India borrowing against it, commonly described as a loan against property, for some other purpose. Both sit inside the same rupee lending framework, though lenders apply their own, sometimes more restrictive, policies on top of the regulatory floor FEMA sets.

Treating the two situations as interchangeable is a mistake worth avoiding at the outset. A purchase loan is tied to a specific asset being acquired and is, from the lender's point of view, a relatively straightforward secured transaction with the property as collateral for its own acquisition. A loan against property, by contrast, asks the lender to lend against an asset the NRI already owns for an unconnected purpose, which changes the lender's risk assessment, its documentation requirements and often its appetite for the transaction in ways a first-time NRI borrower does not always anticipate.

Who may lend, and in what currency

Under the current framework, only an authorised dealer bank, meaning a bank specifically authorised by the Reserve Bank to deal in foreign exchange, or a housing finance company extending loans under conditions the Reserve Bank prescribes, may extend a housing loan in rupees to an NRI or an OCI cardholder. A loan from any other source, including an informal arrangement with a foreign lender or a foreign-currency loan from an overseas bank intended to fund an Indian property purchase, does not fit within this framework and raises separate FEMA questions that this article does not address.

The loan itself must be denominated in Indian rupees. An NRI cannot take what is, in substance, a foreign-currency loan secured against Indian property, and lenders operating in India do not offer one for this purpose. This single point trips up a number of first-time NRI borrowers who assume, reasonably enough from the perspective of their country of residence, that a cross-border property purchase should naturally be financed in the currency they earn and save in.

On quantum, margin money and the repayment period, the regulatory position is that these must be on par with what the same lender would offer a resident borrower for a broadly comparable loan. In practice this means an NRI applicant should not expect, and a lender should not offer, materially more generous or more restrictive terms purely because the applicant is non-resident; whatever variation exists between lenders on these three parameters is lender-specific commercial practice, not a distinct NRI-only regulatory ceiling or floor.

This parity requirement is sometimes misunderstood as a guarantee that an NRI will get the same loan-to-value ratio or the same interest rate as a resident with an identical income and credit profile. It is better understood as a ceiling on how differently a lender may treat an NRI as a class, rather than a guarantee of identical individual outcomes, since a lender remains free to assess an individual NRI applicant's income stability, country of residence, and repayment capacity on their own facts, the same way it would assess any individual resident applicant's facts, before arriving at a specific offer.

Where the money goes: disbursement cannot pass through an NRE or FCNR account

A detail that surprises some borrowers is that the loan amount itself is not credited to the NRI's own NRE or FCNR(B) account. Because an NRE or FCNR(B) account is, broadly, meant to hold the NRI's own foreign-earned funds that happen to be held in rupees or in foreign currency respectively, routing a rupee loan the NRI owes to the lender through that same account would blur the line between the NRI's own money and borrowed money, which the regulatory framework is structured to keep apart.

Instead, for a purchase loan the disbursed amount is paid directly to the seller or the builder, consistent with how a resident borrower's home loan is typically disbursed, and for a loan against property taken for a different stated purpose, the lender disburses according to that purpose, commonly to the NRI's NRO account or directly against invoices or documentation supporting the end-use, depending on the lender's own process. An NRI arranging finance should ask the lender specifically how disbursement will be routed before assuming it will simply land as spendable money in whichever Indian account is most convenient.

Where the purchase involves a staged disbursement tied to construction milestones, as is common for an under-construction apartment bought from a builder, each tranche follows the same rule: it goes to the builder against the relevant milestone, not to the NRI buyer's own account first. An NRI who has budgeted on the assumption that a tranche will briefly sit in their own account before being forwarded to the builder should correct that assumption before the first disbursement date, since the lender's process will not accommodate it.

Repaying the loan from abroad

The regulations and consistent lender practice recognise three channels for an NRI to service a rupee home loan or loan against property. The first is inward remittance through normal banking channels, meaning money sent directly from the NRI's overseas bank account to India specifically to meet the EMI or a lump-sum repayment. The second is repayment by debit to the NRI's own NRE, NRO or FCNR(B) account held in India, so long as there are sufficient funds in that account to cover the instalment. The third, relevant where the financed property is let out, is repayment from the rental income the property generates, credited to the NRI's NRO account and applied from there to the loan.

An NRI who intends to rely mainly on rental income to service the loan should factor in that rent is credited in rupees to an NRO account, which carries its own repatriation restrictions distinct from an NRE account, and that a vacancy period or a tenant default does not suspend the EMI obligation to the lender. Lenders generally require some combination of these three repayment channels to be demonstrated at the time of sanction, rather than accepting a bare assurance that remittances will arrive each month without a documented source.

Whichever channel is used, the repayment has to move through the formal banking system. A lender cannot, consistent with the regulatory framework, accept repayment in cash handed over in India on the NRI's behalf, which is one of the reasons lenders insist on a documented repayment mechanism, usually a standing instruction or auto-debit mandate against a specific account, rather than leaving repayment to ad hoc transfers.

A practical point many NRI borrowers underestimate is the effect of exchange rate movement on a remittance-funded EMI. Where the EMI is met by remitting a roughly fixed amount of foreign currency each month rather than by maintaining a standing rupee balance in an NRE or NRO account, a depreciating rupee can mean the remitted amount comfortably covers, or falls short of, the rupee instalment depending on which way the currency moves, and an NRI who remits the bare minimum each month with no buffer can find a single adverse movement turning a timely payment into a technical default through no fault of planning on the underlying loan itself.

Can a relative in India pay the EMI instead of the NRI borrower?

Yes, within limits that lenders apply with some variation. The Reserve Bank has permitted a resident close relative in India to service an NRI's housing loan EMI by paying it from that relative's own bank account, which is a meaningful practical accommodation for NRIs whose remittance timing does not always line up neatly with the EMI due date, or who want a family member already resident in India to handle routine instalments while larger, less frequent payments are remitted directly by the NRI.

The important qualification is that the relative must pay from their own account; lenders do not accept cash paid in on the borrower's behalf through a relative, for the same reason cash repayment by the NRI directly is not accepted. Precisely which family members qualify as a close relative for this purpose is not uniform across every lender's documentation, and an NRI who intends to structure repayment this way should confirm with the specific lender, before relying on it, exactly which relationships it will accept and what proof of the relationship it will ask for.

This relative-repayment facility should not be confused with a joint loan or a resident co-borrower arrangement. A relative who simply pays the EMI from their own account in this way is not thereby a co-borrower with independent rights against the lender or an ownership claim on the property, unless the loan documentation separately makes them a co-applicant; the facility is a payment convenience, not a restructuring of who legally owes the debt.

Families relying on this arrangement over a long tenure should also plan for what happens if the relative's own circumstances change, through illness, relocation or simply a falling-out within the family, since the loan remains the NRI's own obligation throughout and a lapse in the relative's willingness or ability to keep paying does not shift responsibility back to the lender to absorb; it falls straight back on the NRI borrower, who may by then be out of practice at managing the remittance channel directly.

Joint applications and adding a resident co-borrower

Many NRI home loans are structured as joint applications with a resident family member, most often a spouse, parent or sibling, named as co-applicant and, depending on the lender's policy and the funding of the purchase, as co-owner of the property. A joint application can improve the loan terms available, since the lender is assessing combined income and, in many cases, gains a co-obligor physically present in India, but it also means the resident co-applicant takes on full liability for the loan, not a notional or partial liability limited to some share of the EMI.

Where the co-applicant is also a co-owner, the share of ownership recorded in the sale deed and the share of loan liability recorded in the loan documents should match the parties' actual understanding and, ideally, each other, since a mismatch between who owns what share of the property and who is liable for what share of the debt is a frequent source of family dispute years later, particularly if the relationship between the NRI and the resident co-applicant changes, or if one party wants to sell their share while the other does not.

A resident co-applicant who is not also a co-owner, taking on loan liability purely to strengthen the application without any corresponding stake in the property, should think carefully before agreeing to that structure. That person remains fully liable to the lender for the entire outstanding loan if the NRI borrower defaults or becomes unreachable, while holding no asset of their own to show for having carried that risk, and this asymmetry is worth discussing openly within the family before signing rather than treating the co-applicant's role as a formality.

Co-applicants, guarantors and the power of attorney most NRI loans run on

Because an NRI borrower is frequently not in India for the sanction process, the property registration, or later administrative steps such as a top-up loan or a request to release a document from the lender's custody, most NRI home loans and loans against property are structured around a power of attorney given to a resident in India, commonly a parent, spouse or sibling, authorising that person to sign loan documents, attend registration and generally act for the NRI in dealings with the lender and the sub-registrar.

A number of lenders also require a guarantor where the NRI has no resident co-applicant on the loan, on the reasoning that a guarantor physically present in India gives the lender someone to pursue domestically without having to rely entirely on cross-border enforcement if things go wrong. Lender practice on who can stand as guarantor varies, and some lenders accept either a resident Indian or an NRI holding a valid Indian passport as guarantor, while others insist on a resident guarantor specifically; this is a point to settle with the chosen lender rather than something FEMA itself prescribes in a uniform way.

An NRI relying on a power of attorney for the loan and the property transaction should have it drafted narrowly enough to cover what is actually needed, properly executed and, where executed abroad, authenticated in the manner Indian law requires for a document signed outside India, since a defectively executed power of attorney is one of the more common reasons an NRI loan transaction stalls midway through registration, well after the loan itself has been sanctioned.

The power of attorney holder's authority should also be kept current with the lender's own records throughout the loan's tenure, not just at the point of disbursement. An NRI who revokes an old power of attorney and executes a fresh one, perhaps because the original attorney holder is no longer available, has to make sure the lender's records, as well as the sub-registrar's, reflect the change, since a lender that continues to correspond with, or accept instructions from, a revoked attorney holder because nobody told it otherwise creates a genuine risk of a later dispute over whose instructions actually bound the loan account.

Loan against property: the same framework, used for a different purpose

An NRI who already owns residential, commercial or, subject to the usual restrictions on agricultural land, other eligible property in India can generally borrow against it from an authorised dealer bank or housing finance company, under the same rupee-denomination and parity-with-resident-terms conditions that apply to a purchase loan. The practical difference is the purpose: a loan against property is typically used to fund something other than the acquisition of the mortgaged property itself, whether that is a business need, another investment, or a personal expense.

Lenders differ noticeably in which end-uses they will finance through a loan against property to an NRI, and in the loan-to-value ratio they are prepared to extend, which is often more conservative for a non-resident borrower than for a resident one, even though the underlying FEMA parity requirement speaks to quantum, margin and tenure being comparable rather than guaranteeing an identical loan-to-value outcome in every case. An NRI considering this route should treat the FEMA framework as the regulatory floor and the specific lender's credit policy, which can be considerably more conservative, as the practical ceiling on what is actually available.

Documentation for a loan against property taken by an NRI tends to be more demanding than for a resident borrower on the same security, since the lender has to satisfy itself on title, on the chain of any prior transactions in the property, and, where the NRI acquired the property through inheritance or a gift rather than a direct purchase, on the documents establishing that chain, before it is willing to rely on the property as security for a fresh advance.

Where the property securing the loan is held jointly with other family members, whether siblings who inherited together or a spouse who is a co-owner, every co-owner ordinarily has to join in creating the mortgage, since a lender cannot generally take security over an undivided share without the consent of the other co-owners in any form that gives it an enforceable charge over the whole property. An NRI planning to raise a loan against jointly held property should resolve the consent and documentation of all co-owners well before approaching a lender, rather than after an informal approval has already created an expectation the family has not actually agreed to support.

Switching lenders and transferring the loan

An NRI who wants to move an existing home loan or loan against property to a different lender, typically to secure a better rate or different terms, can generally do so through a balance transfer, in which the new lender pays off the existing loan and the NRI continues repayment to the new lender on fresh terms. The transaction has to satisfy the same currency, eligible-lender and resident-parity conditions at the new lender as it did at the old one, since a balance transfer does not create an exception to the underlying FEMA framework; it simply substitutes one eligible lender for another on the same regulated structure.

The practical friction in an NRI balance transfer is usually less about FEMA and more about synchronising the two lenders, the closure of the old loan, the release of the mortgage or the original title documents held by the old lender, and the creation of a fresh charge in favour of the new lender, within a timeline both lenders are comfortable with, all while the NRI borrower may again be managing the process remotely through a power of attorney holder. Building in extra time for this coordination, rather than assuming it will move at the pace a resident borrower's balance transfer typically does, avoids a gap where the loan is, briefly and awkwardly, neither fully closed with the old lender nor fully settled with the new one.

How lenders assess an NRI applicant's income and country of residence

Beyond the FEMA conditions described above, each lender applies its own underwriting policy to an NRI applicant's income, employment and country of residence, and this policy-level assessment is where most of the real variation between lenders actually shows up, since the regulatory framework leaves it untouched. A lender will typically ask for salary slips or a certified income statement for a salaried applicant, audited financials for a self-employed one, and some proof of continued employment or business operation abroad, translated into English where the originals are not, and often attested by the Indian embassy or consulate in the country of residence or otherwise authenticated in a manner the lender's compliance team will accept.

Country of residence matters to lenders in a way that is not always made explicit at the application stage. Some lenders maintain an internal list of countries from which they will not accept NRI applications at all, or will accept them only with additional conditions, generally tied to concerns about enforceability, sanctions exposure, or the reliability of income documentation available from that jurisdiction. An NRI applying from a country with a smaller Indian diaspora, or from a jurisdiction where remittance controls on the sending side are tighter, should raise this directly with the lender early rather than discovering a policy restriction only after substantial documentation has already been submitted.

Credit history presents its own difficulty, since an NRI's credit record built up abroad does not automatically translate into an Indian credit score, and a lender assessing an NRI applicant with no prior Indian borrowing history is, in effect, extending credit with less of the data it would ordinarily rely on for a resident applicant of comparable income. This is part of why a resident co-applicant, or a guarantor with an established Indian credit history, often meaningfully improves the terms an NRI is offered, independent of the underlying income assessment.

Insurance and other conditions lenders commonly attach

Most lenders require the mortgaged property to be insured for its reinstatement value for the life of the loan, with the lender named as the loss payee or beneficiary under the policy, and an NRI borrower managing the property from abroad should treat the annual renewal of this insurance as a recurring compliance task rather than a one-time condition satisfied at disbursement, since a lapsed policy discovered only at the time of a claim, after damage has already occurred, defeats the purpose of having required the insurance in the first place.

A number of lenders also offer, and in some cases require, a loan-linked life insurance policy on the borrower, so that the outstanding loan is settled from the insurance proceeds if the borrower dies during the tenure, rather than falling on the family, the co-applicant or the guarantor to continue servicing it. An NRI borrower should check whether such a policy exists on their loan, whether it was taken voluntarily or as a condition of sanction, and whether its sum assured still tracks the outstanding loan balance as the loan amortises, since a policy taken at the start of a long tenure for the original loan amount can fall out of step with the actual outstanding balance years later.

Lenders will also typically want the original title documents, or in the case of a loan against property the equivalent title package, deposited with them for the tenure of the loan, which for an NRI borrower raises the practical question of how those documents are collected back at the end of the loan, particularly if the borrower is no longer resident in the city where the property and the lender's branch are both located. Agreeing in advance how original documents will be returned, and to whom, on closure of the loan avoids an awkward and sometimes protracted process of retrieving decades-old paperwork from a lender's archive once the loan is finally paid off.

Taxation sits alongside, not inside, the FEMA question

FEMA governs whether the loan can be made and how it can be serviced; it does not determine how interest paid on the loan, or rental income used to service it, is taxed. An NRI financing a let-out property should expect that rental income is taxable in India regardless of whether it is applied directly to the EMI or remitted abroad first, and that the usual deductions available against house property income under the Income-tax Act apply to an NRI owner broadly as they would to a resident one, subject to the specific conditions attaching to each deduction.

This article does not work through the tax treatment in detail, since the computation of taxable income from a let-out or self-occupied property, and the interaction with withholding obligations where rent is paid to an NRI landlord, raise a distinct set of questions better addressed on their own rather than folded into a financing-focused discussion. An NRI borrower should treat the loan's FEMA compliance and its tax treatment as two separate compliance tracks that happen to arise from the same transaction, not as a single question answered once and for all at the time of sanction.

What happens when an NRI borrower defaults

Nothing about an NRI home loan or loan against property changes the lender's remedies on default. Where the lender is a bank or a financial institution covered by the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002, commonly called SARFAESI, the lender can classify the account as a non-performing asset according to the Reserve Bank's prudential norms and proceed to enforce its security interest directly, without first obtaining a court decree, in the same way it would against a defaulting resident borrower. The borrower's residence outside India is not, by itself, a bar to this process, nor does it entitle the borrower to different notice periods or a different standard of scrutiny before enforcement begins.

What does change, in practice rather than in law, is how quickly and effectively the borrower can respond. An NRI who is not easily reachable at the address the lender holds on file, who has not kept a power of attorney holder briefed on the loan's status, or who is slow to receive and act on a demand notice sent to an Indian address, loses time that a resident borrower facing the same default would not lose, simply because of the practical friction of managing an Indian loan default from abroad. The remedy for this is not a different legal standard, since none exists, but keeping current contact details with the lender, maintaining an active and properly authorised power of attorney holder, and responding to communications from the lender promptly rather than assuming distance will slow enforcement down.

Where a guarantor is in place, the lender's rights against the guarantor are triggered on the same default and are not deferred simply because enforcement against the NRI borrower personally, or against security located in India, might take longer to organise from the lender's end. A guarantor who stood as surety assuming enforcement would necessarily proceed first and primarily against the NRI borrower should understand that this assumption has no basis in how a lender's rights under a standard guarantee actually operate.

An NRI who sees default coming, because a job loss abroad has disrupted the remittance schedule or a tenant has stopped paying rent that was funding the EMI, is in a materially better position reaching out to the lender before an instalment is missed than after several have already lapsed. Lenders generally have more flexibility to restructure a loan, adjust the EMI schedule, or agree a short payment holiday before an account is classified as a non-performing asset than after, and an NRI managing the relationship entirely through infrequent, reactive contact with the lender gives up that flexibility by default, not by any deliberate choice.

Mistakes that recur in NRI home loan and loan against property transactions

A recurring mistake is assuming that because the NRI is creditworthy and the property is clean, the loan will proceed essentially like a resident's loan with only minor paperwork differences. The currency restriction, the disbursement routing away from the NRE or FCNR account, and the documented repayment channel requirement are not minor paperwork differences; they are structural features of the transaction that should be understood and planned for before an agreement to purchase is signed with a tight closing timeline that assumes financing will simply fall into place.

A second recurring mistake is treating the power of attorney as a formality to be executed quickly, close to the transaction date, rather than as a document that needs to be drafted for the specific loan and property transaction, properly authenticated given execution abroad, and shared with the lender early enough for the lender's legal team to review it before the transaction's critical dates. A power of attorney that the lender's legal team flags as deficient after registration has already been scheduled is a frequent and avoidable source of delay.

A third mistake, specific to loans against property, is assuming the FEMA framework alone determines what the lender will finance and at what loan-to-value ratio, when in practice the lender's own, often more conservative, credit policy is the operative constraint. An NRI should get the lender's indicative terms in writing early in the process rather than assuming the regulatory floor described in this article reflects what any particular lender will actually offer.

A fourth mistake is letting documentation on repayment sources go stale over a long loan tenure. A repayment plan built around a particular job, a particular tenant, or a particular relative's willingness to pay the EMI from their own account can look entirely sound at sanction and still fail years later once circumstances change, and an NRI borrower who revisits the repayment plan only when something has already gone wrong is working from a weaker position than one who checks in on it periodically and adjusts the remittance or repayment channel before a gap actually opens up.

An NRI or OCI cardholder financing Indian property, whether buying it or borrowing against one already owned, is operating inside a specific rupee lending framework that fixes who may lend, what currency the loan must be in, and how it may be repaid, while leaving quantum, margin and tenure to track what a resident borrower would get from the same lender. None of this changes what happens if the loan is not repaid: the lender's security and its enforcement remedies apply exactly as they would to a resident default. The practical risk for an NRI borrower lies less in the regulatory framework itself, which is reasonably settled, than in the logistics of managing a cross-border loan, the power of attorney, the repayment channel, and responsiveness to the lender, which is where NRI-specific transactions most often go wrong.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an NRI take a home loan in a foreign currency to buy property in India?

No. A housing loan to an NRI or OCI cardholder for property in India has to be in Indian rupees, extended by an authorised dealer bank or a housing finance company operating under Reserve Bank conditions. There is no route under the current FEMA framework for a foreign-currency loan secured against Indian residential property for this purpose.

Can the loan amount be credited to my NRE account?

No. The loan is disbursed to the seller or builder for a purchase, or applied to the stated purpose for a loan against property, rather than being credited to the NRI borrower's own NRE or FCNR(B) account. Keeping the disbursement out of these accounts is part of how the regulations distinguish the NRI's own funds from borrowed rupees.

How can I repay an Indian home loan while living abroad?

Through inward remittance to India specifically for the EMI, by debit to your own NRE, NRO or FCNR(B) account held in India, or, where the property is let out, from the rental income credited to your NRO account. A resident close relative may also pay the EMI directly from their own bank account, though not in cash.

Do I need a guarantor for an NRI home loan or loan against property?

Many lenders require one if there is no resident co-applicant, though practice varies: some accept a resident Indian or an NRI with a valid Indian passport as guarantor, while others insist on a resident guarantor. Confirm this with the specific lender rather than assuming a uniform rule.

Can I borrow against a property I already own in India as an NRI?

Generally yes, from an authorised dealer bank or housing finance company, under the same rupee-denomination and resident-parity conditions that apply to a purchase loan, though lenders are often more conservative on loan-to-value and eligible end-uses for this kind of loan than for a straightforward purchase loan.

Does my NRI status protect me if I default on the loan?

No. A lender covered by the SARFAESI Act, 2002 can classify the account as a non-performing asset and enforce its security interest in the same way it would against a defaulting resident borrower. Residing abroad is not a defence; it mainly creates practical delay in responding, which is better managed through an active power of attorney holder and current contact details than relied on as protection.

Can a relative repay my NRI home loan EMI every month instead of me remitting it?

Yes, the Reserve Bank permits a resident close relative to pay the EMI from their own bank account. This is a payment convenience and does not, on its own, make that relative a co-borrower or give them rights over the property unless they are separately added as a co-applicant on the loan.

If a jointly owned property is used as security, do all co-owners have to join the loan?

Ordinarily yes. A lender generally will not take an enforceable mortgage over an undivided share without the consent and participation of the other co-owners, so every co-owner's consent and documentation should be resolved before approaching a lender for a loan against jointly held property.
