The West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997: Tenant and Landlord Rights in Kolkata

Kolkata tenancies run on their own statute, separate from both the Transfer of Property Act and Delhi's rent law, with a closed list of eviction grounds, a rent ceiling that can take a lease outside the Act entirely, and a tenant status that survives a notice to quit.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 17 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A landlord in Kolkata who wants an unpaid tenant out, or a tenant who has just been served an eviction notice, is operating under a statute most of India has never heard of: the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997, in force since July 2001, which applies only within Kolkata, Howrah and a handful of other notified municipal areas and nowhere else in the state. It restricts eviction to a closed list of grounds, lets a sufficiently high rent take a tenancy outside its protection altogether, and gives a tenant who has been served a notice to quit a status that neither landlord nor tenant always expects.

Key points

  • The Act applies only within Kolkata, Howrah and other areas the state government has specifically notified, not across all of West Bengal
  • It took effect only on a July 2001 notification, years after it received assent, and repealed the earlier 1956 Act only from that date
  • A non-residential tenancy at a monthly rent above Rs 10,000 in Kolkata or Howrah, or Rs 5,000 elsewhere the Act applies, falls outside the Act's protection entirely
  • Eviction is limited to a closed list of grounds under Section 6, commonly including rent default, unauthorised subletting and the landlord's bona fide requirement, and proving even one ground is enough for a decree
  • A tenant served a notice under Section 6(4) does not become an unlawful occupant; courts have repeatedly refused to let landlords charge market-rate occupation fees while an eviction suit is still pending
  • A tenant who disputes the rent demanded must deposit it along with an application asking the court to determine the rent payable, or risk having the defence to the suit struck off
  • Protection for an heir of a deceased original tenant does not run indefinitely; Calcutta High Court rulings have treated it as lapsing five years after the original tenant's death

A Statute That Applies Only Where the Government Says It Does

Unlike the general law of landlord and tenant under the Transfer of Property Act, which applies across India by default, the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997 is a protective statute that applies only within the areas the state has specifically brought it into force: the Kolkata Municipal Corporation, the Howrah Municipal Corporation, and other municipal areas under the West Bengal Municipal Act, 1993 that the government has separately notified. Outside those areas, an ordinary tenancy in West Bengal is governed by the Transfer of Property Act and the lease terms the parties have actually agreed, not by this Act's protections at all.

The Act itself did not take effect the moment it was passed. Section 1(2) left its commencement to a notification the state government would issue later, and that notification, bringing the Act into force and simultaneously repealing the earlier West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1956, under Section 45, was issued only in July 2001, roughly three to four years after the 1997 Act received assent. Calcutta High Court rulings and a 2024 Supreme Court judgment both treat 2001, not the earlier assent year, as the operative date, which matters for any dispute over a tenancy that began, or a right that is said to have accrued, in the gap between assent and actual commencement.

The 1997 Act replaced its 1956 predecessor rather than simply amending it, and a good deal of the older case law that still gets cited in Kolkata eviction litigation, including on bona fide requirement and on building and rebuilding as grounds for possession, was actually decided under the 1956 Act's differently numbered provisions. Anyone relying on an older-looking judgment in a 1997 Act case should check which Act it was actually decided under before assuming the section numbers carry over unchanged.

The distinction between the two Acts is not purely academic. A tenancy that began while the 1956 Act was still in force, and continued past the 2001 commencement of the 1997 Act, is now governed by the newer statute's grounds and procedure for any dispute arising today, even though the tenancy itself long predates 1997. A landlord or tenant dealing with one of Kolkata's many long-running family tenancies, some of which have continued across more than one generation of both landlord and tenant, needs the 1997 Act's current provisions for any fresh dispute, and should not assume that a right or defence that existed under the older law automatically carries over unchanged into litigation conducted today.

The Rent Ceiling That Can Take a Tenancy Outside the Act Altogether

One feature that surprises landlords and tenants coming from Delhi or other states with their own rent laws is that the West Bengal Act does not protect every tenancy within its geographic reach. Section 3(f) excludes non-residential premises let at a monthly rent above a specified figure, currently ten thousand rupees within the Kolkata and Howrah municipal corporation areas and five thousand rupees in the other areas to which the Act extends, from its protection entirely. That threshold was itself raised, from an earlier three thousand rupees, by a 2002 amendment, so a tenancy that was outside the Act's protection when it began can, in principle, come back within it, or an older figure applied by an earlier judgment can be out of date, depending on when the relevant rent was fixed.

A tenancy priced above that ceiling is not an edge case in commercial Kolkata, where high-value retail and office premises routinely command rents well past the ten-thousand-rupee mark, which means the landlord of such premises can, in principle, give notice and sue for possession under the ordinary Transfer of Property Act and the Code of Civil Procedure without the Act's eviction restrictions ever coming into play. The practical fight in these cases, correspondingly, often shifts to the question of what counts as "rent" for the purpose of the ceiling in the first place. Calcutta High Court rulings have held that charges for amenities actually bundled into the occupation, such as air conditioning or similar facilities, can be added to the base figure to decide whether the threshold is crossed, so a landlord cannot necessarily keep a tenancy inside the Act's protection, or a tenant argue their way out of it, by looking only at the figure printed as "rent" in the lease deed.

A simple comparison shows why the ceiling matters so much in practice. A shop in a Kolkata municipal corporation area let at a monthly rent of Rs 9,500 sits inside the Act, so the landlord needs one of the Section 6 grounds, and the court process that goes with proving it, before recovering possession. The identical shop next door, let at Rs 10,500, sits outside the Act entirely, and its landlord can terminate the tenancy on an ordinary notice under Section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act and sue in ejectment without ever having to establish a bona fide requirement, a default in rent, or any of the other grounds this article discusses. The difference of a thousand rupees in the monthly figure, in other words, can be the difference between a protected, multi-year eviction suit and a comparatively quick ejectment action, which is exactly why landlords negotiating a new Kolkata commercial lease, and tenants accepting one, should treat the rent figure as a protection-status decision as much as a commercial term.

Eviction Is Limited to a Closed List of Grounds

Where the Act does apply, Section 6 restricts eviction to a defined set of grounds, and a landlord cannot simply terminate a tenancy by notice the way an ordinary monthly tenancy under the Transfer of Property Act could be ended. The recognised grounds include default in payment of rent for a specified period within a preceding year, subletting, assignment or a change in the nature of use of the premises without the landlord's written consent, the landlord's own bona fide requirement of the premises, and causing a nuisance or unauthorised construction on the premises, among others the Act lists. Courts treat these grounds as the exhaustive basis for eviction under the Act, not an illustrative list, and have held that a decree can rest on proof of a single ground even where several were pleaded together.

The bona fide requirement ground, carried forward conceptually from the 1956 Act's case law even though the two statutes number it differently, has never been satisfied by a landlord's bare wish to occupy the premises. Courts require a genuine, reasonably held need, tested against the landlord's actual circumstances rather than accepted on the landlord's say-so, and a tenant facing this ground is entitled to put the landlord's claimed need to proof rather than assume a bare assertion will succeed.

Before suing on most of these grounds, a landlord generally has to serve a statutory notice under Section 6(4), which case law treats as a mandatory precondition to the suit rather than a formality that can be cured later, with one narrower ground under the section apparently exempted from this requirement. A suit filed without the required notice, on a ground that actually needs one, risks being thrown out at the threshold regardless of how strong the landlord's underlying case otherwise is, which makes getting the pre-suit notice right as important as the substantive ground itself.

In practice, a Kolkata eviction suit rarely pleads a single, isolated ground. A tenant who has fallen behind on rent has often also sublet part of the premises, or changed how the space is used, without asking the landlord first, and a well-drafted plaint pleads whichever combination of grounds the facts actually support rather than betting everything on one. Because courts have confirmed that proof of any one pleaded ground is enough to sustain a decree, this is not merely a drafting nicety: a landlord whose evidence on the bona fide requirement ground turns out to be thin at trial can still succeed on a cleanly proved rent default, provided that ground was pleaded and the corresponding notice requirements, where they apply, were actually met before suit. A tenant defending such a suit, correspondingly, cannot assume that beating back the landlord's strongest-sounding ground wins the case; every pleaded ground has to be answered on its own facts.

Serving a Notice Does Not End the Tenancy

A notice under Section 6(4) tells the tenant the landlord intends to sue for possession on a specified ground; it does not, by itself, make the tenant's continued occupation unlawful or convert the tenancy into something lesser. The Act is understood to create a statutory tenancy that continues, with the tenant liable to pay the contractual rent as before, until an actual decree for eviction is passed by the court, however long the suit itself takes to reach that point.

This has produced a recurring fight in Kolkata litigation over what a landlord can charge while the suit is still pending. Landlords have repeatedly argued that once notice has been served, the tenant should be treated as a mere tenant at sufferance under the Transfer of Property Act and made to pay market-rate occupation charges rather than the older contractual rent. Calcutta High Court rulings have rejected that argument where the tenancy is actually protected under the 1997 Act, holding that a statutory tenant is a different legal category from a tenant at sufferance, and that courts should not impose occupation charges beyond the statutory rent merely because a suit is in progress. A 2024 Supreme Court ruling involving a tenancy actually governed by the Transfer of Property Act, rather than the 1997 Act, is understood in subsequent Calcutta rulings as distinguishable on exactly this point, precisely because that tenant's position under the general law was not the same as a statutory tenant's position under West Bengal's own Act.

For a landlord, the practical consequence is that filing the eviction suit promptly matters more than extracting a higher rent out of a reluctant tenant during the suit's pendency, since the court is unlikely to backfill that gap once the case is finally decided. For a tenant, the same point cuts the other way: a notice to quit is a serious step that starts the clock on litigation, but it is not, on its own, a basis to pay more than the rent already agreed, or to assume the tenancy has already ended.

Disputing the Rent: The Deposit Procedure and Its Risk

A tenant who genuinely disputes the rent a landlord claims is due, rather than simply refusing to pay, has a specific route under the Act: depositing the disputed amount along with an application asking the court to determine the actual rent payable, rather than withholding payment outright while the dispute is litigated. The deposit and the application are meant to go together; a deposit made without the accompanying application to determine the rent is not treated as compliance with the Act's own machinery for resolving a genuine rent dispute.

The consequence of getting this wrong falls entirely on the tenant. Where a tenant fails to make the required deposit, or fails to accompany it with the application the Act requires, courts have struck off the tenant's defence to the eviction suit altogether, which in practice can mean the suit proceeds to a decree without the tenant's version of events being considered on the merits at all. A tenant who genuinely believes the demanded rent is excessive, or disputes whether a rent increase was validly made, should treat the deposit-and-application route as the primary way to protect their position in the suit, not as a secondary option to be used only if direct negotiation with the landlord fails.

On the landlord's side, the Act also gives a route to seek a formally determined "fair rent" through the Rent Controller, broadly under the Act's provisions dealing with rent fixation, rather than relying on unilateral demands that a tenant can simply dispute indefinitely. A landlord who believes the existing rent has fallen well behind the market, and who wants an enforceable, court-sanctioned figure rather than a figure the tenant can keep contesting deposit by deposit, is generally better served pursuing that fair-rent route than escalating demands outside the Act's machinery.

Fair-rent proceedings tend to run on their own, slower timetable, separate from any eviction suit that might be running in parallel, and a landlord who has both a rent dispute and a separate ground for eviction should not assume winning one automatically resolves the other. A fair-rent order fixes what the tenant owes going forward; it does not, by itself, end the tenancy or decide whether any of the Section 6 grounds have actually been made out. Keeping the two proceedings, and their very different evidentiary requirements, properly distinct in how the case is pleaded and argued avoids the confusion that follows when a landlord's lawyer tries to fold a possession claim and a pure rent dispute into the same set of pleadings without separating what each actually requires to succeed.

Subletting and the Tenant's Own Obligations

The Act places its own obligations on the tenant, separate from the question of what grounds let the landlord evict. A tenant is generally barred from subletting the premises, in whole or in part, without the landlord's written consent, and this restriction runs independently of whether the subletting is otherwise a sensible commercial arrangement; a tenant who sublets a back room to a third party informally, however modest the arrangement, hands the landlord a ready-made ground for eviction if that consent was never actually obtained and recorded in writing.

This matters particularly for small Kolkata retail tenancies and shared commercial premises, where informal sharing arrangements between family members or associated businesses are common and rarely documented. A tenant who wants to bring in a sub-tenant, assign the tenancy, or let a related business use part of the space should obtain the landlord's written consent before doing so, not after the arrangement is already in place, since a subletting ground raised years into an undocumented arrangement is considerably harder to defend than a consent that was simply asked for and recorded at the outset. The same written-consent requirement applies to a change in how the tenant uses the premises; converting a residential tenancy to commercial use, or changing the specific business carried on in a commercial tenancy, without first securing the landlord's consent, falls into the same category of risk as an unauthorised sublet.

Paying Rent the Right Way: Tender, Receipts and the Section 21 Trap

A tenant who wants to avoid a rent-default ground should be as careful about how rent is actually tendered as about whether it is paid at all. Case law on the Act's tender provision has held that simply sending a cheque, without a prior agreement between the parties that payment by cheque counts as valid tender, is not necessarily enough to discharge the tenant's obligation if the landlord later disputes having received payment in a legally effective form. A tenant relying purely on bank transfer records or an uncollected cheque to prove rent was paid on time, without having first confirmed with the landlord, ideally in writing, that this method of payment is acceptable, risks the landlord later arguing that no valid tender was actually made within the time the Act allows.

The safer practice, and the one that gives a tenant the clearest answer to a later default allegation, is to insist on a written receipt for every rent payment, by whatever method, and to keep the landlord's acknowledgment on file rather than relying solely on the tenant's own banking records. Where a landlord starts refusing to accept rent, or to acknowledge receipt, a tenant should treat that refusal as the trigger to use the Section 7 deposit-and-application route discussed above, depositing the rent with the court along with the application to determine it, rather than simply holding onto the money and hoping the refusal is later excused as the landlord's fault.

Who Counts as the Tenant, and for How Long After Death

The Act's own definition of "tenant" extends statutory protection to certain heirs of an original tenant after that tenant's death, which matters enormously in a city where many protected tenancies have run in the same family for generations. That extension is not indefinite. Calcutta High Court rulings have read the definition as limiting an heir's own protected status to five years from the date of the original tenant's death, after which the occupant can no longer claim the statutory tenancy the Act gives to a "tenant" within its own definition, exposing them to summary eviction rather than a full contested suit on one of the Section 6 grounds.

Ownership of the landlord's side of the relationship can also change hands in ways that affect a pending or contemplated eviction claim. A March 2026 appellate ruling of the Calcutta High Court accepted that a person who had received the property under a registered gift deed from a family member stepped into the landlord's own rights for the purpose of an eviction suit, and that the occupant resisting eviction in that case could not claim protected tenant status under the Act at all. Earlier decisions involving different fact patterns, including occupation through a thika tenancy rather than an ordinary premises tenancy, have gone the other way on their own facts, which means a transfer of the landlord's title by gift, inheritance or sale needs to be checked against the specific category of occupation involved rather than assumed to automatically carry eviction rights with it in every case.

Where to Actually File: Civil Judge or Small Cause Court

Section 6 of the Act, on its own wording, points toward a suit before a Civil Judge, which has produced litigation of its own over whether a different, specialised forum within Kolkata can also try these cases. The Presidency Small Cause Court, which handles a significant share of Kolkata's ejectment and rent litigation as a matter of established practice, has had its jurisdiction challenged precisely on the ground that the Act's own text names a Civil Judge rather than that court. Calcutta High Court rulings have upheld the Small Cause Court's power to try these suits despite that wording, which means a landlord or tenant in Kolkata should expect litigation under this Act to proceed in whichever of these two forums local practice and the specific registry actually direct, and should confirm the correct forum with counsel before filing rather than assuming the Act's literal wording settles the question on its own.

Getting the forum wrong at the outset is a genuine, avoidable risk rather than a theoretical one, precisely because the jurisdictional challenge in the reported case law came from a party trying to use exactly this wording mismatch to delay or derail a suit already filed in the Small Cause Court. A landlord who files in the wrong forum, or a tenant who raises a jurisdictional objection without first checking whether it has already been settled against that argument, both risk spending time and cost on a threshold fight that the existing Calcutta High Court authority has already resolved, rather than on the substantive dispute the parties actually care about.

Practical Points for Landlords and Tenants

A landlord planning to recover possession in Kolkata should start by checking whether the Act applies to the specific tenancy at all, given the rent ceiling discussed above, since a tenancy already outside the Act's protection can be ended on notice under the ordinary law without having to prove any of the Section 6 grounds. Where the Act does apply, the landlord needs to identify which specific ground actually fits the facts, serve the Section 6(4) notice correctly before suing on a ground that requires one, and expect the suit itself, once filed, to run for a period during which the tenant's occupation remains lawful at the existing rent rather than at any higher figure the landlord might prefer.

A tenant served with a notice should resist the instinct to either ignore it entirely or simply stop paying rent out of frustration with a disputed demand. Continuing to pay, or depositing disputed rent together with the proper application to have it determined, preserves the defence that a failure to pay, or a bare non-appearance in the rent-fixation process, can otherwise forfeit. An heir occupying premises after an original tenant's death should also take the five-year question seriously well before it becomes urgent, since establishing continued protected status, or negotiating a fresh tenancy directly with the landlord, is considerably easier to arrange before a dispute crystallises than after a landlord has already filed for summary eviction on the basis that the protection has lapsed.

A buyer acquiring tenanted property anywhere in Kolkata or Howrah, whether by purchase, gift or inheritance, should treat the existing tenancy as a core part of the pre-purchase due diligence rather than an afterthought to be dealt with only once the deal has already closed. Confirming who the current occupant actually is under the Act's own definition, whether any subletting has occurred with or without consent, what rent has actually been paid and whether any deposit-and-determination proceeding is already running, and whether the rent sits above or below the Section 3(f) ceiling, answers most of the practical questions a new owner will otherwise discover only once they try to act on the property. Buying a tenanted building without this check can mean inheriting a tenancy that looks straightforward on paper but turns out to be protected, contested, or already mid-litigation, with consequences for both the purchase price that should have been negotiated and the timeline for actually taking possession.

The West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997 protects tenants in Kolkata and Howrah more narrowly than its reputation suggests: it does not reach every tenancy in the state, it stops protecting a non-residential tenancy once the rent crosses a specified ceiling, and even within its reach it limits eviction to grounds a landlord has to actually prove rather than simply assert. None of that makes the Act toothless for a tenant facing eviction, since a notice alone does not end the tenancy and a statutory tenant cannot be squeezed into paying market-rate occupation charges merely because a suit is pending.

For both sides, the practical lesson is the same: the Act's protections and its limits both turn on specific, checkable facts, the actual rent charged, which ground is actually being relied on, whether the required notice and deposit procedures were followed correctly, and how long an heir has occupied the premises since the original tenant's death, rather than on a general sense of how rent control is supposed to work. Getting those facts right at the outset, before a notice is served or a suit is filed, decides more of these cases than any argument made once the matter is already in court.

Because several of the exact figures and provisions discussed in this article, the fair-rent sub-clause, the full letter-by-letter list of Section 6 grounds, and the precise scope of the Section 6(4) notice exception among them, are reported inconsistently even in otherwise reliable commentary, anyone acting on a specific Kolkata tenancy dispute should have the current text of the Act and the relevant Calcutta High Court authority checked directly rather than relying on a general description of the scheme, however carefully sourced.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act apply to every tenancy in West Bengal?

No. It applies only within the Kolkata and Howrah municipal corporation areas and other municipal areas the state government has specifically notified. A tenancy outside those notified areas is governed by the Transfer of Property Act and the parties' own lease terms, not by this Act's protections.

Can a landlord evict a commercial tenant in Kolkata without proving any ground under the Act?

Yes, if the monthly rent exceeds the Act's ceiling, currently Rs 10,000 within the Kolkata and Howrah municipal corporations, for non-residential premises. Such a tenancy falls outside the Act's protection, and the landlord can proceed to terminate it and sue for possession under the ordinary law instead.

Does serving an eviction notice mean the tenant has to leave immediately?

No. A notice under Section 6(4) only starts the process toward a possible suit; it does not end the tenancy. The tenant remains a statutory tenant, liable to pay the existing contractual rent, until a court actually passes a decree for eviction, which can take considerable time.

What happens if a tenant disagrees with the rent a landlord is demanding?

The tenant can deposit the disputed amount together with an application asking the court to determine the rent actually payable, rather than withholding payment outright. Failing to deposit, or depositing without the required application, has led courts to strike off the tenant's defence in the eviction suit.

Can a tenant's children keep living in the premises after the original tenant dies?

For a period, yes, if they fall within the Act's definition of tenant, but Calcutta High Court rulings have treated that protection as lapsing five years after the original tenant's death, after which the occupant can face summary eviction rather than a full contested suit.

Which court actually hears these eviction cases in Kolkata?

The Act's own wording points to a Civil Judge, but Calcutta High Court rulings have also upheld the jurisdiction of the Presidency Small Cause Court, which handles a significant share of this litigation in practice. The correct forum should be confirmed with counsel before filing rather than assumed from the statute's text alone.

Is a landlord's desire to use the premises for their own business enough to get an eviction?

Not on its own. Courts require a genuine, reasonably held need for the premises, not merely a stated preference, and a tenant can require the landlord to actually prove that need rather than accept a bare assertion of bona fide requirement.

Can a Kolkata tenant sublet part of the premises without telling the landlord?

No, not safely. Subletting, assigning the tenancy, or changing how the premises are used without the landlord's written consent is itself one of the recognised grounds for eviction under Section 6. A tenant who wants to bring in a sub-tenant, even a family member or an associated business, should get that consent recorded in writing before the arrangement actually starts, not after it has been running informally for some time.
