Foreign Governing Law and Jurisdiction Clauses: What Indian Courts Actually Enforce

A foreign company drafting its first contract with an Indian counterparty usually wants its own law and its own courts. Indian law lets it have both, within limits that are better understood before the contract is signed than after a dispute has already started in the wrong forum.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 15 November 2026 · Law stated as on 15 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company entering a supply, licensing, or shareholders' agreement with an Indian party is usually advised by its own counsel to insist on its home law and its home courts, on the reasoning that familiar law decided by a familiar court is safer than Indian law decided by an Indian court. Indian law does not forbid that choice. It channels it through two separate questions that a foreign party's own lawyers do not always distinguish clearly: whether the chosen foreign law will actually govern the substance of the contract, and whether the chosen foreign forum will actually get to hear the dispute if the Indian party instead sues in India. The two questions have different answers, and a contract drafted without keeping them separate can leave a foreign party litigating in Delhi or Kolkata despite a clause that looked, on paper, like it had already settled the question.

Key points

  • Indian courts generally respect a contract's choice of a foreign governing law for the substance of the agreement, including in contracts between two Indian parties, subject to the law chosen not being contrary to Indian public policy.
  • A foreign jurisdiction clause is treated differently from a governing law clause; it does not oust an Indian court's jurisdiction where that court is otherwise competent, but Indian courts have developed a settled practice of staying their own hand in favour of the chosen forum unless strong reasons justify proceeding in India.
  • Section 28 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 voids any clause that absolutely restricts a party from enforcing its rights through ordinary legal proceedings, which is why a jurisdiction clause naming one of several competent courts is valid but a clause purporting to extinguish the right to sue anywhere is not.
  • The Supreme Court's 2021 ruling in PASL Wind Solutions v. GE Power Conversion India confirmed that two Indian parties can choose a foreign seat of arbitration, and that doing so does not by itself offend Section 28 or Indian public policy.
  • The Delhi High Court's 2020 ruling in Dholi Spintex v. Louis Dreyfus Company India held that two Indian parties can also choose a foreign law to govern their arbitration agreement, intervening only where the foreign law would amount to a flagrant breach of Indian morality and justice.
  • An anti-suit injunction restraining a party from pursuing proceedings in a foreign court chosen by contract is granted only in limited and well-defined circumstances under the principles the Supreme Court laid down in Modi Entertainment Network v. W.S.G. Cricket, not as a routine remedy for breach of a forum clause.
  • A foreign company's safest route to a forum it controls is usually arbitration with a seat of its choosing, rather than a bare litigation jurisdiction clause, because a foreign arbitral award is enforceable in India under the New York Convention through a route the courts have treated far more predictably than a bare foreign court judgment.

Two Different Clauses, Two Different Questions

Most international contracts carry two clauses that a foreign party's legal team tends to treat as a single package: a governing law clause, naming the law that will interpret the parties' substantive rights and obligations, and a jurisdiction or dispute resolution clause, naming the forum, a particular court or an arbitral seat, where disputes will actually be heard. The two clauses answer different legal questions, and Indian law treats them differently enough that a drafting team which blurs the distinction can end up with a contract that achieves only one of the two outcomes it was trying to secure.

Choosing a governing law is, at bottom, a question about which body of substantive rules decides what the contract means and what remedies follow from its breach. Choosing a forum is a question about which court or tribunal actually has the power to hear the case and whether an Indian court, if approached despite the clause, will decline to exercise jurisdiction it otherwise has. A contract can validly choose English law to govern a supply agreement while still ending up litigated in Delhi if the forum clause was not drafted carefully, or vice versa.

For a foreign company negotiating its first contract with an Indian counterparty, whether a supplier, a licensee, a joint-venture partner, or a borrower, the practical question is rarely abstract. It is whether, if the relationship breaks down, the dispute will actually be heard where the contract says it will be heard, under the law the contract says will apply, and whether whatever is decided there can actually be enforced against assets the Indian party holds in India. The remainder of this piece works through each of those questions using the authority currently available on each.

None of this is a reason to abandon the familiar structure of a governing-law-plus-forum clause altogether. It is a reason to treat the two halves of that structure as needing separate legal sign-off rather than a single paragraph lifted from a precedent agreement used in a different jurisdiction. A clause that worked well in a contract with a counterparty in another country does not automatically translate into the same practical protection once an Indian party and Indian assets are on the other side of the table, because the protection a forum clause actually delivers depends on how an Indian court, applying Indian procedural law, will treat that clause when a dispute eventually lands in front of it.

Choice of a Foreign Substantive Law: The General Position

Indian conflict-of-laws practice, drawing on English common law principles that Indian courts have continued to apply in the absence of a single Indian statute covering choice of law for contracts, generally gives effect to the parties' own choice of governing law for a contract, on the reasoning that party autonomy in contract extends naturally to the choice of the law that will interpret it. A contract between an Indian company and a foreign counterparty that specifies New York law, English law, or Singapore law as the governing law of the agreement will ordinarily be read and applied according to that law by an Indian court or tribunal seized of the dispute, including on questions of contractual interpretation, breach, and the measure of damages.

The qualification that comes with this general rule is that the chosen law cannot be applied where doing so would offend Indian public policy, a limitation that is narrower in practice than it sounds, since Indian courts have generally reserved public-policy objections for genuinely exceptional cases rather than using the exception to second-guess a foreign law's ordinary commercial terms. A foreign law's rules on, say, limitation periods, the measure of damages, or interest rates being more or less favourable to one side than Indian law would have been is not, by itself, the kind of conflict with public policy that displaces the parties' own choice.

A separate and more specific question is whether two Indian parties, with no foreign element to the transaction at all beyond the chosen law itself, can validly choose a foreign law to govern their contract. The Delhi High Court addressed this directly in Dholi Spintex Pvt. Ltd. v. Louis Dreyfus Company India Pvt. Ltd. in 2020, in the context of an arbitration agreement between two Indian entities that had agreed to English law and arbitration under International Cotton Association rules. The Court held that foreign law could govern an arbitration between two Indian parties, intervening to displace that choice only where the foreign law amounts to a flagrant or gross breach of the principles of morality and justice, a standard the Court said should be applied sparingly rather than used to second-guess a commercially negotiated choice.

Section 28 of the Contract Act and Why It Targets Forum Clauses, Not Law Clauses

Section 28 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 renders void every agreement that absolutely restricts a party from enforcing its rights under or in respect of a contract through the ordinary tribunals, or that limits the time within which a party may enforce its rights. The provision was drafted with forum and limitation clauses specifically in view, and courts applying it have consistently distinguished between a clause that leaves a party with no forum at all in which to sue, which Section 28 voids outright, and a clause that merely picks one among several courts that would otherwise independently have jurisdiction over the dispute, which Section 28 does not touch.

The leading authority on this distinction, Hakam Singh v. Gammon (India) Ltd., decided by the Supreme Court in 1971, upheld a contract clause confining disputes to the courts at Bombay even though the contract's performance, and a competing court's jurisdiction under the ordinary provisions of the Code of Civil Procedure, was centred at Varanasi. The Court's reasoning, since followed consistently, is that where more than one court would have jurisdiction under Sections 16 to 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure to try a suit, an agreement between the parties that the dispute will be tried in one of those courts alone does not contravene Section 28, because the parties have not ousted the jurisdiction of a court that had no jurisdiction to begin with; they have simply chosen between courts that were already competent.

A.B.C. Laminart Pvt. Ltd. v. A.P. Agencies, decided by the Supreme Court in 1989, refined this further by holding that a clause need not use words like alone, only, or exclusive to be read as restricting the parties to a single named forum; the surrounding language and the commercial context of the clause can be read to arrive at the same effect. The Court's later ruling in Swastik Gases Pvt. Ltd. v. Indian Oil Corporation Ltd. in 2013 clarified that the absence of such explicit exclusivity words is not, by itself, decisive against reading a clause as exclusive, a clarification that has made the drafting of forum clauses somewhat less dependent on using any particular magic words, though careful drafters continue to use them for the sake of certainty.

What Section 28 does not reach, on the current state of authority, is a clause naming a foreign court as the agreed forum, since that question is treated under a separate body of principle built around the court's discretion to decline jurisdiction in favour of a forum the parties have chosen, discussed in the next section, rather than under Section 28's test of an absolute ouster of the ordinary tribunals. The distinction matters in drafting because a clause confining disputes to a foreign court cannot be struck down simply by invoking Section 28 the way a purely domestic ouster clause sometimes can.

A Foreign Jurisdiction Clause Does Not Oust an Indian Court, but It Usually Wins

An Indian court's jurisdiction to hear a suit against a party or over property or a cause of action connected with India is a matter of statute, principally Sections 16 to 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure, and a private contract between the parties cannot by itself remove jurisdiction a court otherwise has under that statute. This means an Indian party can, as a matter of raw jurisdiction, still file a suit in an Indian court despite a contractual clause naming a foreign court, and the Indian court will not dismiss that suit for want of jurisdiction purely because of the clause.

What the clause does instead is give the Indian court a strong, though not unanswerable, reason to stay its own proceedings or decline to exercise jurisdiction in favour of the forum the parties agreed to. The governing framework for this discretion was set out by the Supreme Court in Modi Entertainment Network v. W.S.G. Cricket Pte. Ltd. in 2003, a case concerning a clause submitting the parties to the non-exclusive jurisdiction of the English courts and English law. The Court held that where parties have agreed to submit to the jurisdiction of a foreign court, whether exclusively or non-exclusively, an Indian court should ordinarily respect that choice and decline to interfere, since the foreign forum cannot be treated as inherently vexatious, oppressive, or inconvenient merely because the parties themselves selected it in advance, having presumably weighed its convenience at the time of contracting.

The Court in Modi Entertainment Network did carve out an exception for genuinely strong reasons that would make continuing in the chosen foreign forum unjust, such as evidence that the foreign proceeding was brought only to defeat the ends of justice, or that the Indian party would be deprived of a remedy that substantial justice requires, but it was careful to frame this as an exceptional departure from the ordinary rule of respecting the parties' choice, not a general invitation to relitigate the convenience question every time a party would prefer to sue in India instead. A foreign company relying on a jurisdiction clause in a dispute with an Indian counterparty should expect this framework, rather than Section 28, to be the actual battleground if the Indian party sues in India despite the clause.

Practically, this plays out as a stay application: the foreign party asks the Indian court before which the Indian party has filed suit to stay its own proceedings on the ground that the parties agreed to litigate in the foreign forum instead. The Indian court hearing that application will weigh the Modi Entertainment Network factors on the material the parties place before it at that stage, which means the foreign party's lawyers need to be ready to argue the stay application in detail, with evidence of why the chosen forum remains the appropriate one, rather than treating the existence of the clause itself as sufficient to end the inquiry.

Anti-Suit Injunctions: When a Court Will Stop a Party From Suing Elsewhere

A closely related but distinct scenario is what happens when, instead of the Indian party suing in India, a party sues abroad despite a clause pointing to an Indian forum, or sues in a non-chosen foreign forum despite a clause pointing to a different one. Here the remedy a party seeks is an anti-suit injunction, restraining the other side from continuing or initiating proceedings in the forum it has wrongly chosen, and Modi Entertainment Network remains the controlling authority on when an Indian court will grant one.

Modi Entertainment Network's own principles treat an anti-suit injunction as an equitable remedy to be exercised with real caution, available in essence where the foreign proceeding is oppressive or vexatious, where the court granting the injunction has personal jurisdiction over the party being restrained, and where the ends of justice require restraining that party from proceeding in a forum that is not the natural or convenient forum for the dispute. The Court was explicit that this calculus does not automatically favour the party asking for the injunction merely because a contractual clause supports its position; the injunction interferes with another court's proceedings, which is itself a serious step courts are reluctant to take except on a clear showing that the case for it has been made out.

For a foreign company that has negotiated an exclusive Indian jurisdiction clause, expecting that clause alone to guarantee an injunction stopping the Indian counterparty from suing in that counterparty's own country if a dispute arises is optimistic. The clause is strong evidence in the anti-suit analysis, but it is evidence feeding into a discretionary test, not an automatic trigger for relief, and the practical reality is that pursuing an anti-suit injunction is itself expensive litigation layered on top of the underlying dispute rather than a shortcut around it.

Why Many Foreign Companies Choose Arbitration Instead of a Bare Court Clause

Given the discretionary and sometimes unpredictable territory a bare foreign court jurisdiction clause can lead into, many foreign companies dealing with Indian counterparties choose arbitration instead, naming a seat outside India, London, Singapore, or wherever the foreign party's own comfort lies, rather than naming a foreign national court as the forum. Arbitration offers two advantages a litigation clause does not: a more settled statutory and treaty framework for enforcement of the eventual outcome, and a body of Indian case law that has moved, over roughly the last two decades, toward supporting rather than second-guessing the parties' choice of forum.

The clearest confirmation of this came from the Supreme Court's 2021 ruling in PASL Wind Solutions Private Ltd. v. GE Power Conversion India Private Ltd., which held that two Indian companies can validly choose a foreign seat of arbitration, rejecting the argument that doing so would be contrary to Section 28 of the Contract Act or to Indian public policy. The Court went further and held that an Indian party to a foreign-seated arbitration can still approach an Indian court under Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 for interim relief, reversing a Gujarat High Court finding that had denied that remedy, which means choosing a foreign seat does not strand a party without any access to Indian interim measures while the arbitration itself proceeds abroad.

A foreign award rendered at a seat outside India, once the arbitration is concluded, is enforceable in India under Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, which gives effect to India's obligations under the New York Convention, through a route Indian courts have applied with considerably more consistency over the past decade than the law on recognising a bare foreign court judgment under Section 44A of the Code of Civil Procedure, discussed in the next section. This enforcement predictability is, in practice, the single biggest reason foreign counsel steer their Indian-facing contracts toward arbitration rather than a plain foreign court jurisdiction clause.

Arbitration also avoids a specific problem that litigation clauses regularly run into: the risk that the Indian party simply never appears, or appears only to contest jurisdiction at every stage, drawing out a foreign court proceeding over years before any enforcement step in India can even begin. An arbitration conducted under institutional rules at a fixed seat has its own timetable and its own mechanism for proceeding against a party that does not participate, and while an uncooperative Indian respondent can still make an arbitration slower and more expensive than it should be, the proceeding is less easily stalled indefinitely than a foreign court action where the respondent's own counsel can exploit every available procedural objection in a jurisdiction that may have little real connection to the dispute.

What an Arbitration Clause Still Needs to Get Right

Choosing arbitration over litigation does not remove every drafting risk; it relocates the risk to a different set of questions. The seat of the arbitration, which determines which national court supervises the arbitration and which country's arbitration law governs matters like the setting aside of an award, needs to be stated clearly and distinguished from the mere venue where hearings happen to take place, since Indian courts have had to resolve a meaningful number of disputes over contracts that named a city as the place of arbitration without making clear whether that city was the seat in the legal sense or simply a convenient hearing location.

A clause that names Indian law as the law governing the main contract while also naming a foreign seat for arbitration is not internally contradictory, since the law governing the contract and the law governing the arbitration agreement and the arbitral procedure can validly differ, but the clause should say so expressly rather than leave the relationship between the two to be inferred, because the inference exercise is exactly the kind of dispute that ends up litigated before the arbitration on the merits can even begin.

Checking the Indian counterparty's asset position matters too, before relying on a foreign seat as the default answer to every enforcement concern, that the Indian counterparty actually has assets either in India or in a jurisdiction where the eventual award can be enforced without a second layer of difficulty. An arbitral award, however cleanly obtained, is only as useful as the assets available to satisfy it, and a foreign seat does nothing to solve an asset-tracing or enforcement problem against an Indian party whose only meaningful assets sit in India.

Enforcing a Foreign Court Judgment in India: Section 44A and Reciprocating Territories

Where a foreign company does end up litigating in its own chosen national court rather than through arbitration, and obtains a money judgment against an Indian party, the route to enforcing that judgment in India depends on whether the judgment comes from a country the Central Government has formally declared to be a reciprocating territory under Section 44A of the Code of Civil Procedure. A judgment from a reciprocating territory can be executed in India broadly as if it were a decree passed by an Indian district court, a comparatively direct route. A judgment from a country that is not a declared reciprocating territory has no equivalent statutory shortcut and instead has to be enforced by filing a fresh civil suit in India on the foreign judgment itself, treating the judgment as the cause of action, which is slower and exposes the judgment to the specific statutory grounds of challenge available against a foreign judgment under Section 13 of the Code.

The list of countries the Central Government has actually notified as reciprocating territories is comparatively short, commonly cited examples include the United Kingdom, Singapore, the United Arab Emirates and Hong Kong, and it does not include several major commercial jurisdictions a foreign counterparty might otherwise expect, a gap that makes the choice between litigation and arbitration a genuinely practical one rather than a theoretical preference, since arbitration's New York Convention enforcement route does not depend on this much narrower reciprocating-territory list at all. A foreign company insisting on its home court's jurisdiction, rather than arbitration, should specifically check the current notified list, and confirm whether its own country still appears on it, before assuming its eventual judgment will be enforceable in India through the more direct Section 44A route.

Even a reciprocating-territory judgment is not automatically executed; Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure sets out six grounds on which a foreign judgment is treated as not conclusive and can be refused effect, broadly where it was not pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction, was not given on the merits, appears on its face to be founded on an incorrect view of international law or a refusal to recognise Indian law where that law was applicable, was obtained in proceedings opposed to natural justice, was obtained by fraud, or sustains a claim founded on a breach of Indian law. An Indian party resisting execution of a foreign judgment will frequently run its defence through one or more of these six grounds rather than disputing the underlying merits a second time.

Timing matters on both the litigation and the execution side. A suit filed in India on a foreign judgment from a non-reciprocating territory, where the judgment itself is the cause of action, is generally governed by the three-year period under Article 101 of the Limitation Act, 1963, running from the date of the judgment. Execution of a reciprocating-territory decree under Section 44A is a separate question from a fresh suit, and the Supreme Court has held that the residuary three-year period applies to that execution application rather than the longer period that governs execution of an ordinary Indian decree, which means a foreign judgment-holder who delays before bringing the judgment to India for enforcement can lose the benefit of the more direct Section 44A route purely through the passage of time.

Drafting Guidance for a Foreign Party Contracting With an Indian Counterparty

A governing law clause should say plainly which country's law governs the substantive contract, and, where arbitration is also chosen, should separately address whether the same law governs the arbitration agreement itself or whether a different law applies to that narrower question, rather than leaving the two to be read together by implication.

Dispute resolution itself should be chosen between litigation and arbitration deliberately rather than by habit, with arbitration generally the safer default for a foreign party dealing with an Indian counterparty given the comparative predictability of enforcement under the New York Convention regime against the narrower and less certain reciprocating-territory regime for foreign court judgments.

Where litigation is chosen instead, the clause should use explicit exclusivity language, words like exclusive, sole, or alone naming the chosen court, even though Swastik Gases confirms this is not strictly required, because explicit language reduces the scope for the kind of interpretive dispute Modi Entertainment Network and the anti-suit cases show Indian courts are willing to engage with at length.

Finally, the clause should be checked against where the Indian counterparty's assets actually sit, since a beautifully drafted foreign forum and foreign law clause secures very little if the eventual judgment or award cannot reach any asset the Indian party actually owns, a due-diligence step that belongs with the commercial and financial diligence on the deal rather than left entirely to the lawyers drafting the dispute resolution boilerplate at the end of the negotiation.

When the Foreign Company Is the One Suing in India

This piece has mostly addressed the position of a foreign company defending its choice of forum against an Indian party that wants to sue at home instead. The reverse situation, a foreign company needing to sue an Indian counterparty in India because the Indian party's only meaningful assets are in India, or because the contract's forum clause itself points to an Indian court, raises a different set of practical steps that a foreign party's own legal team does not always anticipate.

Where the dispute is commercial in the sense the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 defines, broadly, disputes arising out of ordinary transactions of trade and commerce above the specified pecuniary value threshold, the suit will usually have to go through the Commercial Division or the Commercial Court the relevant High Court has constituted, and, before the suit can even be filed, Section 12A of the Act requires the foreign company to first attempt pre-institution mediation, except where the suit contemplates urgent interim relief. A foreign company that skips straight to drafting a plaint, assuming its home jurisdiction's litigation practice of going directly to court, risks having the suit rejected at the threshold for want of compliance with Section 12A.

Serving the Indian defendant is usually the easier half of a foreign company's suit in India, since the defendant is within the court's jurisdiction and ordinary service rules apply without the cross-border service questions that arise when an Indian party sues a defendant abroad. What foreign claimants more often underestimate is the evidentiary groundwork: contracts, invoices, and correspondence generated outside India in a foreign language or format need to be properly proved and, where necessary, translated and authenticated for use before an Indian court, a step that is straightforward in principle but easy to leave until too late in the preparation of the case.

Drafting Mistakes That Undo an Otherwise Careful Clause

The most common mistake this area of practice sees is an inconsistency between the main agreement and its schedules, side letters, or a master framework agreement under which several individual purchase orders or statements of work are issued. A master agreement with a careful English-law, Singapore-arbitration clause does little good if the individual purchase orders issued under it are silent on dispute resolution, or worse, carry their own standard-form clause pointing to a different forum, because a dispute over a specific purchase order can then turn into a preliminary fight over which of two inconsistent clauses actually governs that transaction.

A second recurring mistake is treating a non-exclusive jurisdiction clause as if it carried the same practical effect as an exclusive one. A non-exclusive clause, as Modi Entertainment Network itself recognised, still leaves the parties free to sue in any other court that independently has jurisdiction, which means a foreign company that drafted a non-exclusive English jurisdiction clause expecting it to keep every dispute in England can still find itself defending a suit the Indian party has filed in Delhi or Kolkata, with the non-exclusive clause doing comparatively little to stop that suit from proceeding.

Arbitration clauses carry a third, more specific mistake: naming an arbitral institution without checking that the institution's own rules and the seat named in the clause actually work together, for instance naming a seat in one country while selecting an arbitral institution whose standard rules assume a different default seat absent express agreement. These mismatches rarely surface until a dispute has already arisen and one side is trying to use the inconsistency to argue for a more favourable forum, by which point fixing the clause is no longer an option and the parties are left arguing about what the clause was actually supposed to mean.

What Remains Unsettled

How Indian courts will treat a case where a contract names Indian courts as the exclusive forum but the dispute has a genuinely strong foreign connection, the reverse of the fact pattern most of the reported authority addresses, has not been tested with the same volume of decisions as the foreign-forum scenario, and this piece does not predict how that reverse scenario would be decided. Commentary also records a 2024 Delhi High Court decision read by some as applying Indian law by default to international commercial disputes in certain circumstances even where a foreign governing law was chosen, a reading this piece has not been able to confirm against the judgment's own text in this research, and a foreign company should have that decision's actual scope checked by counsel before assuming either that it changes the general position described above or that it does not.

A foreign company contracting with an Indian party can choose its own governing law with reasonable confidence that an Indian court or tribunal will apply it, and that confidence extends even to contracts between two Indian parties choosing a foreign law, as Dholi Spintex confirms. Choosing a foreign forum is a different exercise: the clause does not strip an Indian court of jurisdiction it otherwise has, but it gives that court a strong basis, under Modi Entertainment Network, to decline to hear the case in favour of the parties' chosen forum, and arbitration at a foreign seat, backed by the New York Convention's considerably more predictable enforcement regime, remains the more reliable way for a foreign party to secure both a forum it controls and an outcome it can actually enforce against assets in India. None of this removes the need for careful, deal-specific drafting checked by counsel on both sides of the transaction before signature, since the authorities discussed in this piece each turned on the particular wording and facts of the clause in front of the court, not on a generic preference for one kind of clause over another.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an Indian court be forced to apply foreign law to a contract?

An Indian court will generally apply the foreign law the parties chose to govern their contract, provided that law is not contrary to Indian public policy. This applies even to a contract between two Indian parties, as the Delhi High Court held in Dholi Spintex v. Louis Dreyfus Company India in 2020.

Does naming a foreign court in a contract stop an Indian party from suing in India?

Not automatically. An Indian court's statutory jurisdiction under the Code of Civil Procedure is not removed by a private contract. The clause instead gives the Indian court a strong basis, under the principles in Modi Entertainment Network v. W.S.G. Cricket, to decline to hear the case and defer to the chosen foreign forum.

Is a clause restricting disputes to one specific Indian city's courts valid?

Yes, provided that city's court is one of several courts that would independently have had jurisdiction under Sections 16 to 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure. The Supreme Court upheld exactly this kind of clause in Hakam Singh v. Gammon (India) Ltd. in 1971, and it does not violate Section 28 of the Contract Act.

Can two Indian companies agree to arbitrate their dispute outside India?

Yes. The Supreme Court held in PASL Wind Solutions v. GE Power Conversion India (2021) that two Indian parties can choose a foreign seat of arbitration, and that this does not violate Section 28 of the Contract Act or Indian public policy.

Can an Indian party still get urgent interim relief in India if the arbitration seat is abroad?

Yes. PASL Wind Solutions also confirmed that a party to a foreign-seated arbitration can approach an Indian court under Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 for interim measures, even though the arbitration itself proceeds outside India.

Is a foreign court judgment automatically enforceable in India?

Only if it comes from a country the Central Government has formally notified as a reciprocating territory under Section 44A of the Code of Civil Procedure, in which case it can be executed broadly like an Indian decree. A judgment from any other country has to be enforced through a fresh civil suit filed in India on the judgment itself.

Why do foreign companies often prefer arbitration over a foreign court clause for Indian contracts?

Mainly because a foreign arbitral award is enforceable in India under the New York Convention through a route Indian courts have applied with more consistency than the narrower reciprocating-territory regime that governs enforcement of a bare foreign court judgment.

Will an Indian court stop a party from suing abroad in breach of a contract's forum clause?

It can, through an anti-suit injunction, but only on the limited grounds set out in Modi Entertainment Network v. W.S.G. Cricket, chiefly where the foreign proceeding is oppressive or vexatious. A forum clause alone does not entitle a party to an injunction as a matter of course.
