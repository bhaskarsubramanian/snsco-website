Protecting a Brand at the Indian Border: Customs Recordal and Anti-Counterfeiting Enforcement

A foreign brand owner who only sues infringers after counterfeit goods have already reached Indian shelves is fighting the problem one market at a time. Customs recordal stops the same goods at the port, and almost no foreign company uses it until after the first infringement lawsuit.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 16 November 2026 · Law stated as on 15 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company entering the Indian market usually budgets for trademark registration and, if things go wrong, for a civil injunction against a specific infringer. Fewer budget for the one mechanism that stops infringing goods before they ever reach an Indian warehouse: recording the mark, copyright or design with Indian customs under the Intellectual Property Rights (Imported Goods) Enforcement Rules, 2007. This piece sets out how that system actually works, what it does not catch, and how it fits alongside the civil injunctions most foreign brand owners already know about.

Key points

  • Section 11 of the Customs Act, 1962 lets the Central Government prohibit the import of goods infringing a specified trademark, copyright, design or geographical indication, and notifications issued under it, read with the Intellectual Property Rights (Imported Goods) Enforcement Rules, 2007, implement that prohibition.
  • A rights holder records their trademark, copyright, design or geographical indication with Indian customs through an online portal; one recordal, once granted, covers all Indian ports rather than requiring a separate filing at each port.
  • Recordal typically runs for a period tied to the underlying right, generally described as five years or the expiry of the IP right, whichever comes first, with renewal available by re-filing.
  • Patents were removed entirely from the customs recordal framework by a 2018 amendment to the 2007 Rules, which took 'patent' out of the Rules' own definition of intellectual property; customs has no statutory power to suspend an import on a patent-infringement complaint, so a foreign patentee cannot use this mechanism at all and must rely on civil remedies under the Patents Act, 1970.
  • Once goods are suspended on suspicion of infringing a recorded right, the rights holder is typically given a short window to confirm the goods are infringing and to pursue further action, and failure to respond within that window can result in release of the goods.
  • Customs recordal is an administrative border-control mechanism, separate from a civil 'John Doe' or 'Ashok Kumar' injunction obtained from a court against unidentified infringers; the two work best used together rather than as alternatives.
  • Customs recordal does not catch every route counterfeit goods take into India, including small personal-use consignments and goods that enter without triggering inspection, so it is one layer of a wider enforcement strategy rather than a complete solution on its own.

Why litigation alone is not enough for a foreign brand owner

A foreign company that discovers counterfeit versions of its product being sold in an Indian market typically reaches first for the remedy its own legal team already understands: a civil suit for trademark infringement, often combined with an urgent application for an injunction. That remedy is real and frequently effective, but it treats each infringing seller as a separate problem to be found, sued and restrained, one at a time, after the counterfeit goods have already entered the country and reached a market, a warehouse or an online storefront.

Customs recordal under Indian law works earlier in that chain. Instead of waiting for counterfeit goods to surface in a market and then identifying who is selling them, a rights holder who has recorded their trademark, copyright, design or geographical indication with Indian customs puts customs officers at every Indian port, airport and land border crossing on notice to look for shipments matching the recorded right, and to suspend clearance of goods suspected of infringing it before those goods are released into the Indian market at all.

This piece works through how that mechanism is actually built under Indian law, because 'we are registered with customs' means very little on its own if a foreign company does not also understand what the registration covers, how long it lasts, and what the company itself has to do once customs actually flags a suspect shipment.

The stakes for getting this wrong are not abstract. A counterfeit product that reaches an Indian market before any enforcement action begins has already done its damage: a consumer who buys it, discovers it is substandard, and blames the genuine brand for the experience is a lost customer the brand owner never gets a chance to win back through a later injunction, however successful that injunction eventually is. Stopping the shipment at the port removes that damage from the equation entirely, rather than merely stopping its continuation after some of it has already occurred.

The statutory basis: Section 11 of the Customs Act and the 2007 Rules

Section 11 of the Customs Act, 1962 empowers the Central Government to prohibit, absolutely or subject to conditions, the import or export of goods of a specified description, for a range of purposes set out in the section. Among those purposes, Section 11(2)(n) covers the protection of patents, trademarks and copyrights, and Section 11(2)(u) covers the prevention of contravention of any law for the time being in force, and together these two clauses provide the statutory hook for border measures against goods that infringe intellectual property rights recognised under other Indian statutes.

The Intellectual Property Rights (Imported Goods) Enforcement Rules, 2007 were made under Section 156(1) of the Customs Act, read with these clauses of Section 11(2), and they set out the actual mechanics: how a rights holder records a right with customs, what happens once a suspect consignment is identified, and how the rights holder and the importer are each brought into that process. The Rules were framed with reference to India's obligations under the TRIPS Agreement and broadly track the World Customs Organisation's model legislation on border enforcement of intellectual property rights, which is why the Indian framework will look structurally familiar to a foreign brand owner who has used a similar customs recordal system in another TRIPS-member jurisdiction.

Once a right is recorded and accepted, the practical effect, as several practitioner sources describe it, is that import of goods alleged to infringe that right is treated as prohibited within the meaning of Section 11, giving customs officers a clear statutory basis to detain a shipment on suspicion, rather than leaving the question to an officer's own general discretion over what to inspect.

This statutory design matters for a foreign company deciding whether recordal is worth the administrative effort. Without a recorded right, an individual customs officer who happens to notice a suspicious shipment has no clear statutory trigger tying that specific consignment to a specific owner's rights, and has to rely on general vigilance or an ad hoc complaint. Recordal converts that uncertain, officer-by-officer vigilance into a standing instruction tied to the brand owner's own registered rights, which is the entire value the mechanism offers over simply hoping customs happens to notice a counterfeit shipment on its own.

What rights can actually be recorded

The notified framework under the 2007 Rules covers trademarks, copyright, industrial designs and geographical indications, and a foreign brand owner holding a registered Indian trademark or an Indian copyright is generally the most straightforward candidate for recordal. Current notifications prohibiting import of goods infringing these rights have been issued and periodically amended, with the operative notification having been revised as recently as 2018 according to practitioner commentary, so a brand owner should confirm the current notification number and scope directly with Indian customs or Indian IP counsel before relying on an older summary.

Patents are not available at all. A 2018 amendment to the 2007 Rules (Notification No. 56/2018-Customs (N.T.)) omitted 'patent' from the Rules' definitions of intellectual property and intellectual property law, which took away customs' power to suspend clearance of a shipment on a patent-infringement complaint. Before 2018 the statutory language did extend to patents, but that is no longer the position: a foreign patentee cannot record a patent with Indian customs today, and should rely entirely on civil remedies under the Patents Act, 1970, since the border mechanism described in this piece simply does not reach patents any longer.

Personal baggage and small, clearly non-commercial consignments are generally carved out of the recordal framework, which matters for a brand owner assessing how much a given enforcement program will actually catch: recordal is built around commercial-scale import, not an individual traveller carrying a handful of counterfeit items for personal use.

How recordal actually works

A rights holder applies for recordal electronically, through the customs department's online IPR recordation portal, filing a separate application for each right to be recorded, trademark, copyright, design or geographical indication, with supporting proof of ownership and registration. Once the Commissioner of Customs grants the recordal application after examination, that single recordal is generally understood to cover every port in India, so a foreign company does not need to file separately at Mumbai, Chennai, Nhava Sheva and every other entry point where its goods, or counterfeits of its goods, might arrive.

Recordal is understood to run for a period commonly described as five years, or until the expiry of the underlying intellectual property right, whichever comes first, after which the rights holder needs to re-file to keep the recordal active. Different practitioner sources describe the renewal process and fee slightly differently, with a modest government fee cited in the low thousands of rupees for a fresh filing, and a foreign brand owner should confirm the current fee and the exact renewal procedure against the portal's current requirements rather than an older published figure, since fee schedules of this kind are revised from time to time.

Because the underlying IP right and the customs recordal are two separate registrations running on two separate clocks, a brand owner should track both expiry dates independently. A trademark that is renewed in good time with the Trade Marks Registry does the brand owner no good at the border if the separate customs recordal has lapsed and nobody noticed, and conversely a live customs recordal built on a trademark registration that has itself lapsed is of doubtful legal value.

What happens when a suspect consignment arrives

Once a right is recorded, customs officers examining import documentation and physical consignments are expected to flag shipments that appear, on the information available at the port, to match the recorded right in a way that suggests infringement, commonly by reference to the brand name, packaging, or other identifying features described in the recordal application. On identifying a suspect consignment, the practice described by multiple practitioner sources is that customs issues a notice of suspension to the importer and notifies the rights holder, giving particulars of the detained consignment.

The rights holder is then generally given an opportunity, under Rule 8 of the 2007 Rules, to inspect the suspended goods, either personally or through an authorised representative, in order to confirm whether the goods are in fact infringing. This inspection step matters in practice, because customs officers are not themselves expert in distinguishing a genuine product from a sophisticated counterfeit, and the rights holder's own confirmation is often the deciding input into whether the suspension is maintained.

Sources differ on the exact clock that then runs, with some describing a response window measured in a small number of days and others describing a longer period before a registration or suspension decision is finalised, and this is a point where a foreign brand owner's India counsel should confirm the operative timeline directly against the current Rules and the specific notice received, rather than rely on a generic figure, because missing a response deadline can result in the goods being released notwithstanding the recordal.

Confirmation, confiscation, destruction and re-export

Where the rights holder confirms the goods are infringing and the matter proceeds, the goods can be seized under Section 110 of the Customs Act and confiscated under Section 111(d), which covers goods imported contrary to a prohibition in force under the Act, including the prohibition generated by a recorded IPR right. Confiscated counterfeit goods are typically destroyed rather than released into the Indian market under any circumstances, since allowing a confiscated counterfeit to be auctioned or otherwise sold would simply move the same infringing product from an importer to a different commercial channel.

Re-export of the goods to the country of origin, rather than destruction, is sometimes discussed as an alternative in international customs enforcement practice generally, but a foreign brand owner should not assume re-export is routinely available or desirable under the Indian framework without specific confirmation, since re-exported counterfeit goods can simply be redirected to a different market, including back into informal channels that eventually re-enter India.

An importer who disputes the infringement finding, or who claims the goods are genuine parallel imports rather than counterfeits, a distinction that raises its own separate body of Indian trademark law on exhaustion of rights, is entitled to contest the customs action, and the rights holder should expect that a disputed consignment may end up before the appropriate customs appellate forum or, in some cases, before a court, rather than being resolved purely at the administrative level.

Cost allocation for storage, demurrage and destruction during this process is worth asking about before an actual dispute arises, since a suspended consignment sitting at a port for weeks or months while a dispute is resolved can accumulate storage and demurrage charges, and the Rules and accompanying practice generally expect the rights holder to bear certain costs connected to the recordal process, including, in some accounts, a bond or security furnished to cover the importer's potential loss if the suspension later turns out to have been wrongly invoked. A brand owner should understand this cost exposure before triggering repeated suspensions, since an aggressive recordal program that generates frequent false positives against genuine goods can become expensive in bonds and storage costs even where the underlying IP protection is working as intended.

Customs recordal versus John Doe and Ashok Kumar injunctions

Indian courts have developed a well-established practice of granting interim injunctions against unidentified or unidentifiable infringers, commonly called John Doe orders, or Ashok Kumar orders, after the name used to describe the unnamed defendant in Indian pleadings. These are temporary injunctions typically sought under Order XXXIX, Rules 1 and 2 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, together with the court's inherent powers under Section 151, on the usual showing of a prima facie case, balance of convenience, and irreparable harm, and Indian courts have granted such orders specifically against unidentified manufacturers and sellers of counterfeit goods as well as against unauthorised streaming or piracy of copyrighted content, the context in which these orders are sometimes more widely known.

The practical difference between the two mechanisms is where they operate and who acts on them. A John Doe or Ashok Kumar order is a judicial injunction, obtained from a civil court, generally enforced with the help of local police or court-appointed personnel against physical premises, markets, or online sellers within India, after infringing goods have typically already entered the domestic market. Customs recordal is an administrative mechanism, operated by customs officers at ports and other entry points, aimed at goods still in the course of import, before they are cleared into the Indian market at all.

Neither mechanism is a substitute for the other, and a foreign brand owner should not treat them as competing choices; they address different stages of the same supply chain and work best deployed together. A brand owner who has recorded its mark with customs and separately holds, or can quickly obtain, a civil injunction is positioned to stop a counterfeit shipment at the port and, for whatever has already slipped through in the past, to move against the market-level sellers distributing it, rather than having to choose one remedy to the exclusion of the other.

What customs recordal does not catch

Customs recordal is built around formally declared, commercial-scale import through recognised ports, airports and land crossings, and it depends on an inspecting officer actually flagging a shipment as a possible match to the recorded right. A counterfeiter who mis-declares goods, routes small parcels through postal or courier channels designed for low-value personal consignments, or simply is not caught by inspection on a given day, is not stopped by the recordal itself, even though the recordal remains formally in force.

Goods that are already manufactured domestically in India, rather than imported, fall entirely outside the customs mechanism, since there is no import event for customs to intercept. A foreign brand owner facing domestically manufactured counterfeits, which is common for products that are easy to replicate locally, needs the civil and criminal enforcement tools available within India, not the border mechanism, no matter how thoroughly the customs recordal has been filed and maintained.

Even where the mechanism works as designed, it depends on customs officers correctly identifying a genuine match between a physical shipment and the recordal application's description of the right, and a sophisticated counterfeiter who closely copies packaging while altering minor details can sometimes pass an inspection that a less careful counterfeit would not. None of this is a reason to skip recordal; it is a reason to treat it as one layer in a wider strategy rather than a complete answer on its own.

Parallel imports and grey-market goods: a different problem again

A foreign brand owner's team at the port sometimes flags a shipment of genuine products, manufactured by the brand owner itself or under proper licence, but imported into India through a channel the brand owner did not authorise for the Indian market, commonly called a parallel import or grey-market good. This is a different legal problem from counterfeiting, because the goods are not fakes; they are authentic products that have simply arrived through an unapproved distribution route, often because pricing or packaging differences make it profitable for a trader to buy them cheaply abroad and resell them in India outside the brand owner's own distribution agreements.

Indian trademark law treats this question through the doctrine of exhaustion of rights, and whether a particular parallel import infringes the Indian trademark owner's rights depends on facts that customs recordal, built as it is around identifying counterfeits rather than adjudicating exhaustion, is not designed to resolve at the port. A foreign brand owner whose concern is grey-market diversion of its own genuine goods, rather than counterfeiting by third parties, needs a different strategy built around its distribution agreements and, where necessary, a civil claim addressing the specific exhaustion question, rather than relying on customs recordal to solve a problem the recordal mechanism was not built to catch.

Flagging this distinction matters because a brand owner's own customs team, briefed only on counterfeiting, can mistakenly treat a parallel-import shipment of genuine goods as a recordal violation, triggering a dispute with an importer who has done nothing illegal under Indian trademark exhaustion principles, which wastes enforcement resources and can create friction with legitimate, if unwelcome, trade channels.

Building a combined strategy before entering the Indian market

A foreign company planning a serious entry into the Indian market, whether through direct sale, a distributor, or an Indian subsidiary, should sequence its IP protection rather than treat trademark registration as the only item on the checklist. Secure the Indian trademark, copyright and design registrations the business actually needs first, since customs recordal depends on an underlying registered right and cannot itself create one. Record the relevant rights with customs as soon as those underlying registrations are in hand, rather than waiting for an actual counterfeiting incident to prompt the filing, because recordal takes time to process and provides no retrospective protection for shipments that cleared before it was granted.

In parallel, identify Indian counsel who can move quickly on a civil injunction application, including a John Doe or Ashok Kumar order where the infringers cannot yet be identified, so that market-level enforcement is not starting from zero the first time counterfeit product is actually found on sale. Brief that counsel in advance on the brand's packaging, authentication features, and how to distinguish a genuine product from a counterfeit at a glance, since the speed of an interim injunction application often depends on how quickly counsel can put together convincing evidence of infringement.

Finally, build a simple internal process for monitoring the Indian market, online marketplaces in particular, for counterfeit listings, and for reporting suspected import-stage counterfeiting to Indian customs once recordal is in place. A recorded right that nobody is actively using, because the foreign company's India-facing team does not know it exists or has moved on since the filing, delivers little of the protection the filing was meant to secure.

Common pitfalls

Sequencing is the most common pitfall: a foreign company applies for customs recordal before its Indian trademark registration has actually been granted, assuming an application alone is sufficient, when the recordal process generally requires evidence of a subsisting registered right rather than a pending application. Confirm the underlying registration is actually granted, not merely filed, before applying for recordal.

Letting the recordal lapse without noticing is a second common pitfall, either because nobody within the foreign company's organisation owns the renewal date, or because the underlying trademark registration itself lapsed first. Assign clear internal ownership of both renewal dates, and treat them as two separate compliance items rather than one.

A third pitfall is treating a customs recordal certificate as a complete substitute for market-level vigilance. Customs recordal addresses the import stage; it does nothing for counterfeits already inside India or manufactured domestically, and a foreign brand owner who stops monitoring Indian marketplaces and physical markets because 'customs has it covered' will usually be surprised by how much counterfeit product is circulating despite the recordal being in force throughout.

Who inside the organisation should actually run this

Customs recordal is often filed by outside counsel at the time of Indian market entry and then effectively orphaned within the foreign company, because no one internally is assigned to own it afterwards. The filing itself is a one-time event; the ongoing value comes from renewal tracking, from briefing the brand's own supply chain and brand protection teams that the recordal exists, and from actually responding promptly when customs flags a suspect shipment, none of which happens automatically once the certificate is granted.

A foreign company with a meaningful India business should assign clear internal ownership, typically within its legal, compliance or brand protection function rather than its local sales team, for tracking recordal and underlying IP registration renewal dates, for maintaining a current set of authentication materials customs and the company's own India counsel can use to distinguish genuine goods from counterfeits, and for ensuring that a response to a customs suspension notice can be mobilised within whatever window the current Rules allow, which, as noted above, can be short.

For a company without a large enough India presence to justify a dedicated internal owner, the practical alternative is a standing arrangement with Indian IP counsel who already hold the authentication materials and are pre-briefed to respond to a customs notice on short notice, rather than starting from zero each time a notice arrives. The lag between a customs suspension notice landing and a brand owner actually confirming infringement is often the single biggest avoidable risk in the entire recordal process, and it is entirely a function of how well the company has prepared in advance, not of anything customs itself does differently from case to case.

Customs recordal will not, by itself, make counterfeiting of a foreign brand's products in India disappear. What it does is move part of the enforcement effort upstream, to the port, where a shipment can be stopped before it ever reaches a market or a warehouse, which is a meaningfully different proposition from suing a seller after the goods are already in circulation.

Used alongside the civil injunction remedies Indian courts already provide, including John Doe and Ashok Kumar orders against unidentified infringers, customs recordal gives a foreign brand owner a genuinely layered enforcement strategy rather than a single point of failure. The filing itself is comparatively inexpensive and administratively straightforward; the discipline of actually keeping it current, and using it once in force, is where most foreign companies fall short.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does a foreign company record its trademark with Indian customs?

The rights holder files an application electronically through the customs department's IPR recordation portal, with proof of the underlying Indian trademark, copyright, design or geographical indication registration. Once the Commissioner of Customs grants the application, the recordal generally covers every Indian port, so a separate filing at each port is not required.

How long does a customs IPR recordal in India last?

Recordal is generally understood to run for a period commonly described as five years or the expiry of the underlying IP right, whichever happens first, after which the rights holder needs to re-file to keep the recordal active. Confirm the exact current renewal fee and procedure with Indian customs or IP counsel, since these details have been revised over time.

Can a foreign company stop counterfeit imports of a patented product at the Indian border?

No, not through customs recordal. A 2018 amendment to the 2007 Rules removed patents from the Rules' own definition of intellectual property, so customs no longer has any power to suspend a shipment on a patent-infringement complaint. A patentee must rely entirely on civil remedies under the Patents Act, 1970, rather than any border measure.

What is the difference between customs recordal and a John Doe or Ashok Kumar order?

Customs recordal is an administrative mechanism operated by customs officers at ports to stop infringing goods during import, before they reach the Indian market. A John Doe or Ashok Kumar order is a civil court injunction against unidentified infringers, typically enforced against markets, premises or online sellers within India, usually after goods have already entered the country. The two address different stages of the same problem and are generally used together.

What happens to counterfeit goods seized by Indian customs?

Goods confirmed as infringing can be seized under Section 110 of the Customs Act and confiscated under Section 111(d), and confiscated counterfeit goods are typically destroyed rather than released into the Indian market, since allowing them to be sold through any channel would simply move the infringing product elsewhere.

Does customs recordal stop counterfeit goods that are manufactured inside India rather than imported?

No. Customs recordal only addresses goods at the point of import; it has no application to counterfeits manufactured domestically within India, which require separate civil and, where applicable, criminal enforcement tools rather than a border measure.

What proof does a brand owner need to give customs when a suspect shipment is flagged?

Under Rule 8 of the 2007 Rules, the rights holder is generally given an opportunity to inspect the suspended goods personally or through an authorised representative, in order to confirm whether they are in fact infringing, and that confirmation is typically the key input customs relies on before maintaining a suspension or proceeding to confiscation.

Can customs recordal be used against genuine products imported through an unauthorised distributor?

Generally, no, not in the way it is used against counterfeits. Genuine goods diverted through an unapproved channel, commonly called parallel imports or grey-market goods, raise a separate question under Indian trademark law's doctrine of exhaustion of rights, and customs recordal is designed to catch counterfeits rather than to adjudicate whether a particular parallel import infringes the brand owner's rights. A brand owner concerned about grey-market diversion of its own genuine goods generally needs a distribution-agreement and civil-law strategy rather than relying on the recordal mechanism.

Does a foreign company need an Indian trademark registration before it can record its mark with customs?

Generally, yes. Customs recordal is built around an underlying registered Indian intellectual property right, so a foreign company should have its Indian trademark, copyright or design registration actually granted, not merely applied for, before filing for customs recordal, and should not assume a pending application alone is sufficient.
