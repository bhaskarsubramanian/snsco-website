Overseas Direct Investment by Indian Promoters and HNI Families: Setting Up a Business Abroad Under the 2022 Rules

What a family business actually has to clear, under FEMA rather than under the glamour of 'going global', before its Indian promoters can open or buy a company abroad.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 14 November 2026 · Law stated as on 14 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An Indian family business that has outgrown the domestic market usually discovers, somewhere in the course of planning its first overseas acquisition or subsidiary, that the harder constraint is not commercial but regulatory: how much the promoter entity or the family members personally can commit abroad, what kind of foreign business they are allowed to invest in at all, and how a foreign holding structure is allowed to relate back to the Indian group. The Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, 2022 answer most of these questions more liberally than the regime they replaced, but the answers come with conditions that a family's own commercial instinct will not always anticipate.

Key points

  • The Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, 2022, notified on 22 August 2022, replaced the earlier ODI regulations and introduced a sharper line between Overseas Direct Investment and Overseas Portfolio Investment.
  • ODI covers any stake in an unlisted foreign entity, a holding of 10% or more in a listed foreign entity, or any stake that carries control; once an investment is classified as ODI it stays ODI even if later diluted below that line.
  • The automatic route covers financial commitment up to 400% of the Indian party's net worth; anything beyond that, or an OPI exceeding 50% of net worth, needs the Reserve Bank's prior approval.
  • Investing in a foreign entity engaged in real estate dealing, gambling, or rupee-linked financial products abroad is prohibited outright, though genuine real estate development for sale or lease is carved out of the real estate restriction.
  • Financial services investment abroad is now open even to Indian entities outside that sector, subject to a three-year net profit track record and, where applicable, regulatory registration and approval.
  • Rule 19(3) eased the old blanket bar on round-tripping structures, but only within a layering limit that commentators still read differently, and only where the structure is not designed to avoid tax.
  • A resident individual can use the overseas direct investment route as well, but only out of funds remitted under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, which caps the exercise at USD 250,000 a year regardless of how the family structures the rest of the transaction.

Why This Is a FEMA Question Before It Is a Commercial One

A family business deciding to open a subsidiary in Dubai, acquire a distributor in the United Kingdom, or set up a holding company in Singapore to consolidate its overseas operations is making a commercial decision, but the legal mechanism that gets it there is regulatory rather than contractual. Any commitment of Indian resources, whether equity, a loan to the foreign entity, or a guarantee given on its behalf, by a person resident in India to a foreign entity is governed by the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 and, since 22 August 2022, specifically by the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, 2022 read with the companion Overseas Investment Regulations and the Reserve Bank's Overseas Investment Directions.

These replaced a patchwork of earlier instruments, principally the Foreign Exchange Management (Transfer or Issue of Any Foreign Security) Regulations and the separate regulations on acquiring immovable property outside India, that had grown increasingly difficult to apply to modern holding structures, deferred consideration, and the layered group structures family businesses now use when they expand abroad. The 2022 framework was built around two new, more precise concepts: Overseas Direct Investment and Overseas Portfolio Investment, treated for the first time as genuinely distinct regimes with different thresholds and different reporting obligations.

For an HNI family weighing a first overseas move, the practical consequence is that the structuring conversation has to start with the FEMA classification question rather than with the tax or corporate structuring question that a family's accountant or the target's own advisers will usually raise first. Getting the ODI or OPI classification wrong at the outset tends to be far more expensive to unwind later than getting the tax structuring slightly suboptimal, because an incorrect classification is itself a contravention with its own compounding or adjudication exposure.

The entity making the commitment also matters under the Rules in a way that family businesses sometimes overlook. An Indian Party eligible to make ODI includes an Indian company, a registered partnership firm, a limited liability partnership, and a body corporate created under a specific Act of Parliament, each of which is treated slightly differently for net worth computation and for which filings the authorised dealer bank will expect. A family that holds its various businesses across a company, an LLP and a partnership firm, which is common where a business has grown organically over two or three generations, has to work out at the outset which of these entities will actually be the Indian Party for a given overseas commitment, since net worth and eligibility are assessed entity by entity rather than for the family as a whole.

Overseas Direct Investment and Overseas Portfolio Investment: The Dividing Line

Overseas Direct Investment covers any stake, however small, in an unlisted foreign entity, a holding of ten per cent or more of the paid-up equity capital of a listed foreign entity, or any stake below that ten per cent threshold that nonetheless carries control over the foreign entity's management or policy decisions, whether through shareholding, board representation, a shareholders' agreement or any other means. Everything that does not meet one of those tests, chiefly a minority, non-controlling holding in a listed foreign company, falls instead under Overseas Portfolio Investment, which is the route used for buying shares of listed foreign companies, foreign mutual funds and similar instruments without any intention of control.

The classification is sticky once made. An investment that qualifies as ODI when acquired continues to be treated as ODI even if the Indian party's stake is later diluted below ten per cent, or if control is lost through a later transaction, unless the investment is actually sold or transferred. A family that brought in co-investors after the initial acquisition, reducing its own stake well below ten per cent, cannot simply recharacterise the remaining holding as portfolio investment to escape ODI's reporting obligations; the original classification follows the investment.

The 2022 Rules also replaced the paperwork that went with each route. Form ODI, used under the old regime for the initial investment and every subsequent event, was replaced by Form FC, while a separate Form OPI was introduced specifically for portfolio investments, a distinction that did not exist with the same clarity before 2022. Getting the right form in front of the authorised dealer bank at each stage, the initial commitment, any restructuring, and eventual disinvestment, is as important procedurally as getting the underlying classification right.

Control itself is not defined narrowly. The Directions test it through shareholding, the right to appoint a majority of directors, voting rights of ten per cent or more combined with another agreed arrangement, or any other contractual or shareholders'-agreement right that lets the Indian party direct the foreign entity's management or policy decisions. A family that structures its first overseas investment as a small minority stake in a listed foreign company, but couples that stake with board nomination rights or a veto over major decisions under a shareholders' agreement, should expect that arrangement to be read as ODI on the control test even though the shareholding alone would have looked like OPI.

The Automatic Route and Its 400% Ceiling

Most ODI by an Indian entity does not need the Reserve Bank's prior approval at all. It proceeds under the automatic route provided the total financial commitment to the foreign entity, equity contributed, any loan extended to it, and any guarantee given on its behalf, taken together, does not exceed 400% of the Indian party's net worth as it stood on the date of its last audited balance sheet. A family holding company with a modest standalone net worth, planning a larger overseas acquisition, often finds this ceiling, rather than the commercial price of the target, to be the binding constraint on how the deal can be funded from India alone.

A parallel limit applies on the portfolio side: Overseas Portfolio Investment beyond fifty per cent of the Indian party's net worth also falls outside the automatic route. Both ceilings are measured against net worth as a point-in-time figure drawn from the last audited accounts, which means a family business planning a large overseas move sometimes has to time the transaction against its own balance sheet date, or infuse fresh equity into the investing entity first, to bring the commitment within the automatic route rather than file for specific approval.

Where a transaction does need to cross either ceiling, the approval route is not closed, but it adds a layer of regulatory discretion and time to the transaction that most family businesses would rather avoid if the deal can instead be structured, staged, or funded differently to stay within the automatic limits. Advisers structuring a larger acquisition will often recommend phasing the Indian party's own commitment, bringing in a co-investor for the balance, or routing part of the funding through the foreign entity's own local borrowing, precisely to keep the Indian leg of the transaction inside the 400% band.

What Cannot Be the Business of the Foreign Entity

Three activities are closed to ODI outright, regardless of the amount involved or the route used. A foreign entity engaged in real estate activity cannot be the subject of ODI from India, where real estate activity is defined to mean buying and selling real estate or trading in transferable development rights, though the definition specifically excludes the development of townships, and the construction of residential or commercial premises, roads or bridges, for sale or lease. A family business that wants to develop property abroad as an operating business, rather than simply hold and trade land, generally falls on the permitted side of this line, but the distinction has to be checked against the specific activity the foreign entity will actually carry on rather than assumed from the word real estate alone.

Gambling in any form is barred without qualification, and dealing in financial products linked to the Indian rupee outside India is barred unless the Reserve Bank gives specific approval for the particular product or structure. Neither prohibition has the kind of carve-out that applies to real estate, and a family business should treat both as effectively absolute for ordinary planning purposes.

Financial services is treated differently from the other three, and considerably more liberally than it was treated before 2022. An Indian entity that is itself engaged in financial services activity can invest in a foreign entity also engaged in financial services, and, in a genuine change from the earlier regime, an Indian entity that is not in the financial services business at all can now do so too, provided it has posted net profits in each of the preceding three financial years, a condition that does not require those profits to have come from financial services activity itself, and, where the Indian entity is itself regulated by a financial sector regulator, that it is appropriately registered and has obtained that regulator's approval for the overseas investment. Outside the GIFT City International Financial Services Centre, the foreign entity itself generally cannot be in banking or insurance, though insurance that is merely incidental to supporting the foreign entity's core business, such as general or health cover for its own operations, is excluded from that restriction.

For an Indian entity investing into the GIFT City International Financial Services Centre specifically, the net profit condition does not apply at all, which is a deliberate policy choice to make IFSC the easier entry point for a family business testing an overseas financial services idea for the first time. Where a sectoral regulator's approval is still needed for an IFSC-bound investment, the Directions provide that the approval is treated as deemed if the regulator has not decided the application within forty-five days, which removes the open-ended waiting period that used to attach to this kind of approval under the earlier framework.

Round-Tripping and the Layering Limit Under Rule 19(3)

Round-tripping, structuring an investment so that money leaves India through a foreign entity and then flows back into India as if it were foreign investment, was never expressly addressed under the pre-2022 regime, which the Reserve Bank instead policed indirectly by treating such structures as outside what counted as a bona fide business activity eligible for the automatic route. The 2022 Rules took the more direct approach of addressing it head-on in Rule 19(3), and in doing so actually relaxed the position for many ordinary family-business structures rather than tightening it, by permitting certain cross-border arrangements that previously needed specific Reserve Bank approval to proceed under the automatic route instead.

The relief is conditional rather than open-ended. A financial commitment to a foreign entity that has itself invested, directly or indirectly, into India is permitted under the automatic route only where the resulting structure does not exceed what the Rule treats as two layers of subsidiaries, and only where the arrangement is not designed, or does not have the effect of, avoiding tax. For a family business that wants to hold its overseas and Indian operations under a single foreign holding company, rather than keep them in entirely separate ownership chains, this is the provision that decides whether the structure can be built under the automatic route or needs specific approval first.

How exactly to count the two layers is where legal opinion currently divides, and this piece states that divide rather than resolve it. One reading treats the Indian promoter's investment into the foreign holding company as the first layer and that company's investment back into an Indian step-down entity as the second, which would permit a reasonably simple two-tier structure under the automatic route. Another reading counts differently and would treat some structures that look permissible on the first reading as already exceeding the limit. Commentators who have examined the Rule closely acknowledge this is unsettled, and a family business building a structure that depends on which reading applies should get the point tested with the authorised dealer bank, and ideally confirmed in writing, before committing to the structure rather than discovering the disagreement during a later compliance review.

Who Is Exempt From the Layering Limit

The layering restriction in Rule 19(3) does not apply uniformly across every Indian entity making an overseas commitment. Banks, systemically important non-banking financial companies, insurance companies, and government companies are carved out of the restriction altogether, reflecting the reality that regulated financial institutions and public sector entities often operate overseas structures for reasons, and under their own sectoral supervision, that the layering rule was never meant to reach.

For an ordinary unlisted family business, none of these exemptions will usually be available, which means the layering question in the previous section is not an academic one for most HNI promoters planning an overseas structure. A family business that also happens to control an NBFC or an insurance subsidiary within the group should specifically check whether that regulated entity's own overseas commitments fall inside or outside the exemption, since the answer can differ across entities within the same promoter group depending on which entity is actually making the financial commitment.

Reporting: Form FC, Form APR and the Late Submission Fee

Every financial commitment under the ODI route is reported to the Reserve Bank, through the authorised dealer bank, on Form FC, both at the time the commitment is first made and again whenever the structure is restructured, additional funds are committed, or the investment is disinvested, with restructuring and disinvestment events generally expected to be reported within thirty days of the event. The bank then forwards the filing to the Reserve Bank, and the Unique Identification Number allotted to the foreign entity at the outset is used to track every subsequent filing against it.

On an ongoing basis, an Annual Performance Report on Form APR has to be filed for each foreign entity in which the Indian party holds ODI, due by 31 December each year, based on the foreign entity's audited financial statements for the preceding financial year, or, where a foreign jurisdiction does not mandate an audit, on an alternative basis the Directions permit. Missing this filing is one of the more common contraventions FEMA compounding applications deal with, precisely because it recurs every year and because the foreign entity's own audit timetable does not always align neatly with the Indian filing deadline.

Delay on any of these filings attracts a late submission fee under the schedule the Reserve Bank put in place alongside the 2022 Rules: a flat fee for a delayed Form APR or Form OPI filing, and a fee calculated with reference to the amount involved and the length of the delay for a delayed Form FC filing. Persistent delay beyond what the late submission fee schedule covers can itself mature into a reportable contravention requiring compounding under Section 15 of FEMA, which is the same mechanism and, as things stand today, broadly the same cost structure described for inbound FEMA contraventions elsewhere on this site.

The Unique Identification Number allotted to the foreign entity at the time of the first Form FC filing is the thread that ties every later filing together, and the authorised dealer bank, not the Reserve Bank directly, is usually the first point of contact for an Indian party trying to confirm what it has already filed for a given foreign entity, particularly where the original investment was made some years ago by a different generation of the family or a different set of advisers. Before planning any new commitment to an existing foreign entity, a family business should ask its bank to reconcile the UIN's filing history against its own records, since a gap found at that stage is considerably cheaper to fix than one found later during a buyer's due diligence.

Using the Liberalised Remittance Scheme Instead of a Corporate Vehicle

A promoter does not always want to route an overseas investment through the family's Indian holding company, whether for tax reasons, succession planning, or simply because the opportunity is personal rather than corporate. A resident individual can make an overseas direct investment in that personal capacity, but only out of funds remitted under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, and the scheme's own cap, currently USD 250,000 per financial year per individual, applies regardless of how large the underlying transaction is or how the rest of it is funded from other sources.

The same core prohibitions that bind a corporate ODI investor bind an individual as well: no investment in a foreign entity engaged in real estate dealing or gambling, and no use of the funds for purposes the current account rules under FEMA separately prohibit, such as lottery tickets or similarly barred remittances. Because the cap is modest relative to the size of most genuine business acquisitions, individual-route ODI tends to work best either for a smaller, standalone venture the family member is building personally, or as one tranche within a larger structure where the balance of the funding comes from the family's corporate entity under its own, considerably larger, 400% net worth ceiling.

A family weighing whether to use the individual LRS route, the corporate ODI route, or some combination of the two for a single overseas opportunity should treat that as a structuring decision made with FEMA counsel and the family's tax advisers together. The choice affects the immediate remittance cap, how dividends, management fees and eventual exit proceeds flow back into India, and how the holding is eventually treated on succession within the family.

Repatriation and What Happens to Unremitted Returns

ODI is not a one-way gate. Dividends, interest, and the proceeds of any disinvestment or winding up of the foreign entity are required to be repatriated to India through the normal banking channel within the period FEMA and the Directions prescribe, save to the extent the Indian party is permitted to retain and reinvest those proceeds in further overseas investment rather than bring them home. A family business using a foreign holding structure to retain earnings abroad for reinvestment, rather than remit them each year, needs that reinvestment itself structured and reported correctly, since simply leaving money in the foreign entity's accounts without any further ODI or OPI filing is not the same thing as a permitted reinvestment.

Where a foreign entity is wound up, sold, or otherwise ceases to be held, the exit itself is reported on Form FC, and the sale proceeds or liquidation distribution are expected to be repatriated within the period the Directions allow, subject to the same exceptions for permitted reinvestment. A family that has built up a foreign structure over several years, often with the original filings made by an earlier generation or a different set of advisers, should specifically reconstruct this repatriation history before any exit, since an unreported or unrepatriated balance from years earlier is exactly the kind of gap that surfaces, usually at the least convenient moment, during the due diligence for the eventual sale.

Write-off of an investment that has genuinely failed, where the foreign entity has become commercially unviable and the Indian party wants to recognise the loss rather than keep carrying a dead holding on its books, is also permitted under the 2022 framework in circumstances the earlier regime treated more restrictively. This is a separate filing from an ordinary disinvestment and needs its own supporting record, typically the foreign entity's own financial statements showing the extent of the loss and, where available, an auditor's or liquidator's confirmation, before the authorised dealer bank will process it as a write-off rather than query it as an unreported disinvestment.

Pledges, Guarantees and Funding the Foreign Entity Beyond Equity

Financial commitment under the 2022 Rules is defined broadly enough to catch more than straightforward equity. A loan extended by the Indian party to the foreign entity, and a guarantee given by the Indian party on the foreign entity's behalf, both count toward the same 400% net worth ceiling as an equity contribution would, which means a family business cannot simply fund an overseas subsidiary through intercompany debt or a parent guarantee to sidestep the equity limit; the regulatory ceiling looks at the exposure as a whole.

Guarantees in particular need careful drafting for FEMA purposes, since a guarantee that is open-ended in amount or duration is harder to value for the purpose of the net worth ceiling than one with a defined cap and tenor, and the Directions expect the commitment to be quantified at the time it is undertaken rather than left open for later reconciliation. A family business guaranteeing a foreign subsidiary's local bank facility should have that guarantee's maximum exposure, and its FEMA classification as part of the overall financial commitment, confirmed with the authorised dealer bank before the guarantee is executed, not after the facility has already been drawn.

A guarantee that is invoked, where the foreign lender calls on the Indian party to actually pay because the foreign entity has defaulted, is itself a reportable event, and the amount paid under the guarantee is generally treated as a fresh financial commitment rather than simply an incident of the original guarantee already reported. A family business that has given several guarantees across a foreign group's various operating entities should keep a running tally of its total contingent exposure against the 400% ceiling, not just the exposure under each individual guarantee considered on its own, since the ceiling looks at the Indian party's financial commitment to the foreign entity as a whole.

Deferred Consideration and Other Flexibilities the 2022 Rules Added

One of the more practical improvements the 2022 Rules brought for acquisition-minded family businesses is express allowance for deferred payment of consideration in an overseas acquisition, rather than requiring the entire purchase price to be remitted upfront as the earlier regime effectively assumed. This lets a family business structure an overseas acquisition with an earn-out or a staged payment schedule tied to the target's performance, a structure that is common internationally but had sat awkwardly against the older Indian regulatory framework.

The 2022 framework also eased rules around what happens when a foreign entity itself needs restructuring, write-off of the investment where the foreign entity has genuinely failed commercially, and the pledge of shares of the foreign entity as security for funding, areas where the earlier regulations had been either silent or restrictive enough that most transactions needed specific Reserve Bank approval as a matter of course. None of this removes the underlying classification and reporting obligations described earlier; it simply gives the family's deal team more room to structure the commercial terms of an overseas transaction without that structuring itself becoming a separate regulatory problem.

How This Interacts With Succession Planning for the Family Business

A foreign holding structure built through ODI does not sit outside the family's succession planning; it becomes one more asset class that a will, a family settlement or a private family trust has to address specifically, and often with more friction than a purely domestic asset because the foreign entity's own jurisdiction will usually have its own rules on how shares pass on death, separate from Indian succession law. A family that has already set up a private trust for succession planning, of the kind many HNI families now use, should specifically consider whether the trust is the right vehicle to hold the ODI investment itself, since transferring an existing ODI holding into a trust after the fact raises its own FEMA reporting questions about the change in the registered holder of record.

Where the family's overseas structure includes a step-down subsidiary that itself holds operating assets, succession planning also has to grapple with control, not just ownership, since FEMA's own classification of the investment as ODI depends in part on control being retained at the Indian party level. A succession plan that splits economic ownership among several family members while leaving day-to-day control with one branch of the family needs to be checked against this classification question as carefully as it is checked against the family's own governance preferences.

A Practical Sequence for a First Overseas Structure

The first step is establishing the Indian investing entity's net worth as of its last audited balance sheet and working out, before any term sheet is signed with the foreign target, whether the proposed financial commitment, equity, loan and guarantee taken together, sits inside the 400% automatic-route ceiling or will need specific approval.

The second step is confirming the foreign entity's own activity against the prohibited categories, real estate, gambling and rupee-linked financial products, and, where the activity touches financial services, confirming the Indian entity's three-year profit track record and any regulatory registration or approval that condition requires before the investment is made rather than after.

The third step, relevant wherever the proposed structure involves more than a single foreign holding company sitting above a single operating entity, is testing the structure against the layering limit in Rule 19(3) with the authorised dealer bank in advance, given how unsettled the layer-counting question currently is, rather than assuming a structure that looked acceptable in a law firm's memo on someone else's deal will necessarily be read the same way for this family's own structure.

The fourth step is building the ongoing compliance calendar into the family office's own processes from day one: the Form FC filing at closing, the annual Form APR tied to the foreign entity's audited accounts, and a clear internal owner for making sure the December filing deadline does not slip simply because the family's attention has moved on to the next transaction.

The fifth step, easy to skip in the excitement of closing a first overseas deal, is asking the authorised dealer bank to reconcile the UIN and filing history for any related foreign entity the family group already holds, however old or dormant, before the new structure goes live. A family that discovers an unreported legacy holding only after its new, carefully compliant structure is already in place usually ends up compounding the old contravention under conditions less favourable than if the gap had been found and disclosed proactively at the outset.

What Remains Unsettled

The layer-counting question under Rule 19(3) is the clearest open issue in this area as the law stands today: commentators who have examined the provision closely read it differently, and neither this piece nor the secondary commentary it draws on can state with confidence which of the competing readings the Reserve Bank will apply to a borderline structure, so that question should be put to the authorised dealer bank, and ideally to the Reserve Bank directly through the bank, before a family commits to a structure that depends on the answer. Separately, how the condition against financial commitments designed to avoid tax will actually be tested in practice, beyond the bare wording of the Rule, has not yet settled into a clear administrative pattern that this piece can describe with confidence, and a family should treat any structure whose principal purpose looks like tax avoidance rather than a genuine business reason as a live risk under this condition regardless of how the layering question is eventually resolved.

Overseas Direct Investment under the 2022 Rules gives an Indian family business considerably more room than the regime it replaced to build a genuine overseas presence, hold group companies under a single foreign structure, and fund an acquisition with deferred consideration rather than a single upfront payment. None of that room is unconditional. The 400% net worth ceiling, the prohibited activities, the financial services track record, and the still-unsettled layering limit each have to be checked against the family's specific proposed structure before it is built, not read generally and assumed to fit, because the cost of unwinding a structure that turns out to sit on the wrong side of one of these lines is considerably higher than the cost of confirming it in advance.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Overseas Direct Investment and Overseas Portfolio Investment?

ODI covers any stake in an unlisted foreign company, a 10% or greater stake in a listed foreign company, or any stake that carries control. OPI covers everything else, chiefly a smaller, non-controlling stake in a listed foreign company or a foreign mutual fund, bought without any intention of control.

How much can an Indian company commit to a foreign entity without needing RBI approval?

Up to 400% of the Indian party's net worth, taken as equity, loans and guarantees together, under the automatic route. A commitment above that figure, or portfolio investment above 50% of net worth, needs the Reserve Bank's prior approval.

Can an Indian company invest in a foreign financial services business if it is not itself in financial services?

Yes, since the 2022 Rules, provided it has posted net profits in each of the preceding three financial years and, where it is itself regulated by a financial sector regulator, is appropriately registered and has that regulator's approval for the investment.

Is buying property abroad through an overseas company allowed under ODI?

Not if the foreign entity's business is dealing in real estate or trading transferable development rights, which is prohibited outright. Developing property abroad as an operating business for sale or lease, rather than trading land, falls outside that prohibition.

Can a resident individual make an overseas investment personally rather than through a company?

Yes, but only out of funds remitted under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, which currently caps the remittance at USD 250,000 per financial year, regardless of the size of the underlying opportunity.

What happens if the Annual Performance Report for a foreign subsidiary is filed late?

A late submission fee applies under the schedule introduced alongside the 2022 Rules, and persistent or prolonged delay can mature into a FEMA contravention that needs to be regularised through compounding under Section 15 rather than through the late fee alone.

Does round-tripping, where money goes out and comes back into India through a foreign entity, remain prohibited?

It is no longer prohibited outright. Rule 19(3) of the 2022 Rules permits certain structures where a foreign entity invests back into India under the automatic route, but only within a layering limit that is still read differently by different commentators, and only where the structure is not designed to avoid tax.

Does an ODI investment stop being ODI if the Indian party's stake is later diluted below 10%?

No. Once an investment is classified as ODI, it continues to be treated as ODI for FEMA purposes even if the stake later falls below 10% or control is lost, unless the investment is actually sold or transferred.
