Elderly Parents in India, Children Abroad: Maintenance, Gifts, Power of Attorney and the Family Home

The Senior Citizens Act was not written with the diaspora in mind, yet most of the families actually using it today have at least one child in the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom. Here is where the statute helps, where it stops, and what an NRI child can and cannot do from abroad.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 5 November 2026 · Law stated as on 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An ageing parent living alone in Delhi, Kolkata or a small town in Punjab, and a son or daughter settled in New Jersey, Toronto or Leicester: this is now one of the most common fact patterns behind a Senior Citizens Act complaint, a disputed gift deed, or a frantic call about a caretaker who has stopped answering the phone. The law was drafted for a country where children mostly lived down the street. Families now have to work it from two time zones, and the gaps that creates are rarely the ones anyone expects.

Key points

  • Jurisdiction under the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 follows the senior citizen's own residence, not the respondent's. A parent living in India can bring a claim in the local Tribunal even if every one of their children lives abroad; distance is not a shield against the proceeding itself.
  • The Tribunal can order a monthly allowance, but most States still apply the Act's original ceiling of roughly Rs 10,000 per senior citizen per month under Section 9 read with the State rules, a figure courts themselves have called outdated. A long-pending amendment bill to remove that ceiling has lapsed and has not been re-enacted as of this writing.
  • A gift or transfer a parent made to a child can sometimes be undone under Section 23 of the Act if it was conditional on being looked after and that condition was broken, but the Supreme Court and several High Courts disagree on how explicit that condition has to be in the document itself. This guide does not repeat that procedure in full; our dedicated guide on revoking a gift deed under the Senior Citizens Act covers it, and is linked below.
  • There is no Indian equivalent of the American or British medical power of attorney. A general power of attorney lets someone operate a bank account or sign property papers; it does not let that person consent to a parent's surgery or withdraw life support. The tools that actually exist for that, a nominated representative under the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, and an advance medical directive recognised since Common Cause v. Union of India, work differently and have to be set up separately.
  • A power of attorney signed abroad for use in India needs to be executed in a form Indian authorities will accept before it is sent home: typically notarisation followed by either an apostille, for the many countries that are now parties to the Hague Convention, or attestation by the Indian consulate, followed by stamping in India within the time the State's stamp law allows.
  • Whether a lawyer can even appear before the Maintenance Tribunal on a family's behalf is itself contested. Section 17 of the Act purports to bar legal representation before the Tribunal, but the Delhi and Punjab and Haryana High Courts have read that bar away, while the Karnataka High Court has gone further and declared the provision unconstitutional. The position can genuinely differ depending on where the Tribunal sits.
  • Protecting an elderly parent's home from an overstaying tenant, a dishonest caretaker, or outright encroachment is mostly a matter of police registration, documentation and the State rules framed under Section 32 of the Act, not a single dramatic lawsuit. The groundwork is far easier to do before a problem starts than after.

A statute written for one country, now used by two

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 assumes a fairly simple world: a parent in difficulty, an adult child nearby with the means to help, and a local tribunal that can order that child to pay. Nothing in the Act's text contemplates the respondent being nine and a half hours away in London or twelve and a half hours away in San Francisco. In practice, though, a large share of the calls that come into a firm handling this area now involve exactly that fact pattern. One child stayed back, or came back; the others built careers in the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom; and the parent's day-to-day welfare, the gift deed signed a decade ago, the caretaker hired after a fall, the tenant who moved into an empty floor, all now have to be managed with at least one decision-maker on a different continent.

This guide works through that fact pattern specifically: what the Act still does for a family split this way, where the ordinary advice given to any senior citizen stops being enough, and what a child living abroad can and cannot actually do. It deliberately does not repeat the mechanics of revoking a gift deed, which we cover in full in a separate guide linked below, and it treats the Act's criminal and civil remedies as tools to reach for in a particular order, not as a single undifferentiated remedy.

What the Act covers, and who it covers

A "senior citizen" under the Act is a person of sixty years or above, Indian citizen or not. "Parent" covers a biological, adoptive or step-parent, whether or not they are themselves a senior citizen, which matters where a parent in their late fifties is cared for by an adult child. "Children" means a son, daughter, grandson or granddaughter, but excludes a minor. "Maintenance" is defined broadly enough to include food, clothing, residence and medical attendance and treatment, which is why Tribunal orders routinely go beyond a bare cash figure and direct a child to arrange specific care.

The obligation to maintain a parent or senior citizen falls first on children and grandchildren with sufficient means. Where a senior citizen has no children, the Act shifts that obligation onto a "relative", defined narrowly as a legal heir, not a minor, who is in possession of the senior citizen's property or stands to inherit it; a Kerala High Court division bench has held that mere possession of the property, without the person also being a legal heir, is not enough to fix liability under this route. Where more than one relative would inherit, the burden is apportioned in the ratio of their expected shares.

An application for maintenance is made to the Tribunal constituted under the Act, chaired by the Additional District Magistrate or the Sub-Divisional Magistrate of the area, sitting with two other members, one of whom must be a woman. A senior citizen who is unable to apply personally can have the application made on their behalf by any person or registered organisation authorised by them, and the Tribunal itself can act on its own motion in an appropriate case, which matters where a parent is too frail, or too reluctant within the family, to formally initiate the complaint themselves.

Jurisdiction does not move just because the respondent did

The single most useful fact for a family split across countries is also the one that gets misunderstood most often: under Section 6 of the Act, a maintenance proceeding can be brought in the district where the senior citizen resides or last resided, or in the district where the child or relative resides. That is a choice given to the applicant, not a requirement that the case follow the respondent. A parent living out their life in Kolkata or Ludhiana does not have to, and generally should not try to, pursue a claim in a foreign jurisdiction simply because the children who should be supporting them now live there. The claim is brought exactly where it would have been brought if everyone still lived in the same city.

What this means practically is that a child's relocation abroad does not, by itself, defeat the Tribunal's jurisdiction or delay the proceeding on a jurisdictional technicality. It does, however, concentrate almost all of the real difficulty into a different stage of the process: getting the notice to the respondent, and then enforcing whatever order the Tribunal eventually passes. Those two problems are worth treating separately, because the law is considerably weaker on the second than on the first.

It is also worth being realistic about scope. Not every strained relationship between a parent and an adult child qualifies. The Bombay High Court has observed that every conflict between a senior citizen and their offspring does not automatically attract the Act's jurisdiction; a 2025 Patna High Court ruling similarly held that a Tribunal has no jurisdiction over a complaint that neither seeks maintenance nor challenges a property transfer. The Act exists to secure food, shelter, care and the integrity of a parent's property against a child or relative with the means to help and the duty to do so, not to resolve every family grievance that happens to involve a senior citizen.

Serving notice abroad, ex parte orders, and appearing by video

Once a claim is filed against a child who lives abroad, the practical question becomes how the Tribunal actually gets notice to them. The Act and the State rules made under it provide for service, including through authorities the Central Government may notify for this purpose, but this is genuinely one of the less developed corners of the statute, and secondary commentary on how reliably it functions is thin. In practice, families and Tribunals often fall back on service by registered post or courier to the known foreign address, combined with service on any local address or local relative the respondent maintains, and on proof that the respondent was otherwise made aware of the proceeding, for example through a relative, a lawyer, or prior correspondence.

If a respondent does not appear despite service, the Tribunal is not powerless; like most tribunals of this kind, it can proceed and pass an order in the respondent's absence. An ex parte order of this kind is vulnerable to being set aside later if the respondent can show that service was genuinely defective, which is precisely why the quality of the service record, courier tracking, delivery confirmation, any acknowledgment, matters far more in an NRI case than in a purely local one. A family pursuing a claim against an overseas child should keep that paper trail meticulously; a family defending one should not assume that simply being abroad will excuse a failure to respond once proper service is shown.

On the other side of this, an NRI respondent who does want to participate, rather than default, generally has two practical options: appearing by video conferencing where the Tribunal permits it, a practice that became far more common after 2020 and has generally continued since, or authorising someone in India to appear on their behalf. That second option runs straight into the question of legal representation discussed next, because who exactly can stand up in the room matters as much as who is willing to.

Can a lawyer, or a power of attorney holder, actually appear?

Section 17 of the Act states, notwithstanding anything in any other law, that parties before the Tribunal or the Appellate Tribunal are not entitled to be represented by a legal practitioner. Read literally, that would mean an NRI child cannot simply instruct an advocate in India to appear on their behalf while they stay abroad, and would have to appear in person or by video conferencing in every case.

That literal reading has not survived intact. The Delhi High Court, agreeing with an earlier view of the Punjab and Haryana High Court, held that Section 17 does not bar legal representation before the Maintenance Tribunal, reasoning that the "notwithstanding any other law" language refers to law currently in force and does not override a party's ordinary right to be represented by counsel. The Karnataka High Court went further still and declared Section 17 unconstitutional and ultra vires, on the basis that barring representation in proceedings that can affect a person's property and maintenance liability amounts to a denial of a fair hearing. Other courts have taken a narrower view permitting representation only in limited circumstances. The result is that whether your lawyer can appear for you, or whether you will need to appear personally or by video link, can genuinely depend on which State's Tribunal is hearing the case, and this is worth confirming locally before assuming either answer.

Separately from legal representation, a general power of attorney can be used to authorise a relative or trusted representative in India to handle the practical, non-advocacy side of a case, filing documents, collecting orders, coordinating with counsel where representation is permitted, without that necessarily counting as the barred "representation" itself. This is a narrower and more reliable use of a power of attorney than treating it as a substitute for appearing in the proceeding.

What happens if the order is simply ignored from abroad

A Tribunal's maintenance order is enforceable within India largely as other such orders are, through the machinery available to the Tribunal and, on default, through penal consequences built into the Act itself. Separately, Section 24 makes it a criminal offence for a person who has the care or protection of a senior citizen to leave them in a place with the intention of wholly abandoning them, punishable with imprisonment of up to three months, a fine of up to Rs 5,000, or both. Courts have read "abandonment" strictly: the Kerala High Court has held that the offence requires a total neglect, leaving the senior citizen without any arrangement for their care, not merely a strained relationship or reduced contact. Simply living abroad and sending less money than a parent would like does not, by itself, make out this offence.

The honest difficulty is enforcing an Indian order or an Indian criminal complaint against a person and their assets that are entirely outside India. Commentary on this point is candid that it is a genuinely weak link in the statute: there is no developed reciprocal enforcement mechanism between India and the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom specifically for Senior Citizens Act orders, and recovery against a non-resident child who simply refuses to pay will usually have to proceed against whatever assets or presence that child retains in India, if any, rather than against income or property abroad. Families should set expectations accordingly: the Act is considerably more effective as a tool to secure cooperation from a child who still has ties, family, property, visits, to India than as a tool to compel payment from someone who has none.

This is also why, in practice, many of these disputes are resolved not through a contested order but through the Tribunal's conciliation function, or through direct negotiation once a formal notice makes clear that a complaint has been filed. An NRI child who intends to meet their obligations is usually better served by engaging promptly, even remotely, than by assuming distance makes the proceeding irrelevant to them.

Revoking gifts and transfers made to a child: the short version

Where a parent transferred a house or other property to a child, by gift or otherwise, on the understanding that the child would look after them, and that understanding has broken down, Section 23 of the Act allows the Tribunal to declare the transfer void if it finds the transfer was made subject to the condition that the transferee would provide basic amenities and physical needs, and the transferee has failed to do so. The Supreme Court's decision in Sudesh Chhikara v. Ramti Devi treated an express condition of this kind as essential, and a later decision, Urmila Dixit v. Sunil Sharan Dixit, is reported to have taken a more purposive view of how that condition can be shown; High Courts remain divided on how explicit the document itself needs to be, with the Karnataka High Court holding that a gift deed to a close relative need not contain an express recital, and other courts, including a 2026 Chhattisgarh High Court decision, going further in the same direction, against a competing Kerala line that insists on an express written condition.

We have deliberately not repeated that procedure here in full; our dedicated guide on revoking a gift deed under the Senior Citizens Act, linked below, walks through the Section 23 application itself, what it takes to show the condition was broken, and how this interacts with a separate civil suit to cancel the deed under the Transfer of Property Act. What is worth flagging for an NRI family specifically is only this: a Tribunal exercising this power is not, on the weight of current authority, entitled to go further and generally re-open the fairness of a gift simply because it was made to one child and not another, or to adjudicate a broader family dispute dressed up as a Section 23 application. The remedy is tied tightly to the maintenance condition and its breach, and a petition that strays from that will usually fail regardless of how it is framed.

Caretaker and tenant verification for a parent living alone

A disproportionate share of the genuinely frightening calls in this area, a caretaker who has started controlling a parent's movements, a long-term tenant who has stopped paying and now refuses to leave, begin with a hire or a letting that was never properly verified in the first place. The groundwork here is unglamorous but effective. Domestic help and live-in caretakers should be engaged through a registered placement agency wherever one is available, with the agency's own verification on file, and separately registered with the local police station, a facility most metropolitan police forces now offer online as well as in person. The same applies to a tenant: police verification of a tenant is not uniformly mandated by central law, the Model Tenancy Act, 2021 itself does not make it compulsory, but a number of States and city police commissionerates require or strongly expect it as a matter of local practice, and it remains one of the cheapest pieces of insurance a family can buy before, rather than after, handing someone the keys to a parent's home.

For a family coordinating this from abroad, the practical sequence that works best is: do the verification and sign the registered rent agreement or the caretaker engagement letter before the NRI child leaves India on a visit, or have a trusted local relative or an advocate complete it under a specific power of attorney limited to that purpose; keep copies of the verification receipt, the agreement, and the caretaker or tenant's identity documents with at least two people, one in India and one abroad; and set a fixed, calendared point, not an open-ended "check in sometime", at which someone physically visits the parent and the property. Problems with caretakers and tenants are rarely sudden; they are usually visible for months before a family abroad notices, precisely because no one was checking on a schedule.

Power of attorney from abroad: what it does and does not reach

A power of attorney signed by an NRI child is the single most useful document for managing a parent's affairs in India from a distance, and also the one most often assumed to do more than it actually does. A properly executed power of attorney can authorise a relative, or the parent themselves if they are able, to operate bank accounts, pay bills and taxes, deal with a tenant, file or defend proceedings before authorities that permit representation, and sign documents relating to property on the NRI's behalf. It does not, by itself, give the holder any authority over medical treatment decisions for the parent; that is a separate legal problem covered in the next section.

To be usable in India, a power of attorney signed in the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom should generally be executed before a notary public in that country and then authenticated for use in India. For a country that is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, which includes all three, an apostille obtained from the relevant designated authority is generally accepted by Indian authorities without further legalisation by an Indian mission; where any doubt exists, or where a particular registering or banking authority in India insists on it, attestation by the Indian consulate or embassy in that country, in addition to or instead of an apostille, remains the safer route, and consulates in the US, Canada and the UK offer this attestation service directly. Once the document reaches India, it should be stamped under the applicable State's stamp law, generally within three months of its receipt in India, a deadline that is frequently missed because families treat the apostille or attestation as the final step rather than the second-to-last one.

Registration of a power of attorney relating to immovable property executed outside India is a genuinely unsettled point; some practitioners treat it as compulsorily registrable once it reaches India, while a clarification attributed to the Inspector General of Registration in at least one State has been read as saying registration is not compulsory for a power of attorney executed outside India in this context. Given that disagreement, the safer and more common practice, particularly where the power of attorney will be used to sell or otherwise deal with property, is to have it adjudicated and stamped promptly and, out of caution, registered in India as well, rather than relying on an exemption that is not uniformly accepted across registering authorities. We cover the mechanics of executing and using a power of attorney from abroad, including for property transactions and court appearances generally, in a separate guide linked below; this section focuses only on what is distinctive about using one specifically in support of an elderly parent's day-to-day welfare.

Healthcare decisions from abroad: the gap an ordinary power of attorney cannot fill

This is the point at which the American or British framework an NRI child may be used to, a healthcare power of attorney or a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare, simply does not have an Indian equivalent, and assuming otherwise is a genuine source of crisis during a medical emergency. An ordinary power of attorney executed for property and financial matters gives the holder no standing to consent to or refuse treatment, surgery, or end-of-life care on a parent's behalf; Indian hospitals generally look to the patient themselves if competent, or to the next available family member physically present, rather than to a named attorney-in-fact for this purpose.

Two distinct legal tools exist instead, and they serve different situations. First, for a person facing a mental illness specifically, the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 allows any adult to execute a written advance directive under Section 5 setting out how they wish to be treated, and separately, under Section 14, to appoint a nominated representative, who need not be named in the same document, to make certain decisions on their behalf if they later become unable to. If no nominated representative has been appointed and no advance directive exists, Section 14 sets out a fallback order of who is treated as the representative, generally a willing relative before a caregiver, though at least one High Court has noted the Act gives incomplete guidance on exactly how this fallback should work in a contested family. Second, and more broadly, following the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in Common Cause v. Union of India, and the simplified procedure the Court laid down in a 2023 order revisiting its own 2018 guidelines, a competent adult can execute an advance medical directive, commonly called a living will, that sets out their wishes on withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment in a terminal or vegetative condition, to be given effect through a defined medical board process rather than through an attorney-in-fact's say-so.

For an NRI family, the practical takeaway is to treat these as separate instruments from the property power of attorney, to have a parent execute them, while competent and willing, specifically and in the form the current guidelines require, and not to assume that the person named as power of attorney holder for banking and property purposes automatically has any voice in a hospital corridor. Where no advance directive or nominated representative has been set up in advance, the family member physically present in India at the time of a medical crisis will generally be the one hospitals turn to as a practical matter, regardless of what a property power of attorney says, which is itself a strong reason to have at least one adult child or close relative based in India, or to make firm advance arrangements, rather than leaving this entirely to be worked out in the moment.

Protecting the parents' home from encroachment

Section 32 of the Act empowers State Governments to frame rules for the protection of life and property of senior citizens, and several States have used this to direct the local police to take specific steps where a senior citizen's safety or property is at risk, including acting on complaints of encroachment or illegal occupation. In practice, the most useful and most underused step available to a family is simply registering the parent, and the property, with the local police station's senior citizen cell; most metropolitan commissionerates now run one, and registration typically brings periodic welfare check-ins by local police, which is itself a meaningful deterrent against both opportunistic encroachment and caretaker abuse. The national Elder Line helpline, 14567, operating across most of the day, every day, is a further, non-emergency channel for guidance and intervention, though it is not a substitute for the police or for court process where either becomes necessary, and for a genuine emergency the ordinary police emergency number remains the first call.

Beyond registration, the documentation that actually prevents an encroachment dispute from becoming a prolonged litigation is unglamorous: the original title documents and the latest property tax receipt kept somewhere safe and known to more than one family member, the mutation and revenue records updated in the parent's name and kept current, photographs and, where there is any realistic risk, a periodic professional survey of boundaries for an independent house or agricultural land, and a single point of contact, ideally someone local rather than only the NRI child, who visits the property on a fixed schedule rather than only when a problem is reported. Where a relative, tenant or outsider has already moved onto or taken over part of a parent's property, the remedies available, and their real limits, including why a civil suit for possession is usually unavoidable once occupation has hardened into a real dispute, are covered in detail in our separate guide on that subject, linked below; this section is about the preventive steps that make reaching for that guide less likely to be necessary.

What the NRI child can, and cannot, do from where they are

Pulled together, the answer to "what can I actually do from the US, Canada or the UK" looks like this. An NRI child can be the applicant who sets a parent's maintenance proceeding in motion, through a properly authorised representative, or support one as a respondent, by engaging promptly, appearing by video conferencing where permitted, and instructing counsel, subject to the Section 17 uncertainty discussed above. They can execute a power of attorney, properly apostilled or consular-attested and later stamped in India, to manage banking, bills, tenancy and property matters. They can, and should, set up an advance medical directive or a Mental Healthcare Act nominated representative for a parent while that parent is competent and willing, rather than discovering in a hospital waiting room that no such arrangement exists. They can register a parent and their property with the local police senior citizen cell, and can fund, from abroad, the practical steps, a registered caretaker, a verified tenant, a periodic property survey, that actually prevent most of the disputes this guide discusses.

What an NRI child cannot do is rely on physical distance to avoid the jurisdiction of an Indian Tribunal, which attaches to where the parent lives, not where the respondent does; cannot treat a general power of attorney as authority to make medical decisions; cannot assume a gift deed is safe from challenge merely because it has stood unchallenged for years, Section 23 has no limitation period tied to the age of the deed itself, only to the conduct that allegedly breached its condition; and cannot assume that an order obtained against them in India will be straightforwardly unenforceable simply because they have no presence there, since most of these families do retain some property, bank balance or periodic visit that an enforcement process can reach, even where a full cross-border enforcement mechanism does not exist.

A working checklist for families split across borders

Set up, while the parent is competent and willing, three separate documents rather than one: a power of attorney for property and financial matters, properly apostilled or consular-attested if signed abroad; a Mental Healthcare Act advance directive and nominated representative appointment, or at minimum a clear written statement of the parent's wishes shared with the family; and, where relevant to the parent's wishes, an advance medical directive on life-sustaining treatment in the form the current Supreme Court guidelines require.

Register the parent and the property with the local police senior citizen cell, and keep the registration number and the nearest station's contact details with every adult child, not only the one in India. Verify any caretaker through a registered agency and separately with the police; verify and formally document any tenancy, including police verification where local practice expects it, before, not after, letting someone into the property.

Keep the parent's title documents, tax receipts and identity documents both physically secure and digitally backed up and accessible to at least two family members in different locations. Fix a real, calendared schedule for someone to visit the parent and the property in person, and treat any gap in that schedule as the trigger to check in, rather than waiting for a problem to surface on its own.

If a dispute over maintenance, a gift, or occupation of the property does arise, engage an advocate promptly rather than attempting to manage the Tribunal process entirely by remote correspondence, confirm at the outset whether that Tribunal's practice permits legal representation or requires personal or video appearance, and treat the first notice received, whether as applicant or respondent, as the point to get documentation in order, not the point to start assembling it.

None of the individual pieces of this area of law are unusually complicated on their own. What makes it difficult for families split between India and the diaspora is that the pieces that matter most, who the Tribunal can actually reach, what a power of attorney can and cannot authorise, who gets to make a medical decision when no one has planned for it, sit in different statutes with different assumptions, and the gaps between them only become visible at the worst possible moment: a parent in a hospital bed, a caretaker who has stopped cooperating, a sibling who has gone silent after years of informal arrangements.

The practical answer is almost always the same, done early rather than reconstructed later: proper documents, properly authenticated if signed abroad; a clear, written understanding within the family of who decides what; and routine, scheduled attention to a parent's welfare and property rather than attention that arrives only once something has already gone wrong. The Senior Citizens Act, the Mental Healthcare Act's nominated representative, and an advance medical directive are all genuinely useful tools. They work best when a family treats them as ordinary estate and welfare planning, set up calmly, rather than as remedies reached for only in crisis.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my parents file a maintenance case against me even though I live abroad and send money every month?

Yes, the proceeding can be filed in the district where your parent lives, which is unaffected by where you live, and sending money does not by itself prevent a Tribunal from examining whether the maintenance actually being provided is adequate. In practice, if you are genuinely meeting your parent's needs, this is usually resolved through the Tribunal's conciliation process or direct correspondence once a notice is served, rather than through a contested order, but you should not assume the case will be dismissed purely because you are remitting funds.

Is there a cap on how much maintenance a Tribunal can order?

Most States still apply a ceiling in the region of Rs 10,000 per senior citizen per month under the Act and the State's own rules, a figure several High Courts have publicly criticised as outdated given inflation. A central amendment bill that would have removed this ceiling lapsed with the dissolution of an earlier Lok Sabha and has not been re-enacted, so this limit remains the working figure in most States as on today's date; confirm the current position under the specific State's rules before relying on any higher figure.

Can I revoke a gift deed I signed years ago in my child's favour if they later stop looking after me?

Potentially, under Section 23 of the Senior Citizens Act, if the transfer was made on condition that the transferee would provide basic amenities and physical needs and that condition has been broken, though courts disagree on exactly how explicit that condition needs to be in the document itself. The age of the deed is not, by itself, a bar; what matters is the condition and its breach. We cover the actual procedure for this in a separate, dedicated guide, which this article deliberately does not repeat.

Can a lawyer represent me before the Maintenance Tribunal if I cannot travel to India?

It depends where the Tribunal sits. The Delhi and Punjab and Haryana High Courts have held that Section 17 of the Act does not bar legal representation before the Tribunal, and the Karnataka High Court has gone further and struck the provision down as unconstitutional, but other courts have taken a narrower view. Confirm the current position in the relevant State before assuming your lawyer can simply appear in your place, and have a fallback plan to appear by video conferencing if representation turns out not to be permitted.

Can I give my sibling in India a power of attorney to make medical decisions for our parents?

Not in the way a healthcare power of attorney works in the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom. An ordinary power of attorney does not authorise anyone to consent to or refuse medical treatment on your parent's behalf. The closer Indian equivalents are a nominated representative appointed under the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, and, for decisions on life-sustaining treatment specifically, an advance medical directive executed under the Supreme Court's living-will guidelines. These need to be set up as their own documents, not folded into a property power of attorney.

Does a power of attorney I sign in the US, Canada or the UK need to be attested by the Indian consulate?

Not necessarily, if the country is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, which the US, Canada and the UK all are; an apostille from the designated authority in that country is generally accepted in India without further consular legalisation. Even so, many families obtain consular attestation as well where there is any doubt about how a particular Indian bank or registering authority will treat the document, and the document still needs to be stamped in India, generally within three months of its arrival, regardless of which authentication route was used.

What can I do if a tenant or caretaker in my parents' house refuses to leave or starts controlling their affairs?

Start with the local police senior citizen cell and, if the person is a tenant, treat it as an occupation dispute requiring the usual tenancy remedies for your State rather than assuming the Senior Citizens Act will order an eviction; several High Courts, including Calcutta, have held that the Tribunal can secure maintenance but cannot itself order eviction from property. Where the situation has already hardened into a dispute over possession, the remedies, and their real limits, are covered in detail in our separate guide on relatives, tenants and encroachers occupying an NRI's property.

What happens if my sibling abroad simply ignores a maintenance order against them?

Non-payment can lead to enforcement and, separately, abandonment under Section 24 is a criminal offence carrying up to three months' imprisonment or a fine of up to Rs 5,000, though courts read "abandonment" strictly and require genuine, total neglect rather than mere non-payment or reduced contact. The realistic limitation is that enforcing an Indian order against a person and assets entirely outside India is difficult, since there is no developed reciprocal enforcement mechanism for these orders with the US, Canada or the UK; recovery in practice usually has to look to whatever presence, property or periodic visits that sibling still has in India.
