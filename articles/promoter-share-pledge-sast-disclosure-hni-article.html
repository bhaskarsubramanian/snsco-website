Pledging Promoter Shares: SEBI's Disclosure Rules and What a Pledge Actually Risks

A promoter family that pledges listed shares to raise money takes on a disclosure duty that runs on a seven-day clock, and a February 2026 SEBI circular has just tightened what happens when that pledge is invoked

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 26 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Pledging shares is one of the fastest ways for a promoter family to raise money against a listed company's stock without selling it. It is also one of the more closely watched transactions under Indian securities law, carrying its own disclosure timeline, its own additional duty once the pledge grows large, and, after a February 2026 SEBI circular, a new notice requirement before a lender can actually sell what was pledged.

Key points

  • Under Regulation 31 of the SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations, 2011, a promoter who pledges or otherwise encumbers shares, and any person acting in concert with the promoter, must disclose the encumbrance, its invocation and its release, each within seven working days of the event.
  • A 2019 SEBI circular added a further disclosure layer: once the combined encumbrance of the promoter group reaches 50% of their own shareholding in the company, or 20% of the company's total share capital, the promoter must disclose the detailed reasons for the encumbrance, not merely its existence.
  • The disclosure obligation is read strictly. SEBI's enforcement record treats the seven-working-day clock as running from the date the encumbrance, invocation or release actually occurs, with no allowance for when the promoter claims to have learned of it.
  • The Supreme Court's 2022 ruling in PTC India Financial Services Ltd v. Venkateswarlu Kari clarified that registering a pledgee as a depository 'beneficial owner' does not itself transfer title in the pledged shares, and directed SEBI to re-examine how the takeover and insider trading regulations interact with a pledge.
  • A SEBI circular dated 5 February 2026 revised the depository-level framework for creating and invoking a pledge, requiring pledge request forms to carry specific undertakings and, per market commentary, a reasonable prior notice to the pledgor before a lender invokes or sells pledged securities.
  • For a promoter family, a disclosed pledge is read by the market as a signal of liquidity stress on the company's own stock, often moving the price before the money is even drawn down, which makes the structuring and timing of a pledge a reputational decision as much as a financing one.
  • An encumbrance is not confined to a formal pledge. SEBI has penalised promoters for failing to disclose other arrangements, such as sale restrictions in a debenture trust deed, that function as an encumbrance even without using that word.

Why a share pledge is not a private matter between promoter and lender

A promoter family that needs liquidity, whether to fund a new venture, meet a tax demand, or simply avoid selling down a stake in the family's own listed company, will frequently turn to a loan against shares rather than an outright sale. The shares stay with the family in every sense that matters to control and dividend entitlement, while the lender holds a pledge as security that can be invoked if the loan is not repaid or if the share price falls below a maintenance level set in the loan documents.

What makes this different from an ordinary personal loan is that the shares being pledged belong to a listed company, and the promoter pledging them is, almost by definition, someone whose shareholding and conduct the market watches closely. SEBI's regulatory architecture responds to that fact with a disclosure regime that treats the creation, invocation and release of a pledge as market-sensitive information in its own right, independent of whether the underlying loan itself is ever disclosed.

The origin of this regime is instructive. The requirement traces back to the discovery, after the Satyam Computer Services collapse, that a promoter's shares had been pledged extensively without shareholders or the market being told, leaving investors unaware of exactly how exposed the promoter's holding, and by extension the company's perceived stability, actually was. Every subsequent tightening of the disclosure rules has been aimed at preventing a repeat of that specific blind spot.

Two decades on, the underlying economic motivation for a promoter to pledge shares has not changed, even as the regulatory response around it has grown considerably more detailed. Promoters still pledge shares to fund personal needs, to inject capital into a related but separately held business, or to bridge a short-term cash requirement without triggering a taxable sale of the underlying shares. What has changed is how much the market, and the regulator, now expect to be told about that decision as it happens, rather than discovering it only once a default forces the pledge into the open through an invocation.

Regulation 31: the basic disclosure obligation

Regulation 31 of the SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations, 2011 is the operative provision. It requires the promoter of every listed target company, together with persons acting in concert with the promoter, to disclose details of shares encumbered by them, in the form specified by SEBI, within seven working days of creating the encumbrance. The same seven-working-day clock applies separately to invoking the pledge and to releasing it, so a single pledge transaction can generate up to three distinct disclosure events over its life, each with its own independent timeline.

The term encumbrance is defined broadly under the regulations to cover a pledge, lien, negative lien, non-disposal undertaking, and any other arrangement restraining or restricting the free and marketable title to the shares. This breadth matters because promoters sometimes assume that only a formal pledge deed registered with a depository counts. SEBI's enforcement history does not support that narrow reading. An adjudication order discussed in regulatory commentary treated restrictions on share sales contained in a debenture trust deed as an encumbrance in substance, triggering the same disclosure duty even though the word pledge never appeared in that document.

Annual confirmation is a further, separate layer under Regulation 31(4): promoters of listed companies must declare, once a year, that they and persons acting in concert have not created any encumbrance over their shares other than what has already been disclosed during that financial year. This converts the disclosure obligation from a purely event-driven duty into something closer to an ongoing certification, and a promoter who has simply forgotten to disclose an older encumbrance usually discovers the gap precisely when this annual declaration comes due.

The strict reading of the seven-day clock

SEBI's adjudication orders on this provision leave little room for a promoter to argue that the clock should run from when they became aware of the encumbrance, invocation or release, rather than from the date the event itself occurred. One adjudication order, summarised in regulatory commentary, held that the obligation runs strictly from the date of creation, invocation or release of the encumbrance, and treats a knowledge-based or intimation-based defence as having no application to this particular timeline.

This strictness has a practical consequence for how pledges should be structured from the outset. A promoter who pledges shares through an intermediary, a family office, or a holding entity that is itself a person acting in concert, needs a contractual mechanism that notifies the promoter of exactly when the pledge, and any later invocation, is actually created in the depository system, rather than relying on being told informally after the fact. Where that internal notification chain is weak, the seven-working-day window can expire before the promoter even learns there is anything to disclose, and SEBI's enforcement approach does not treat that organisational gap as an excuse.

The consequence of a missed or late disclosure is not limited to a notional default. SEBI has the power to levy monetary penalties under Section 15A of the SEBI Act for violations of the SAST Regulations, and persistent or large non-disclosures have, in past enforcement actions against listed company promoters, attracted penalties running into substantial sums, calculated with reference to the gravity and duration of the non-disclosure rather than as a fixed, nominal figure.

When the pledge grows large: the 2019 reasons-for-encumbrance disclosure

A SEBI circular dated 7 August 2019 added a disclosure obligation that goes beyond simply confirming that an encumbrance exists. Once the combined encumbrance created by the promoter, together with persons acting in concert, equals or exceeds 50% of their own shareholding in the company, or 20% of the company's total share capital, whichever threshold is reached first, the promoter must also disclose detailed reasons for the encumbrance. This disclosure sits on top of, rather than in place of, the basic Regulation 31 disclosure, and it continues each time the already-breaching level of encumbrance increases further from where it previously stood.

The circular took effect from 1 October 2019, and promoters who had already crossed the threshold as of 30 September 2019 were required to make their first reasons-based disclosure within days of that effective date rather than waiting for the next encumbrance event to trigger it. The listed company itself has a corresponding duty to put the contents of that disclosure on its own website within two working days of receiving it from the promoter, so the information reaches the market through two separate channels, the stock exchange filing and the company's own site.

The policy logic behind the 50%-or-20% threshold is straightforward once stated: a modest pledge covering a small fraction of a promoter's holding tells the market little about the promoter's overall financial position, but a pledge that covers half or more of what the promoter owns, or a fifth of the entire company, is a meaningfully different signal about how much of the family's wealth and control is standing behind a lender's recovery rights. SEBI chose to make the market ask why, rather than leaving the size of the pledge to speak for itself.

PTC India Financial Services v Venkateswarlu Kari: what a pledge is not

A pledge of dematerialised shares creates a distinct legal question that the Supreme Court addressed directly in PTC India Financial Services Ltd v. Venkateswarlu Kari, decided on 12 May 2022. The dispute arose from an insolvency context, where the question was whether a lender who had been registered as the depository's recorded beneficial owner of pledged shares, following invocation of the pledge, had thereby become the owner of those shares in a way that affected the insolvency proceedings of the entity whose shares had been pledged.

The Court held that registration as a beneficial owner under the Depositories Act, 1996 does not, by itself, amount to a transfer of title. Beneficial ownership in the limited sense the Depositories Act uses the term, to identify who is entitled to deal with a dematerialised security through the depository system, is not the same as beneficial ownership in the general legal sense of actually owning the underlying asset. A pledgee who invokes a pledge and gets itself registered as beneficial owner in the depository records has taken a step toward enforcing its security, not completed a sale or transfer of the shares to itself.

The significance of the ruling for promoters and lenders goes beyond the specific insolvency dispute it resolved. The Court directed SEBI to re-examine the interplay between the Depositories Act and the Takeover Regulations in light of this clarification, and separately observed that the takeover regulations, as they stood, could operate as a detriment to a lender trying to enforce a pledge, since invocation and registration as beneficial owner does not, without more, give the lender the clean, immediate ownership a conventional sale would. Commentary following the ruling also flagged that SEBI's insider trading framework, which had previously treated a pledge as falling within the definition of a trade for certain purposes, needed to be revisited in light of the same distinction between depository registration and actual title.

The February 2026 circular: new protection before a pledge is sold

SEBI issued a circular on 5 February 2026, addressed to all depositories, titled Creation/Invocation of pledge of securities through depository system, revising paragraph 4.13 of its Master Circular for Depositories dated 3 December 2024. The circular requires both depositories, NSDL and CDSL, to adopt a common pledge request form carrying specific undertakings by the parties to abide by the Indian Contract Act, 1872, the Depositories Act, 1996, SEBI's regulations, circulars and depository bye-laws.

The operative change market commentary has focused on is the notice requirement before a pledge is invoked or the pledged securities sold. Where earlier practice allowed a pledgee to invoke a pledge and move fairly quickly to recovery once a default occurred, the revised framework requires a reasonable prior notice to the pledgor before invocation or sale proceeds, with both the pledgor and the pledgee to be separately informed by the depository participant once an invocation does take place. Depositories were required to implement the revised framework by 6 April 2026, and NSDL's own operating circular dated 20 March 2026 directed participants to obtain a signed client undertaking for both pledge creation and invocation requests from that date onward.

For a promoter family, this change shifts the practical balance of a loan-against-shares arrangement modestly but meaningfully in the pledgor's favour. A lender can no longer treat invocation as an administrative formality completed the moment a maintenance-margin breach or payment default occurs. There is now a notice step built into the depository-level process itself, separate from whatever notice provisions the loan agreement between promoter and lender already contains, and a promoter family negotiating a pledge arrangement should check that the loan documentation does not purport to contract around this depository-level protection in a way that conflicts with it.

Reading a pledge disclosure as an outsider: what the market actually sees

A disclosed pledge reaches the market as a bare stock exchange filing, usually without the commercial reason behind it unless the 50%-or-20% threshold under the 2019 circular has been crossed. Analysts and institutional shareholders nonetheless treat even a modest, newly disclosed promoter pledge as a signal worth investigating, because the most common reasons promoters pledge shares, funding a related business, meeting a personal liability, or covering a shortfall elsewhere in the group, are each, in their own way, informative about the promoter's broader financial position even when the company's own balance sheet looks unaffected.

This market reaction is why promoter families with a listed entity increasingly try to structure financing needs to avoid a share pledge altogether where a comparable facility is available against other collateral, reserving the pledge route for situations where no other security is practically on offer. Where a pledge is unavoidable, the reasons disclosure under the 2019 circular, once the threshold is crossed, becomes an opportunity rather than only a burden: a clearly and specifically worded reasons disclosure, prepared in advance rather than drafted hurriedly once the threshold is reached, tends to be read far more calmly by the market than a bare, unexplained filing that leaves analysts to speculate.

A separate signal, easy to overlook, is the release disclosure, which gets comparatively less market attention than the original pledge but matters just as much to a careful reader of a promoter's filings. A pattern of pledges that are created and quickly released, cycle after cycle, often points to short-term bridge financing rather than structural stress, while a pledge that persists and grows over successive quarters without release tells a different story, and the two patterns should not be read the same way simply because both involve the same word, pledge, in the filing.

Structuring a pledge to manage disclosure risk, not avoid it

None of the disclosure framework described here is designed to be avoided, and attempting to structure around it generally creates a worse outcome than simply complying with it on time. Arrangements that dress an encumbrance up as something else, whether through a non-disposal undertaking that is not labelled a pledge, or through a holding structure that tries to keep the encumbrance one step removed from the named promoter, have been treated by SEBI as encumbrances in substance regardless of their form, and the attempt at disguise itself tends to be treated as an aggravating factor once discovered rather than a mitigating one.

What a promoter family can properly manage is the timing and sequencing of disclosure, not its occurrence. Building an internal compliance checkpoint, so that the person responsible for SEBI filings is notified automatically and immediately when any family entity creates, invokes or releases an encumbrance anywhere in the group, closes the most common gap that leads to a late or missed disclosure. This is particularly important where the pledge is created by a family trust, an investment holding company, or an overseas entity acting in concert with the named promoter, since the disclosure duty under Regulation 31 extends to persons acting in concert and does not stop at the individual whose name appears on the company's shareholding pattern.

Loan documentation is the second place where disclosure risk is genuinely manageable. A loan-against-shares facility should be negotiated with an explicit timeline for the lender to notify the promoter the moment an encumbrance event, including a partial invocation triggered by a margin shortfall, is recorded in the depository system, since the promoter's own seven-working-day disclosure clock starts running from that event regardless of whether the lender has told them about it yet. A promoter who learns of an invocation only when a shareholder or journalist asks about it has already lost the ability to disclose on time, however blameless the delay in actually finding out may have been.

Where this intersects with insider trading and open offer obligations

A share pledge does not, by itself, trigger an open offer obligation under the SAST Regulations, because pledging shares does not change who holds voting rights or control over them in the ordinary course. That position changes the moment a pledge is invoked and the pledgee becomes entitled to exercise voting rights or otherwise acquire control-relevant rights over the shares, at which point the acquisition and takeover thresholds under the SAST Regulations can come into play for the pledgee in the same way they would for any other acquirer crossing the relevant shareholding or control thresholds.

A related question families often raise is whether pledging or releasing a pledge over their own shares counts as trading for the purposes of SEBI's insider trading framework, given that promoters are frequently also designated persons with access to unpublished price-sensitive information about their own company. SEBI's own 2015 guidance at one point treated a pledge as falling within the definition of trade for certain disclosure purposes, a position commentary has since argued needs to be reconsidered in light of the Supreme Court's clarification in PTC India Financial Services that a pledge, without invocation, does not transfer beneficial ownership in the way an ordinary trade does. Promoters who are also designated persons under their company's own insider trading code should, regardless of how that broader regulatory question eventually settles, continue treating a pledge during a trading window closure or while in possession of unpublished price-sensitive information with the same caution they would apply to an outright sale, since the company's own code of conduct, rather than only the SAST Regulations, is often the stricter and more immediately binding constraint.

A promoter planning to pledge shares should therefore clear the transaction against the company's own trading window calendar before approaching a lender, rather than treating the pledge as outside the scope of that calendar simply because no shares are changing hands in the ordinary sense. A trading window that is closed ahead of quarterly results, for instance, is typically closed for every transaction the company's code defines as dealing in securities, and a well-drafted code will usually address a pledge expressly rather than leaving the promoter to guess whether it is covered.

Persons acting in concert: the disclosure net is wider than the promoter's own name

Regulation 31's disclosure duty does not stop at the individual promoter named in a company's shareholding pattern. It extends, in the same terms, to every person acting in concert with that promoter, a phrase the SAST Regulations define widely enough to capture family members, holding companies, trusts and other entities that cooperate with the promoter for the purpose of acquiring or holding shares or voting rights, or exercising control. A family office that pledges shares on behalf of a promoter's adult children, or a private trust that holds a portion of the family's stake and pledges it to fund an unrelated venture, falls within this net even if the named promoter personally does nothing.

This wider scope creates a coordination problem that families with layered holding structures underestimate. Where shares are spread across a promoter individually, a Hindu Undivided Family, one or more private trusts, and a holding company, each entity needs its own internal process for flagging an encumbrance event to whoever is responsible for the group's SEBI filings, because a pledge created by the trust is, for disclosure purposes, treated as if the named promoter had created it. A family that only tracks pledges created in the promoter's personal name, while leaving the trust and the holding company to manage their own borrowing independently, is almost guaranteed to miss a disclosure at some point as the group's financing needs grow more complex.

The practical fix most family offices eventually adopt is a single, consolidated register of every encumbrance across every entity in the promoter group, reconciled against the depository records for each entity on a fixed schedule rather than only when a filing deadline is already close. This does not remove the underlying compliance burden, but it converts a scattered, entity-by-entity risk into a single point of oversight that can catch a missed disclosure before the seven-working-day window closes rather than after.

A second, related gap shows up where a family has only recently brought a trust or holding company into existence as part of a succession or tax restructuring, and the entity that now holds the shares was not the one the lender's documentation, or the family's own compliance process, was originally built around. Every time shares move between family entities as part of a reorganisation, whoever is responsible for Regulation 31 filings should treat that movement itself as a trigger to re-check whether any pre-existing encumbrance needs to be re-disclosed against the new holding entity, rather than assuming the earlier disclosure against the old entity still covers the position once the shares have moved.

A pledge against borrowed money is still governed by the loan documents

SEBI's disclosure framework governs what the market is told about a pledge. It says very little about what happens between promoter and lender once a pledge is actually invoked, which is instead a matter of the loan agreement and the pledge deed the parties have negotiated. A loan-against-shares facility typically fixes a loan-to-value ratio at the outset, sets a maintenance margin the promoter must keep the pledge above as the share price moves, and specifies what counts as a trigger event entitling the lender to call for additional shares as top-up collateral or, failing that, to invoke the pledge over what has already been pledged. These commercial terms are negotiated privately between promoter and lender and sit entirely outside SEBI's disclosure framework, which is concerned only with what the market is told, not with how generous or strict any particular underlying lending facility happens to be in its own separately negotiated commercial terms.

Promoter families negotiating these facilities for the first time sometimes focus heavily on the interest rate and the initial loan-to-value ratio while giving comparatively little attention to the margin call and cure-period mechanics, which is usually the wrong order of priorities. A facility with a slightly higher cost of funds but a longer cure period before invocation, and a clearer, pre-agreed process for topping up collateral with additional shares or cash rather than facing an immediate sale, is often the more valuable term for a family whose main concern is retaining control of the pledged shares through a short-term market downturn.

The February 2026 depository circular's notice requirement before invocation sits alongside whatever notice terms the loan agreement itself already contains, and the two are not automatically the same thing. A loan agreement might, for instance, allow the lender to treat a single day's breach of the maintenance margin as an immediate trigger for invocation, while the depository-level process now requires a separate, reasonable notice to the pledgor before that invocation is actually processed through the depository system. Families should read both documents together rather than assuming the newer, SEBI-mandated protection automatically overrides or supplements a shorter contractual notice period in their favour; the safer course is to negotiate the loan agreement's own notice terms to be at least as protective as the depository-level requirement, rather than relying on the regulatory layer to compensate for a weaker contractual one.

Lessons from well-known pledge episodes

Several listed Indian companies have, over the years, gone through episodes where heavy promoter pledging became public knowledge in a way that moved the stock sharply once the market understood the scale involved, rather than through a single dramatic disclosure. The pattern in most of these episodes was not that the pledges were individually undisclosed, since the seven-working-day filings were generally made as required, but that each incremental pledge was read in isolation by casual observers rather than as part of a cumulative trend that, viewed together across several quarters, told a much more concerning story about the promoter's overall leverage against the company's stock.

This is precisely the gap the 2019 reasons-for-encumbrance circular tried to close by forcing a qualitative explanation once the cumulative threshold is crossed, but the broader lesson for a promoter family is that cumulative disclosure, not just point-in-time compliance, is what actually protects the company's market standing. A family that pledges a further small tranche of shares every few months, each one individually under the radar of the 50% or 20% threshold, can still end up with the bulk of its holding encumbered without ever having had to explain why, simply because no single disclosure crossed the line that triggers the reasons requirement. Market analysts who track promoter shareholding patterns closely have, in practice, learned to add up these incremental filings themselves rather than waiting for the company to explain the cumulative position, so a family relying on staying just under the threshold is relying on a form of market inattention that tends not to last.

The more durable lesson from these episodes is that a promoter family's own credibility, once a heavily pledged position becomes a matter of market commentary, is not automatically restored the moment the underlying loans are repaid and the shares released. The release disclosure removes the legal encumbrance, but it does not, on its own, undo whatever view analysts and institutional shareholders had already formed about the family's reliance on pledge financing, which is one further reason to treat the decision to pledge as a reputational and strategic matter for the family's listed vehicle, not simply a financing decision left to whichever family member happens to need the money fastest.

A compliance checklist before signing a pledge

Before a promoter family executes a pledge over listed shares, a short set of questions is worth working through methodically rather than leaving to the lender's standard documentation to cover by default. First, which exact entity within the family structure will create the pledge, and has that entity's own Regulation 31 disclosure process been confirmed as current and functioning, rather than assumed to be someone else's responsibility within the group.

Second, where does this pledge sit against the 50% and 20% thresholds under the 2019 circular once combined with every other encumbrance already created by the promoter and persons acting in concert, and if the threshold will be crossed, has the reasons disclosure already been drafted so it can be filed on time rather than composed under pressure once the filing deadline is already close. Third, what does the loan agreement say about margin calls, cure periods and the lender's invocation rights, and does that sit comfortably alongside the notice requirement the February 2026 depository circular now layers on top of the contractual terms.

Fourth, has the listed company's own compliance team, beyond just the promoter's personal advisors, been looped in early enough to prepare the company-level website disclosure that Regulation 31 and the 2019 circular both require within their own separate timelines, since a company that first learns of a major promoter pledge from a stock exchange filing rather than from the promoter directly is rarely able to publish its own required disclosure within the time the rules allow. Working through these four questions before signing, rather than after a default has already triggered an urgent compliance scramble, is the single most effective way a family can keep a pledge transaction from becoming a disclosure problem in its own right.

A fifth question, smaller than the first four but still worth asking, is who within the family or its advisory team is tracking the calendar date on which the annual Regulation 31(4) confirmation falls due, since this is the point at which an old, half-forgotten encumbrance most often resurfaces as a compliance gap rather than at the time it was originally created. Families that keep this date on the same compliance calendar as board meetings, annual return filings and other recurring corporate obligations tend to treat it as routine; those that leave it to be picked up informally each year are the ones most likely to discover, only once the declaration is already overdue, that an encumbrance created months earlier was never properly disclosed in the first place.

A share pledge gives a promoter family a way to raise money without giving up ownership, but it comes wrapped in a disclosure regime that was built, quite deliberately, to stop exactly the kind of silent, build-up-then-collapse exposure that first exposed the gap in the law. Regulation 31's seven-working-day clock, the 2019 reasons-for-encumbrance threshold, and the February 2026 notice requirement before invocation each address a different point in the life of a pledge, and a family that treats all three as part of the same compliance discipline, rather than as separate hurdles to clear one at a time, is far less likely to be caught out by any of them.

The PTC India Financial Services ruling is a useful reminder that a pledge, even once invoked, is still a security interest rather than a sale, and that distinction continues to shape how SEBI, the depositories and the courts treat a pledged shareholding at every stage from creation to recovery.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does pledging shares require shareholder approval or board approval?

The SAST Regulations impose a disclosure obligation on the promoter and persons acting in concert, not a prior shareholder or board approval requirement for the act of pledging itself. Separate approval requirements can arise under the company's own articles, loan covenants, or stock exchange listing agreements, which should be checked independently of the SEBI disclosure timeline.

How quickly must a promoter disclose a share pledge under SEBI's rules?

Within seven working days of creating the encumbrance, under Regulation 31 of the SAST Regulations, 2011. The same seven-working-day period applies separately when the pledge is invoked and again when it is released, so each event has its own independent disclosure deadline.

What happens once a promoter's pledged shares cross 50% of their holding?

A SEBI circular of 7 August 2019 requires the promoter, once the combined encumbrance with persons acting in concert reaches 50% of their own shareholding in the company or 20% of the company's total share capital, to disclose detailed reasons for the encumbrance, not just its existence, with the listed company required to publish that disclosure on its own website within two working days.

Can a lender automatically become the owner of pledged shares once it invokes the pledge?

No. The Supreme Court held in PTC India Financial Services Ltd v. Venkateswarlu Kari (2022) that registration as a depository 'beneficial owner' after invocation does not by itself amount to a transfer of title. The pledgee's registration is a step in enforcing the security, not an automatic transfer of ownership.

What changed for pledgors under SEBI's February 2026 circular?

The circular dated 5 February 2026 revised the depository framework for creating and invoking a pledge, standardising the pledge request form across NSDL and CDSL and introducing a requirement for reasonable prior notice to the pledgor before a pledge is invoked or the pledged securities sold, in addition to notifying both parties once invocation occurs.

Does a non-disposal undertaking count as an encumbrance that must be disclosed?

Yes. The SAST Regulations define encumbrance broadly to include a pledge, lien, negative lien, non-disposal undertaking and any other arrangement that restrains or restricts free and marketable title to the shares, and SEBI has treated restrictive covenants in loan or debenture documents as encumbrances in substance even where the word pledge is not used.

Does pledging shares trigger an open offer obligation for the lender?

Not merely by creating the pledge, since the promoter retains voting rights and control in the ordinary course. An open offer or other takeover obligation can arise for the pledgee only once the pledge is invoked and the pledgee acquires control-relevant rights crossing the thresholds the SAST Regulations separately prescribe for any acquirer.
