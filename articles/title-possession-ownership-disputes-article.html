Title, Possession and Ownership Disputes: Verifying a Deed, Adverse Possession, and Cancelling a Registered Sale

A clear sale deed, a long occupation and an unchallenged mutation entry each feel like proof of ownership. Indian law treats them as three separate, frequently contradictory, questions.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 5 November 2026 · Law stated as on 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A buyer who checks the encumbrance certificate and the mutation register before paying for a plot has done something useful, and still has not necessarily checked that the seller can convey what the sale deed promises. A family that has occupied a corner of the ancestral house for forty years without anyone objecting has not necessarily acquired it. A co-owner holding a registered sale deed for the whole property has not necessarily sold anything beyond their own share. Each of these situations turns on a distinct line of law, title verification, adverse possession, the cancellation of instruments, and the rights of co-owners, and the Supreme Court has restated or refined each of them within the last three years. This piece works through what a buyer actually needs to check, what a long occupier actually has to prove, when a registered deed can be undone, and what a co-owner can and cannot do with jointly held land, as the law stands today.

Key points

  • An encumbrance certificate and a mutation entry are necessary checks before buying property, but neither proves title. Courts treat mutation as a revenue record for tax and administrative purposes, not as an adjudication of ownership, and a clean encumbrance certificate only confirms that no registered transaction against the property was found for the period it covers.
  • An unregistered agreement to sell, even one backed by part payment and possession, conveys no title and creates no interest in the property. The Supreme Court restated this in Rano Singh v. Gurbax Singh (2026 INSC 1137), decided 16 October 2026, following Vinod Infra Developers Ltd. v. Mahaveer Lunia (2025) and the long-standing position in Suraj Lamp & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana, (2012) 1 SCC 656.
  • Adverse possession requires the person claiming it to prove possession that is open, continuous, hostile to the true owner's title and held for the full statutory period, twelve years under Article 65 of the Limitation Act, 1963 for most private land. The burden sits on the person claiming adverse possession, not on the title holder, and long possession by itself, without proof of hostility and the owner's knowledge, is not enough.
  • Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur, (2019) 8 SCC 729, settled that a person who has perfected title by adverse possession can sue to have that title declared, and is not limited to raising it as a defence when sued by the owner. The Supreme Court reaffirmed this in Idu (through LRs) v. Nizam Din (D) through LRs on 29 January 2024.
  • Cancelling a registered sale deed ordinarily requires a civil suit under Section 31 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, not a unilateral cancellation deed. The relief is discretionary, the limitation period is usually three years under Article 59 of the Limitation Act from when the facts justifying cancellation became known, and whether that three-year period or the twelve-year period under Article 65 applies depends on whether the real relief sought is cancellation of the document or recovery of possession.
  • A co-owner cannot transfer the entire undivided property and bind the other co-owners. The Supreme Court held in S.K. Golam Lalchand v. Nandu Lal Shaw, 2024 INSC 676, that a co-owner's share must first be determined and demarcated by partition before the whole property can be validly conveyed; a co-owner may sell only their own undivided interest.
  • Partition is generally the correct remedy for a co-ownership dispute, and many courts have held that a bare suit for injunction is not maintainable where partition offers an equally effective remedy under Section 41(h) of the Specific Relief Act. An injunction remains available against a co-owner in exclusive possession who is excluding the others or damaging the property, and a co-owner facing a sale of a share in a family dwelling house to a stranger has a right of pre-emption under Section 4 of the Partition Act, 1893, which applies specifically to an undivided family's dwelling house, not to co-owned property in general.

Three separate questions that get asked as one

Clients rarely walk in asking about Section 31 of the Specific Relief Act or Article 65 of the Limitation Act. They walk in with a document, a family argument, or a notice from a buyer's lawyer, and they want to know one thing: who owns this property. The honest answer is usually that ownership, possession and the paper record are three different questions, each with its own evidentiary standard and its own forum, and a family dispute often turns on which of the three is actually in play.

Title asks what the law of property recognises as a valid chain of conveyance, running from a legitimate original owner through registered instruments, inheritance, or a decree, to the person claiming to be the owner today. Possession asks who actually controls and uses the land, which the law protects in its own right even against a true owner who tries to retake it by force rather than through court. The paper record, a mutation entry, a khata, a property tax receipt, records what a revenue or municipal authority believes to be true for its own administrative purposes, and courts have been consistent that this record is evidence of possession at best, never conclusive proof of title.

The practical cost of confusing these three questions shows up at the worst possible moment, usually after money has changed hands or after a family member has died and the others discover that an uncle's long, quiet occupation of one wing of the house was never regularised. This piece takes the three questions in a sequence that mirrors how disputes actually arise: verifying title before a purchase, the doctrine of adverse possession for long occupiers, cancelling a registered deed that was obtained wrongly, and the special rules that apply when the property is held by more than one owner.

Verifying title before you pay: what actually needs checking

A buyer's due diligence in India typically starts with three documents: the seller's title deed and the chain of deeds behind it, an encumbrance certificate from the sub-registrar's office, and the current mutation entry in revenue or municipal records. None of these, taken alone, proves that the seller can convey clear title, and together they still leave gaps that only a physical and litigation check can close.

The title chain should be traced back far enough to cover at least one complete, uninterrupted period of ownership, commonly recommended at around thirty years for urban property, though older or agricultural land sometimes calls for a longer look depending on how the property was last partitioned or inherited. Each link in the chain, a sale deed, a gift deed, a will, a succession or legal heir certificate, should be checked for proper execution, registration where registration was required, and consistency of description (survey number, boundaries, extent) with the links before and after it.

The encumbrance certificate, issued by the sub-registrar, lists registered transactions, mortgages, leases, and charges against the property for the period requested. A clean certificate is reassuring but not dispositive: it only reflects what was registered in that jurisdiction for that period, so an unregistered claim, a pending civil suit that has not resulted in a registered lis pendens notice, or a transaction registered under a different description of the property will not show up. Buyers are generally better served obtaining their own certificate directly rather than relying on a copy supplied by the seller.

Mutation is where buyers most often misplace their confidence. A mutation entry updates the revenue or municipal record of who the authorities will treat as the person liable for property tax and whom they will deal with for permissions and utility connections. It is an administrative convenience, not a judicial finding on title, and a seller's name appearing in the mutation register does not establish that the seller actually owns the property if the underlying transfer that produced that entry was itself defective or contested. Where a title chain shows any gap, an inheritance that was never formally settled, a partition that was agreed verbally but never reduced to a registered deed, a sale by someone who turns out to have been only one of several co-owners, mutation will not fill it, and the buyer needs that gap closed before paying rather than after.

An agreement to sell and possession without a registered deed

Many disputes begin with a buyer who paid, took possession, and believed that an agreement to sell, perhaps coupled with a power of attorney in the buyer's favour, had effectively made them the owner. Indian law has been consistent on this point for over a decade and has restated it again this year.

In Suraj Lamp & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana, (2012) 1 SCC 656, decided on 11 October 2011 by a bench of R.V. Raveendran, A.K. Patnaik and H.L. Gokhale JJ., the Supreme Court held that sale transactions structured through a general power of attorney, an agreement to sell, and a will, often used to avoid stamp duty and registration, do not convey title and create no interest in immovable property. Under the Transfer of Property Act, a sale of immovable property worth more than a modest statutory threshold can only be made by a registered instrument of conveyance.

That position was reaffirmed in Vinod Infra Developers Ltd. v. Mahaveer Lunia (2025), reported as holding that an unregistered agreement to sell creates no right, title or interest in immovable property even where the purchaser has paid the price and taken possession, and again, very recently, in Rano Singh v. Gurbax Singh (D) through LRs, 2026 INSC 1137, decided 16 October 2026. That case traced an agreement to sell a plot in Delhi's Friends Colony back to 1952 and a suit filed in 1975, and the Supreme Court held that the agreement gave the original allottee's successors no title and that a possession suit built on it had to fail, because only a registered sale deed, which a different party had obtained decades later, actually conveyed ownership.

The remedy for a buyer stuck with only an agreement to sell is a suit for specific performance against the seller, to compel execution and registration of a proper sale deed, not a suit for possession or title as though the sale had already been completed. This has a direct limitation consequence: a suit for specific performance must generally be filed within three years, under Article 54 of the Limitation Act, 1963, of the date fixed for performance, or, if no date was fixed, of the date the plaintiff had notice of the seller's refusal to perform, so a buyer who waits, hoping the dispute will resolve itself, risks losing the claim entirely rather than merely weakening it.

Adverse possession: the twelve-year clock and what it actually demands

Adverse possession is the doctrine under which a person who is not the true owner can, after holding the property for long enough and in the right manner, acquire a title that courts will recognise and protect, even against the original owner. For most private immovable property, the relevant period is twelve years, fixed by Article 65 of the Limitation Act, 1963, which governs a suit for possession of immovable property based on title, as opposed to Article 64, which governs a purely possessory suit and runs twelve years from the date of dispossession rather than from when adverse possession began. Government property carries a longer thirty-year period under Article 112.

The twelve years under Article 65 does not simply run from whenever the occupier moved in. It runs from the point at which the occupier's possession became adverse to the true owner, meaning possession that is hostile to the owner's title, not possession held with the owner's permission, under a lease, as a licensee, or as a co-owner sharing the property. A tenant who overstays after a lease ends, or a family member allowed to live on a portion of ancestral land out of generosity, does not automatically convert that permissive occupation into adverse possession merely by continuing to occupy the property; something more is needed to put the true owner on notice that the occupier is now claiming the property as their own, against the owner's title, rather than with the owner's continued tolerance.

The burden of proving this lies on the person claiming adverse possession, not on the title holder. The claimant must show, by clear and specific evidence rather than general assertion, that possession was open, continuous for the full statutory period without a material break, exclusive, and hostile to the knowledge of the true owner, so that the owner had, or reasonably should have had, the opportunity to object and did not. Courts have repeatedly cautioned that long possession alone is not the same as adverse possession; a person who has simply been on the land for decades, without anything to show that the possession was ever adverse to a known owner's title, has proved occupation, not ownership.

Ravinder Kaur Grewal and the sword-and-shield question

For years, Indian courts debated whether adverse possession could only be raised defensively, as a shield when the true owner sued to evict the occupier, or whether the occupier who had completed the statutory period could go further and actively sue for a declaration of ownership, using the doctrine offensively, as a sword. In Gurdwara Sahib v. Gram Panchayat Village Sirthala (2014), the Supreme Court had taken the narrower view, holding that a plaintiff could not found a suit for declaration of ownership on adverse possession.

A three-judge bench reversed that position in Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur, (2019) 8 SCC 729, also reported as 2019 SCC OnLine SC 975 and decided on 7 August 2019 by Arun Mishra, S. Abdul Nazeer and M.R. Shah JJ. The Court held that once possession for the statutory period perfects title in the occupier's favour, that title exists as a matter of substantive law and can be asserted actively, not merely held in reserve for when the original owner chooses to sue. The person who has completed twelve years of hostile, open possession can bring their own suit for a declaration of title and, if needed, possession, rather than waiting to be sued.

The Supreme Court reaffirmed this in Idu (through LRs) v. Nizam Din (D) through LRs, decided 29 January 2024 by Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha and Aravind Kumar JJ. and reported as 2024 LiveLaw (SC) 82. The High Court in that case had taken the view that adverse possession could only ever be a defence, treating a plaintiff's reliance on it as inherently defective; the Supreme Court set that reasoning aside, holding that Ravinder Kaur Grewal had already settled that a plaintiff may found a suit on adverse possession, and that lower courts were not free to revive the older, narrower position Gurdwara Sahib had represented.

What adverse possession still cannot do

Winning this doctrine is harder in practice than the headline sword-and-shield principle suggests, and courts have been careful to keep it that way rather than turn every long tenancy or informal family arrangement into a route to ownership. A claim of adverse possession generally fails where the claimant's own pleadings or conduct show that the true owner's title was acknowledged at some point during the occupation, since an admission of the owner's title at any point resets the clock and defeats the claim of hostility for everything that came before it. It also fails where the possession, however long, began with the owner's permission and was never clearly repudiated in a way the owner could have noticed and challenged.

Co-ownership raises its own complication. A co-owner's possession of the whole or part of a jointly held property is, as a starting position, treated as possession on behalf of all the co-owners, not as possession hostile to them, since co-owners are each entitled to use the common property. A co-owner who wants to convert that shared occupation into an adverse claim against the other co-owners needs clear proof of an actual ouster, a definite, communicated act excluding the others from their rights, not simply continued, unchallenged use of a larger share than their entitlement.

None of this changes the underlying evidentiary demand: a court weighing an adverse possession claim is looking for specific, dated, corroborated facts, a boundary wall built at an identifiable time, tax payments made in the occupier's own name over the statutory period, an earlier dispute in which the occupier's claim to ownership, not merely possession, was asserted and not seriously contested, rather than a general impression that nobody in the family ever raised an objection.

Cancelling a registered sale deed under Section 31

A registered sale deed carries real evidentiary weight, but registration under the Registration Act, 1908 does not cure every defect in how the deed came about. Where a deed was obtained by fraud, forgery, impersonation, without the true owner's knowledge, or by a person who had no authority to execute it, such as a co-owner purporting to sell the whole property or a power of attorney holder acting beyond their authority, the remedy is a civil suit, not a self-help cancellation.

Section 31 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 allows a person against whom a written instrument is void or voidable, and who has a reasonable apprehension that the instrument, if left outstanding, may cause serious injury, to sue for an order adjudging it void or voidable and directing it to be cancelled. The relief is discretionary rather than something the plaintiff gets as of right: the section uses the word "may," and courts have treated that language as leaving room to weigh the equities, including delay, the plaintiff's own conduct, and whether a more complete remedy is available in the same suit. A registered instrument cannot generally be cancelled by the parties themselves executing a further registered cancellation deed where the underlying facts are genuinely disputed; that kind of dispute belongs before a civil court, which alone can adjudicate whether the instrument should be set aside.

The distinction between a void and a voidable instrument matters for who needs to sue at all. An instrument that is voidable, meaning it has some legal effect until successfully challenged, generally needs to be formally set aside under Section 31 before the person affected can safely treat it as a nullity. An instrument that is void from the outset, for instance one executed by someone with no title or authority to the property at all, has no legal effect regardless of whether it is formally cancelled, though a person affected by such a deed will usually still want a declaratory decree, so that the deed is removed from the record and cannot cloud a future transaction or mislead a subsequent purchaser.

Limitation for a cancellation suit, and why the right article is not always obvious

Suits to cancel an instrument are generally governed by Article 59 of the Limitation Act, 1963, which fixes a three-year period for a suit to cancel or set aside an instrument or decree, or to rescind a contract. The three years run from when the facts entitling the plaintiff to have the instrument cancelled first became known to them, which is a question of fact in every case and not simply the date the instrument was executed or registered.

Registration complicates that question of knowledge rather than resolving it. Some courts have treated registration under Section 3 of the Registration Act, 1908 as giving a plaintiff constructive notice of the deed from its execution date, reasoning that the register is a public record anyone affected could have checked, which can shorten the window considerably for a plaintiff who delays. Other courts have resisted applying that reasoning mechanically where the plaintiff had no realistic occasion to check the register, for instance a family member living elsewhere who had no reason to suspect a fraudulent sale by a relative in possession. Because this point is genuinely contested rather than settled, a claim that looks time-barred on a strict constructive-notice reading may still survive if the facts support a later date of actual knowledge, and that factual case needs to be pleaded with specific dates rather than left general.

Article 59 is also not automatically the only candidate. Where the substance of what the plaintiff actually wants is possession of the property rather than merely the removal of a document from the record, courts look past the label of the plaint to what relief is really being sought, and a suit substantially about recovering possession can fall under Article 65's twelve-year period even where it also asks, as a step along the way, for the offending sale deed to be declared void. A plaintiff who sues only for cancellation, and separately needs possession, should generally plead and seek both reliefs together in the same suit rather than litigating cancellation first and possession later, since splitting the claims risks one relief becoming time-barred while the other is still being pursued.

A co-owner selling the whole plot: Section 44 and the Golam Lalchand rule

Jointly owned property produces its own recurring dispute: one co-owner executes a registered sale deed purporting to convey the entire property, collects the full price, and leaves the other co-owners to discover the sale only afterward, sometimes when the buyer attempts to take possession or seek mutation in their own name.

Section 44 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 limits what a co-owner can actually transfer. A co-owner of undivided property can transfer only their own undivided interest in it; they cannot, by executing a deed describing the whole property, convey more than their own share, regardless of what the deed says on its face or what price was paid for the whole.

The Supreme Court applied this squarely in S.K. Golam Lalchand v. Nandu Lal Shaw @ Nand Lal Keshri @ Nandu Lal Bayes & Ors., 2024 INSC 676, holding that a co-owner of a property that has not been partitioned cannot transfer the entire property without first having their own share determined and demarcated, and that a sale of the whole property by one co-owner, without a prior partition, is not binding on the other co-owners. On the facts, the Court granted a permanent injunction restraining the purchaser from taking or disturbing possession of the property until a proper partition had been carried out, while leaving the purchaser's own remedy, to pursue partition and claim whatever share the selling co-owner was actually entitled to convey, open through the ordinary courts.

The practical effect for a buyer is a sharp warning against relying on a single co-owner's signature for a jointly held property: a buyer who pays for the whole plot but takes a deed from only one of several co-owners acquires, at most, that co-owner's undivided share, and has to pursue partition, not a straightforward possession claim, to actually realise anything from the purchase. For the other co-owners, the remedy is not necessarily to wait for the buyer to attempt possession; a suit challenging the sale as not binding on their shares, combined with a partition claim, is usually the more direct route.

Partition: dividing the property, selling it instead, and a co-owner's right of pre-emption

Where co-owners cannot agree on how to divide jointly held property, a civil suit for partition is the standard remedy, aimed at ending the joint holding and giving each co-owner a defined, separate share, either by physical division of the land by metes and bounds or, where that is not practicable, by sale of the whole and division of the proceeds according to each co-owner's entitlement. Courts generally prefer an actual physical division where the property can be sensibly split without destroying its value, reserving sale as the fallback where division would leave the shares impractically small or would require demolishing or severely devaluing a structure that stands on the land.

The Partition Act, 1893 adds a specific protection for a co-owner facing a transfer to an outsider, but only where the property is a dwelling house belonging to an undivided family; it does not extend to co-owned agricultural, commercial or other non-dwelling property. Where a stranger who has purchased a co-owner's share in such a family dwelling house sues for partition, Section 4 of the Act allows the remaining co-owner, on request, to have the transferred share valued and to buy it out at that valuation instead of allowing the stranger into joint possession or forcing a sale of the whole house to a third party. This right of pre-emption generally applies when the transferee, not the original co-owners among themselves, is the one pressing for partition, and courts have held it is not available to be invoked at the execution stage after a partition decree has already become final.

Limitation for a partition claim runs differently from a straightforward possession suit. Article 110 of the Limitation Act, 1963 gives a person excluded from joint family property or joint possession twelve years from the date of exclusion to claim their share, a provision that has defeated long-delayed partition claims where decades passed between the exclusion and the suit, and that makes early documentation of any exclusion, and an early response to it, important for a co-owner who suspects a sibling or relative is treating jointly held property as exclusively their own.

Injunction or partition: picking the right remedy

A co-owner who discovers that another co-owner is about to sell, lease, mortgage, or materially alter jointly held property often wants an immediate injunction rather than the slower route of a partition suit. Whether that works depends heavily on the specific facts, and courts have not treated the two remedies as interchangeable.

Several High Courts have held that where partition offers an equally efficacious remedy, a suit for a bare injunction, without also seeking partition, is not maintainable, relying on Section 41(h) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, which bars an injunction where the plaintiff has an equally efficacious relief available through ordinary legal proceedings other than an injunction suit. The reasoning is that an injunction alone does not resolve the underlying co-ownership dispute, it only freezes one particular act, while partition actually settles what each co-owner is entitled to.

The exceptions matter in practice. Courts have granted an injunction, without requiring a full partition suit first, where a co-owner in actual possession is excluding the other co-owners from the property entirely, or is causing material waste or permanent alteration that a later partition could not undo, since in those situations an injunction addresses an urgent, specific harm that a partition decree, which can take years to obtain, would not prevent in the meantime. An injunction has also been used to restrain a co-owner from transferring the entire undivided property to a third party without first obtaining a partition, consistent with the Section 44 and Golam Lalchand position that such a transfer cannot bind the other co-owners in any event. A co-owner weighing which suit to file is usually better advised to combine the claims, an injunction as an interim measure together with partition as the final relief, rather than choosing one to the exclusion of the other.

A practical sequence before relying on any of this

A buyer facing an uncertain title should work through the chain of deeds first, then the encumbrance certificate, then mutation, treating each as confirming a different, narrower fact rather than as cumulative proof of ownership, and should specifically ask whether any link in the chain involves a co-owner selling what might be more than their own share, since that single fact pattern defeats more purchases than outright forgery does.

A long-term occupier considering an adverse possession claim should gather, before filing anything, the specific, dated evidence a court will actually ask for: tax receipts or utility bills in the occupier's own name over the full statutory period, any prior correspondence or dispute in which ownership, not just occupation, was asserted, and a clear account of when and how the occupation turned hostile to the true owner's title, since a vague claim of "we have always lived here" does not meet the burden Ravinder Kaur Grewal and the Article 65 case law actually impose.

Anyone considering a suit to cancel a registered deed should fix the date they actually learned the relevant facts as precisely as possible before filing, because that date decides whether Article 59's three years have already run, and should plead both cancellation and possession together where both are genuinely in issue, rather than risk one relief becoming time-barred while pursuing the other. A co-owner facing an unauthorised sale by another co-owner should treat partition as the primary claim and injunction as, at most, an accompanying interim measure, and should move promptly once exclusion from the property becomes clear, since Article 110's twelve years, like Article 65's twelve years for adverse possession, run from the date of exclusion and not from the date the co-owner eventually decides to act on it.

Title, possession and the administrative record answer different questions, and the law keeps them apart deliberately. A clean encumbrance certificate and an unbroken mutation entry are worth having, but neither substitutes for tracing the actual chain of conveyance, and an agreement to sell, however long it has been acted on, is still not a sale deed. Ravinder Kaur Grewal and Idu confirm that a completed period of hostile possession can found a claim rather than merely resist one, but the burden of proving that hostility in specific, dated terms remains on the occupier, and co-ownership keeps its own, often stricter rules about when possession or a sale can bind anyone beyond the person who actually holds a clear share.

The common thread across verifying a title, claiming adverse possession, cancelling a deed and resolving a co-ownership dispute is documentation fixed in time: when a fact was known, when possession turned hostile, when an exclusion happened, when a share was actually conveyed rather than merely described on paper. Limitation periods in this area run from those specific moments, not from when a dispute finally reaches a lawyer, and a family or buyer who waits to establish them loses options that were available at the time the dispute first arose.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a mutation entry in my name mean I own the property?

No. Mutation is an entry in revenue or municipal records used mainly to identify who is liable for property tax and whom the local authority deals with administratively. It is evidence of possession at best, and courts have consistently held that a genuinely disputed question of ownership has to be resolved through a civil suit for declaration of title, not through a revenue officer's mutation order.

I have paid for a plot under an agreement to sell and taken possession, but there is no registered sale deed yet. Do I own it?

No, not yet. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held, most recently in Rano Singh v. Gurbax Singh (2026 INSC 1137) and Vinod Infra Developers Ltd. v. Mahaveer Lunia (2025), following Suraj Lamp & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana, (2012) 1 SCC 656, that an unregistered agreement to sell conveys no title and creates no interest in immovable property, even with part payment and possession. Your remedy is a suit for specific performance to compel the seller to execute and register a proper sale deed, and that suit generally has to be filed within three years of the date fixed for performance or of the seller's refusal to perform.

How long does someone have to occupy land before they can claim ownership by adverse possession?

Twelve years for most private immovable property, under Article 65 of the Limitation Act, 1963, running from when the occupier's possession became hostile to the true owner's title, not necessarily from when the occupation began. Government property carries a longer thirty-year period under Article 112. The twelve years is only the minimum; the occupier still has to prove the possession was open, continuous, exclusive and hostile throughout, which is a separate and often harder evidentiary burden.

Can someone who has been in adverse possession for long enough actually sue to be declared the owner, or can they only use it as a defence if the true owner sues them?

They can sue to be declared the owner. The Supreme Court settled this in Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur, (2019) 8 SCC 729, overruling the earlier, narrower view in Gurdwara Sahib v. Gram Panchayat Village Sirthala (2014), and reaffirmed it in Idu (through LRs) v. Nizam Din (D) through LRs on 29 January 2024. Once title has been perfected by completing the statutory period of hostile possession, it can be used offensively, as a sword, rather than held back purely for defence, as a shield.

How do I get a registered sale deed cancelled if I believe it was obtained by fraud or by someone with no authority to sell?

Generally through a civil suit under Section 31 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, asking the court to adjudge the instrument void or voidable and direct its cancellation. The relief is discretionary rather than automatic, and the suit is usually subject to a three-year limitation period under Article 59 of the Limitation Act, running from when you first knew the facts justifying cancellation, so the date of actual knowledge needs to be pinned down and pleaded precisely.

One of my co-owners sold the entire property without telling the rest of us. Is the sale valid?

Not against the other co-owners' shares. Under Section 44 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, a co-owner can transfer only their own undivided interest, and the Supreme Court confirmed in S.K. Golam Lalchand v. Nandu Lal Shaw, 2024 INSC 676, that a co-owner cannot validly convey the whole, undivided property without first having their share determined and demarcated through partition. The buyer, at most, acquires the selling co-owner's own share and has to pursue partition to realise anything concrete from the purchase.

Can I just get an injunction to stop a co-owner from selling or disturbing jointly owned property, instead of filing a full partition suit?

It depends on the facts. Many courts hold that a bare injunction suit is not maintainable where partition is an equally effective remedy under Section 41(h) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, since an injunction does not itself resolve the co-ownership. Courts have allowed an injunction where a co-owner in possession is excluding the others or causing permanent damage to the property, or to restrain an attempted sale of the whole undivided property without partition, but the safer course in most cases is to combine an injunction as interim relief with a partition suit as the final relief, rather than relying on an injunction alone.
