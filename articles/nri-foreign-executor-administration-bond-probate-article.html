Acting as Executor From Abroad: Bonds, Attorneys and Probate Practice for NRI Executors

An executor named in a Will can live in Chicago, London or Dubai and still administer an Indian estate, but the Indian Succession Act's rules on attorneys and administration bonds decide how much that costs and how long it takes

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 28 November 2026 · Law stated as on 28 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A parent in Kolkata names a daughter in Toronto as executor of the Will. The daughter cannot simply email the bank and ask it to release the funds. Indian probate practice has its own, quite specific rules for an executor who lives outside the country, and getting them wrong adds months to administering an estate that is usually already under family pressure.

Key points

  • Indian succession law does not disqualify a person from acting as executor merely because they are a foreign national or reside outside India; residence and nationality are not grounds of exclusion under the Indian Succession Act, 1925.
  • Where the named executor is absent from the state in which the application is made and no executor present is willing to act, Section 241 of the Indian Succession Act allows the court to grant letters of administration with the Will annexed to that executor's constituted attorney, limited until the executor obtains probate personally.
  • Section 291 generally requires every grant of letters of administration, other than one made under Section 241, to be secured by an administration bond with sureties; the Section 241 route is itself an exception to that requirement in most cases.
  • Section 291(2) narrows the Section 241 exception for a deceased who was Hindu, Muslim, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain, allowing the court to still demand a bond even where letters issue to an absent executor's attorney, and recent Delhi High Court orders show this area of practice is still actively contested.
  • The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 removed the colonial-era Section 213 requirement that made probate a precondition to enforcing certain Wills in Calcutta, Madras and Bombay, which changes, for many Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist and Jain families, whether probate even needs to be sought before the executor question arises at all.
  • A power of attorney executed abroad by an NRI or foreign executor needs apostille or consular attestation and stamping in India before an Indian probate court will act on it, and courts have generally been willing to dispense with the executor's personal appearance once a properly attested power of attorney is on record.
  • Where one of several named executors is unwilling or unable to act, that executor can renounce, allowing the remaining executors or, where none remain, the next eligible applicant, to proceed without needlessly delaying the whole estate.

The call that comes after the funeral

Families rarely think carefully about who they are naming as executor when they draft a Will, beyond a general sense of who is most responsible or most trusted among their children. It is common, across the NRI families this firm advises in Delhi NCR and Kolkata, for the chosen executor to be the child who has settled abroad, often precisely because that child is seen as the most organised or the most emotionally equipped to manage the task from a distance.

The difficulty surfaces only when that executor actually has to act. Indian banks, registrars and courts are used to dealing with executors who can walk into a branch or a courtroom in person, and an executor based in Chicago, London or Dubai has to work through a different, more procedural route built around powers of attorney, attested documents and, in some circumstances, a bond that has to be furnished before the court will formally recognise the executor's authority over the estate.

None of this makes the task impossible. It does make it slower and more paperwork-heavy than families expect, and the single biggest source of delay is not the law itself but families discovering these requirements only after the death, rather than planning the executor's practical ability to act while drafting the Will in the first place.

The practical cost of getting this wrong is rarely abstract. A flat cannot be sold, a demat account stays frozen, and a fixed deposit continues earning the deceased's own tax liability rather than being distributed, all while a grieving family waits for paperwork that a little foresight at the drafting stage could have shortened considerably. Advising on executor selection is, in this sense, as much a part of Will drafting as deciding who inherits what.

Who may be an executor, and why residence abroad is not a disqualification

The Indian Succession Act, 1925 does not restrict who may be appointed or who may act as executor by reference to nationality or place of residence. A testator is free to name a child, sibling, friend or professional adviser living anywhere in the world, and the appointment is not invalid merely because that person happens to be an NRI, an OCI cardholder or a foreign national with no Indian citizenship at all.

What the Act does regulate closely is the mechanism by which an absent executor exercises that authority, because Indian probate courts have always had to deal with executors who cannot be physically present, going back to a period when overseas postings and travel times made personal attendance genuinely difficult for reasons quite different from today's international careers. The mechanism built for that older problem, the Section 241 attorney route, still does the same job for today's NRI executor, with modern formalities layered on top for attestation and identity verification.

The first right to seek probate under Section 222 of the Act belongs to the executor or executors expressly named in the Will, ahead of any other person who might otherwise apply for letters of administration. An NRI executor does not lose this priority merely by living abroad; the priority follows the appointment in the Will, and the practical question is only how that priority is exercised from a distance.

Probate, letters of administration, and why the 2025 repeal of Section 213 changes when either is even needed

Probate is the court's formal certification that a Will is genuine and that the executor named in it is authorised to administer the estate. Letters of administration serve a similar function where there is no valid Will, or where the named executor cannot or will not act, in which case the court appoints an administrator, with the Will annexed where one exists under Section 234 but no willing or eligible executor remains to take probate of it.

Until recently, Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act made probate, or letters of administration, a precondition for an executor or beneficiary named in certain Wills to establish any right in an Indian court at all, a rule that applied specifically to Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists and Jains in respect of immovable property situated within the former presidency towns of Calcutta, Madras and Bombay. The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025, which received presidential assent on 20 December 2025 and was brought into force the following day, omitted Section 213 entirely.

For many Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist and Jain families in Kolkata, this means probate is no longer a mandatory first step before a Will can be relied upon in court, though other provisions on probate, letters of administration and succession certificates remain fully in force and families frequently still choose to obtain probate voluntarily, because it gives a bank, registrar or purchaser the comfort of a court-certified document rather than a private Will that has never been tested. Where a family does choose to seek probate or letters of administration despite the repeal, usually to obtain clean, bankable title to immovable property or securities, the executor's own residence abroad and the bond question discussed below come back into play exactly as before.

Section 241: the attorney of an absent executor, and its limits

Section 241 of the Act addresses precisely the situation of an executor who is outside the state in which the application has to be made and where no executor present in that state is willing to act. In that situation, the court may grant letters of administration with the Will annexed to the constituted attorney of the absent executor, rather than requiring the executor to appear personally or wait indefinitely until they can travel to India.

The grant to the attorney is expressly limited. It operates only until the executor either obtains probate personally or the grant is otherwise revoked, and the attorney administers the estate as the executor's representative rather than acquiring any independent right in their own name. If the NRI executor later visits India and wishes to take probate directly, the Section 241 grant to the attorney gives way to that personal application.

This route works well for an executor who genuinely cannot be present but has a trusted person in India, often another family member or the firm instructed to administer the estate, who can hold the constituted power of attorney and deal with the court, banks and registrars on the executor's behalf. It is not a device for avoiding scrutiny of the executor's own eligibility or the Will's validity; the court still examines the Will itself exactly as it would on any other probate application.

The administration bond under Section 291, and the fight over who is exempt

Section 291(1) requires every person to whom letters of administration are granted, other than a grant made under Section 241, to furnish a bond to the District Judge with one or more sureties, conditioned on the due administration of the estate. The purpose, as courts have repeatedly explained, is to protect beneficiaries and creditors against mismanagement or diversion of the estate by someone the court has not otherwise tested, since an administrator, unlike an executor named by the testator personally, is often a stranger to the deceased's own choice of trustee.

The Section 241 route is built as an exception to this bond requirement in the ordinary case, which is one of its real practical advantages for an NRI executor's attorney. Reported decisions describe Section 291(1) as mandating a bond in every case of letters of administration barring the exception provided for the grant to an attorney of an absent executor, which is precisely the Section 241 scenario this article has been describing.

Courts have differed on how far this bond requirement reaches into probate itself, as opposed to letters of administration strictly speaking. Some rulings treat Section 291 as confined to letters of administration and not applicable to a straightforward grant of probate to a named executor, while others read the District Judge's power to demand security as extending to certain probate grants as well. An NRI executor should not assume, from general reading, that probate is always bond-free; the position depends on the specific facts and the practice of the court the application is made to, and needs to be checked at the outset of the specific matter.

Section 291(2): the extra condition for Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist and Jain estates

Section 291(2) carves back into the bond requirement a specific category of case. Where the deceased was Hindu, Muslim, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain, or falls within certain other categories the sub-section identifies, the general Section 241 exception for an absent executor's attorney does not operate in the same unqualified way, and the District Judge retains the power to demand a bond from the person to whom letters, or in some readings probate itself, is granted.

The practical effect for an NRI executor administering a Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist or Jain family's estate in Delhi or Kolkata is that the comfortable assumption, Section 241 applies, so no bond is needed, cannot be taken for granted without checking Section 291(2)'s specific carve-out against the facts of the estate. The executor's attorney may still be asked to furnish sureties, particularly where the court has any reason to be cautious about the size of the estate, the number of beneficiaries, or disputes already apparent among the family.

This is one of the genuinely unsettled corners of Indian probate practice, and families should not rely on a template answer found online without confirming the current position with the specific court the application will go to.

The 2026 Delhi bond-waiver orders and why the law here is still moving

A cluster of Delhi High Court orders through late 2025 and 2026 shows this question is being actively litigated rather than settled. Several petitioners who were sole beneficiaries and sole applicants under a proved Will have sought to be excused from furnishing an administration bond altogether, arguing there is no competing interest in the estate for a bond to protect once the only beneficiary is also the only applicant. Orders in matters described in reporting as involving petitioners such as Versha Charan and Vivek Mathur record the court weighing this argument, with reference to earlier decisions in matters described as Sanjay Suri and Arvind Nanda, while at least one other 2026 order directed an executor to furnish an administration bond with a surety notwithstanding a grant of probate.

No single, final appellate pronouncement settling this question across India has emerged from the material available at the time of writing. An NRI executor or their attorney should treat the possibility of being asked for a bond, and the possibility of being excused from one on a reasoned application if the facts genuinely support it, as a live, fact-specific question to be argued on the particular estate rather than a foregone conclusion either way.

Where a bond is demanded, it typically has to be matched by one or more sureties acceptable to the court, which can itself be a practical obstacle for an estate where the family's other members are also abroad. Discussing in advance who in India might stand surety, or whether a bank guarantee or other security the court will accept is a realistic alternative, saves real time once the estate is actually open for administration.

Powers of attorney for an executor abroad: apostille, consular attestation and stamping

Whichever route an NRI executor takes, whether appointing an attorney under Section 241 or instructing an attorney to represent them in a personal probate application, the power of attorney executed abroad needs to be properly authenticated before an Indian court, bank or registrar will act on it. For a country that is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, this generally means the document is notarised locally and then apostilled by the competent authority in that country. For a country that is not a party to the Convention, the document instead needs attestation by the Indian embassy or consulate in that country.

Once the apostilled or consular-attested power of attorney reaches India, it still needs to be stamped under the relevant state's stamp legislation, usually within a specified period of its first use or receipt in India, before it can be relied upon in a court filing or placed before a bank or sub-registrar. An improperly stamped power of attorney is one of the most common, and most avoidable, reasons an otherwise ready probate application gets stuck at the filing stage.

Executors should also keep the power of attorney's scope precisely matched to what is actually needed, naming the specific court, the specific estate and the specific acts the attorney is authorised to perform, rather than relying on an old, generic power of attorney drafted for an unrelated purpose years earlier. Courts and registrars scrutinise the scope of the instrument closely in probate matters precisely because an executor's authority over a deceased person's estate is not something they treat casually.

Appearing before the probate court in Delhi and Kolkata without personally appearing

Indian courts have generally shown themselves willing to dispense with an executor's personal appearance where a validly executed and properly attested power of attorney is on record and the attorney appears instead to represent the executor's interests in the proceeding. This is consistent with the broader approach Indian procedural law takes to litigants who are genuinely unable to appear in person, including provisions allowing evidence to be recorded by video-conference for a witness located abroad in appropriate cases.

Probate proceedings in Delhi and Kolkata typically require citations to be published, affidavits of assets and valuation to be filed, and, where the Will is contested or a caveat is entered, a more involved trial-like process to prove execution. An NRI executor's attorney manages all of this on the ground, but major decisions, such as whether to contest a caveat, how to value and distribute specific assets, or whether to accept a settlement with a disputing family member, remain the executor's own to make and should be communicated to the attorney in writing rather than left to the attorney's own discretion.

Where the estate includes assets across more than one state, for instance a flat in Noida and a demat account held with a Kolkata-based broker, the executor's attorney may need a grant recognised or re-sealed, or may need to coordinate parallel filings, and should flag this cross-jurisdictional dimension to the court at the outset rather than discover it midway through administration.

What an NRI executor can and cannot do before the grant issues

Before probate or letters of administration are actually granted, an executor's authority is limited, and this applies equally to a resident and an NRI executor. An executor can generally take steps necessary to preserve the estate, such as ensuring property is secured and insured, but cannot distribute assets to beneficiaries, sell immovable property, or close out investments as though the grant had already issued.

Banks and registrars in India are, in this firm's experience, cautious about releasing funds or transferring securities on the strength of a Will alone, without either probate, letters of administration, or in some cases a succession certificate limited to movable assets, particularly where the amounts involved are significant or the family situation suggests any possibility of dispute. An NRI executor who assumes a certified copy of the Will will be enough to unlock an Indian bank account is usually disappointed, and building the realistic timeline for obtaining the grant into the family's expectations early avoids unnecessary friction with relatives waiting for distribution.

Multiple executors, renunciation and what happens when one simply will not act

Wills often name more than one executor, sometimes deliberately to spread the burden, sometimes simply because the testator could not choose between children and named them jointly. Where one named executor is an NRI unwilling or unable to take on the practical burden of administering an Indian estate, that executor can formally renounce the executorship, allowing the remaining named executor or executors to proceed without needing that person's active participation.

Renunciation has to be done formally, usually by a signed instrument filed with the probate application, and an executor who simply stays silent and uninvolved, without formally renouncing, can leave the administration in an ambiguous position that invites exactly the kind of family dispute the Will was meant to prevent. Where every named executor is unwilling or unable to act, the application proceeds instead as one for letters of administration with the Will annexed, under Section 234, to whichever eligible person next applies.

Families planning a Will where the natural choice of executor lives abroad should discuss this possibility candidly at the drafting stage, naming a willing, India-based alternate executor to step in if the NRI executor is unable to manage the practical burden when the time comes, rather than leaving the family to improvise a solution after the fact.

An alternate executor clause costs nothing to include and almost never causes offence when it is explained honestly as a practical contingency rather than a vote of no confidence in the first choice. It is one of the simplest additions a drafter can make to a Will that otherwise names an NRI as sole executor, and it is surprising how rarely families think to ask for it until this specific problem is pointed out to them.

What the delay actually costs a family, and why it is worth planning around

The individual steps described above, locating and attesting a power of attorney, filing a probate or letters of administration petition, publishing citations, and, where the court asks for one, arranging an administration bond and sureties, each add weeks rather than days once an NRI executor is involved, and they compound rather than running in parallel in most registries. A family that assumed the estate would be settled within a couple of months of the death is often still waiting well past that point, not because anything has gone wrong, but because nobody budgeted for the apostille chain and the court's own listing schedule.

This matters most where the estate includes an asset that cannot simply sit idle, a business that needs an authorised signatory, a tenanted property whose rent needs collecting and accounting for, or a loan account that continues to accrue interest. Families in this position sometimes apply for limited, interim relief from the court, such as permission to collect rent or maintain insurance pending the final grant, rather than waiting for the full administration to conclude before anything at all can be done.

The honest advice to give a family at the Will-drafting stage, long before any of this becomes urgent, is to weigh the sentimental choice of executor against the practical one. Naming an NRI child as executor is not wrong, and is often exactly what the family wants, but it should be paired with a clear-eyed conversation about the attorney, the sureties and the timeline that choice will actually involve, so that the choice is made with full information rather than discovered the hard way after the event.

A practical sequence for an executor living outside India

Begin by establishing, with Indian counsel, whether probate or letters of administration are actually required for this specific estate at all, given the 2025 repeal of Section 213 for many Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist and Jain estates, or whether the family can proceed on the strength of the Will alone for most assets and reserve a court application only for whichever specific asset, typically immovable property or listed securities, actually demands it.

Where a court application is needed, decide early whether the executor will apply personally on a future visit to India or proceed through an attorney under Section 241, and if the latter, identify a trusted, India-based person to hold that power of attorney and begin the apostille, consular attestation and stamping process immediately, since this step alone can take several weeks depending on the country involved.

Ask Indian counsel specifically, rather than assuming either way, whether an administration bond is likely to be demanded in this estate given the religion of the deceased, the composition of the family, and the practice of the particular court, and identify potential sureties in India before the question becomes urgent. Finally, keep every beneficiary informed of the realistic timeline from the outset; an estate that takes the time these formalities genuinely require causes far less family friction than one where beneficiaries were led to expect distribution in weeks and are still waiting many months later.

Where the estate is substantial enough, or the family relationships fraught enough, that a contest seems likely, it is worth asking counsel at the outset whether a caveat has already been or is likely to be lodged by a disappointed relative, since a caveated matter moves into a more adversarial, evidence-led process that an executor abroad will need to resource quite differently from an uncontested grant. Building this possibility into the executor's expectations from the first conversation, rather than treating a caveat as an unforeseen emergency, keeps the family's response measured rather than reactive.

An NRI or foreign-based executor loses none of the authority a Will gives them merely by living outside India, but exercising that authority runs through a more procedural path than an executor physically present in Delhi or Kolkata would face, built around Section 241's attorney mechanism, the apostille or consular attestation chain, and the still-unsettled question of when an administration bond will actually be demanded.

None of these steps is, on its own, especially difficult, and most can be started well before a death actually occurs if a family is willing to plan for it. The families who struggle are almost always the ones who assumed naming a trusted child abroad as executor was the end of the planning, rather than its beginning, and who only learned about Section 241, apostille chains and administration bonds at the moment they had the least patience left to absorb them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an NRI or a foreign citizen be named as executor of a Will governing property in India?

Yes. The Indian Succession Act, 1925 does not disqualify a person from acting as executor on the basis of nationality or residence abroad. The practical question is how that executor exercises authority from outside India, typically through Section 241's attorney mechanism.

Does an NRI executor have to travel to India to obtain probate?

Not necessarily. Under Section 241 of the Indian Succession Act, where the executor is absent and no executor present in the state is willing to act, the court can grant letters of administration with the Will annexed to the executor's properly constituted attorney, limited until the executor personally takes probate.

Will an NRI executor's attorney have to furnish a bond to the court?

It depends on the facts. Section 291(1) generally exempts a Section 241 grant to an absent executor's attorney from the bond requirement, but Section 291(2) allows the court to still demand a bond where the deceased was Hindu, Muslim, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain, and recent High Court orders show this remains a genuinely contested question that should be checked for the specific estate.

Is probate still mandatory for a Hindu family's Will in Kolkata?

Generally no, for the categories of immovable property Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act used to cover, since the Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 omitted that section with effect from 21 December 2025. Families often still choose to obtain probate voluntarily for the certainty it gives banks, registrars and purchasers, and other provisions on probate and letters of administration remain in force.

How does a power of attorney executed abroad get recognised by an Indian probate court?

It generally needs to be notarised and then apostilled where the country is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, or attested by the Indian embassy or consulate where it is not, and then stamped under the applicable Indian state's stamp law before it can be relied upon in court or before a bank or registrar.

Does an NRI executor have to personally appear in the Indian probate court?

Usually not. Indian courts have generally been willing to dispense with an executor's personal appearance where a validly attested power of attorney is on record and the constituted attorney appears to represent the executor's interests in the proceeding.

What happens if an NRI named as executor does not want the responsibility?

That executor can formally renounce the executorship by a signed instrument filed with the probate application, allowing any remaining named executor to proceed, or, if none remain, allowing an application for letters of administration with the Will annexed to proceed instead.

Can an NRI executor access the deceased's Indian bank account before probate is granted?

Generally no. Banks typically require probate, letters of administration or a succession certificate before releasing significant funds, and an executor's authority before the grant is largely limited to preserving the estate rather than distributing or liquidating it.
