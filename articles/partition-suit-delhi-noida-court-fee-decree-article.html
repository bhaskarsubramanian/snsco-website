The Suit for Partition in Delhi and Noida: Court Fees, the Two-Decree Structure and How Long It Actually Takes

Why a civil suit for partition does not end with a single decree, how Delhi's and Uttar Pradesh's court-fee rules can turn on whether a co-owner is already in possession, and what the Limitation Act does and does not bar

By Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 23 November 2026 · Law stated as on 23 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family in Delhi or Noida that jointly owns a house, a plot or ancestral agricultural land, and that can no longer agree on how to use or sell it, usually assumes a partition suit will end the matter once a judge decides each party's share. In practice, a civil court's decree on shares is only the first of two decrees a partition suit produces, and a co-owner planning to file one needs to understand both that two-stage structure and a court-fee rule that depends on a fact many plaints get wrong: whether the person filing is actually in possession of the property already.

Key points

  • Order 20 Rule 18 of the Code of Civil Procedure splits a partition decree into two stages: an initial decree, called a preliminary decree where the property is not government land, followed by a final decree passed only after the actual division is carried out, usually through a court-appointed commissioner under Order 26 Rule 13.
  • The suit remains pending between these two stages, and the Supreme Court has held there is no executable decree at all until the final decree is passed, which means a co-owner cannot take possession of their share simply on the strength of the preliminary decree.
  • In Delhi, which follows the Punjab High Court Rules and Orders alongside the Court Fees Act, 1870, a co-owner pleaded as being in actual or constructive possession generally pays a fixed court fee under Article 17(vi) of Schedule II, while a co-owner who has been excluded from possession and seeks restoration of joint possession must pay an ad valorem fee calculated under Section 7(iv)(b).
  • The Limitation Act, 1963 sets no specific time limit for a partition suit as such, since a co-owner's right to seek partition does not itself expire; the twelve-year bar that does arise comes instead from proving that another co-owner's possession has turned adverse through a clear ouster, or that the plaintiff was excluded from a joint family's property and became aware of that exclusion more than twelve years before filing.
  • Court-fee valuation and jurisdictional valuation are treated separately under the Punjab High Court Rules applicable in Delhi: the court fee follows the Court Fees Act's own rules, while the pecuniary jurisdiction of the court is fixed by the value of the entire property, not merely the plaintiff's claimed share.
  • Uttar Pradesh, and therefore Noida and Greater Noida, applies its own version of the Court Fees Act with distinct ad valorem provisions, and a Noida litigant should not assume the Delhi possession-based rule, or any specific fraction of share value, applies without checking the current UP Act text and recent Allahabad High Court rulings.
  • A final decree application is not treated as a fresh suit, and courts have held it is not independently subject to the general three-year limitation under Article 137 of the Limitation Act in the way a standalone application would be, though a party should still move the final decree stage without unreasonable delay once the preliminary decree is passed.

Why a partition decree is not one event but two

A partition suit is unusual among civil proceedings in that winning the suit does not, by itself, give a co-owner anything they can act on. Order 20 Rule 18 of the Code of Civil Procedure, which governs how a court decrees a suit for partition of property or for separate possession of a share in it, distinguishes between the court's decision on who is entitled to what share, and the physical division of the property that actually implements that decision. Where the property is not land assessed to the payment of revenue to the government, which covers the great majority of urban residential, commercial and agricultural land that is not itself a government revenue estate, Rule 18(2) directs the court to pass what case law has settled into calling a preliminary decree, declaring the rights of the several parties interested in the property, and to pass a further, final decree later once the actual partition or separation has been effected.

This two-stage structure is not a procedural formality that can be collapsed into a single hearing. The preliminary decree establishes, often after a full trial on contested questions of title, who the co-owners are and in what shares they hold the property, but it does not itself tell any party which specific portion of the property they get, how a property that cannot be physically divided without loss of value will be dealt with, or how accounts between the co-owners, for rent collected, taxes paid, or improvements made by one party, will be settled. All of that is worked out at the final decree stage, and the suit itself remains pending, not concluded, throughout the interval between the two decrees.

Where the property in question is land assessed to the payment of revenue to the government, a less common situation for most urban Delhi or Noida families but one that still arises for agricultural holdings on the fringes of Noida and Greater Noida, Rule 18(1) applies instead, and the court simply passes a decree declaring the rights of the parties without the preliminary and final decree distinction that Rule 18(2) creates, leaving the actual partition of such land to be carried out through the revenue authorities rather than through a court-appointed commissioner. A family with mixed holdings, some urban residential property and some agricultural land on the city's edge, should expect the two categories of property to follow these two different procedural tracks within what might otherwise feel like a single family dispute, rather than assuming one uniform process will apply to the entire estate, and should plan for the possibility that one part of the dispute resolves on a materially different timeline than the other.

From preliminary decree to final decree: what actually happens in between

Once a preliminary decree is passed, the next procedural step is usually a reference to a court-appointed commissioner under Order 26 Rule 13 of the Code, who is directed to physically inspect the property, propose a workable division consistent with the shares the preliminary decree has fixed, and report back to the court. Where the property can be divided by metes and bounds without destroying its character or value, a house that can be split into separate, independently accessible portions, or a large plot that can be subdivided, the commissioner's report typically forms the basis for the final decree allotting specific portions to specific parties. Where the property cannot be sensibly divided in this way, a single residential flat, for instance, the court can instead direct a sale of the property and division of the proceeds according to the shares fixed in the preliminary decree, a course co-owners and their counsel should anticipate and plan for from the outset rather than assume a physical division will always be available.

The Supreme Court settled a long-standing question about enforceability during this interval in Shankar Balwant Lokhande v. Chandrakant Shankar Lokhande, holding that there is no executable decree in a partition suit until the final decree is actually passed; the preliminary decree is a stage in a suit that remains pending, not a self-contained adjudication a party can execute to obtain possession. This means a co-owner who has obtained a favourable preliminary decree, establishing, say, a half share in the property, generally cannot go to the executing court and demand possession of that half; the final decree, specifying exactly what portion corresponds to that half or ordering a sale, is ordinarily the document execution actually proceeds on. A 2026 Supreme Court ruling has since narrowed this rule's practical reach: where a preliminary decree itself already specifies a complete mechanism for dealing with the property, including a sale or auction fallback if physical division proves impracticable, that decree may be treated as both preliminary and final and executed without a further, separately filed final decree application, since its substance rather than its label controls.

A 2026 Supreme Court ruling on treating the final-decree stage practically

A recurring practical problem in partition suits is a decree that already specifies what should happen if the property cannot actually be divided by metes and bounds, typically a sale by public auction with division of the proceeds, and a party then being told that the decree is only 'preliminary' and that a fresh, separately numbered final decree application must still be filed before execution can proceed. The Supreme Court addressed exactly this in Jennifer Messias v. Leonard G. Lobo, 2026 INSC 502, decided 18 May 2026 by Justices K.V. Viswanathan and S.V.N. Bhatti. A trial court's 13 April 2012 decree had conclusively fixed the parties' shares and their entitlement to possession and mesne profits, and had itself provided for a sale by public auction and division of the proceeds if physical partition proved impracticable; a court-appointed Commissioner duly reported that physical division was impracticable, but the Madhya Pradesh High Court twice halted execution, holding the decree was merely preliminary and sending the decree-holder back to file a separate final decree application.

The Supreme Court held that the High Court had gone by the decree's label rather than its substance: because the decree had already conclusively determined every question a final decree would otherwise need to resolve, including the fallback sale mechanism, it functioned as both a preliminary and a final decree, and no separate final decree application was required before execution could proceed, since a decree can in appropriate cases be partly preliminary and partly final. The Court also held that an order made under Sections 2 to 4 of the Partition Act, 1893 is itself a deemed decree within the meaning of Section 2(2) of the Code of Civil Procedure. The appeal was allowed and the executing court's order directing auction and division of the sale proceeds was restored. The practical lesson for drafting and reading a preliminary decree in Delhi or Noida is to look closely at what the decree itself already specifies: a decree that only declares shares without addressing how an indivisible property is to be dealt with still needs a separate final decree application and a commissioner's process, but a decree that already builds in a complete mechanism, including a sale or auction fallback, may be executable without that further application, whatever label the decree carries, and a court or executing authority should not insist on a fresh final-decree filing merely because the decree is headed 'preliminary'.

Delhi's court-fee rule: everything turns on whether you plead possession

Delhi's court-fee practice in partition suits is governed by the Court Fees Act, 1870 as it applies to Delhi, read together with the Punjab High Court Rules and Orders, which continue to apply in Delhi for matters the Delhi High Court has not separately regulated. The central distinction Delhi courts have drawn is between a co-owner who is already in possession, actual or constructive, of the property and simply wants that possession formally divided, and a co-owner who has actually been excluded or ousted from the property and is seeking restoration of joint possession as part of the partition relief.

Where the plaintiff pleads, and the court accepts, that they are in constructive possession, on the settled principle that every co-owner is presumed to be in possession of the whole property, or at least of their undivided share, along with the other co-owners, the applicable court fee is a fixed fee under Article 17(vi) of Schedule II to the Court Fees Act, a modest, non-ad-valorem amount that does not depend on the value of the property or the plaintiff's share. This fixed-fee treatment reflects the underlying legal position that a co-owner in possession is not asking the court to give them something they do not already have; they are asking the court to formalise and separate an entitlement they already, in law, hold jointly.

Where the plaint itself shows, or the court finds, that the plaintiff has actually been excluded from possession, whether by another co-owner's exclusive occupation, a sale to a third party, or some other act of ouster, Delhi courts have required ad valorem court fee under Section 7(iv)(b) of the Act, calculated on the market value of the plaintiff's share, since the plaintiff in that situation is not simply formalising an existing joint possession but is actually seeking to be restored to possession they do not currently enjoy, a materially different relief from the court-fee perspective. Delhi High Court decisions have specifically held that where a plaint is silent on the question of constructive possession, or where the pleaded facts do not support a presumption of possession, the plaintiff can be directed to pay the correct ad valorem fee rather than being allowed to proceed on the lower fixed fee.

Why this distinction matters more than it might first appear

The gap between the two fee treatments is not trivial, and it has a practical consequence beyond the immediate cost of filing. A plaint drafted carelessly, asserting a right to partition without clearly pleading the specific facts that establish actual or constructive possession, risks being treated as falling into the ad valorem category by default, which can mean a court fee running into a substantial sum where the property is valuable, payable upfront or shortly after the court raises the question, often at a stage when the plaintiff has not budgeted for that outlay. Conversely, a plaintiff who is genuinely excluded from the property but pleads constructive possession loosely, hoping to pay the lower fixed fee, risks having the plaint rejected or the suit stayed until the correct fee is paid, which can also affect limitation if the defect is not promptly cured.

The safer drafting practice, for a Delhi partition suit, is to plead the actual facts of possession precisely and honestly rather than reaching for whichever characterisation produces the lower court fee, since the court is not bound by how a plaint characterises possession and will look at the substance of what is actually pleaded and, eventually, proved. Where the facts genuinely support constructive possession, a co-owner living elsewhere but with recognised access to and use of the property, receiving a share of rent, or otherwise exercising incidents of ownership, that should be pleaded specifically rather than left as a bare assertion, since a bare assertion invites exactly the scrutiny that leads courts to direct the higher, ad valorem fee instead.

Jurisdictional value is a separate question from court-fee value

A further point that catches out plaints drafted without close attention to Delhi's specific rules is that the court fee payable and the value used to determine which court has pecuniary jurisdiction over the suit are not necessarily the same figure. Under the Punjab High Court Rules applicable in Delhi, the jurisdictional value for a partition suit is generally fixed by reference to the value of the entire property, not merely the value of the share the plaintiff is claiming, even where the court fee itself, calculated under the Court Fees Act, is assessed on a different basis, whether fixed under Article 17(vi) or ad valorem on the plaintiff's share under Section 7(iv)(b).

This separation matters because a suit concerning a high-value property can fall within the pecuniary jurisdiction of a more senior court, potentially a Commercial Court or the Delhi High Court's original side depending on the figures involved, even where the court fee actually paid is the modest fixed amount applicable to a possession-based partition claim. Counsel filing a partition suit in Delhi should calculate the two figures separately from the outset, valuing the whole property for jurisdictional purposes and valuing the specific relief sought for court-fee purposes, rather than assuming a single valuation exercise serves both functions, since getting this wrong can result in a plaint being returned for presentation before the correct court even after the correct court fee has been paid.

Noida and Greater Noida: the Uttar Pradesh position is not simply Delhi's rule carried across the border

A family with property or members on both sides of the Delhi-Uttar Pradesh border, a common situation for families with roots in Delhi who have since acquired property in Noida or Greater Noida, should not assume the Delhi possession-based fee rule applies unchanged once the relevant property sits in Uttar Pradesh. The Uttar Pradesh Court Fees Act contains its own ad valorem provisions, distinct in numbering and in some particulars from the Court Fees Act as it applies in Delhi, and this research did not find a UP-specific ruling confirming the exact fraction of share value, whether a quarter, a half, or the full share value, that applies to a partition suit valuation in the way Delhi's possession-based distinction has been confirmed by Delhi High Court authority; different reported figures appear in material from other states applying their own, separately amended versions of the Act, and none of that out-of-state authority should be treated as settled law for Uttar Pradesh without independent confirmation.

What can be said with reasonable confidence for a Noida or Greater Noida filing is that the general principle distinguishing a co-owner in possession from one who has been excluded is likely to inform how a UP court approaches the court-fee question, since this distinction traces back to common law principles about constructive possession among co-owners that are not unique to Delhi's rules, but the specific statutory mechanics, which section of the UP Act applies, whether a fixed or ad valorem fee results, and at what rate, should be confirmed against the current UP Act text and any recent Allahabad High Court rulings on partition-suit valuation before a plaint is filed, rather than simply assumed by analogy to Delhi practice without any further local inquiry. A plaintiff and their counsel filing in Gautam Buddh Nagar should budget for the possibility that the correct fee may need to be clarified with the court registry or through a specific application at the time of filing, rather than treated as settled by reference to Delhi authority that does not bind a UP court.

Limitation: there is no fixed period for seeking partition, but there is one for proving ouster

A common misconception is that a partition suit, like many other civil claims, must be filed within a fixed number of years of some triggering event. The Limitation Act, 1963 does not, in fact, prescribe any specific limitation period for a suit for partition as such, reflecting the underlying legal principle that a co-owner's right to seek partition of jointly held property does not itself expire merely through the passage of time; the possession of one co-owner is, in law, treated as possession on behalf of all the co-owners, so the mere continuation of a joint holding for many years, without more, does not by itself bar a later co-owner from seeking division.

What can, and often does, bar a partition claim is a different and more demanding showing: that another co-owner's possession has actually turned hostile and adverse to the plaintiff's interest, amounting to an ouster, and that this adverse, exclusionary possession has continued openly for twelve years or more before the suit was filed. Courts have been clear that the burden of proving ouster rests on the party asserting it, generally the defendant resisting the partition claim, and that mere exclusive physical occupation by one co-owner, paying all the outgoings or managing the property alone, is not by itself proof of ouster, since co-owners commonly arrange their affairs so that one of them manages or occupies the property for practical convenience without that arrangement being intended, or understood, as a claim to exclusive ownership.

A related but distinct limitation question arises where a member of a joint family claims to have been excluded from ancestral property; in that context, courts have looked to when the exclusion became known to the plaintiff as the starting point for the twelve-year period, rather than to the date exclusion is alleged to have actually begun, which can matter considerably where a family member living away from the property only later discovers that their interest has been denied. Because the authorities applying these principles have not spoken with complete uniformity on which specific Limitation Act article governs a given fact pattern, a co-owner anticipating a limitation defence should gather and preserve, as early as possible, clear evidence of when any exclusion or denial of title actually came to their knowledge, since that date, not simply the date they stopped living at the property, is likely to be the one that matters.

Whether a final-decree application has its own separate limitation clock

A further limitation question that has caused confusion in practice is whether an application to pass the final decree, once the preliminary decree has become final, must itself be filed within the general three-year period prescribed under Article 137 of the Limitation Act, 1963 for applications generally. Authority on this point has treated an application for a final decree as part of the pending suit rather than as an independent application attracting its own, separate limitation period, reasoning that the suit itself has not concluded and that Article 137 is concerned with distinct applications made outside a pending proceeding rather than with the continuing stages of one and the same suit.

This should not, however, be read as giving a party unlimited time to move the final-decree stage without consequence. A court faced with an application to proceed to a final decree many years after the preliminary decree was passed, with no explanation for the intervening delay, retains the ordinary discretion to scrutinise that delay, including by asking whether the parties' conduct in the interim is consistent with having abandoned the claim, or whether intervening events, such as further transfers of the property or deaths among the parties requiring fresh impleadment of legal heirs, have materially changed the facts since the preliminary decree was passed. The practically sound course remains to move the final-decree application promptly, generally within months rather than years of the preliminary decree becoming final, rather than relying on the absence of a hard limitation bar as licence to delay.

Section 97 CPC: appeal the preliminary decree, or lose the right to challenge it later

One of the more consequential procedural traps in a partition suit is Section 97 of the Code of Civil Procedure, which bars a party from challenging the correctness of a preliminary decree in a later appeal against the final decree, where that party had the opportunity to appeal the preliminary decree itself and did not do so. Courts applying this provision have treated an unappealed preliminary decree as attaining finality on every question it actually decided, shares, title, and the entitlement of each party, which means a co-owner dissatisfied with how the preliminary decree fixed the shares must raise that challenge by way of appeal against the preliminary decree itself, within the ordinary limitation period for filing that appeal, rather than waiting to see how the final division plays out and raising the objection only then.

Courts have read this bar broadly, extending it to challenges going to the legal validity of the preliminary decree and not merely to factual disputes about its correctness, and have applied the same bar where an appeal against the preliminary decree was in fact filed but was dismissed, treating that dismissal, once it becomes final, as equally conclusive. A narrow exception has been argued, though not uniformly confirmed as settled law, where the preliminary decree is attacked as having been obtained through fraud or collusion, since a decree procured by fraud is in a different category from one merely decided incorrectly on the merits, but a party relying on this exception should not assume it will automatically succeed and should, wherever genuinely possible, simply appeal the preliminary decree itself rather than hope a fraud exception will later excuse the failure to do so.

The practical consequence for a co-owner in a Delhi or Noida partition suit is that the preliminary decree stage, not the final decree stage, is usually the real opportunity to contest questions of title and share, and a party who lets that stage pass without appealing, whether out of inattention, cost considerations, or a mistaken belief that the matter can be revisited at the final-decree stage, generally forecloses that challenge permanently. Counsel should accordingly treat the preliminary decree with the same seriousness as a final judgment for the purpose of deciding whether to appeal, rather than as an interim order that can comfortably be left unchallenged while the suit continues.

Accounting for rent and profits between co-owners before the property is finally divided

A partition suit frequently involves more than a bare division of property, because one co-owner has often been collecting rent from a tenant occupying part of the jointly held property, or otherwise deriving an income from it, while the other co-owners have received nothing from that arrangement. Courts have treated an accounting for such rent and profits as implicit in a partition suit even where the original plaint did not specifically pray for it, holding that the absence of a prayer for mesne profits or rendition of accounts from the date of suit does not prevent the court from entertaining an application on that question at a later stage, generally before the final decree is passed.

The characterisation of this relief matters: because a co-owner in possession and collecting rent is not, in the ordinary case, a trespasser or a person in wrongful possession as against the other co-owners, the relief courts actually grant is usually framed as an account of the rent collected and its fair distribution among the co-owners in proportion to their shares, rather than as mesne profits in the strict sense that applies against a wrongdoer in unlawful possession. A recent Delhi High Court decision illustrates this approach directly, holding that where one co-sharer had rented out a portion of a jointly owned property and collected the rent, the court should hold an inquiry to fix the appropriate rent and direct that it be contributed to a common fund for distribution among all the co-sharers in their declared proportions, on the reasoning that every part of an undivided property remains jointly owned by all the co-owners until the property is actually divided by metes and bounds.

Whether this accounting can still be pursued after the final decree has already been passed without any such claim having been raised earlier is a question courts have not answered uniformly; some authority suggests the enquiry into profits should ordinarily have been concluded by the time the final decree is drawn up, while other decisions have left open whether a separate suit for an account, resting on its own distinct cause of action, remains available afterward. The safer course for a co-owner who knows another co-owner has been collecting rent or other income from the property is to raise the accounting claim explicitly during the partition suit itself, ideally before the final decree is passed, rather than assuming the point can simply be taken up separately once the physical division is complete, and to keep careful, contemporaneous records of tenancy agreements, rent receipts and bank deposits wherever those are available, since the commissioner conducting the inquiry will generally rely more heavily on documentary evidence of this kind than on the parties' competing oral accounts of how much was actually collected.

Drafting and sequencing a partition suit in Delhi NCR

A plaint for partition filed in Delhi or Noida should set out the complete chain of title establishing each co-owner's share, whether through inheritance under the applicable personal law, a registered conveyance, or a combination of both across generations, since disputes over the correct shares are usually the most contested part of the preliminary decree stage and the part most likely to require oral and documentary evidence at trial. The plaint should also specifically plead the facts bearing on possession, as discussed above, rather than leaving that question to be inferred, and should anticipate, where the property cannot realistically be divided by metes and bounds, that the final relief may take the form of a sale and division of proceeds rather than physical division, pleading that alternative specifically rather than leaving it to emerge only at the final-decree stage.

Where family members are scattered across Delhi, Noida and sometimes further afield, counsel should also address at the outset which co-owners need to be impleaded as parties, since a partition decree binds only the parties actually before the court, and a co-owner left out of the suit retains an independent right to seek their own partition later, potentially unsettling an arrangement the other co-owners believed was final. Securing complete impleadment of every co-owner, including minor co-owners through a appropriate guardian and any co-owners who may dispute their own exclusion from a family settlement, is accordingly one of the more consequential early decisions in structuring a Delhi NCR partition suit, since an incomplete suit risks producing a decree that resolves the dispute only on paper while leaving a further partition claim available to whoever was left out.

A family considering litigation should also weigh whether a negotiated family settlement or a registered partition deed might resolve the dispute without a suit at all, since a suit for partition, even handled efficiently, usually takes considerably longer than a consensual division the co-owners themselves agree to and record. Where even partial agreement exists, agreement on most of the property with one disputed portion, or agreement on shares but not on which specific portion each party gets, structuring the litigation narrowly around the actual point of disagreement, rather than placing the entire property before the court as though nothing were agreed, can meaningfully shorten both the preliminary and final decree stages, since the court then has less to adjudicate and the commissioner's task at the final-decree stage is correspondingly narrower.

Registering the final decree and updating revenue and municipal records

Once a final decree is passed, whether dividing the property by metes and bounds or directing a sale and division of proceeds, the parties still need to ensure the division is reflected in the underlying property and revenue records, since a court decree settles the legal question of entitlement but does not by itself automatically update every register a buyer, lender or tax authority will later look to. Where the final decree allots specific, physically demarcated portions to specific parties, mutation of those portions in the municipal corporation's property tax records, and, for land falling under revenue jurisdiction, in the khasra, khatauni or other revenue record maintained by the local revenue authority, should be pursued promptly using a certified copy of the final decree, since an un-mutated decree leaves the practical record of ownership out of step with the legal position the court has fixed.

Section 17(2)(vi) of the Registration Act, 1908 generally exempts a court decree or order from compulsory registration, on the reasoning that a decree adjudicates pre-existing rights rather than operating as a fresh conveyance executed by the parties, a position the Supreme Court has repeatedly applied starting with Bhoop Singh v. Ram Singh Major, though the Court has carved out an exception for a compromise decree dealing with immovable property beyond what was actually in dispute in the suit, since that kind of decree functions, in substance, as a transfer the parties have engineered through the litigation rather than a genuine adjudication. Whether this exemption extends cleanly to every partition decree specifically is a point on which reported decisions have not spoken with one voice, some treating a partition decree as falling squarely within the Section 17(2)(vi) exemption and others suggesting registration may still be required in particular circumstances, so a party relying on a final decree to deal with their allotted portion afterward, selling it, mortgaging it, or gifting it, should confirm the current, case-specific position with the relevant Sub-Registrar rather than assuming the decree alone will be accepted without further documentation in every instance, and should budget for the cost and time of a separate registered instrument as a contingency even where the decree itself is expected to suffice.

A partition suit in Delhi or Noida is better understood from the outset as two linked proceedings rather than one: a preliminary stage that fixes who owns what share, often the more heavily litigated of the two where title or the fact of possession is disputed, and a final stage that actually divides the property or orders its sale, without which no co-owner can execute anything at all. Treating the preliminary decree as the end of the matter, rather than as a staging post, is one of the more common and avoidable sources of delay and confusion in these suits.

Getting the court fee right at the outset, by pleading the actual facts of possession honestly rather than choosing whichever characterisation produces the lower fee, and confirming the correct provision under whichever state's Court Fees Act actually governs the property, in Delhi or in Uttar Pradesh, avoids one of the most common procedural stumbles in these cases. Equally, understanding that there is no fixed limitation period for seeking partition as such, but a real twelve-year exposure once a co-owner can show ouster has been clearly established and has run its course, should shape both when a co-owner decides to file and what evidence they gather well before any suit is actually drafted, and appealing an unfavourable preliminary decree without delay, rather than hoping the point can be raised again at the final-decree stage, remains the single most important procedural decision most co-owners in this kind of litigation ever make.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a partition decree final once the court decides each party's share?

No. Order 20 Rule 18 of the Code of Civil Procedure splits a partition decree into a preliminary decree, which fixes the shares, and a final decree, which actually divides the property or orders its sale, passed only after a court-appointed commissioner's report or equivalent process. The suit remains pending between the two, and the Supreme Court has held there is no executable decree until the final decree is passed. Section 97 of the Code also bars challenging the correctness of an unappealed preliminary decree in a later appeal against the final decree, so questions of title and share are best contested at the preliminary-decree stage itself.

How much court fee is payable for a partition suit in Delhi?

It depends on whether the plaintiff is in possession of the property. A co-owner pleaded as being in actual or constructive possession generally pays a fixed court fee under Article 17(vi) of Schedule II to the Court Fees Act, 1870. A co-owner who has been excluded from possession and is seeking restoration of joint possession must instead pay an ad valorem fee under Section 7(iv)(b), calculated on the value of their share.

Does the Uttar Pradesh court-fee rule for partition suits match Delhi's?

The general distinction between a co-owner in possession and one excluded from possession likely carries over, but the specific statutory provisions and any fixed fraction of share value differ under the Uttar Pradesh Court Fees Act. A Noida or Greater Noida litigant should confirm the current UP Act text and recent Allahabad High Court authority rather than assume Delhi's rule applies unchanged.

Is there a time limit for filing a suit for partition?

The Limitation Act, 1963 does not prescribe a specific period for filing a partition suit as such, since a co-owner's right to seek partition does not itself expire with time. A partition claim can, however, be barred where a co-owner proves that another co-owner's possession turned adverse through a clear ouster that has continued openly for twelve years or more.

What counts as ouster between co-owners for limitation purposes?

Mere exclusive occupation or management of jointly owned property by one co-owner is not, by itself, proof of ouster, since co-owners often arrange for one of them to occupy or manage the property for convenience. Ouster requires clear evidence that the excluded co-owner's title was actually denied, and the burden of proving it, and that it has continued for the required period, rests on the party asserting it.

Who decides how a property is physically divided after a preliminary decree?

The court typically appoints a commissioner under Order 26 Rule 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure to inspect the property and propose a division consistent with the shares fixed in the preliminary decree. Where the property cannot be meaningfully divided by metes and bounds, the court can instead order a sale and division of the proceeds according to those shares.

Is there a deadline to apply for the final decree after the preliminary decree?

Courts have treated an application for the final decree as part of the pending suit rather than an independent application subject to the general three-year limitation under Article 137 of the Limitation Act. That said, a party should move the final-decree stage promptly once the preliminary decree becomes final, since an unexplained long delay can still invite scrutiny from the court.

What happens if one co-owner has been renting out part of the jointly owned property?

Courts generally treat an account of the rent and its fair distribution among co-owners as implicit in a partition suit, even if not specifically claimed in the original plaint, and this can be raised before the final decree is passed. A recent Delhi High Court ruling directed an inquiry into rent collected by one co-sharer and its contribution to a common fund for distribution among all co-sharers in their declared proportions.
