Cheque Dishonour Under Section 138: Notice, Presumptions, Interim Compensation and Director Liability

The statute reads as a short, mechanical sequence of dates. In practice almost every stage, the notice, the presumption, the interim deposit and the appeal, now carries its own body of Supreme Court qualification.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 5 November 2026 · Law stated as on 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A dishonoured cheque looks like the simplest complaint a lawyer can file: a bounced instrument, a statutory notice, and a trial that is supposed to run as a summary proceeding. It rarely stays simple. The thirty-day notice window and the one-month complaint window each carry their own, sometimes unforgiving, limitation law. The presumption that the cheque was issued for a real debt is rebuttable, and the Supreme Court has now set out exactly what an accused has to do to rebut it. Interim compensation and the deposit required for an appeal both turn on words the statute leaves discretionary but that courts, for years, treated as fixed. A director's name on a cheque or a board resolution does not, by itself, put that director in the dock. This piece sets out the sequence as it now stands, with the qualifications that actually decide most of these cases.

Key points

  • A Section 138 complaint has two separate limitation clocks. The statutory notice must issue within thirty days of learning of the dishonour, and the drawer then has fifteen days to pay; if payment is not made, the complaint must generally be filed within one month of the cause of action, which arises on expiry of those fifteen days. Courts have treated the one-month complaint period as capable of condonation for sufficient cause, but delay in issuing the notice itself is treated far more strictly.
  • The Supreme Court held in Sumit Bansal v. MGI Developers and Promoters, 2026 INSC 40, decided 8 January 2026, that several cheques arising from a single transaction do not merge into one cause of action; each dishonoured cheque that completes the statutory sequence can support its own, separate complaint.
  • Sections 118 and 139 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 raise rebuttable presumptions that a cheque was issued for consideration and for the discharge of a real debt or liability. The Supreme Court in Rajesh Jain v. Ajay Singh, 2023 INSC 888, decided 9 October 2023, held that an accused's burden to rebut these presumptions is not a heavy one and can be discharged on a preponderance of probability, through direct or circumstantial evidence, but it still has to be discharged with something more than a bare denial.
  • Interim compensation under Section 143A, up to twenty percent of the cheque amount, is discretionary rather than automatic. The Supreme Court held in Rakesh Ranjan Shrivastava v. State of Jharkhand, decided 15 March 2024, that the word "may" in the section cannot be read as "shall," and that a court must record brief reasons and make at least a prima facie assessment of the complaint's merits before ordering it.
  • An appellate court hearing a Section 138 appeal can require the convicted drawer to deposit a minimum of twenty percent of the fine or compensation as a condition of suspending the sentence, under Section 148. Surinder Singh Deswal v. Virender Gandhi, (2019) 11 SCC 341, treated this twenty percent as close to a floor, but Jamboo Bhandari v. M.P. State Industrial Development Corporation Ltd., decided 4 September 2023, and Muskan Enterprises v. State of Punjab, 2024 INSC 1046, confirmed that an appellate court retains discretion to waive or reduce the deposit in a genuinely exceptional case, provided it records reasons.
  • A company director's liability under Section 141 depends on the role actually pleaded and proved, not on designation. The Supreme Court held in K.S. Mehta v. Morgan Securities and Credits Pvt. Ltd., 2025 INSC 315, that merely attending board meetings does not make a non-executive or independent director liable, and in HDFC Bank Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra, 2025 INSC 759, that there is no blanket rule that every director is in charge of a company's day-to-day affairs; the complaint must specifically allege that the person was in charge of, and responsible for, the conduct of the company's business.
  • A civil recovery suit and a Section 138 complaint on the same cheque can proceed together; the criminal case decides whether a statutory offence was committed, while the civil suit decides the underlying debt, and one does not have to wait for the other. Compounding remains available at any stage under Section 147, though the Supreme Court has made clear that the graded-cost guidelines in Damodar S. Prabhu v. Sayed Babalal H., (2010) 5 SCC 663, are not a rigid formula to be applied in every case.

Why a one-page cheque produces years of litigation

Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 is drafted as a short, almost mechanical sequence: a cheque is dishonoured for insufficiency of funds or because it exceeds an arrangement with the bank, the payee gives notice, the drawer gets a window to pay, and a failure to pay within that window completes the offence. In volume, Section 138 complaints have for years been among the largest single category of criminal cases pending before magistrates in India, a fact the Supreme Court itself has had to confront directly through a dedicated suo motu proceeding discussed later in this piece.

What keeps these cases in court for years is rarely a dispute about whether the cheque bounced. It is almost always a dispute about one of a small number of recurring points: whether the notice and the complaint were filed within time, whether the presumption that the cheque was issued for a real debt has been rebutted, how much interim compensation or deposit the drawer has to pay before or during an appeal, and whether a named director or partner can actually be held liable at all. Each of these points has its own, fairly developed line of Supreme Court authority, much of it updated within the last three years, and getting any one of them wrong at the drafting or filing stage can cost a complainant or an accused the whole case long before the trial court ever gets to the merits of the debt.

The statutory sequence: notice, the fifteen days, and the one-month complaint window

The sequence begins when the cheque is returned unpaid by the drawee bank for insufficiency of funds or because it exceeds an arrangement made with the bank. The payee or holder then has thirty days from the date of learning of the dishonour to issue a written notice to the drawer demanding payment of the cheque amount. The drawer has fifteen days from receipt of that notice to make the payment, and only if payment is not made within those fifteen days does the offence under Section 138 become complete, with the cause of action to prosecute arising on the day after the fifteen days expire.

Section 142(b) then requires the complaint to be made within one month of the date on which the cause of action arose, subject to a proviso allowing a court to take cognizance of a complaint filed after that period if the complainant satisfies the court that there was sufficient cause for the delay. Courts have treated this as genuine discretion rather than a formality: a complainant has to actually explain the delay, and a court is not expected to condone it automatically simply because the proviso exists. The practical effect of getting the sequence wrong has been illustrated starkly in recent reporting on a 2025 Supreme Court decision quashing a complaint filed on the thirty-fifth day after the cause of action arose, where the Court treated the one-month period as a limit the complainant had failed to justify crossing.

Delay in issuing the notice itself is treated more strictly again. Several High Courts have held that Section 142's condonation proviso covers only the one-month period for filing the complaint, not any delay in sending the thirty-day notice, and that the general power to condone delay under Section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963 does not apply where a special statute like this one has its own, express limitation period. A payee who waits too long after learning of a dishonour, hoping to negotiate a settlement before formally invoking Section 138, risks losing the right to prosecute that particular cheque altogether, independent of whatever recovery options remain open through a civil suit.

Multiple cheques, one transaction: why each dishonour can be its own complaint

A recurring defence argument, where a single underlying transaction produced several post-dated or instalment cheques, is that the cheques together represent one liability and so should give rise, at most, to one combined cause of action rather than a separate prosecution for each dishonoured instrument. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning in Sumit Bansal v. MGI Developers and Promoters, 2026 INSC 40, decided 8 January 2026 by Sanjay Karol and Prashant Kumar Mishra JJ.

The dispute arose from an agreement to sell dated 7 November 2016, under which the complainant had paid money to a developer who issued several cheques that were later dishonoured. The High Court had partly quashed one of the resulting complaints on the theory that multiple cheques covering the same underlying liability should be treated as substitutes for one another, producing a single composite cause of action. The Supreme Court set that view aside, holding that a separate cause of action arises on each dishonour provided the statutory sequence, notice, fifteen days, and non-payment, is independently completed for that cheque, and that the fact several cheques trace back to one commercial transaction does not by itself merge them into a single prosecution.

The Court also reiterated a more general point about how these complaints get challenged before trial: whether a legally enforceable debt actually existed is ordinarily a matter for the trial court to decide on evidence, not something a High Court should resolve at the quashing stage under Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, a power the Court described as one to be used sparingly rather than as a substitute for trial.

The presumption of debt: Sections 118 and 139, and what it takes to rebut them

Section 118 of the Act raises a general presumption, until the contrary is proved, that a negotiable instrument was made or drawn for consideration, reversing the ordinary civil rule that a party relying on a contract has to prove consideration for it. Section 139 goes further for cheques specifically, presuming that the holder of a cheque received it for the discharge, in whole or in part, of a debt or other liability, again unless the contrary is proved. Together these provisions mean that once a complainant establishes the basic facts, that the cheque was signed by the accused and was dishonoured, the burden shifts to the accused to show that the cheque was not, in fact, issued for a debt or liability.

The question that produced years of inconsistent trial court practice was exactly how much the accused has to show to discharge that burden. The Supreme Court answered it in Rajesh Jain v. Ajay Singh, 2023 INSC 888 (also reported as 2023 SCC OnLine SC 1275), decided 9 October 2023 by Aravind Kumar and S.V.N. Bhatti JJ. The Court held that the accused's burden is not a heavy one and can be discharged on a preponderance of probability, consistent with the standard set out earlier in Rangappa v. Sri Mohan, (2010) 11 SCC 441, and that the accused may rely on direct evidence, circumstantial evidence, or inconsistencies and admissions drawn out of the complainant's own case, rather than being required to produce an affirmative case of their own to the same standard a prosecution would need.

What the accused cannot do, on this authority, is simply deny the transaction and expect the presumption to fall away on its own. The Supreme Court in Rajesh Jain itself went on to apply this standard against the accused, restoring a conviction after the trial court and High Court had accepted a bare denial as sufficient to rebut the presumption, and the judgment sets out a structured list of questions a court should put to an accused once the presumption applies, aimed at testing whether the explanation offered actually holds together on the specific facts rather than simply asserting that no debt existed.

Interim compensation under Section 143A: discretionary, not automatic

The Negotiable Instruments (Amendment) Act, 2018 inserted Section 143A, allowing a trial court, while the complaint is pending, to direct the drawer to pay the complainant interim compensation of up to twenty percent of the cheque amount, intended to give a complainant some relief before the trial concludes rather than leaving them without any payment for years. Many trial courts, reading the twenty percent figure as close to automatic once a complaint is admitted, ordered it as a matter of course.

The Supreme Court corrected that practice in Rakesh Ranjan Shrivastava v. State of Jharkhand, decided 15 March 2024. The Court held that Section 143A uses the word "may," which cannot be read as "shall," and that reading it as mandatory would mean an accused in every Section 138 complaint having to pay up to a fifth of the cheque amount merely because a complaint was filed, before any finding on guilt. The Court directed that an application for interim compensation requires the trial court to make at least a prima facie evaluation of the merits of the complaint and to record brief reasons for its decision, whether it grants the relief or declines it, and that the amount actually ordered, up to the twenty percent ceiling in Section 143A(2), can properly be less than the maximum depending on that assessment.

For a complainant, this means an application for interim compensation needs to be supported by more than a recital that Section 143A exists; it should point to the specific material, the cheque, the dishonour memo, the notice and the absence of a credible defence raised so far, that makes the complaint look strong enough to justify an interim payment. For a drawer resisting the application, the response should engage with that same material rather than arguing only that the provision is discretionary in the abstract, since the discretion is exercised on the facts of the specific complaint, not as a general policy against interim payments.

Appeals and Section 148: the twenty percent deposit and its exceptions

A drawer convicted under Section 138 and sentenced to a fine, compensation, or imprisonment has a right of appeal, and Section 148, also inserted by the 2018 amendment, lets the appellate court suspend the sentence pending appeal on the condition that the appellant deposits a minimum of twenty percent of the fine or compensation awarded by the trial court. The provision was intended to discourage appeals filed mainly to delay payment rather than to genuinely contest the conviction.

In Surinder Singh Deswal v. Virender Gandhi, (2019) 11 SCC 341, the Supreme Court read Section 148 as having two steps: the appellate court first decides whether to order a deposit at all as a condition of suspending the sentence, and once it decides to do so, a minimum of twenty percent must be ordered. High Courts generally treated this as leaving little room for discretion once an appellate court decided to grant suspension, applying the twenty percent floor almost as a fixed rule.

The Supreme Court qualified that reading in a decision dated 4 September 2023, in Jamboo Bhandari v. M.P. State Industrial Development Corporation Ltd. (reported as 2023 LiveLaw (SC) 776), holding by Abhay S. Oka and Pankaj Mithal JJ. that the word "may" in Section 148 still leaves the appellate court with discretion to decline the deposit, or to accept something less, in a genuinely exceptional case, provided the court records reasons for doing so. The Court set aside an order that had treated the deposit as compulsory and sent the matter back for fresh consideration on that basis. Muskan Enterprises v. State of Punjab, 2024 INSC 1046, reaffirmed the same point the following year, again remitting a case where the deposit had been treated as an absolute condition rather than a discretionary one.

The twenty percent deposit therefore remains the ordinary expectation on an appeal, and an appellant should not assume an exception will be made without a specific, well-supported case for one, built around genuine financial incapacity or some other circumstance that would make the deposit manifestly unjust rather than merely inconvenient. A bare assertion of hardship, without supporting material, is unlikely to persuade an appellate court to depart from what Surinder Singh Deswal still treats as the default position.

Company directors and Section 141: designation is not enough

Where the drawer of a dishonoured cheque is a company, Section 141 extends liability to every person who, at the time the offence was committed, was in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the company's business, along with the company itself. Complainants have for years tended to name every director listed in a company's filings, on the assumption that board membership alone is enough to attract liability, and the Supreme Court has pushed back on that assumption repeatedly.

The requirement traces back to S.M.S. Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. Neeta Bhalla, which required a complaint to specifically plead a director's active role in the company's affairs rather than naming them by designation alone. The Supreme Court restated and sharpened this in two 2025 decisions. In K.S. Mehta v. Morgan Securities and Credits Pvt. Ltd., 2025 INSC 315, the Court held that merely attending board meetings does not impose Section 141 liability on a non-executive or independent director, who cannot be prosecuted without a specific allegation of direct involvement in the conduct giving rise to the offence. In HDFC Bank Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra, 2025 INSC 759, the Court went further, holding that there is no universal rule that a director is in charge of a company's day-to-day affairs simply by virtue of holding that office, and that the complaint has to make a clear, specific averment of that person's role.

Later reporting on a Supreme Court decision concerning an office-bearer of a society applied the same reasoning outside the strict corporate context, holding that a managerial title alone does not invoke liability for cheque dishonour without an allegation of an active role in the conduct of business. For a complainant, the practical lesson is to plead specifics, what the named individual actually did, signed, or decided, rather than relying on a list of directors pulled from a company's annual return. For a director facing such a complaint, the specific pleading requirement is usually the first and most effective point to test, often at the quashing stage, before any of the merits of the underlying debt are reached.

Territorial jurisdiction: the 2015 fix to Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod

Before 2015, a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court in Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v. State of Maharashtra, (2014) 9 SCC 129, decided 1 August 2014, had held that a Section 138 complaint could be filed only in the court within whose jurisdiction the drawee bank, the bank on which the cheque was drawn, was located, regardless of where the payee presented the cheque for collection or where the notice was issued. This forced many payees to prosecute cheque dishonour in a city far from their own place of business, often the drawer's chosen bank location, which complainants criticised as rewarding drawers who banked deliberately far from the payee.

Parliament responded with the Negotiable Instruments (Amendment) Ordinance, 2015, later enacted as Act 26 of 2015, which amended Section 142 and inserted Section 142A. The amendment allows a complaint to be filed where the payee or holder in due course delivered the cheque for collection through an account, among other permitted venues, and Section 142A was drafted to apply notwithstanding anything in the Code of Criminal Procedure or any judgment, decree or order of any court, with the clear intent of displacing Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod going forward and also covering proceedings that were pending when the amendment came into force. Reports on later Supreme Court treatment of the amended provisions describe the position as only partially overriding the earlier ruling in substance: Section 142(1)(b)'s own language on when prosecution may begin still has to be read alongside Section 142A, so a jurisdiction objection in an ongoing matter should be checked against both the amended text and how a given court has applied it, rather than assumed to be settled simply because the 2015 amendment exists.

A civil suit alongside the criminal complaint

A complainant is not required to choose between a Section 138 prosecution and a civil suit to recover the same underlying debt. The two proceedings serve different functions: the criminal complaint determines whether the statutory offence of dishonouring a cheque for insufficiency of funds, after notice and the fifteen-day window, has been committed, while a civil suit determines the private rights and liabilities between the parties on the underlying transaction, and a civil suit filed on the same facts as a pending Section 138 complaint does not duplicate or bar it.

This matters practically because the remedies differ in what they can achieve. A Section 138 conviction can result in a fine or compensation, generally capped with reference to the cheque amount, along with the possibility of imprisonment, but the compensation ordered in a criminal case is not always calculated the same way a civil decree for the full commercial loss, including interest and consequential damages, would be. A complainant with a larger claim than the cheque amount covers, for instance interest accrued over years of non-payment or other contractual damages, is generally better served pursuing both remedies rather than treating the criminal complaint as a substitute for a civil recovery suit, while being careful that each cheque relied upon in the criminal complaint is pursued within its own notice and complaint timelines regardless of how the parallel civil suit is progressing.

Compounding and settlement

Section 138 is compoundable under Section 147 of the Act, and the Supreme Court has held that compounding remains available at any stage of the proceedings, including after conviction and during an appeal, and not merely at the start of the trial. In Damodar S. Prabhu v. Sayed Babalal H., (2010) 5 SCC 663, decided 3 May 2010, a three-judge bench laid down a graded scale of costs intended to be paid to a legal aid fund when compounding happens later rather than earlier in the process, meant to discourage accused persons from delaying settlement until the eve of conviction.

More recent Supreme Court decisions have treated those graded guidelines as persuasive rather than binding in every case. Reporting on a 2025 decision describes the Court declining to apply the Damodar Prabhu cost schedule mechanically where the complainant was not seeking any additional payment beyond the settled amount and the accused was genuinely unable to pay an extra cost, and an earlier decision, Sanjeev Kapoor v. Chandana Kapoor and a related line of authority including Sanjabij Tari v. Kishore S. Borkar, had already modified aspects of the original cost guidance. For practitioners, the guidance is still useful as a reference point for negotiating a settlement figure and timing, but it should not be treated as an inflexible tariff that overrides a settlement the parties have actually reached on their own terms.

The pendency problem and the Supreme Court's own directions

The volume of Section 138 litigation became serious enough that the Supreme Court registered a suo motu proceeding, In Re: Expeditious Trial of Cases Under Section 138 of the N.I. Act, 1881, Suo Motu Writ (Criminal) No. 2 of 2020, to work out why these cases were taking so long to resolve and what could be done about it. A committee headed by a former Bombay High Court judge examined the causes of delay and reported back to the Court.

On 16 April 2021, a Constitution Bench directed, among other things, that magistrates must record specific reasons before converting a Section 138 complaint from a summary trial into a summons trial under the second proviso to Section 143, noting that unexplained conversions had been a significant contributor to the backlog, since a summons trial runs on a slower procedural track than the summary trial the statute otherwise contemplates. High Courts, including Delhi's, issued practice directions implementing this. On 19 May 2022, the Court went further and directed the creation of Special Courts, staffed in part by retired judicial and administrative officers, as a pilot project for cheque dishonour cases running from 1 September 2022 to 31 August 2023, alongside directions to High Courts to report on compliance with the earlier orders.

The practical consequence for anyone litigating a Section 138 matter today is that a magistrate's decision to convert the trial to a summons procedure, or a court's general handling of case management in these matters, is now subject to a specific Supreme Court direction that can be invoked if a case seems to be drifting without justification, and a party frustrated by delay in a specific court has a documented basis, these directions and the High Court practice notes implementing them, to press for the summary procedure the statute was designed around rather than accepting an open-ended summons trial as inevitable.

A practical sequence for drawers and payees

A payee holding a dishonoured cheque should fix, in writing to themselves if nothing else, the exact date the dishonour memo was received, since the thirty-day notice clock and, through it, the one-month complaint clock both run from dates that are easy to get wrong if they are reconstructed from memory months later. Where several cheques from one transaction have bounced, each should be tracked on its own timeline rather than assumed to share a single limitation clock, consistent with Sumit Bansal, and a complaint naming a company's directors should specifically plead what each named individual actually did rather than listing designations from a corporate filing.

A drawer facing a complaint should resist the instinct to simply deny the underlying debt and expect the Section 139 presumption to collapse on its own; the better course, consistent with Rajesh Jain, is to build a specific, evidence-backed account of why the cheque was not issued for a real debt or liability, whether that is a security cheque never meant to be presented, a transaction that was cancelled, or payment already made through another channel. Where conviction looks likely, an early, considered decision about settlement, weighed against the graded costs in Damodar Prabhu and the twenty percent deposit a losing appeal would otherwise require under Section 148, is often the more economically rational course than an appeal pursued mainly to defer payment, particularly once Jamboo Bhandari and Muskan Enterprises make clear that the deposit will be waived only on a genuinely exceptional showing, not as a matter of course.

Section 138 still reads, on its face, as a short mechanical chain of dates, dishonour, notice, fifteen days, complaint. What has changed, almost entirely through Supreme Court decisions of the last three to six years, is how much discretion sits inside words the statute leaves open: "may" in Section 143A and Section 148, "in charge of and responsible for" in Section 141, and "the contrary is proved" in Sections 118 and 139. None of these provisions now works as a fixed formula, and treating any of them as automatic, interim compensation as a given, the twenty percent appeal deposit as unwaivable, a director's name as sufficient for liability, is now a mistake a court is likely to correct.

The cases that actually get decided on their underlying facts, rather than on a procedural misstep, are the ones where the dates were fixed precisely from the outset, the presumption was met with a specific account rather than a denial, and any claim for interim relief or any application to waive a deposit was supported with the particular material a court now expects before exercising its discretion either way.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do I have to send a cheque bounce notice and then file a complaint?

The statutory notice must be sent within thirty days of learning that the cheque was dishonoured. The drawer then has fifteen days from receiving that notice to pay. If payment is not made, the cause of action arises on expiry of those fifteen days, and the complaint generally has to be filed within one month of that date, under Section 142(b) of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, though a court can take cognizance of a later complaint if the complainant shows sufficient cause for the delay. Delay in sending the notice itself is treated much more strictly and is generally not condonable.

If several cheques from the same deal bounce, is that one case or several?

It can be several. The Supreme Court held in Sumit Bansal v. MGI Developers and Promoters, 2026 INSC 40, decided 8 January 2026, that cheques arising from a single transaction do not merge into one cause of action, and a separate complaint can be brought for each cheque where the statutory sequence of notice, the fifteen-day window and non-payment is independently completed for that cheque.

I am the accused in a cheque bounce case and I say there was no real debt. Do I have to prove that, or does the complainant have to prove the debt existed?

Once the complainant shows the cheque was signed by you and was dishonoured, Sections 118 and 139 of the Act presume it was issued for a real debt or liability, and the burden shifts to you to show otherwise. The Supreme Court held in Rajesh Jain v. Ajay Singh, 2023 INSC 888, decided 9 October 2023, that this burden is not a heavy one and can be met on a preponderance of probability, through direct or circumstantial evidence, but a bare denial of the debt, without more, has not been treated as enough.

Can the court order me to pay the complainant something before my case is even decided?

Possibly, under Section 143A of the Act, which allows interim compensation of up to twenty percent of the cheque amount while the complaint is pending. The Supreme Court held in Rakesh Ranjan Shrivastava v. State of Jharkhand, decided 15 March 2024, that this is discretionary rather than automatic; the court must make at least a prima facie assessment of the complaint's merits and record reasons before ordering it, and can order less than the twenty percent ceiling.

I was convicted and want to appeal. Do I have to deposit twenty percent of the fine before my sentence is suspended?

That is the ordinary rule under Section 148 of the Act, as applied in Surinder Singh Deswal v. Virender Gandhi, (2019) 11 SCC 341. The Supreme Court has since clarified, in Jamboo Bhandari v. M.P. State Industrial Development Corporation Ltd. (4 September 2023) and Muskan Enterprises v. State of Punjab, 2024 INSC 1046, that the appellate court retains discretion to waive or reduce the deposit in a genuinely exceptional case, provided it records reasons, so it is not an absolute, unwaivable condition, though it remains the default outcome in most appeals.

Can every director of the company that issued the cheque be prosecuted along with the company?

No. Section 141 requires that the person was, at the relevant time, in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the company's business, and the complaint has to specifically plead that role. The Supreme Court held in K.S. Mehta v. Morgan Securities and Credits Pvt. Ltd., 2025 INSC 315, that merely attending board meetings does not create liability for a non-executive or independent director, and in HDFC Bank Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra, 2025 INSC 759, that there is no universal rule that every director is in charge of day-to-day affairs simply by holding that office.

Can I file a civil suit to recover the money as well as a Section 138 complaint for the same cheque?

Yes. A civil suit and a Section 138 complaint on the same cheque serve different purposes, the civil suit determining the underlying debt and the criminal complaint determining whether a statutory offence was committed, and courts have treated both as maintainable together without one barring the other. A complainant seeking recovery beyond what a criminal court's compensation order is likely to cover, such as accrued interest, is generally better served pursuing both rather than relying on the criminal complaint alone.
