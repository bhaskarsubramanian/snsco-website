What Happens to a Family Business's Trademarks and Patents When the Founder Dies

Transmission, assignment and recordal of intellectual property after death, and what family-brand disputes teach about paperwork nobody got around to signing

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family business usually has a will for the house, a will for the shares, and nothing at all for the brand name on its signboard. The business's trademarks, patents and copyrights do not pass automatically the way a bank balance does. Each has its own statute, its own register and its own proof-of-title requirement, and getting this wrong has split real businesses apart.

Key points

  • A trademark, patent or copyright does not transfer to an heir merely because the owner has died; each statute requires a separate application to record the change of proprietorship on the relevant register.
  • Transmission of a trademark on death is governed by Rules 75 to 77 of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017, which require an application for entry of transmission along with proof of title, typically a succession certificate, probate or letters of administration, or a registered family settlement.
  • For a granted patent, Section 68 of the Patents Act, 1970 makes an assignment or transmission void against the grantee unless it is in writing and the Controller has been asked to register it under Section 69, which also covers transmission by operation of law such as inheritance.
  • Copyright in an unpublished manuscript passes under Section 20 of the Copyright Act, 1957 as part of a bequest of the manuscript itself, unless the will says otherwise, but this presumption is limited to what the author owned immediately before death and does not extend to a commissioned or employee work whose copyright already sits with someone else.
  • An unregistered trademark or trade name has no register entry to amend at all; the heirs' rights rest on proving continuous use and goodwill, which is exactly the kind of proof that gets fought over once the founder who could have testified is gone.
  • The Supreme Court's ruling in Haldiram Bhujiawala v Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar shows what an undocumented family brand costs: decades of litigation among branches of one family over a name none of them had cleanly assigned when the business divided.
  • A will, a family settlement or a franchise and licence agreement that is silent on IP continuity leaves the executor applying each statute's recordal procedure from scratch, usually at the same time as the family is still arguing about who was meant to get what.

Why the brand's paperwork matters more than the brand's turnover

Most family businesses that reach a second generation have, by then, dealt with succession for the house, the factory land and the bank accounts. The name over the shop door gets far less attention, and that is backwards, because the name is frequently the most valuable asset the business owns and the one with the least forgiving transfer rule.

A trademark, a patent and a copyright are each creatures of a separate statute, and each statute keeps its own register of who currently owns the right. Nobody is automatically entered on that register because a will was probated or a legal heir certificate was issued. Someone has to apply, under that statute's own rule, to have the register changed, and until that happens the register still shows the deceased founder as the owner.

This is not a technicality that resolves itself with time. A registered proprietor who is dead cannot sign a licence, cannot sue an infringer in the proprietor's own name without the applicant first proving title, and cannot renew the registration when the ten-year term runs out. Banks, franchisees and distributors who dealt with the business on the strength of its registered mark will ask, sooner rather than later, who now holds it.

The valuation consequence follows from the legal one. A buyer carrying out due diligence on a family business, or a bank assessing security for a loan against the brand, will discount a mark whose ownership chain has a gap in it, because the gap is a live risk that the mark could be challenged or that a licence granted by the estate could later be unwound. A clean, recorded chain of title is worth real money to the business quite apart from any litigation it avoids.

A trademark is property, but registration is not an estate asset by itself

Section 2(1)(zb) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 defines a trademark and Section 28 gives the registered proprietor the exclusive right to use it, but the Act does not say anything about what happens to that right on the proprietor's death beyond treating it as transmissible property, the same way a lease or a debt is transmissible. The mechanics of the transfer are left to the Trade Marks Rules.

Where a trademark is held in the name of a sole proprietor, the position on death is straightforward in principle and awkward in practice: the mark should pass to whoever inherits the proprietor's business under the will or under the law of intestate succession, but the Trade Marks Registry has no way of knowing who that is until someone tells it, with proof.

Partnership-held marks, common in older family food, textile and trading businesses, raise a more tangled position still, because a partner's death can dissolve the firm under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 unless the partnership deed says the firm continues with the surviving partners, and the trademark then has to be reconciled with whatever the surviving or reconstituted firm actually is.

Transmission on the Trade Marks Register: Rules 75 to 77 and proof of title

The Trade Marks Rules, 2017 deal with assignment and transmission together, because the Registry treats a voluntary sale of a mark and an involuntary transfer by inheritance as the same administrative event: a change in who is entitled to be shown as proprietor. The application for entry of such a transmission is filed under the rule that follows the Registry's assignment provisions, and it must be accompanied by proof of title.

Proof of title for a transmission by succession is ordinarily a probated will, letters of administration, a succession certificate from a civil court, or, where the family has instead executed a registered family settlement or release deed among themselves, that document together with evidence that it has actually been acted upon. The Registry examines the document on its face; it does not adjudicate a succession dispute, and if the applicants cannot agree on title, the Registry's practice is to require them to sort that out in a civil court first.

Until the entry is made, the register continues to show the deceased as proprietor, which is why solicitors advising a family business almost always recommend filing the transmission application early, alongside the broader estate administration, rather than waiting for every other asset to be settled first. A pending infringement suit or a licence renewal due in the interim will otherwise stall on exactly this gap.

Where the will or the law of intestate succession leaves the mark to more than one heir jointly, the Registry will record all of them as joint proprietors, and the siblings or cousins who now jointly own the mark inherit, along with it, the ordinary rule that one joint proprietor cannot licence or assign the mark without the others' consent. Families who want one branch to run the business and hold the mark alone, while compensating the other heirs in cash or other assets instead, need to say so in the settlement itself; the Registry will not infer that division on its own.

Unregistered marks and trade names: no register, no shortcut

A great many family businesses, particularly older ones built on word of mouth, never registered the name they trade under at all. There is nothing to transmit on a register that does not exist, and the heirs' claim rests entirely on common law passing-off principles: continuous, open use of the mark in connection with the business, and the goodwill that use has generated.

Passing off protects goodwill, not a document, and goodwill ordinarily travels with the business it was built for. Where one branch of a family continues to run the original business under the original name and another branch starts a competing business under the same or a deceptively similar name, the goodwill-based claim usually favours whichever branch can show it carried on the actual trade the name was known for, not whichever branch simply asserts descent from the founder.

Proving that, years after the founder's death, depends on evidence the founder would have been the best witness to give: when the name was first used, where, under what style, and with what reputation. Families that register nothing while the founder is alive are, without realising it, choosing to have this fought out in court by people who were not there when it mattered.

The Haldiram dispute: a cautionary tale about an unregistered family firm

The Supreme Court's decision in Haldiram Bhujiawala v Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar, (2000) 3 SCC 250, arose from exactly this kind of family situation, though its holding was narrower than the popular retelling suggests. The dispute was between branches of a family that had built the Haldiram name into a well-known sweets and snacks brand, and the specific legal question before the Supreme Court was whether an unregistered partnership firm, suing in the trading name, could maintain an action for passing off and infringement at all, given the bar in Section 69 of the Partnership Act on an unregistered firm enforcing a right arising from a contract.

Its holding was that a suit for infringement of a registered trademark, and a passing-off action, are not actions to enforce a right arising from a contract within the meaning of the Partnership Act bar, so an unregistered firm could still sue to protect the mark. That is useful law for any family firm that has never formally registered its partnership, but it does not answer who, within the family, actually owns the mark once the branches have gone their separate ways commercially.

What the broader Haldiram litigation across the Delhi courts over the following two decades illustrates, independent of that single point of law, is the cost of letting a valuable family name grow for decades without a clean assignment or partition of the intellectual property when the business itself divided. Different branches ended up litigating, in overlapping suits, over use of the name in different cities and product lines, long after the people who built the original goodwill were no longer available to settle the question informally.

Patents: Section 20 before grant, Sections 68 and 69 after grant

A patent application and a granted patent are treated somewhat differently under the Patents Act, 1970 when the applicant or patentee dies. Section 20 deals with the position before grant: where a person who would be entitled to a patent application by operation of law, such as inheritance, the Controller may, on a request made in the prescribed manner before grant, direct that the application proceed in that claimant's name. Section 20(4) separately lets the application continue in the name of surviving joint applicants, with the deceased applicant's legal representative's consent, where one of several joint applicants dies before grant.

Once a patent has been granted, Section 68 governs its transfer: an assignment, a mortgage, a licence or any other interest in a patent is not valid against a later-registered interest unless the transfer is in writing, reduced to a formal document, and an application to register the transfer is made to the Controller within the prescribed time. Section 69 is the registration provision itself, and its scope expressly extends to a person who has become entitled to a patent or a share in it by operation of law, which covers inheritance alongside a commercial assignment.

The practical sequence for a deceased patentee's heirs is therefore to establish title, usually through the same succession certificate, probate or letters of administration used for the trademark transmission, and then apply under Section 69 to have the register of patents at the patent office amended. Renewal fees fall due annually regardless of whose name is on the register, and a lapsed patent for non-payment during a succession dispute is difficult and sometimes impossible to restore.

A founder who filed the same invention abroad, whether directly or through the Patent Cooperation Treaty national-phase route, leaves the heirs with a separate transmission exercise in every country where a corresponding patent was granted, each under that country's own patent office procedure. Heirs of an inventor with a small international patent family should treat this the same way as a multi-country trademark portfolio: a checklist of jurisdictions, registration numbers and renewal dates, checked against the single Indian succession document that will have to be adapted to each office's local requirements.

Copyright: the manuscript rule, and the more common employee and commissioned work

Section 20 of the Copyright Act, 1957 contains a specific and fairly old rule: where a person bequeaths, under a will or codicil, the manuscript of a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work, the bequest is deemed to include the copyright in that work unless the will shows a contrary intention. The Act's explanation defines manuscript as the original document embodying the work, whether handwritten or not. The copyright that passes under this rule is limited to whatever the testator actually owned in that work immediately before death; it cannot create a larger right than the deceased had.

This rule matters for a publishing house, a design studio or a family business built on a founder's original writings, compositions or artistic designs, where the physical or digital manuscript and the authorship were never separated out from the business's other assets in the will. It matters far less for most commercial family businesses, where the more common copyright questions are about works created by employees in the course of employment, or works commissioned from an outside designer for a fee, both of which are usually already owned by the business under separate provisions of the Act rather than by the deceased individual personally.

Where copyright in a logo, packaging design or catalogue was commissioned from an outside designer, the family should check the commissioning contract before assuming the business owns it outright. A contract that is silent, or that only grants a licence rather than an assignment, can leave the designer's own heirs as the people whose consent is needed for the mark's continued commercial use, a complication that surfaces only when someone finally goes looking for the original engagement letter.

Designs: a shorter monopoly that lapses faster than families expect

Registered designs under the Designs Act, 2000 protect the visual appearance of an article, such as packaging shape or product ornamentation, for an initial period of ten years, extendable once by five years. Because the term is short and the extension has to be applied for before the initial period expires, a design registration left unattended during a succession dispute is at real risk of lapsing altogether, after which there is no fallback goodwill-based protection comparable to passing off for an unregistered trademark.

Transmission of a registered design on death follows a scheme broadly similar to trademarks: an application to record the change of proprietorship, supported by proof of title, filed with the Patent Office, which also administers the Designs Act. Family businesses with a signature product shape or distinctive packaging should treat the renewal and transmission deadlines for a registered design with at least as much urgency as the trademark, precisely because the shorter term leaves less room for the succession paperwork to catch up later.

None of these renewal clocks pause for a family dispute. A registered trademark has to be renewed every ten years under Section 25 of the Trade Marks Act, a registered design has the ten-year term just described, and a granted patent lapses unless the annual renewal fee is paid on time, whatever the state of the succession argument. The safer course, where a transmission application will take time because heirs disagree, is to have whoever is in de facto control of the business, or the named executor, pay each renewal fee under protest or without prejudice to the ownership dispute, rather than let a registration lapse while the larger question is worked out; restoration of a lapsed registration is possible within a limited window on payment of a surcharge, but it is not guaranteed, and a third party filing for the same or a similar mark during the gap can complicate the family's position considerably.

Drafting choices that prevent the next dispute

The single most useful step a family business can take while the founder is alive is to say, in the will or in a separate deed, exactly who is to receive the trademarks, patents, copyrights and registered designs, rather than letting them fall into a residuary clause alongside furniture and bank balances. A specific bequest naming the intellectual property, and naming the person or entity meant to hold it going forward, gives the Trade Marks Registry, the Patent Office and the Copyright Office a document they can act on without an independent succession dispute.

Where more than one heir is expected to continue using the family name, in different cities or for different product lines, the better practice is to resolve that in advance through a registered family settlement that either assigns the mark to a single holding entity which licenses it back to each branch, or partitions the mark itself into clearly delineated, non-overlapping uses. Doing this after a dispute has already started costs far more, in money and in family relationships, than doing it before one does.

A business already run through a company or an LLP should also check whether its trademarks and patents are registered in the name of the entity rather than in the founder's personal name. Many older family businesses registered marks personally, before the company was incorporated, and never executed the assignment into the company's name that was always meant to follow. That gap becomes a succession problem the day the individual, not the company, dies.

Two further mistakes turn up again and again once a dispute actually starts. One is treating a verbal or informal understanding among siblings about who would run which branch of the business, and under which name, as sufficient, without reducing it to a registered family settlement or partition deed that the Trade Marks Registry and Patent Office can act on; informal understandings are rarely disputed while the founder is alive to enforce them and are very often disputed once that is no longer so. The other is assuming that a will which deals in full detail with immovable property and bank accounts has, by implication, also dealt with the intellectual property, when in fact it is silent on it and the IP falls into a residuary clause shared among heirs who never discussed how a single trademark is meant to be used by several different, sometimes competing, family businesses at once.

Licences and franchise agreements: what continuity clause actually says

Family businesses that have licensed their name to franchisees, or that themselves hold a licence from a foreign brand owner, should read the licence or franchise agreement's own clauses on death and assignment before assuming the arrangement survives the licensor's or licensee's death unchanged. Many such agreements make the licence personal to the named licensor or franchisor, terminable on death or on a change of control, rather than automatically binding on the estate.

Where the agreement is silent, the general law of succession to contracts applies, subject to the contract's own terms on assignability: a licence that is not expressly made personal, and that does not prohibit assignment, generally binds the licensor's legal representative to honour it, but the licensee is well advised to get the new, actual titleholder's written confirmation that the licence continues, rather than relying on the estate's silence as acceptance.

This is also the point at which the trademark transmission application becomes urgent for a franchising family business specifically, because franchisees, suppliers and banks dealing under letters of credit will usually ask for evidence of who currently holds the registered mark before renewing their own arrangements, and an unresolved entry on the Trade Marks Register is not evidence a franchisee's own counsel will accept without question.

Cross-border complications for NRI and HNI families with marks registered abroad

A family business that has expanded its trademark registrations to the Gulf, the United Kingdom, the United States or under the Madrid Protocol's international registration system faces a transmission process in each of those jurisdictions separately, governed by that jurisdiction's own law, even though the underlying succession question, who inherited the founder's estate, is the same question answered once under Indian succession law.

An Indian probate or succession certificate is not automatically recognised by a foreign trademark office; most require either a local grant of probate or letters of administration resealed or newly obtained in that jurisdiction, or a certified, apostilled or consular-attested copy of the Indian order along with that office's own transmission form. Families with marks registered in several countries should expect to run this process more than once and should budget the time for it, because a foreign registration left unrenewed during a protracted Indian succession dispute can lapse independently of anything happening in India.

Where an NRI heir is among those entitled to the IP, there is no separate FEMA restriction on an NRI holding Indian intellectual property as an heir; the complication is procedural rather than regulatory, turning on getting documents executed and attested from abroad in a form each registry will accept, not on any cap on foreign ownership of the mark itself.

A practical sequence for the executor or the family

The first step is an inventory: list every registered trademark, patent and design in the deceased's name or the firm's name, across every jurisdiction, with its registration number and renewal date, because renewal deadlines do not pause for a succession dispute and a lapsed registration is far harder to revive than to renew on time.

Establishing title comes next, through whichever route the family's situation calls for, a probated will, letters of administration, a succession certificate, or a registered family settlement among consenting heirs, since every subsequent recordal application depends on this document existing in a form the relevant registry will accept.

Filing the transmission or recordal application with each registry separately follows that: trademark transmission with the Trade Marks Registry under the Trade Marks Rules, patent transmission under Section 69 of the Patents Act, and any design transmission with the same Patent Office, rather than assuming that settling the estate generally will update these registers automatically. None of them do.

A fourth step, often skipped, is to review every licence, franchise and distribution agreement under the mark or patent for a death or change-of-control clause, and to send licensees and franchisees formal notice of the new titleholder once the recordal is complete, so that commercial counterparties are not left guessing who they should now be paying royalties to.

Where IP succession disputes are actually fought

Disputes over who owns a family trademark after the founder's death can surface in more than one forum depending on their posture. A straightforward application to record a transmission, where the heirs agree on title, is decided administratively by the Trade Marks Registry or the Patent Office on the documents filed.

Where the heirs disagree about who is entitled to the mark, the registries will generally decline to resolve that disagreement themselves and will expect the parties to obtain a civil court's determination of title first, typically in a suit for declaration and partition, or in proceedings for probate or letters of administration where a will is contested. A passing-off or infringement action to protect the mark in the meantime is filed in the commercial division of the relevant High Court or district court, which has the pecuniary jurisdiction to hear it, and such a suit can proceed on an interim basis even while the underlying title dispute is still pending elsewhere.

Control disputes dressed up as IP disputes shift the forum again: where the business operates through a company and the real fight is over control of the company that holds the mark or patent rather than the IP itself, the appropriate venue moves, to the National Company Law Tribunal under an oppression and mismanagement or class action route, with the trademark or patent then following whatever that tribunal eventually orders about control of the company.

What remains unsettled or worth rechecking before relying on this

The precise rule numbers in the Trade Marks Rules, 2017 governing transmission and proof of title should be confirmed against the current consolidated text before a specific filing, since the Rules have been amended more than once since 2017 and a renumbering cannot be ruled out.

Readers should also confirm the current provisions of the Patents Act, 1970 and the Designs Act, 2000 against the consolidated statute in force on the date of filing, since both have seen periodic amendment, and should treat the description of Haldiram Bhujiawala v Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar here as limited strictly to the point it actually decided, the maintainability of a suit by an unregistered partnership firm, rather than as authority on any wider question of family succession to the mark itself.

A family business that has spent a generation building a name worth protecting owes that name the same documentary care it gives its land records and its bank accounts. The statutes that govern trademarks, patents, copyright and designs each have their own transmission procedure, their own proof-of-title requirement and their own renewal clock, and none of them pause to wait for the family to agree on who inherits what.

Getting the paperwork right while the founder is alive, through a specific bequest, a registered family settlement, or a clean assignment into the company's name, costs a fraction of what it costs to litigate the same question after the founder is gone and the branches of the family have each formed their own view of what was always meant to happen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a trademark automatically pass to the legal heir when the registered proprietor dies?

No. The Trade Marks Register continues to show the deceased as proprietor until an heir files an application for entry of transmission under the Trade Marks Rules, 2017, supported by proof of title such as a probated will, letters of administration, a succession certificate, or a registered family settlement. Until that entry is made, the heir cannot deal with the mark on the register as its recorded owner.

Can an unregistered family trade name still be protected after the founder's death?

Yes, through a passing-off action based on continuous use and the goodwill the name has acquired, but there is no register entry to simply amend. The heirs must be prepared to prove when and how the name was used and what reputation it built, which is harder once the founder who could testify to that history is no longer available.

What happens to a granted patent if the patentee dies before renewal fees are paid?

The patent remains in force only if renewal fees are paid on time regardless of whose name is on the register. Heirs should apply under Section 69 of the Patents Act, 1970 to register their title as soon as it is established, since a lapsed patent during an unresolved succession dispute is difficult to restore.

Does copyright in a book or design automatically go to whoever inherits the physical manuscript?

Under Section 20 of the Copyright Act, 1957, a bequest of the manuscript of a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work is presumed to include the copyright in it, unless the will says otherwise, but only to the extent the author actually owned that copyright at death. A commissioned or employee-created work is usually already owned by the business under separate provisions and is not affected by this manuscript rule.

Can heirs who disagree about who owns a family trademark get the Trade Marks Registry to decide?

Generally no. The Registry records a transmission administratively when the applicants agree and the paperwork is in order, but where heirs dispute entitlement, the Registry typically expects that dispute to be resolved in a civil court first, through a suit for declaration or through contested probate proceedings, before it will amend the register.

Does a foreign trademark registration automatically transfer along with the Indian one?

No. Each foreign trademark office, and the international registration system under the Madrid Protocol, has its own transmission procedure and its own documentary requirements, which usually call for a local grant of probate or an apostilled and attested copy of the Indian succession document. Families with marks registered in multiple countries should expect to repeat the recordal process in each jurisdiction.

Should a family business register its trademark in the company's name or the founder's personal name?

Registering in the operating company's or LLP's name avoids the succession problem entirely, because the entity does not die. Many older family businesses registered marks personally before incorporation and never executed the follow-on assignment into the company, which turns the founder's eventual death into an avoidable transmission exercise.

What is the first thing an executor should do about a deceased founder's intellectual property?

Prepare a complete inventory of every registered trademark, patent and design in the deceased's or the firm's name, across every jurisdiction, with registration numbers and renewal dates, because renewal deadlines continue to run during a succession dispute and missing one can cost the family the registration altogether.
