Guardianship for an Adult With a Disability in India: the National Trust Act and the RPwD Act

Two separate statutes let a family get a guardian appointed for an adult relative with a disability, and neither one is the law most people reach for first.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 18 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A parent asking who will manage their adult child's affairs once they themselves are gone is usually reaching for the wrong statute. The Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, the law most families and even some lawyers mention first, governs guardianship of a minor, and it stops applying the day that minor turns eighteen. For an adult with autism, cerebral palsy, an intellectual disability or another condition that affects their ability to manage their own affairs, Indian law runs two separate and only partly overlapping tracks instead: a guardian appointed through a Local Level Committee under the National Trust Act, 1999, for four specifically named disabilities, and a court or state-appointed authority granting limited guardianship under Section 14 of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, for a wider class of disabilities defined by that Act. A private trust under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 sits beside both tracks rather than inside either one, and families who treat a trust as a substitute for a guardian, or a guardian as a substitute for a trust, usually discover the gap only after it has already caused a problem.

Key points

  • A guardian for an adult with autism, cerebral palsy, an intellectual disability (what the National Trust Act, 1999 still calls mental retardation) or a combination of these, called multiple disabilities under the Act, is appointed by a Local Level Committee constituted under Section 13 of that Act, not by a civil court.
  • For disabilities outside those four categories, or where a family prefers a judicial forum, Section 14 of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 lets a district court or a State-notified designated authority (commonly the District Collector, though the notified authority differs by State) appoint a limited guardian, a status meant to support specific decisions rather than displace the person's own will generally.
  • Courts applying Section 14 have repeatedly rejected the idea of permanent or plenary guardianship for a person with a disability; the guardianship the provision allows is limited in scope and subject to periodic review by the appointing court or authority.
  • A guardian appointed under either statute can be removed for abusing, neglecting or mismanaging the property of the person they were appointed for, on an application by a parent, relative or registered organisation, a protection families should know exists before they need it rather than after.
  • A private trust set up under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 can hold and manage property for a disabled beneficiary for decades without anyone being appointed that beneficiary's guardian at all, which is why a trust and a guardianship order answer different questions and families who need durable financial management often need both.
  • Mental illness, as distinct from the four conditions the National Trust Act covers and the broader disabilities the RPwD Act covers, is governed by its own separate framework under the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, built around a nominated representative and an advance directive rather than a guardian in the conventional sense.
  • Courts have flagged real gaps in this scheme, including the absence of any clear mechanism for appointing someone to make decisions for a person in a prolonged unconscious or vegetative state, a gap neither the RPwD Act nor the Mental Healthcare Act currently closes.

Two Questions Families Run Together, and a Third Statute That Answers Neither

A family with an adult child or sibling who has a significant disability is usually trying to answer two different questions at once, and the law treats them as genuinely separate. The first question is who will take decisions on this person's behalf, consent to their medical treatment, operate their bank account, sign documents for them, where they cannot reliably do these things themselves. That is a guardianship question, and it is answered, for an adult, by one of two statutes depending on which disability is involved. The second question is how this person's money and property will actually be managed over a lifetime that, in many of these cases, will outlast the parents who are currently doing the managing informally. That is a trust question, answered by an entirely different statute, the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, and it does not require anyone to be a guardian at all.

The Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, is the statute most people encounter first, usually because it is the one taught in law school and the one that comes up constantly in child custody disputes. It governs guardianship of a minor's person and property, and its jurisdiction ends when the minor turns eighteen. For a family whose concern is specifically their adult relative's disability rather than their age, this Act has already stopped being the relevant one, and a petition filed under it for an adult will be met with an objection on exactly this ground. The two statutes that actually apply to an adult, the National Trust Act and the RPwD Act, are the subject of the next several sections, and the trust question is addressed separately later in this piece.

The National Trust Act, 1999: Four Named Disabilities, Not a General Disability Law

The National Trust for Welfare of Persons with Autism, Cerebral Palsy, Mental Retardation and Multiple Disabilities Act, 1999 does exactly what its full title says and no more. It applies to four categories: autism, cerebral palsy, what the Act's own 1999 vocabulary still calls mental retardation and current practice generally refers to as intellectual disability, and multiple disabilities, meaning a combination of two or more of the disabilities the Act and related disability legislation recognise. A family dealing with a disability that does not fall within one of these four categories, a physical disability alone, a sensory disability alone, or most forms of mental illness, is outside this Act's scope entirely and needs to look to the RPwD Act instead, discussed below.

Where the Act does apply, it creates a statutory body, the National Trust, and gives it the power under Section 13(1) to constitute a Local Level Committee for any area it specifies. This committee, not a civil court, is the forum where a guardianship application for one of the four covered disabilities is actually decided, a structural choice that reflects the Act's origins as a welfare and support statute for these specific conditions rather than a general disability law grafted onto the existing civil court system.

Families sometimes assume that because a Local Level Committee is an administrative body rather than a court, its process is informal to the point of being unreliable. In practice, the committee works through a defined application procedure, and the more realistic practical difficulty families report is less about the forum's formality and more about the committee actually sitting with the frequency and geographic reach a family dealing with a disabled adult can depend on, a point worth raising directly with the National Trust's regional office if a Local Level Committee in a family's area is not functioning.

How a Guardian Is Actually Appointed: Section 14's Application Route

Section 14(1) of the National Trust Act lets a parent or relative of a person with one of the four covered disabilities apply to the Local Level Committee for appointment of a guardian of their choice to manage the affairs of that person. The application goes on the prescribed form under the Act's rules, and the committee scrutinises it, calling for whatever supporting documentation or additional information it needs before deciding. The National Trust's own published guidance adds two practical conditions that families preparing an application should know before they file rather than after a rejection: the proposed guardian generally has to be an Indian citizen, and both the guardian and the person they would be appointed for are expected to live within the Local Level Committee's own jurisdiction, which is why a family split between cities sometimes has to think carefully about which relative is realistically positioned to hold this role.

Where no suitable parent or relative is available or willing, Section 14(2) allows a registered organisation working with persons with these disabilities to take on the role instead, a provision that matters for families without a natural candidate among their own relatives, or for a disabled adult whose parents have already died without the question having been resolved while they were alive.

A guardian appointed this way is not given an open-ended mandate to do as they see fit with the disabled person's property. The appointment is specifically for managing the affairs of the person concerned, and the committee retains supervisory authority over how that guardian actually performs the role, which is the basis for the removal power discussed next.

Removal, Abuse and Mismanagement: the Safeguard Most Families Never Read Until They Need It

Appointing a guardian is not the end of the Local Level Committee's involvement. Section 17 of the Act allows a parent, relative or registered organisation to apply to the committee for removal of a guardian who is abusing or neglecting the person they were appointed for, or mismanaging their property. This is the Act's principal internal safeguard against exactly the scenario families worry about most when a sibling other than the primary caregiver is given this role, and it is worth a family actually reading this provision before a dispute arises rather than discovering its existence only once a disagreement among relatives has already escalated.

The practical value of Section 17 depends on someone being positioned to notice abuse, neglect or mismanagement and willing to bring it to the committee's attention, which in a family where every adult relative has some stake in the outcome can be a genuine gap. Families who anticipate this kind of tension sometimes build in an informal reporting arrangement, a relative or a trusted family friend who checks in periodically and is prepared to use Section 17 if something looks wrong, rather than relying on the statute's existence alone to deter poor conduct.

A removal application under Section 17 does not leave the disabled person without anyone in charge in the interim. The committee that removed a guardian remains the body that appoints a replacement, and families bringing a removal application should come prepared with an alternative candidate already identified, since a committee faced with a removal application and no obvious successor is left having to find one itself, which adds delay at exactly the point a disabled person's affairs most need continuity rather than a gap.

The RPwD Act's Wider Reach: Limited Guardianship Under Section 14

For disabilities the National Trust Act does not cover, and for families who prefer a judicial forum even where it does, Section 14 of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 provides a second, broader route. The RPwD Act applies to a far larger set of conditions than the four the National Trust Act names, and its guardianship provision deliberately departs from the older model of full, plenary guardianship that effectively substitutes the guardian's decisions for the disabled person's own across the board.

What Section 14 creates instead is limited guardianship, a status courts applying the provision have consistently described as built around support rather than substitution: a guardian appointed under this route takes decisions jointly with the person with a disability, in accordance with that person's will to the extent it can be ascertained, and the appointment is meant to be tied to specific decisions or a specific situation rather than operating as a blanket authority over the person's entire life. A Karnataka High Court decision applying the provision has gone as far as holding that only limited guardianship is available under the Act, rejecting the idea of permanent guardianship for a person with a disability outright.

The proviso to Section 14(1) builds a further check into the scheme: wherever limited guardianship is granted, the support being provided is required to be reviewed by the appointing court or authority, so the arrangement is not meant to be set once and left alone for the rest of the disabled person's life. Families should treat this review requirement as a recurring obligation rather than a formality to be noted once at the time of the original order.

District Court or Designated Authority: a Forum Question That Varies by State

Section 14(1) itself gives a family two possible forums, the district court or an authority the State Government has notified for this purpose, and which one actually has jurisdiction in a given case has turned out to be more contested than the provision's text might suggest. A Kerala High Court ruling held that the district court and the State's notified designated authority have concurrent jurisdiction, meaning either forum can validly entertain a Section 14 application, a reading that followed an earlier order in which a different bench had declined jurisdiction because the State had notified a specific authority and treated that notification as excluding the court. Families filing in Kerala today can rely on the concurrent-jurisdiction position as the more recent and more considered view, though the point illustrates how unsettled even a basic forum question can remain for several years after a statute is enacted.

Which body a State has actually notified as its designated authority differs from State to State, and families should not assume the position in one State applies in another. A Karnataka High Court decision identifies the District Collector, as notified by the State Government, as the designated authority for that State's Section 14(1) applications, while a set of Andhra Pradesh rules describes a designated authority made up of the Collector together with specified district officials, and names a different body again, the Director of the relevant State welfare department, as the appellate authority for orders made under Section 14(3). A family should check their own State's specific notification before assuming either the district court or a particular officer is automatically the right forum, rather than relying on how the process works in a different State.

This state-by-state variation is one of the clearest illustrations in this whole area of law of why a national statute does not always produce a uniform national procedure, and it is also why a lawyer helping a family through this process has to start by confirming the local position rather than working from a template built for a different State.

Delhi NCR and West Bengal families should treat this as a jurisdictional check to run early rather than late. The notification a State has issued under Section 14, naming its designated authority and setting out the procedure before that authority, is a document worth obtaining and reading before an application is drafted, since a petition filed before the wrong forum does not simply get transferred automatically to the right one; it is far more often dismissed on that ground, with the family having to start again, having lost whatever time the first, misdirected attempt took.

Gaps the Courts Have Already Flagged

The RPwD Act's limited guardianship model was designed to move Indian law away from the older, more paternalistic plenary guardianship framework, and on its own terms it does that. It was not designed, however, to solve every situation a family dealing with disability actually faces, and courts applying it have started pointing to specific gaps rather than papering over them. One reported Delhi High Court matter noted that no rules have yet been made for providing what the Act calls total support, the category of assistance meant for a person who needs the most intensive level of decision-making help, which leaves a family in that situation without a clear administrative pathway even though the Act itself contemplates one existing.

A separate, starker gap concerns a person in a prolonged unconscious or vegetative state, where a court has observed that neither the RPwD Act, 2016 nor the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (discussed in the next section) provides a clear mechanism for appointing someone to take medical and financial decisions on that person's behalf. Families confronting this specific situation, most often after a severe accident or medical event rather than a disability present from birth, should expect that the existing statutory routes were not built with their situation squarely in mind, and that a court may have to improvise a solution on the facts of the particular case rather than apply a settled procedure.

What an Application Actually Requires: Disability Certificates and Documentary Proof

Neither route opens with a blank request for a guardian. Both the Local Level Committee under the National Trust Act and the district court or designated authority under the RPwD Act expect the application to be supported by a disability certificate issued by a competent medical authority, confirming both the nature of the disability and, in practice, its degree or percentage where the relevant disability assessment guidelines call for one. Families who have not kept this certification current, because the disabled relative has not needed to produce it for years, often find that the first real step in the process is not filing the guardianship application itself but getting an up-to-date certificate from a government hospital or a medical board authorised to issue one, a step that can itself take weeks depending on how backlogged the local board is.

Beyond the medical certificate, the application typically needs proof of the family relationship between the applicant and the disabled person, proof of the proposed guardian's identity and address, and some account of the property or affairs the guardianship is actually meant to cover, since both the Local Level Committee and a court deciding a Section 14 RPwD application are assessing not just whether a guardian is needed in the abstract but whether the specific person proposed is suitable for the specific responsibilities involved. A family that goes into this process with these documents already assembled, rather than treating the application form as the starting point, generally moves through the committee or court stage considerably faster than one that has to gather everything after the fact.

Where the disabled person is able to express any preference about who should be appointed, both statutes build that preference into the process rather than treating it as irrelevant. The National Trust Act route lets a parent or relative apply for a guardian of their choice, language that has been read to include some weight for the disabled person's own view where they can communicate one, and the RPwD Act's limited guardianship model is explicit that decisions are to be taken in accordance with the person's own will so far as it can be ascertained. A family assembling the application should document the disabled relative's own preference, where one exists and can be meaningfully expressed, rather than assuming the committee or court will simply defer to whichever relative has applied.

Why the Order Matters Beyond the Family: Banks, Insurers and Government Schemes

A guardianship order's practical value shows up most clearly at the exact moments a family discovers that an informal arrangement, however well it has worked within the household, does not satisfy a third party outside it. A bank asked to let a parent operate their adult child's account, or to accept instructions on that account after the account holder can no longer give them personally, will generally ask for a formal guardianship order before doing so, not a letter from the family explaining the situation. The same is true of an insurer processing a claim, a government department disbursing a disability pension or scheme benefit, or a hospital seeking consent for a significant medical procedure where the patient cannot meaningfully give it themselves.

This is one of the most common reasons families end up starting the guardianship process years later than they ideally should, having managed perfectly well without any formal order for a long time and then running into a specific institutional requirement that an informal arrangement cannot satisfy. Families should treat the absence of a formal guardianship order as a live risk even while things are going smoothly, precisely because the moment it becomes a problem, a banking freeze, a stalled insurance claim, a hospital unable to accept consent, is rarely a moment that allows for the weeks or months the application process can take to run its course from a standing start.

Several public schemes aimed at persons with disabilities, including some administered through the National Trust itself, such as support for guardianship-linked welfare schemes, also condition certain benefits on a formally recognised guardian being in place, which gives families a further practical reason to treat this as administrative groundwork worth completing well before it becomes urgent rather than a step to defer indefinitely.

Mental Illness Runs on a Separate Track: the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017

A family whose relative's condition is a mental illness rather than one of the developmental or intellectual disabilities discussed so far is, in an important sense, outside both statutes covered above. The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 governs this situation through a different legal concept entirely. Section 5 of that Act gives every adult the right to make a written advance directive setting out how they wish to be treated or not treated for a mental illness in the future, a document that becomes binding on the mental health establishment treating that person once validly made, and which is void to the extent it conflicts with any other law in force.

Section 14 of the same Act, worth distinguishing carefully from the differently numbered Section 14 of the RPwD Act discussed earlier in this piece, since the two are entirely separate provisions in separate statutes, lets an adult appoint a nominated representative to take decisions and act on their behalf for mental healthcare purposes. Where the person has not directly appointed someone, Section 14(4) sets out an order of preference for who steps into that role instead, giving first preference to whoever was named as nominated representative inside that person's own advance directive, with relatives forming the fallback where no such nomination exists.

This framework is built around a person who still has the capacity to plan ahead, through an advance directive and a nomination made while they are able to make these choices, rather than around a court retrospectively appointing someone after capacity has already been lost. Families supporting a relative with an ongoing mental illness should treat setting up the advance directive and nominated representative while that relative is willing and able to participate as the priority, rather than waiting for a crisis and then discovering that neither the National Trust Act nor the RPwD Act's guardianship provisions were written for this condition at all.

Where a Trust Fits In: the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 Route

Everything described so far answers the question of who takes decisions for a disabled adult. None of it, on its own, answers the separate question of how that person's money and property are actually managed over what is, for most families in this situation, a multi-decade horizon. A private trust set up under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 answers that second question directly, and it does so without requiring anyone to hold the status of guardian at all.

A trust created for the benefit of a disabled family member works through trustees, who hold and manage the trust property according to the terms the person creating the trust has set out, applying income and capital for the beneficiary's maintenance, medical care and general welfare as the trust deed directs. Because the trustees' authority comes from the trust deed itself rather than from a guardianship order, a family can set this structure up, and start funding it, well before any guardianship question has even arisen, and it continues operating on its own terms regardless of whether a Local Level Committee or a court ever appoints anyone as the beneficiary's guardian.

This is also why a trust and a guardianship order are not substitutes for each other, whatever families sometimes assume at the outset. A trust manages money. A guardian takes personal decisions, consents to medical treatment, deals with government authorities, and handles matters a trust deed was never written to cover. A family that sets up a well-funded trust but never resolves who is legally authorised to consent to a hospital admission, or to operate a bank account in the beneficiary's own name outside the trust, has solved only one half of the problem, and the half it left unsolved tends to surface at exactly the moment, a medical emergency, a bank's compliance query, a dispute among relatives, when a family is least prepared to deal with it from scratch.

Families also ask whether setting up a trust avoids the need to go through the National Trust Act or RPwD Act process at all, on the theory that if the money is already professionally managed, a guardian becomes unnecessary. It does not. A trust deed can direct trustees to apply funds for the beneficiary's maintenance without the beneficiary personally handling any money, which genuinely reduces how often a guardian's authority over finances is actually exercised, but it does nothing for medical consent, government paperwork in the beneficiary's own name, or any situation where a third party specifically needs to see a guardianship order rather than a trust deed. The two documents answer different questions asked by different audiences, the trust deed by trustees managing assets, the guardianship order by hospitals, banks and authorities dealing with the person directly, and a family should expect to need both rather than treating one as having quietly made the other optional.

Combining a Trust With a Guardian: Drafting Choices That Actually Matter

Families who do set up both a trust and a guardianship arrangement still face real drafting choices about how the two should interact, and getting these wrong can create friction between a well-meaning guardian and well-meaning trustees even where both are acting entirely in the beneficiary's interest. A trust deed can name the beneficiary's guardian as one of the trustees, which keeps day-to-day decision-making in the hands of a single person familiar with the beneficiary's actual needs, or it can deliberately keep the two roles separate, appointing an independent professional trustee alongside a family member guardian, as a check against either role being misused without the other noticing.

Succession planning for the trustee role deserves the same attention families usually give to succession planning for the guardian role and often do not. A trust intended to operate for decades needs a mechanism for replacing a trustee who dies, becomes incapacitated, or simply becomes unable to continue, built into the trust deed itself rather than left to be worked out informally when the need arises, and the same is true in parallel for who steps into the guardian's role if the person currently holding it, often an ageing parent, predeceases the beneficiary they were appointed for.

Families should also be deliberate about how the trust deed describes the circumstances in which trustees are to apply trust funds, striking a balance between giving trustees enough discretion to respond to a beneficiary's actual, changing needs over a long period, and giving them so much open-ended discretion that a disagreement among family members about how that discretion should be used becomes, in effect, unresolvable without litigation.

A further choice families tend to underestimate is how the trust deed and the guardianship arrangement should speak to each other on paper, rather than existing side by side with no cross-reference at all. A trust deed that expressly records who currently holds the guardianship role, and sets out what happens to the trustees' obligations if that role passes to someone else later, gives a bank, a court or a successor trustee a single consistent account of the family's arrangements rather than two documents that a sceptical third party has to reconcile for themselves years after they were drafted, often without either drafter still being available to explain the intention behind them.

A Sequencing Checklist for Families Starting This Process

A family beginning this process from scratch is usually better served by resolving things in a particular order rather than trying to set up a guardian, a trust and every supporting document simultaneously. The first step is identifying which statute actually applies, the National Trust Act for one of its four named disabilities, or the RPwD Act for everything else that Act covers, since the entire subsequent process, the forum, the application form, the specific protections available, follows from this choice. Confirming the applicable State's specific notification under the RPwD Act, where that route applies, should happen at this same early stage rather than after an application has already been filed in the wrong forum.

The second step is deciding who within the family is realistically positioned to act as guardian, accounting for the residency and citizenship conditions the National Trust Act's guidance describes and for the practical question of who will actually be available, geographically and over time, to perform this role for as long as it is needed. Where no family member fits, looking into a registered organisation willing to take on the role under either statute should happen before, not after, a Local Level Committee or court application is drafted.

Only once these two questions are settled does setting up or reviewing a trust become the sensible next step, since a trust deed drafted without knowing who the guardian will be, or without knowing which statute's guardianship regime applies, tends to need revisiting anyway once that information is available. Families who already have an informal arrangement, an ageing parent currently managing everything without any formal guardianship or trust in place, should treat the absence of either as the risk to address first, since it is precisely this informal arrangement that collapses without warning when the parent currently running it dies or becomes incapacitated themselves.

A final, often overlooked step is making sure the disability certificate and any supporting medical documentation stay current rather than treating the one obtained for the original application as permanent. A certificate several years old, or one issued before the family actually needed it for anything beyond a school admission or a travel concession, can itself become the reason an application stalls at the committee or court stage, and renewing it before filing rather than after a query is raised saves a family weeks it will not get back once the process has already started.

What Remains Unsettled

Several aspects of this framework remain genuinely unsettled rather than merely under-used. The concurrent jurisdiction of the district court and a State's designated authority under the RPwD Act's Section 14 has been addressed directly in Kerala but has not, on the sources available, been uniformly settled across every State, and a family outside Kerala should not assume the same answer applies without checking their own State's case law and notifications. The absence of rules for the Act's total support category, and the complete absence of a clear statutory mechanism for a person in a prolonged vegetative or unconscious state, remain open gaps that neither Parliament nor, on the available record, the courts have yet closed fully.

What is settled, and worth families holding onto amid these open questions, is the basic architecture: a minor's guardianship ends at eighteen regardless of disability, an adult with one of four named conditions has a dedicated committee-based route under the National Trust Act, an adult with a disability outside those four conditions has a court or authority-based limited guardianship route under the RPwD Act, a person with a mental illness has a separate, planning-focused regime under the Mental Healthcare Act, and none of these three tracks does the job of a trust, which has to be set up separately if durable financial management is the actual goal.

Families dealing with an adult relative's disability are usually trying to solve a decision-making problem and a money-management problem at the same time, and Indian law answers these with three different guardianship-adjacent statutes and a fourth, entirely separate trust law, none of which substitutes for any other. Getting the guardian question right starts with correctly identifying which of the National Trust Act or the RPwD Act actually applies, and getting the money question right means setting up a trust on its own terms rather than assuming a guardianship order will cover it.

The statutory gaps courts have already identified, on total support rules and on persons in a vegetative state, are a reminder that this area of law is still being worked out in practice, case by case, rather than fully settled on paper. Families planning ahead should build in flexibility, in how a trust deed is drafted and in who is positioned to step into a guardian's role later, rather than assuming today's arrangement will need no revisiting as the disabled family member, and the rest of the family, grow older.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a guardian appointed for my minor child under the Guardians and Wards Act continue once they turn 18 if they have a disability?

No. Guardianship under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 is tied to minority and ends when the child turns eighteen regardless of disability. A fresh application has to be made under the National Trust Act, 1999 or Section 14 of the RPwD Act, 2016, whichever applies to the disability involved, to have a guardian appointed for that person as an adult.

Which law applies if my adult child has an intellectual disability?

The National Trust Act, 1999 applies, since intellectual disability (what the Act calls mental retardation) is one of its four named categories, along with autism, cerebral palsy and multiple disabilities. A guardian is appointed through a Local Level Committee under that Act rather than by a civil court.

What if my relative's disability is not autism, cerebral palsy, an intellectual disability or multiple disabilities?

Section 14 of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 applies instead, covering the wider range of disabilities that Act recognises. An application for limited guardianship is made to the district court or to the authority your State has notified for this purpose, and which one has jurisdiction can depend on your specific State.

Can a guardian under either law make every decision for the disabled adult?

Not under the RPwD Act. Courts have held that Section 14 allows only limited guardianship, built around supporting specific decisions in line with the person's own will, and the Act requires periodic review of the support being provided rather than a one-time, open-ended appointment.

Is a private trust the same thing as getting a guardian appointed?

No. A trust under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 manages the beneficiary's money and property according to the trust deed, and it can operate without anyone being appointed guardian at all. Guardianship deals separately with personal decisions such as medical consent. Families with a disabled adult relative typically need both, not one instead of the other.

Can a guardian be removed if they mismanage the disabled person's property?

Yes, under Section 17 of the National Trust Act a parent, relative or registered organisation can apply for removal of a guardian who is abusing, neglecting or mismanaging the person's affairs. The RPwD Act's periodic review requirement under Section 14 serves a similar supervisory purpose for guardians appointed under that Act.

What happens if my relative's condition is a mental illness rather than a developmental disability?

The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 applies instead of the National Trust Act or the RPwD Act's guardianship provisions. It works through an advance directive under Section 5 and a nominated representative under Section 14 of that Act, both of which an adult sets up for themselves while they have the capacity to do so, rather than a guardian appointed after the fact.

What if my relative is in a permanent vegetative or unconscious state rather than having a disability from birth?

This is a recognised gap in the current framework. Courts have noted that neither the RPwD Act, 2016 nor the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 provides a clear statutory mechanism for appointing someone to take decisions for a person in this specific situation, and families facing it should expect to need a court's intervention on the particular facts rather than a settled procedure.
