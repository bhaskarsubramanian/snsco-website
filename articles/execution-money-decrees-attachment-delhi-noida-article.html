Execution of Money Decrees in Delhi and Noida: Attachment, Sale and the Objections That Stall Both

Winning a suit settles liability; getting the money or the property out of a judgment debtor's hands is a second, separate fight governed by Order 21 of the CPC

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A money decree or a decree for possession is a piece of paper until someone acts on it. Part II of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Sections 36 to 74, together with Order 21's more than a hundred rules, govern that second stage, and it is often this stage, not the trial, where disputes run for years longer than the parties expect. This piece sets out how execution actually works for a money decree or a decree for possession obtained in a Delhi district court, the Delhi High Court, or the Gautam Buddh Nagar Commercial Court, what a judgment-debtor can genuinely object to, and where the process most commonly stalls.

Key points

  • Execution is a separate proceeding governed by Sections 36 to 74 and Order 21 of the CPC; a decree-holder files an execution petition in the court that passed the decree, or in a court to which it has been transferred under Section 39, and must do so within twelve years of the decree becoming enforceable under Article 136 of the Limitation Act, 1963.
  • Section 51 gives the executing court several modes at once: delivery of property, attachment and sale, arrest and civil detention, appointment of a receiver, or any other mode the nature of the relief requires; attachment and sale of property remains the mode used most often against a judgment-debtor who has assets but will not pay voluntarily.
  • Attaching movable property follows Rules 41 to 50, attaching immovable property follows Rule 54 and requires a prohibitory order either proclaimed locally or sent for registration, and Section 60's proviso exempts specific categories of property, tools of the trade, necessary wearing apparel, and a portion of salary among them, from attachment altogether.
  • A third party claiming an interest in attached property, or a judgment-debtor disputing that particular property is liable to attachment, raises the objection under Order 21 Rule 58, and the executing court must adjudicate it as a formal question of right, title or interest rather than brush it aside; an order under Rule 58 is itself appealable as a decree.
  • Sale of immovable property under Rules 64 to 73 requires a proclamation stating the property, its estimated value and any encumbrances, and the sale can be set aside under Rule 90 for a material irregularity or fraud in conducting it, or under Rule 89 if the judgment-debtor deposits the decretal amount plus five per cent before the sale is confirmed.
  • Delhi's district courts currently execute decrees up to the same roughly Rs 2 crore pecuniary limit that applies to filing a suit there, with a Full Court proposal pending, as of this writing, to raise that ceiling; a decree above that value, or one passed by the Delhi High Court's original side, is executed by the High Court itself unless transferred.
  • Where the judgment-debtor's assets lie outside the territorial jurisdiction of the court that passed the decree, commonly a Delhi decree against a debtor who holds property in Noida or Greater Noida, the decree-holder must apply under Section 39 to transfer the decree for execution to the Gautam Buddh Nagar court, since Section 38 does not let the original court reach property beyond its own limits on its own authority.
  • An execution application filed more than two years after the decree triggers a mandatory show-cause notice to the judgment-debtor under Order 21 Rule 22, and courts have consistently held that Section 5 of the Limitation Act's power to condone delay does not rescue an execution petition filed outside the twelve-year window; once that period runs out, the decree-holder's right to execute is generally treated as extinguished, not merely delayed.

A decree is not self-executing

A party who has spent years litigating a money claim to judgment is often surprised to learn that the decree does not, by itself, move any money or hand over any property. The decree records that the plaintiff has a legal right to recover a sum, or to possession, or to some other relief; realising that right against a defendant who does not pay or vacate voluntarily requires a second proceeding, execution, conducted under an entirely different set of rules from the trial that produced the decree. Part II of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Sections 36 to 74, and the detailed mechanics in Order 21, supply that second proceeding.

This distinction matters practically because the two stages run on different clocks and different standards of proof. A trial court decides liability on the balance of the evidence led at trial. An executing court, by contrast, is not meant to reopen that question at all; Section 47 confines it to questions relating to the execution, discharge or satisfaction of the decree, and courts have repeatedly cautioned against letting execution proceedings become a second round of the original suit under the guise of an objection. Judgment-debtors who understand this distinction raise narrower, more targeted objections and tend to fare better than those who try to relitigate the merits at the execution stage.

A decree obtained before a Commercial Court under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, whether in Delhi or in Gautam Buddh Nagar, is executed no differently once judgment is pronounced. The Act changes how a commercial dispute is pleaded, case-managed and tried, pre-institution mediation under Section 12A, timelines for written statements, and summary judgment among them, but it does not create a separate execution code of its own. Once the Commercial Court's decree is signed, Part II of the CPC and Order 21 take over in exactly the form described in this piece, which is a point worth making because parties sometimes assume, wrongly, that a faster trial track also means a faster or different route to actually collecting the money.

Which court executes: the passing court, transfer, and Section 39

Section 38 states the basic rule plainly: a decree may be executed either by the court that passed it, or by the court to which it has been sent for execution. Section 37 extends the meaning of the court which passed the decree to cover, in certain situations, the court of first instance even where an appellate court has varied the decree, and the court of a successor in jurisdiction where the original court has since ceased to exist or lost jurisdiction over the subject matter.

Section 39 is the provision a decree-holder actually invokes when the judgment-debtor's property or person lies outside the passing court's local limits. On the decree-holder's application, the court that passed the decree can send it for execution to another court of competent jurisdiction, a step that becomes necessary, for instance, when a Delhi court's decree needs to be executed against a flat or a bank account located in Noida or Greater Noida. Section 39(4) is explicit that nothing in the section itself authorises the court which passed the decree to execute it against property outside its own local limits; without a transfer, the passing court simply has no power to attach an asset sitting in another court's territory.

Once a decree is transferred, Section 42 gives the transferee court the same powers in executing it that the original court would have had, including, where the decree permits, the power to proceed against partners of a firm or other persons bound by the decree. The transferee court does not revisit the decree's correctness; it steps into the passing court's shoes purely for the purpose of execution. A decree-holder who skips this step and tries to execute directly in the wrong court risks having the application returned for want of jurisdiction, losing time that, given the twelve-year outer limit discussed below, is rarely available to spare.

A decree-holder with assets to pursue in more than one place is not confined to a single transfer either. Section 39 permits sending the decree for execution to several courts at once where the judgment-debtor's property lies in more than one jurisdiction, so a decree passed in Delhi against a debtor who holds a flat in Noida and a separate commercial unit in Gurugram can, in principle, be transferred to both places simultaneously rather than one after the other, provided the decree-holder does not recover the same amount twice over from parallel proceedings.

Starting execution: the application, its contents, and the oral route

Order 21, Rules 10 to 14 govern how execution begins. The decree-holder applies in writing, stating the particulars Rule 11 requires: the decree's number and date, the parties, the amount still due if it is a money decree, the mode of execution sought, and, where immovable property is to be attached, a description sufficiently precise to identify it without ambiguity. Rule 11 also allows an oral application in a narrow situation, where the judgment-debtor is present in court at the time the decree is passed, letting the court order immediate execution, arrest included, without waiting for a formal written application and its accompanying paperwork.

A defective or incomplete application is not a mere technicality the court will overlook. Courts applying Rule 13 in particular have returned applications for immovable property attachment that did not identify the property with enough particularity, since the attaching order itself has to describe the property clearly enough for a prohibitory order to issue and for third parties dealing with it to be put on notice. Decree-holders who prepare the execution petition with the same care given to the original plaint, attaching a certified copy of the decree, a tabulated statement of the amount due with interest calculated to date, and clear property descriptions drawn from the title documents or municipal records, avoid a round of returns and re-filings that otherwise eats into the limitation period.

Rule 22 adds a further step that catches many decree-holders by surprise. Where the execution application is filed more than two years after the date of the decree, or against the legal representative of a deceased judgment-debtor, the executing court must issue a notice calling on the judgment-debtor to show cause why the decree should not be executed, before any coercive step is taken. Courts have treated this notice as mandatory rather than discretionary once the two-year threshold is crossed, and an execution proceeding that skips it has been held open to challenge for that reason alone. Filed within two years, by contrast, no such notice is ordinarily required and the court can proceed straight to the coercive process the decree-holder has asked for.

The twelve-year limit, and why it is rarely forgiving

Article 136 of the Limitation Act, 1963 sets the outer limit for executing any decree, other than one granting a mandatory injunction, or any order of a civil court, at twelve years. The clock ordinarily starts running from the date the decree becomes enforceable, which is usually the date of the decree itself for a straightforward money decree, but can shift where the decree is later amended or where an appeal keeps the matter pending, since the decree as it stands after appeal is the one that actually falls to be executed. Time during which execution was validly stayed by a court order is excluded from the twelve years, which can matter considerably where the judgment-debtor has obtained a stay pending appeal or revision.

What makes Article 136 a harder deadline than most limitation periods a litigator deals with is the near-uniform judicial view that Section 5 of the Limitation Act, which allows a court to condone delay in filing an application or appeal for sufficient cause, does not extend to an execution petition filed after the twelve years have run. Several High Courts have held in terms that an application under Article 136 filed beyond the period cannot be maintained at all, and that the executing court has no inherent power to revive it through condonation once the statutory period is exhausted. A decree-holder who sits on a decree, whether out of a mistaken belief that time is unlimited or simply from inattention, therefore risks losing the fruits of years of litigation to a deadline that courts are reluctant to relax.

The practical lesson for a Delhi or Noida decree-holder is to calendar the twelve-year date the day the decree is obtained, not years later when a dispute over execution actually arises, and to file at least one execution application, even a modest one seeking only partial relief, well before that date if full recovery is not yet achieved. A fresh execution application after an earlier one has been dismissed for non-prosecution, or after the decree-holder withdraws it, generally still has to fall within the original twelve years running from the decree, not from the date of the earlier application, which is a trap worth keeping firmly in mind.

Section 51's menu: attachment, delivery, arrest and the residual power

Section 51 lists the modes available to an executing court once it is satisfied the decree should be executed: delivery of any property specifically decreed, attachment and sale, or sale without attachment, of any property, arrest and detention in civil prison, appointment of a receiver, or any other mode the nature of the relief requires. The decree-holder chooses which mode or modes to seek in the execution application, and the court retains discretion over whether arrest and detention, the most drastic of the listed modes, is actually warranted on the facts, since the proviso to Section 51 requires the court to be satisfied, among other things, that the judgment-debtor is likely to abscond or has the means to pay but has refused or neglected to do so, or has disposed of property dishonestly to defeat the decree.

For an ordinary money decree against a solvent but uncooperative debtor, attachment and sale of property remains the mode decree-holders turn to most often, precisely because arrest requires the heightened satisfaction Section 51 demands and a receiver is usually reserved for situations involving an ongoing business or rental income rather than a one-off debt. Attachment itself divides along the line between movable and immovable property, each with its own procedural rhythm under Order 21, which the next two sections of this piece take up in turn.

A decree-holder choosing between these modes should also weigh their relative cost and speed, not just their theoretical reach. Attaching a bank account through the garnishee route under Rule 46 is comparatively quick once the account is identified, since the bank itself has no real incentive to resist a validly served order. Attaching and selling immovable property is slower and more expensive, court fees, valuation costs and the proclamation process all add weeks before a sale can even be held, but it reaches value that a bank account attachment might not, particularly against a judgment-debtor who keeps minimal balances precisely to frustrate recovery. Arrest and detention, available only on the heightened showing Section 51's proviso demands, is rarely the first mode sought in commercial decrees between businesses, and is used more often, where it is used at all, as leverage once other modes have stalled against a debtor who plainly has the means to pay.

Attaching movable property, bank accounts and salary

Rules 41 to 50 of Order 21 govern attachment of movable property. Where the property is already in the judgment-debtor's possession, Rule 43 allows the court's officer to seize it, subject to Rule 41's power to instead examine the judgment-debtor on oath about assets where seizure is impractical or the property is not readily identifiable. Where the movable property, a debt owed to the judgment-debtor, a share in a company, or money in a bank account, is not in the judgment-debtor's own possession but is held by a third party, Rule 46 provides the garnishee route, attaching the debt or deposit by a written order served on the person who holds it, commonly a bank, directing that it not be paid out except in satisfaction of the decree.

Section 60 of the CPC, read with its proviso, sets limits on what can be attached at all. Certain property is wholly exempt: necessary wearing apparel, cooking vessels, beds and bedding, tools of a trade for an artisan, and the like, along with a right to future maintenance and, in the case of salary, a specified portion that the section protects from attachment to leave the judgment-debtor some means of subsistence. A decree-holder attaching a judgment-debtor's salary through the employer therefore recovers only the non-exempt portion each month, which can make salary attachment a slow route to full recovery compared with attaching a lump sum asset, but a dependable one where the judgment-debtor has no other attachable property.

Bank account attachment in practice moves quickly once the garnishee order under Rule 46 is served, since banks generally comply promptly to avoid being made liable themselves for paying out attached funds. The more common friction point is identifying which bank and branch actually hold the judgment-debtor's funds before the application is filed, since Rule 41's examination of the judgment-debtor on oath, while available, depends on the judgment-debtor answering truthfully, and a debtor intent on frustrating the decree has every incentive not to.

Attaching and selling immovable property

Attachment of immovable property proceeds under Rule 54, which requires the court to make a prohibitory order, in effect a direction that the judgment-debtor is forbidden from transferring or charging the property, and that no one is to take it from him by purchase, gift or otherwise. The order is to be proclaimed at some place on or adjacent to the property, and, by amendment applicable where the state has provided for it, intimated to the registering authority for the district in which the property lies, so that a subsequent attempted sale or mortgage of the attached property shows up at the registration stage. Section 64 reinforces the attachment's bite by making a private transfer or charge of attached property, made after attachment, void against claims enforceable under the attachment, though not void for all purposes.

Sale of the attached immovable property, once the decree-holder presses ahead rather than settling for a lesser recovery, follows Rules 64 to 73. The court orders a proclamation of sale under Rule 66, stating the property to be sold, any encumbrances it is subject to, the amount for which the sale is ordered, and such other particulars as the court considers material for a purchaser to judge the nature and value of the property, typically including an estimated value arrived at after hearing both sides. Rule 84 requires the auction purchaser to deposit twenty-five per cent of the purchase price immediately on the sale being knocked down, with the balance due within the period the rules prescribe, and a purchaser who defaults on the balance forfeits the deposit and the property is resold.

Two routes exist to undo a completed sale before it becomes final. Rule 89 lets the judgment-debtor, or any person entitled to share in the proceeds, apply to set the sale aside by depositing the decretal amount in full along with five per cent of the purchase price for the auction purchaser, a provision aimed at giving the debtor a last genuine chance to save the property rather than lose it to auction. Rule 90, by contrast, lets the judgment-debtor or any other person whose interests are affected by the sale apply to set it aside for a material irregularity or fraud in publishing or conducting the sale, though the applicant must also show that the irregularity or fraud actually caused substantial injury, a mere technical defect that caused no real prejudice is not enough on its own.

When a third party objects: Order 21 Rule 58 claims

Attachment does not always proceed against property that unambiguously belongs to the judgment-debtor. A spouse, a business partner, a tenant, or a purchaser under an unregistered or disputed agreement may each claim that the attached property is actually theirs, or that they hold an interest in it the attachment should not override. Order 21 Rule 58 is the mechanism for raising that claim before the executing court itself, rather than requiring a separate suit, and the rule directs the court to adjudicate the claim on its merits as a question of fact and law, not to dispose of it summarily.

The executing court's inquiry under Rule 58 is meant to go to the substance of the claimed right, title or interest in the property, which is a wider inquiry than simply asking who is in physical possession, though some older authority had confined it to possession alone; the better and more recent view, consistent with the rule's own language, is that the court must go into the question of title where that is what the claim actually raises. An order allowing or disallowing the claim under Rule 58 is treated as a decree for the purpose of appeal, meaning the losing party can appeal it in the ordinary way rather than being left only with a fresh suit, which the rule's own scheme otherwise generally bars once the claim has been adjudicated on merits.

For a decree-holder, a Rule 58 claim filed by a third party is not something to dismiss as a nuisance tactic without examination, since a genuine claim, if ignored or wrongly rejected, can derail a sale later on appeal or revision. For a judgment-debtor or an affected third party, the claim has to be filed and pressed with actual evidence of title or possession, bare assertion rarely succeeding, since the court is required to decide the claim much as it would decide any other contested question of fact in a trial, on the material actually placed before it.

Possession decrees and resistance under Rules 95 to 106

Where the decree is for possession of immovable property rather than money, Rule 35 lets the executing court direct that the judgment-debtor, or any person bound by the decree who is in possession, be removed, with police assistance if necessary, and the decree-holder put in possession. Complications arise when someone who was not a party to the suit resists the delivery of possession, or is found in occupation and refuses to vacate, a scenario Order 21's Rules 97 to 106 address in detail.

Where a person other than the judgment-debtor resists or obstructs delivery of possession, the decree-holder can apply under Rule 97 for the court to inquire into the resistance; if the court finds the resistance was by the judgment-debtor, or by someone acting on the judgment-debtor's behalf without a bona fide claim of right, Rule 97 and the related rules allow the court to direct that possession be delivered regardless, using force if necessary. Where the resisting person claims an independent right to possession in their own capacity, not derived from or collusive with the judgment-debtor, Rule 101 requires the court to determine that claim on its merits as well, rather than mechanically overriding it, since a genuine third party in independent possession is not meant to be dispossessed without being heard.

A decree-holder executing a possession decree in Delhi or Noida against a property that has since been occupied by a tenant, a licensee, or a purported subsequent purchaser should expect the Rule 97 to 106 inquiry to take real time, since the court cannot simply assume the occupant is a front for the judgment-debtor; it has to be satisfied of that, or of the occupant's lack of an independent right, on the evidence presented. Building that evidence, lease documents, payment records, and the timeline of when the occupant actually entered, before filing the execution application for possession saves a round of adjournments once the resistance is raised.

Executing a decree against a company, a firm, or a corporate debtor already before the NCLT

A decree-holder executing against a partnership firm proceeds under Order 21 Rule 50, which lets execution issue against the firm's property in the firm name without first joining every partner individually, and also allows execution against an individual partner's personal property where that partner has been given an opportunity to show cause why they should not be held liable, a step courts treat as a genuine procedural safeguard rather than a formality. Execution against a company follows the ordinary Order 21 route against the company's own assets, since a decree against a company is a decree against the corporate person, not against its directors or shareholders, who are not personally liable to have their own property attached merely because the company they run has lost a suit.

The complication a Delhi or Noida decree-holder runs into most often with a corporate judgment-debtor is a parallel insolvency proceeding. Section 14(1)(a) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 imposes a moratorium, once the National Company Law Tribunal admits a corporate insolvency resolution process against the judgment-debtor, that in terms bars the institution of suits and the continuation of pending suits or proceedings against the corporate debtor, including execution of any judgment, decree or order in any court of law. A decree-holder who had already started execution, attachment orders issued, a sale proclamation out, finds that process frozen the moment the moratorium takes effect, and has to instead lodge a claim with the resolution professional for adjudication within the insolvency process rather than continuing to press the civil court for recovery.

This is the point at which execution law and insolvency law genuinely intersect, and it is worth a decree-holder checking, before investing further in attachment or sale proceedings against a corporate debtor that looks financially distressed, whether an insolvency application is already pending or imminent before the NCLT. Once a resolution plan is approved and binds the corporate debtor under Section 31 of the IBC, a decree that was not accounted for in that plan is generally treated as extinguished for the amount beyond what the plan provides, which makes lodging the claim with the resolution professional within the process, rather than sitting on the civil decree and waiting for the moratorium to lift, the step a decree-holder cannot afford to miss. Our earlier piece on debt recovery compares the SARFAESI, DRT and IBC routes directly and sets out how a secured or unsecured creditor should weigh pressing ahead with execution against filing, or responding to, an insolvency application instead.

Execution in Delhi's district courts and the Delhi High Court

A decree passed by a Delhi district court is, as a rule, executed by that same court, following Section 38, so long as the judgment-debtor's assets or person remain within Delhi's territorial limits. Delhi's district courts presently exercise civil jurisdiction, and correspondingly execute decrees, up to the pecuniary ceiling that applies to filing the original suit there, commonly understood to sit at roughly Rs 2 crore, with disputes above that value falling to the Delhi High Court's original side. A Full Court committee of the Delhi High Court has recommended raising that district-court ceiling substantially, to Rs 10 crore on one account reported in mid-2026, with the Delhi High Court Bar Association opposing the change and litigation over the committee's constitution still pending as of this writing; the ceiling that actually governs a given decree should be checked against the High Court's most current notification rather than assumed from either figure.

A decree passed by the Delhi High Court on its original side is, correspondingly, executed by the High Court itself unless the decree-holder applies under Section 39 to transfer it elsewhere because the judgment-debtor's assets lie outside Delhi altogether. This is the route a Delhi decree-holder would use to reach, say, a flat the judgment-debtor owns in Noida, applying first to the Delhi court that passed the decree for an order transferring it to the Gautam Buddh Nagar court for execution against that specific asset, since the Delhi court cannot issue an attachment order that reaches across the Delhi-Uttar Pradesh boundary on its own.

Execution in Noida and Greater Noida

Our earlier piece on commercial litigation in Noida and Greater Noida set out which forum, the Gautam Buddh Nagar Commercial Court or the ordinary civil court, hears a given dispute there in the first place, and that same court is, as a rule, the one that executes its own decree against assets within its territorial limits. A decree passed in Gautam Buddh Nagar against a judgment-debtor who holds property or a bank account within the district proceeds through the same Order 21 machinery described above, attachment, proclamation, sale or garnishee, without needing a transfer.

The transfer question arises in the other direction as often as the Delhi-to-Noida direction discussed earlier, where a decree passed in Gautam Buddh Nagar needs to be executed against assets the judgment-debtor holds in Delhi or elsewhere in the NCR. The decree-holder applies to the Noida court under Section 39 for transfer to the court with territorial jurisdiction over those assets, and the receiving court then exercises the transferee powers Section 42 confers, following the same procedure a Delhi-origin transfer would follow in reverse. Decree-holders who litigated in Noida precisely because that was where the contract was performed should not assume, once the decree is in hand, that the same court can reach assets the debtor has since moved, or always held, across the state line.

Why execution stalls, and what actually moves it along

In practice, the delays decree-holders encounter in Delhi and Noida execution proceedings tend to come from a narrow set of recurring causes rather than from any single defect in the law itself. Incomplete or imprecise property descriptions in the execution application, discussed earlier, cause returns and re-filings. Genuine uncertainty over where the judgment-debtor's attachable assets actually sit, particularly where a debtor has moved funds or transferred property to relatives shortly before or after the decree, forces the decree-holder into a slower process of locating assets through Rule 41 examination or independent investigation before any attachment can even be ordered. Rule 58 claims by relatives or associates of the judgment-debtor, some genuine and some plainly collusive, add a further layer of adjudication the court cannot skip over.

What tends to move execution along fastest is preparation before filing rather than persistence after. A decree-holder who has, ahead of the execution application, identified the specific immovable property or bank accounts to be attached, obtained updated title or account records, and calculated the amount due with precision, gives the executing court little room to delay on procedural grounds. Where the judgment-debtor is known to have assets outside the passing court's jurisdiction, applying for transfer under Section 39 promptly, rather than after the first attempt at local attachment has already failed, saves the time that a decree-holder, bound by the twelve-year outer limit discussed earlier in this piece, genuinely cannot afford to lose.

Pulling an up-to-date encumbrance search and the latest revenue or municipal record for the property before seeking attachment is a small step that avoids a larger one later. An attachment order describing a property by an outdated khasra number, an old municipal ward reference, or a survey number that has since been subdivided, can be challenged as not properly identifying the property at all, forcing a fresh application and a fresh round of the Rule 54 process. The same care pays off at the proclamation stage under Rule 66, where a proclamation that understates or overstates the property's encumbrances invites an objection under Rule 90 from either the judgment-debtor or a disappointed bidder, each capable of delaying confirmation of a sale the decree-holder had expected to be the end of the matter.

Execution of a decree in Delhi or Noida follows a settled statutory scheme, Sections 36 to 74 of the CPC and the detail in Order 21, but a settled scheme is not the same as a quick one. The modes available under Section 51, the distinct procedures for movable and immovable property, the genuine protections Rule 58 and Rules 97 to 106 give to third parties, and the hard twelve-year outer limit under Article 136 of the Limitation Act, all have to be worked through correctly and, in most cases, promptly.

A decree-holder who treats execution as a formality to be dealt with eventually, rather than as a second contested proceeding requiring its own preparation, usually pays for that assumption in years of delay. A judgment-debtor, conversely, has real and legitimate objections available under the rules discussed here, exemption of specific property under Section 60, a genuine third-party claim under Rule 58, an irregularity in the sale process under Rule 90, but those objections succeed on their merits and on the evidence, not on delay for its own sake.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a decree-holder have to execute a money decree in Delhi or Noida?

Twelve years from the date the decree becomes enforceable, under Article 136 of the Limitation Act, 1963. Courts have consistently held that Section 5 of the Limitation Act's power to condone delay does not extend this period, so an execution petition filed after the twelve years will ordinarily be rejected as time-barred rather than considered on sufficient cause.

Can a judgment-debtor's salary be attached in full to satisfy a decree?

No. Section 60 of the CPC protects a specified portion of salary from attachment so the judgment-debtor retains some means of subsistence, and the proviso also exempts specific categories of property such as necessary wearing apparel and tools of trade entirely. Attachment of salary therefore recovers only the non-exempt portion each pay cycle, which can take considerably longer than attaching a lump sum asset.

What happens if someone other than the judgment-debtor claims the attached property is theirs?

They can file a claim under Order 21 Rule 58, and the executing court must adjudicate it as a genuine question of right, title or interest rather than dismiss it summarily. The court's decision on the claim is treated as a decree for the purpose of appeal, so either side can appeal an adverse ruling instead of starting a fresh suit.

Is a notice to the judgment-debtor always required before execution starts?

Not always. Order 21 Rule 22 requires a show-cause notice only where the execution application is filed more than two years after the decree, or against the legal representative of a deceased judgment-debtor. Filed within two years of the decree, execution can generally proceed without that notice.

A Delhi decree-holder has a judgment-debtor who owns property in Noida. Which court attaches it?

The Delhi court that passed the decree cannot attach property outside Delhi on its own authority under Section 39(4) of the CPC. The decree-holder must apply to the Delhi court for an order under Section 39 transferring the decree to the Gautam Buddh Nagar court, which then executes it against the Noida property using the same powers the original court would have had.

Can an auction sale of attached property be reversed after it happens?

Before confirmation, yes, in two situations. Under Rule 89, the judgment-debtor can have the sale set aside by depositing the full decretal amount plus five per cent of the purchase price for the auction purchaser. Under Rule 90, any affected person can apply to set the sale aside for a material irregularity or fraud in conducting it, but must also show the irregularity actually caused substantial injury, not merely that a technical defect occurred.

What is the current pecuniary limit for Delhi's district courts to execute a decree?

Delhi's district courts presently execute decrees up to roughly the same pecuniary ceiling that applies to filing the original suit there, commonly understood to be around Rs 2 crore. A Full Court proposal to raise this ceiling substantially has been reported as pending, so the exact current figure should be checked against the Delhi High Court's latest notification before relying on it.
