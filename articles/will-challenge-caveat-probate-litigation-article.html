Challenging a Will in India: Caveat, Probate Litigation and the Propounder's Burden of Proof

A will does not prove itself. Once someone lodges a caveat, the person who wants it enforced has to satisfy a court it is genuine, and the law places that burden squarely on them.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 19 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A will that looks complete on its face, signed, witnessed and seemingly in order, can still be stopped cold the moment a family member who stands to lose by it walks into the probate registry and lodges a caveat. From that point on, the document is no longer presumed valid merely because it exists. The person propounding it has to prove it was executed by a testator of sound mind, free of pressure, and that any feature a court finds suspicious has been explained away to that court's satisfaction. High net worth families, where a will often departs from what intestate succession would have produced, are the ones most likely to see this process tested, and the ones with the most to lose from getting the paperwork wrong at the time of execution.

Key points

  • A caveat under Sections 283 and 284 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 converts an uncontested probate petition into a contentious proceeding tried broadly as a civil suit under Section 295
  • The propounder of a will always carries the burden of proving its due execution and the testator's sound disposing mind, a rule the Supreme Court fixed in H. Venkatachala Iyengar v. B.N. Thimmajamma as far back as 1958 and has applied consistently since
  • Where the court finds the will surrounded by suspicious circumstances, unnatural exclusions, an unexplained role for the propounder in drafting or execution, or a shaky signature, the propounder's burden becomes heavier and the suspicion has to be removed before the will can be accepted
  • The Supreme Court held in 2025, in Ramesh Chand v. Suresh Chand, that at least one attesting witness must be examined to prove a will under Section 67 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (formerly Section 68 of the Evidence Act, 1872), whether or not anyone is contesting it
  • A caveat cannot be used to settle who actually owns the property the will describes; probate decides only that the will is genuine and that it should be acted upon, leaving title disputes to be fought separately
  • Once probate is granted it operates as a judgment in rem under Section 41 (now Section 35 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam), binding everyone, not merely the parties who appeared, and can only be undone later through revocation under Section 263 or an attack on grounds of fraud or lack of jurisdiction
  • The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 removed the general requirement under the now-omitted Section 213 that certain wills be probated before an executor or legatee could establish a right under them, but a caveat and the litigation it produces still arise wherever someone actually applies for probate or letters of administration, voluntarily or because a bank, registrar or foreign authority insists on it
  • A Supreme Court ruling in April 2026, S. Leorex Sebastian v. Sarojini, restored a revocation of probate for suppressing a registered interest in the estate from the court, a reminder that a grant already obtained is not the end of the risk if the petition concealed something material

Why This Still Matters After Probate Stopped Being Mandatory

For decades, Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 made probate a precondition for establishing a right as executor or legatee under certain wills, principally those made by Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains, and by some Parsis, where the will or the testator's assets fell within the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of the High Courts at Calcutta, Madras or Bombay. The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025, which received presidential assent on 20 December 2025, omitted Section 213 altogether, so that requirement no longer exists as a general matter. A separate article on this site sets out that change and its practical effect in full, and this one does not repeat it.

What the repeal did not do is remove probate litigation from the picture. Families still apply for probate voluntarily, because banks, share registrars, land registration authorities and courts abroad routinely ask for it before releasing assets or recognising a transfer, and because a probate decree gives an executor a degree of protection no informal arrangement can match. Letters of administration with the will annexed, sought where the named executor has died, declined, or was never properly appointed, raise the identical questions about proof and objection. Wherever someone actually files that petition, whether out of necessity or simply caution, the caveat mechanism and the propounder's burden this article describes apply in full, regardless of what Section 213 used to require.

High net worth families are disproportionately exposed to this kind of dispute, not because their wills are less carefully drafted, but because a will that departs meaningfully from what intestate succession would have produced, favouring one child over several, cutting out a spouse from a second marriage, or directing a family business to one branch of the family, gives a disappointed heir an obvious reason to object. The remedy the law gives that heir is the caveat, and the result of filing one is that the will's validity has to be proved in court rather than simply assumed.

The Petition, the Citation and the Caveat

An application for probate, or for letters of administration with the will annexed, is made under Section 276 of the Act by petition to the court having jurisdiction, ordinarily the District Judge, or the High Court exercising ordinary original civil jurisdiction in Calcutta, Mumbai and Chennai where the estate or a significant part of it falls within that jurisdiction. The petition has to identify the testator, the date of death, state that the document annexed is the testator's last will, that it was duly executed, and set out the petitioner's claim to be appointed, typically as the executor named in the will itself.

Before any grant issues, Section 283(1)(c) empowers the court to direct that citations be issued to persons who have, or claim to have, an interest in the estate, so that they have an opportunity to object before the matter is decided rather than after. Courts have read the class of people entitled to such notice broadly: a Bombay High Court ruling held that the expression "person having interest" should be construed liberally to cover anyone who may be adversely affected by the grant, and other authority goes so far as to say that a mere possibility of an interest is enough to let someone oppose.

The mechanism by which that opposition actually reaches the file is the caveat, governed by Section 284. A person who wishes to contest the petition lodges a caveat with the court, typically supported by an affidavit setting out the grounds of objection, before the grant is made. A caveat by itself decides nothing; one Madras High Court description calls it nothing more than a warning that the application is likely to become contentious and will have to be tried as a suit. Equally, the absence of a caveat has consequences of its own: a Karnataka High Court ruling held that a person who never entered a caveat has no standing to object to a grant after the fact, which is why anyone with a genuine objection needs to act before the grant, not after.

From Petition to Suit: What Section 295 Actually Changes

Once a caveat is on file and the petition becomes contested, Section 295 provides that the proceeding is to be tried as nearly as may be in the manner followed for ordinary civil suits. Courts have described the effect carefully rather than treating it as a formality: one ruling notes that the contentious proceeding does not become an ordinary suit in every respect, it merely takes the form of a regular suit for the limited purpose of applying the Code of Civil Procedure's machinery, pleadings, discovery, examination and cross-examination of witnesses, and a reasoned judgment, to what remains a probate proceeding at its core.

There is a genuine split in how automatically this conversion happens. A Madras High Court view treats Section 295 as leaving the court no discretion once there is real contention; the matter must proceed as a suit and cannot be decided summarily. A Bombay High Court view reads the same section as merely empowering the court to treat the matter as a suit if it considers that appropriate, rather than compelling it in every case. For a propounder or an objector in a given proceeding, the practical answer depends on which High Court's precedent governs, and on how the particular judge reads the caveat and the objections raised in it, which is why the pleadings at this stage, framing the issues with precision rather than a general denial, carry more weight than they might in an ordinary suit.

What this conversion means in practice for a family contesting a will is that the dispute stops being a paper exercise decided on affidavits and becomes a trial. Witnesses to the will's execution, the doctor who may have examined the testator close to the date of signing, the draftsman who prepared the document, and often the propounder personally, are examined and cross-examined in open court, with the finding turning on how that evidence holds up rather than on the document's appearance alone.

Costs in a contested probate matter follow the court's general discretion under the Code of Civil Procedure once the proceeding takes the form of a suit, and that discretion varies between courts and on the specific facts of each case rather than following any fixed rule for testamentary matters as a class. A family deciding whether to file a caveat on a genuine but still developing suspicion should treat the costs position as something to ask local counsel about for the specific forum involved, rather than assume it automatically mirrors an ordinary civil suit argued purely on the merits in every single respect.

The Propounder's Burden: Venkatachala Iyengar and What Followed

The foundational statement of who carries the burden in a contested will case comes from the Supreme Court's 1958 decision in H. Venkatachala Iyengar v. B.N. Thimmajamma, reported at AIR 1959 SC 443. The Court held that the propounder of a will must prove it was validly executed, that the testator had testamentary capacity and a sound disposing mind at the time, and that the testator understood and approved the document's contents. Where there is nothing suspicious about the circumstances, proof of a valid signature and testamentary capacity may suffice. Where the will is surrounded by suspicious circumstances, the propounder's burden becomes heavier, and the suspicion has to be removed to the court's satisfaction before the will can be accepted as the testator's genuine last wish.

On the facts before it, the Court found the propounder's own close involvement in drafting and procuring execution of the will, combined with bequests that unnaturally favoured his own sons over other, closer relatives who would otherwise have inherited, suspicious enough to require an explanation the propounder could not supply. The will was refused probate. That combination, a propounder who played an active role in preparing or arranging execution of a document from which he or someone close to him stands to benefit disproportionately, remains the fact pattern courts return to most often when deciding whether a will's circumstances cross the line into suspicious.

The Supreme Court revisited the same framework in Meena Pradhan v. Kamla Pradhan, decided on 21 September 2023, upholding a will that excluded the testator's first wife and her children in favour of his second wife and their daughter. The Court held that suspicious circumstances must be real, grounded in the facts, and not merely the product of a doubting mind, and that an allegation of bigamy, however serious in itself, is simply not relevant to whether the will was validly executed. The ruling is a useful corrective against treating every unequal distribution, or every allegation that reflects badly on the testator's personal life, as automatically suspicious; the suspicion has to attach to the execution of the document itself, its genuineness, the testator's capacity, or the propounder's role in procuring it, not to the testator's broader conduct.

Suspicious Circumstances Are Not the Same Thing as Undue Influence or Fraud

Family members contesting a will tend to use the language of suspicion, undue influence and fraud interchangeably, but Indian courts place the burden of proof differently depending on which of these is actually being argued, and getting this wrong can cost an objector the case even where the underlying facts are strong. Where the objection is that the will's own circumstances, an unexplained role for the propounder in its preparation, an unnatural exclusion, a shaky signature, raise suspicion about whether it reflects the testator's genuine wishes, the burden to dispel that suspicion sits with the propounder, as Venkatachala Iyengar holds and as the Supreme Court reaffirmed in Gurdial Singh v. Jagir Kaur, decided on 17 July 2025, which held that probate should be granted only once the propounder has dispelled the suspicious circumstances and satisfied the court's conscience that the will reflects the testator's free will.

Where the objection instead is that the will was procured through undue influence, fraud or coercion, a materially different claim, that someone actively overbore the testator's free will or deceived them into signing, the burden shifts the other way. The Supreme Court held in Surendra Pal v. Saraswati Arora, a 1974 decision, that where a caveator alleges undue influence, fraud or coercion, the onus of proving that allegation rests on the caveator, not on the propounder. Later High Court decisions applying the same principle, including in Shivakumar v. Sharanabasappa, treat allegations of fabrication, fraud or undue influence as pleas the challenger has to make out with evidence, rather than suspicions the propounder has to pre-empt.

There is a recognised exception that matters particularly for family businesses and elderly testators who relied heavily on one relative, agent or caregiver. Where the relationship between the testator and the principal beneficiary was a fiduciary one, or one in which the beneficiary held a position of real dominance over the testator, whether as a caregiver, a financial agent holding a power of attorney, or a trusted employee managing the testator's affairs, courts have placed the burden back on that beneficiary to show the transaction was not the product of undue influence. Afsar Shaikh v. Soleman Bibi, a 1976 Supreme Court decision concerned with a gift rather than a will, is the authority usually cited for this shift, and the same reasoning has been extended to wills where the propounder occupied a comparable position of dominance over a testator who depended on them. An objector who can show that the propounder managed the testator's finances, lived with them, or controlled their access to other family members in the period before the will was signed is, in substance, arguing for this shifted burden rather than for the ordinary suspicious-circumstances rule, and should plead the point that way rather than leaving the court to work it out unaided.

Section 67 BSA: The Attesting Witness Requirement Does Not Bend for an Uncontested Will

A will is a document the law requires to be attested, and Section 63 of the Indian Succession Act sets out the attestation requirements themselves, at least two witnesses each seeing the testator sign or receiving an acknowledgment of the signature, and each signing in the testator's presence. Proving that this actually happened is a separate, evidentiary question, governed today by Section 67 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which replaced Section 68 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 without changing its substance: where a document is required by law to be attested, it cannot be used as evidence until at least one attesting witness has been called to prove its execution, if such a witness is alive, subject to the process of the court, and capable of giving evidence.

The Supreme Court's decision in Ramesh Chand (D) Thr. LRs v. Suresh Chand, reported as 2025 LiveLaw (SC) 862, addressed a Delhi High Court ruling that had treated the attesting-witness requirement as mandatory only where heirs actually dispute the will, and dispensable otherwise. The Supreme Court disagreed in clear terms, holding that the requirement applies to every will sought to be relied on as evidence, irrespective of whether anyone opposes it. A will that has sailed through without a caveat is not thereby exempt from this proof; the requirement is about what Section 67 demands of the document, not about whether a particular party happened to object.

A separate line of authority addresses what happens when no attesting witness can actually be produced, now Section 68 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (formerly Section 69 of the Evidence Act), which allows proof through other means, such as establishing the handwriting of the testator and of one attesting witness, but only once the propounder has first shown that no attesting witness is available to be called, a condition the Supreme Court has treated as one to be satisfied before the alternative route can be used at all, not a shortcut a propounder can reach for out of convenience.

What a Caveat Cannot Decide: Probate Is Not a Title Suit

A common misunderstanding, particularly among family members who file a caveat expecting the probate court to resolve who actually owns the property the will describes, is about what the proceeding is actually for. Probate decides whether the document is the testator's genuine, validly executed last will and whether it should be acted upon as such. It does not decide, and the Supreme Court has said so directly, who has title to the properties the will purports to dispose of; a grant of probate does not adjudicate those disputes at all.

The practical consequence is that a family locked in a dispute over both the will's validity and who actually owns a particular property, say where one branch claims the asset was never the testator's to give because it belonged to a joint family or was held in trust, cannot expect the probate proceeding to resolve the ownership question even after it has fully tried the validity question. That dispute has to be litigated separately, in a civil suit for declaration of title or partition, which may run in parallel with, or follow, the probate proceeding itself. Families who treat a caveat as a vehicle for raising every grievance about the estate often find the probate court declining to engage with arguments that belong in a different suit altogether, which wastes time and costs that could have gone into the proceeding that actually mattered.

This separation of questions matters most where the testator purported to will away an asset that, on examination, was never theirs alone to give, a flat registered jointly, a shareholding held as a nominee for a family trust, or agricultural land subject to tenancy or local land-ceiling restrictions that limit who can inherit it at all. A probate grant that declares the will genuine does not cure any of these underlying defects in the testator's title, and a beneficiary who receives probate in their favour can still find themselves fighting a second round of litigation, this time over whether the asset the will purported to give them was the testator's to dispose of in the first place. Raising the title question early, in a properly framed separate suit rather than folded into the probate objections, at least lets both disputes proceed on their own tracks instead of each one waiting on the other to finish.

After the Grant: Revocation Under Section 263

A grant of probate is not necessarily the end of the story. Section 263 allows the court to revoke or annul a grant for just cause, and the section's explanations set out circumstances that are deemed to amount to just cause, including that the proceeding to obtain the grant was defective in substance, that the grant was obtained fraudulently by making a false suggestion or by concealing material facts, or that the grant has become useless and inoperative through circumstances since arising. The Bombay High Court held, in Sarwan Kumar Jhabarmal Choudhary v. Sachin Shyamsundar Begrajka, decided in 2025, that these explanations are illustrative rather than exhaustive, so a court can find just cause on facts that do not fit neatly into any of the listed categories, depending on the circumstances of the case.

The Supreme Court applied this framework in S. Leorex Sebastian v. Sarojini, decided on 21 April 2026 and reported at 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 408, restoring a District Court's order revoking a probate grant. The Court treated the petitioner's fraudulent suppression of a registered interest in the estate, material information the citation process should have surfaced had it been handled properly under Section 283(1)(c), as falling squarely within the fraud and concealment grounds under Section 263's explanations, and reiterated that probate is a judgment in rem, binding the whole world, which is precisely why the integrity of the process that produces it, full and honest disclosure at the petition stage, matters as much as the merits argued at trial.

A grant that survives the caveat stage, in other words, is not immune from later challenge if it later emerges that the petition concealed something a citation should have brought to light. Families who suspect that a probate was obtained by hiding an interested party's claim, rather than simply disputing the will's genuineness up front, have a distinct remedy in revocation, though one that comes with its own timing constraints, discussed next.

Limitation: When the Clock Starts Running

The Indian Succession Act does not itself prescribe a limitation period for either a probate petition or an application for revocation, which for years fed an argument that no limitation applies at all because the right to apply is a continuing one. The Supreme Court closed off that argument as a general proposition in Kunvarjeet Singh Khandpur v. Kirandeep Kaur, holding that Article 137 of the Limitation Act, 1963, the residuary three-year period running from when the right to apply accrues, extends to petitions filed under any Act to a civil court, probate petitions included, and that the period runs from when the right to apply actually accrues rather than from the date of the testator's death.

The Supreme Court's 2026 decision in Dhiraj Dutta v. Anirban Sen, reported as 2026 INSC 602, applied the same Article 137 framework to an application for revocation of probate, and held that where the ground for revocation is concealment or fraud discovered only later, the three-year period begins to run from the date the aggrieved party actually came to know of the ground, not from the date the grant itself was made. This is reported as a 2026 ruling and the full judgment was not separately verified for this article beyond the reporting available, so a family relying on the date-of-knowledge rule in a specific case should confirm the judgment's exact terms before pleading limitation on that basis.

The practical point for anyone who suspects a will was wrongly probated, or who only belatedly learns of facts that would support revocation, is that delay has consequences even though the statute itself is silent. Waiting to gather every piece of evidence before acting can cost the very remedy the evidence was meant to support, which is why a protective application, filed as soon as the ground for revocation becomes apparent, is usually the safer course even if the supporting material is still being assembled.

The Forum and the Route of Appeal

Jurisdiction over a probate petition ordinarily sits with the District Judge of the district where the testator had a fixed place of residence at death, or, in the three presidency towns, with the High Court exercising its ordinary original civil jurisdiction, which for Kolkata-based families means the Calcutta High Court's original side rather than a district court. Once the matter becomes contentious under Section 295 and is tried in the manner of a suit, the usual rules of civil procedure, including the taking of evidence and the framing of issues, apply to that trial regardless of which forum is hearing it.

An order of a District Judge refusing or granting probate is appealable to the High Court under Section 299. Where the order under appeal was itself passed by a High Court's single judge on its original side, a further question arises over whether a Letters Patent Appeal lies to a Division Bench; the Supreme Court has held that it does, relying on the relevant Letters Patent clause for the High Court concerned, though at least one High Court has reasoned, on a related point, that because Section 299 does not use the words "decree" or "judgment", an ordinary first appeal under the general civil procedure framework does not lie from every order a District Judge passes in a probate case. Which of these applies to a particular order depends on the forum, the nature of the order, and the specific High Court's own rules, and is worth confirming before assuming an appeal route is open.

For families with assets in Kolkata, Delhi or Noida, the practical choice of forum rarely involves genuine doubt about which court has jurisdiction, since that follows from where the testator resided or where the immovable property lies, but it does affect how the contentious proceeding is actually conducted. A testamentary suit on the Calcutta High Court's original side follows that court's own original-side procedure and its own roster for testamentary matters, while an equivalent dispute over a testator who lived in Noida or Greater Noida is more likely to proceed before the District Judge at Gautam Buddha Nagar, with the slower pace that can come from a district court's general civil docket rather than a dedicated testamentary bench. Neither forum changes the substance of the propounder's burden or the Section 67 witness requirement, but the choice of forum does affect how quickly a contested probate matter is likely to be decided, which is a factor worth weighing when a family is deciding whether to settle a dispute rather than litigate it to judgment.

Practical Steps for a Family Expecting a Will to Be Contested

Families who anticipate that a will departing from equal distribution will draw an objection are better served preparing for that at the drafting and execution stage than after a caveat has already been filed. Contemporaneous medical evidence of the testator's capacity, obtained close to the date of execution rather than reconstructed afterward from memory, carries far more weight than a later affidavit from relatives asserting the testator was perfectly lucid. A draftsman and witnesses who are independent of the principal beneficiary, rather than drawn from that beneficiary's own circle, reduce the single factor Venkatachala Iyengar treats as most suspicious, a propounder's own hand in procuring the document.

Keeping the attesting witnesses traceable matters just as much as choosing them carefully. Ramesh Chand makes clear that a will cannot be proved, contested or not, without calling at least one of them, so a witness who has since moved abroad, changed contact details, or fallen out of touch with the family creates a real evidentiary gap years later, when the will is finally put to proof. A short written record made at the time, noting who was present, in what order the signatures occurred, and any medical observation available on the day, costs little to prepare and can matter a great deal if the will is challenged a decade on.

Families with NRI members face a particular version of this problem, since an attesting witness who has since settled abroad may not be available to appear in person when the will is finally put to proof, sometimes years after execution. Evidence on commission, taken before a court or consular authority in the witness's country of residence and sent back for use in the Indian proceeding, remains available for exactly this situation, but arranging it adds time and cost to an already slow proceeding, which is one more reason to choose attesting witnesses who are likely to remain reachable in India, rather than relying on a family member who is only visiting at the time the will happens to be signed.

For a family that has already received a citation or is considering whether to lodge a caveat, the decision is time sensitive. A caveat has to be filed before the grant is made, and standing to object afterward is far weaker once a grant has issued, limited in practice to the revocation route under Section 263 and its own separate evidentiary threshold. Anyone with a genuine stake in the estate who learns of a probate petition should treat the citation, or even informal word that a petition has been filed, as the moment to act, not as something to defer until the matter feels more urgent.

Business families who use a will to direct shares in a private company, a partnership interest or a family-run enterprise to a particular branch of the family face a sharper version of this risk, since the same facts that make such a will likely to be contested, unequal treatment of children or grandchildren, a controlling role played by one beneficiary in the testator's later years, can also disrupt the running of the business while the proceeding is pending. Pairing the will with a clear, contemporaneously documented account of why a particular successor was chosen, kept separately from the will itself and not shown to beneficiaries in advance, gives the propounder material to work with if a suspicious-circumstances objection is later raised, without turning the will itself into a document that explains or defends its own terms.

What Remains Unsettled

Three points in this area are better treated as open than as settled, and a family or practitioner relying on them in a live matter should check the current position rather than this article alone. Whether Section 295 makes conversion to a suit automatic once any real contention exists, or leaves the court a discretion to decide the point summarily, is answered differently by the Madras and Bombay High Courts, and the Supreme Court has not resolved that specific conflict in the material reviewed for this article. Whether a Letters Patent Appeal lies from a single judge's decision under Section 299, and whether an ordinary first appeal lies from a District Judge's probate order at all, depend on the specific High Court's Letters Patent and on decisions that do not entirely agree with each other. And the date-of-knowledge rule for limitation on a revocation application, from the 2026 Dhiraj Dutta ruling, is reported only through secondary coverage at the time of writing, without independent access to the full judgment text, so its precise scope should be confirmed against the Supreme Court's own record before being relied on in a pending matter.

None of the law described here makes a will harder to write. It makes a contested will harder to enforce without the paperwork and the witnesses to back it up, which is exactly the point of placing the burden on the propounder rather than on the family member who objects. A will that excludes an obvious heir, favours one branch of a family business, or simply looks unusual against what intestate succession would have produced is not thereby invalid, but it is the kind of will most likely to be tested, and the test is not forgiving of gaps in how it was executed or recorded.

A caveat is a warning, not a verdict, and the litigation that follows one turns on evidence a court can actually examine: witnesses who can be found and cross-examined, medical opinion contemporaneous with the signing, and a propounder whose own role in procuring the document can withstand scrutiny. Families who want a will to survive a challenge are better off building that evidentiary record before the testator's death than arguing, years later, that the suspicion a court has already noticed should be ignored.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a caveat in a probate case, and who can file one?

A caveat under Section 284 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 is a formal objection lodged with the court before a grant of probate or letters of administration is made, turning the petition into a contested proceeding. Courts read the class of people who can file one broadly, extending it to anyone who has, or may plausibly have, an interest in the deceased's estate, beyond the circle of named heirs.

Who has to prove that a contested will is genuine?

The person propounding the will, usually the executor named in it, carries the burden throughout. The Supreme Court fixed this in H. Venkatachala Iyengar v. B.N. Thimmajamma in 1958, and later decisions, including Meena Pradhan v. Kamla Pradhan in 2023, have applied the same rule: the objector does not have to disprove the will, the propounder has to prove it.

What makes a will's circumstances legally suspicious?

There is no fixed checklist, but courts repeatedly point to the propounder's own active role in drafting or procuring execution of a document from which they benefit, an unnatural or unexplained departure from how the testator's estate would otherwise have been divided, or doubts about the testator's physical or mental condition at the time of signing. The Supreme Court has cautioned that the suspicion must be real and tied to the will's execution, not simply any fact that reflects badly on the testator.

Is it still necessary to call a witness to prove a will if nobody is contesting it?

Yes. The Supreme Court held in Ramesh Chand v. Suresh Chand in 2025 that Section 67 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (formerly Section 68 of the Evidence Act) requires at least one attesting witness to be examined to prove a will, whether or not the will is being actively disputed by any heir.

Can a probate proceeding decide who owns the property described in the will?

No. Probate decides only whether the will is genuine and should be given effect; it does not adjudicate disputes about title to the underlying property. A family disputing both the will's validity and ownership of a specific asset needs a separate civil suit for the ownership question.

Can a will still be challenged after probate has already been granted?

Yes, through an application for revocation under Section 263 of the Indian Succession Act, which allows a grant to be revoked for just cause, including fraud or concealment of material facts in the original petition. The grounds listed in the section are illustrative rather than exhaustive, as the Bombay High Court held in 2025, and the Supreme Court restored a revocation on fraud grounds in April 2026 in S. Leorex Sebastian v. Sarojini.

Is there a time limit to challenge a will or apply for revocation of probate?

The Succession Act itself does not prescribe one, but the Supreme Court has held that the residuary three-year period under Article 137 of the Limitation Act, 1963 applies, running from when the right to apply accrues. For revocation on grounds of concealment discovered later, a 2026 Supreme Court ruling is reported to have held that the period runs from the date of actual knowledge, though that ruling's exact scope should be verified before being relied on.

Is probate still necessary now that Section 213 of the Succession Act has been repealed?

The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 removed the general requirement that certain wills be probated before a right under them could be established in court. Probate itself has not disappeared, and families still seek it where a bank, registrar or foreign authority insists on it, or simply to obtain the certainty a court decree provides; wherever that petition is actually filed, the caveat and burden-of-proof rules described here continue to apply.
