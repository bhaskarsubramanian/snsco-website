Enforcing a Foreign Money Judgment in India: Section 44A, Reciprocating Territories and the Fresh-Suit Route

Why a UK judgment and a US judgment are enforced in India through two entirely different procedures, what Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure actually lets an Indian court reopen, and what a foreign judgment creditor needs ready before either route can move.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 14 November 2026 · Law stated as on 13 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company that has won a money judgment against an Indian counterparty, or an NRI who has obtained a judgment abroad against a relative or debtor who has since moved assets to India, usually assumes the hard part is already over. It is not. Indian law treats a judgment from London very differently from a judgment from New York, even though both are foreign court judgments for the same kind of claim, and picking the wrong route can cost years. This piece sets out the two separate procedures the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 actually provides, which countries qualify for the faster of the two, what an Indian court can and cannot reopen when asked to recognise a foreign judgment, and the practical sequence a judgment creditor should follow before either procedure can begin to move.

Key points

  • Section 44A CPC lets a decree from a court in a 'reciprocating territory' be executed in India directly, as if it had been passed by an Indian district court, without a fresh trial on the merits.
  • The list of reciprocating territories is short and specific: it includes the United Kingdom, Singapore, Hong Kong, Bangladesh, Malaysia, New Zealand, Fiji, Papua New Guinea and the United Arab Emirates among others, but does not include the United States or Canada.
  • A judgment from a non-reciprocating territory, including the United States and Canada, cannot be executed under Section 44A at all; the judgment creditor instead has to file a fresh civil suit in an Indian court, using the foreign judgment itself as evidence of the debt under Section 13 CPC.
  • Section 13 makes a foreign judgment conclusive on the merits already decided, but only if none of six specific exceptions applies, covering jurisdiction, whether the case was heard on the merits, errors of law, natural justice, fraud and judgments that would enforce a claim founded on a breach of Indian law.
  • A suit to enforce a foreign judgment under Section 13 must generally be filed within three years of the date of the foreign judgment under Article 101 of the Limitation Act, 1963, a clock that keeps running even while an appeal is pending in the foreign court.
  • Enforcing a foreign court judgment runs on an entirely different track from enforcing a foreign arbitral award, which is governed by Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 and the New York Convention rather than by Sections 13 and 44A of the CPC.
  • Recovering the judgment amount is only the first step for a foreign judgment creditor; repatriating it out of India afterward raises its own separate FEMA compliance questions that need to be worked out before, not after, the money is recovered.

Two routes, and the one question that decides which applies

Indian law does not treat every foreign money judgment the same way. The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 provides two entirely separate mechanisms, and which one a judgment creditor can use turns on a single threshold question: was the judgment passed by a court in a country the Government of India has formally notified as a 'reciprocating territory' under Section 44A. Get this wrong, and a creditor can spend months pursuing an execution petition that an Indian court will simply refuse to entertain, or conversely file an unnecessary fresh suit when a faster execution route was actually available.

If the answer is yes, the judgment can be executed in India much as an Indian decree would be, through an execution petition rather than a fresh trial. If the answer is no, which covers the large majority of the world's major economies, including the United States and Canada, the judgment itself has no direct executory force in India at all. It becomes, at most, strong evidence in a fresh civil suit that the judgment creditor has to file from scratch in an Indian court, relying on Section 13 CPC to treat the foreign court's findings as conclusive unless the judgment debtor can bring the dispute within one of six narrow statutory exceptions.

The distinction is not a technicality that only litigators need to worry about. It changes the timeline, the cost, and the risk of the judgment debtor successfully reopening issues the foreign court had already decided, by years rather than months, so it needs to be worked out before a judgment creditor commits to either a particular Indian counsel's strategy or a particular limitation calculation.

The confusion this creates is not academic. Judgment creditors and even some Indian counsel sometimes assume, because the United Kingdom and a scattering of Commonwealth and Gulf jurisdictions are reciprocating territories, that the United States must be one too, given how much cross-border commercial litigation runs through US courts. It is not, and has never been notified as one, which is precisely the kind of assumption this piece is written to correct before it costs a client a year of avoidable delay.

What qualifies: judgment, decree and the kinds of claims this covers

Both routes are concerned with a foreign judgment that amounts to a money decree, a final determination by a foreign court that the defendant owes the claimant a specific or ascertainable sum. A judgment that only grants an injunction, a declaration, or a decree for specific performance of an act to be done outside India raises separate and more complicated recognition questions beyond the scope of straightforward money-decree enforcement, and a judgment in matrimonial, custody or probate proceedings is generally addressed under its own distinct body of Indian private international law rather than through Section 44A or an ordinary Section 13 suit.

Section 13's own text excludes certain categories from conclusiveness in substance rather than merely in form: a foreign judgment that would, if enforced in India, sustain a claim founded on a breach of a law in force in India is one of the six exceptions discussed below, which in practice has been understood to keep purely penal and revenue judgments, such as a foreign tax assessment or a foreign criminal fine, outside the scope of what an Indian court will recognise as a private money decree at all.

A foreign arbitral award sits entirely outside this framework. An award is enforced under Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, which implements India's obligations under the New York Convention, and runs on its own procedure, timeline and set of narrow defences, distinct from both Section 44A and Section 13. A judgment creditor who actually holds an arbitral award, not a court judgment, should look to this site's separate guide on enforcing foreign arbitral awards in India rather than to the CPC route described here.

A money judgment arising from a contract that itself violates Indian law when performed, for instance a transaction structured to avoid Indian foreign exchange control requirements, risks falling within the sixth Section 13 exception discussed below even where the foreign court applying its own law found nothing wrong with the claim. A judgment creditor whose underlying transaction had any Indian law dimension, a payment originally due from an Indian party, a guarantee given by an Indian entity, or security over Indian assets, should have that dimension specifically reviewed before assuming the foreign judgment will be recognised in India without difficulty.

The reciprocating territories list: who is on it, and who is conspicuously not

Section 44A applies only to a decree from a 'superior court' of a country the Central Government has specifically notified, by name, as a reciprocating territory in the Official Gazette. The list has been built up through individual notifications over several decades rather than through one single consolidated statute, and commonly reported compilations place the United Kingdom, Singapore, Bangladesh, Malaysia, Trinidad and Tobago, New Zealand, the Cook Islands including Niue, Western Samoa, Hong Kong, Papua New Guinea, Fiji and Aden on it, with the United Arab Emirates added by a specific notification issued on 17 January 2020 that also named the particular UAE courts, including the Federal Supreme Court and the Dubai Courts, recognised as superior courts for this purpose.

What matters just as much as who is on the list is who is not. The United States and Canada have never been notified as reciprocating territories, and nor have most European Union member states. A judgment from a US federal court, a Canadian provincial court, or a French or German court cannot be executed under Section 44A no matter how final or how clearly a money decree it is; the judgment creditor is confined to the fresh-suit route under Section 13, described below, for enforcement in India.

Because the list is built one notification at a time and has not been consolidated into a single current schedule that this piece can reproduce with certainty, a judgment creditor should always confirm the current gazetted status of the specific foreign court involved before assuming either route applies, rather than relying on a general list such as the one above, which can be added to or, in principle, removed from by further notification.

Executing a reciprocating-territory decree under Section 44A

Where the originating court is in a reciprocating territory, Section 44A lets the judgment creditor file a certified copy of the decree, together with a certificate from the foreign superior court stating what, if anything, has already been satisfied, directly with an Indian district court of competent jurisdiction, typically where the judgment debtor resides or holds assets. Once filed, the Indian court treats the decree as if it had itself passed the decree, and proceeds straight to execution, through attachment and sale of property, garnishee proceedings or arrest in the limited circumstances the CPC otherwise allows for an Indian decree, without reopening the underlying dispute or conducting anything resembling a fresh trial.

This does not mean the Indian court's role is purely mechanical. Section 44A's own proviso preserves the judgment debtor's right to raise, by way of objection to execution, any of the grounds on which the judgment would not have been treated as conclusive under Section 13 had it come up in an ordinary suit, so the six exceptions discussed in the next section remain available as a defence even in an execution proceeding, just argued at the execution stage rather than through a separate trial. A Bombay High Court ruling reported in early 2026 specifically addressed the character of this proceeding, holding that execution under Section 44A is not equivalent to instituting a fresh civil suit, which affects questions such as what procedural rights an objecting judgment debtor actually has at that stage.

In practice, the Section 44A route is considerably faster than a fresh suit precisely because it skips the trial stage entirely: there is no cause of action to plead, no limitation period for filing a fresh claim to calculate, and no need to prove the underlying debt afresh through Indian evidence rules. The judgment creditor's main task is procedural, assembling the certified decree, the foreign court's certificate, and whatever translation or authentication the Indian court requires, and then defending the limited Section 13-style objections the debtor is entitled to raise.

The certificate the foreign superior court is expected to provide under Section 44A is not a mere formality: it has to state the extent, if any, to which the decree has already been satisfied or adjusted, since an Indian executing court will only execute the decree to the extent it remains unsatisfied. A judgment creditor should request this certificate from the foreign court registry at the same time as the certified copy of the decree itself, rather than discovering only once the Indian proceeding is under way that the foreign court's own procedure for issuing such a certificate is unfamiliar to the registry staff handling the request.

The fresh-suit route: when the judgment is only evidence, not a decree

For a judgment from any other country, including the United States, Canada and most of continental Europe, there is no shortcut. The judgment creditor has to file an ordinary civil suit in an Indian court with jurisdiction over the judgment debtor or the debtor's assets, pleading the foreign judgment as the cause of action and asking the Indian court to pass its own decree based on it. Section 13 CPC is what gives this suit its practical force: it makes the foreign judgment conclusive as to any matter directly adjudicated between the same parties, so the Indian court generally does not re-try the underlying dispute on its merits, but it treats the judgment as conclusive evidence rather than as a decree already capable of execution.

Because the suit is an ordinary civil suit rather than an execution petition, a judgment creditor with a straightforward money claim can usually seek a summary procedure under Order XXXVII of the CPC, which is designed for suits on a liquidated demand and allows an Indian court to grant a decree without a full trial unless the defendant can show a triable defence, meaning a defence falling within one of the six exceptions under Section 13 or disputing some other genuine issue about the debt itself. A defendant who cannot show such a defence risks a decree being passed against it fairly quickly, which is the main practical benefit of structuring the claim this way rather than as an ordinary suit.

The judgment creditor still has to prove the foreign judgment was validly passed, typically through a certified or exemplified copy, and establish that it is a final judgment of a competent court on the merits, which is itself one of the six grounds a defendant can dispute under Section 13. This is why the foreign-judgment route, even where it succeeds, is understood by Indian practitioners as materially slower and more contested than a Section 44A execution, even when run as a summary suit: there is simply more for the defendant to put in issue.

A further practical point often missed is that the fresh suit, because it is a suit like any other, exposes a foreign plaintiff to Order XXV of the CPC, which allows an Indian court to order a plaintiff residing outside India and without sufficient immoveable property within India to furnish security for the defendant's costs before the suit proceeds further. A foreign judgment creditor planning a Section 13 suit should budget for this possibility and, where it can, be ready to show property or assets within India, or otherwise be prepared to post the security the court directs, rather than treating the suit's progress as assured once it is filed.

Section 13's six exceptions, one at a time

Section 13 states that a foreign judgment is conclusive, except in six situations. The first is where the judgment was not pronounced by a court of competent jurisdiction, judged by Indian principles of private international law on when a foreign court has jurisdiction over the defendant or the subject matter, not simply by whether the foreign court itself thought it had jurisdiction. The second is where the judgment was not given on the merits of the case, which generally excludes a judgment obtained by default or dismissal on a purely technical or procedural ground rather than after the foreign court actually considered the substance of the claim.

The third exception covers a judgment that appears on its face to be founded on an incorrect view of international law or a refusal to recognise Indian law where Indian law was applicable, which matters where the underlying contract or transaction had an Indian law connection the foreign court got wrong. The fourth is where the proceedings in which the judgment was obtained were opposed to natural justice, typically meaning the defendant was not given proper notice or a fair opportunity to be heard, not merely that the defendant disagrees with how the foreign court weighed the evidence.

The fifth exception, obtained by fraud, requires fraud in the sense of fraud on the court or on the opposing party in procuring the judgment itself, not merely an argument that the original claim was factually wrong. The sixth, and the one most often invoked in commercial disputes, is where the judgment sustains a claim founded on a breach of a law in force in India, which can bring judgments based on foreign exchange control violations, judgments enforcing an agreement illegal under Indian law, or judgments of a penal or revenue character within its reach. A defendant resisting enforcement, whether in a Section 44A execution or a Section 13 suit, succeeds only by bringing the case squarely within one of these six grounds, not by reopening the foreign court's assessment of the facts generally.

None of the six exceptions is available merely because the judgment debtor would have argued the case differently, or because Indian substantive law on the underlying contract differs from the foreign law the foreign court actually applied. The exceptions are deliberately narrow gateways, not a general invitation to relitigate, and Indian courts applying Section 13 have consistently treated them that way, which is precisely why the enforcement mechanism exists at all rather than requiring every foreign judgment creditor to prove its entire case over again from scratch in India.

Limitation: three years for the fresh suit, and an unsettled question for execution

A suit to enforce a foreign judgment under Section 13 is governed by Article 101 of the Limitation Act, 1963, which allows three years from the date of the foreign judgment. This period runs regardless of whether an appeal against the judgment is still pending in the foreign country, so a judgment creditor who waits for a foreign appeal to be exhausted before filing in India risks running out of time, and should generally file the Indian suit within the three years and deal with any need to stay it pending the foreign appeal separately, rather than waiting.

The limitation period applicable to an execution petition under Section 44A has in fact been settled by the Supreme Court. In Bank of Baroda v. Kotak Mahindra Bank Ltd (17 March 2020), the Court held that Section 44A is a purely enabling, procedural provision that does not itself prescribe a limitation period, that the twelve-year period under Article 136 applies only to decrees of Indian civil courts, and that an application to execute a foreign decree under Section 44A instead falls under the residuary Article 137, carrying a three-year period. Where execution has already begun in the originating country, that three-year period runs only once those foreign execution proceedings are finally concluded, not from the date of the decree itself. A judgment creditor relying on Section 44A should plan around this three-year, not twelve-year, period.

Defences a judgment debtor actually runs, and the ones that usually fail

In practice, the defences that succeed cluster around service and jurisdiction: a judgment debtor who can show it was never properly served with the foreign proceedings, or that the foreign court's basis for jurisdiction over it would not be recognised under Indian private international law principles, such as jurisdiction asserted solely on the claimant's residence rather than on the defendant's presence or submission, stands a real chance of defeating enforcement under the first or fourth exceptions. A debtor who simply argues that the foreign court reached the wrong conclusion on the facts or the foreign law applicable to the contract, without more, is arguing exactly the kind of merits challenge Section 13 is designed to foreclose, and such arguments are routinely rejected.

An ex parte foreign judgment, one obtained without the defendant appearing, deserves particular care from both sides. It is not automatically unenforceable in India; what matters is whether the defendant was properly served and given a genuine opportunity to defend, judged against Indian, not necessarily the foreign court's own, standards of adequate notice. A defendant who was served but chose not to appear generally cannot later succeed on a natural justice argument merely because it did not participate, whereas a defendant who can show it was never validly served at all, under the rules the foreign court itself was supposed to follow, has a genuine basis to resist enforcement under the fourth exception.

A debtor also sometimes argues that the foreign proceeding was brought in a forum chosen purely for the claimant's convenience, with no real connection to the dispute, hoping to bring the case within the first Section 13 exception on jurisdiction. This argument fares better where the contract itself contained an exclusive jurisdiction clause pointing elsewhere, or where the defendant had no presence, no assets and no dealings in the forum country at all, than where the defendant simply preferred to be sued in India and the foreign court's jurisdiction rested on an ordinary and recognised basis, such as the defendant's own submission to that court's jurisdiction by appearing and contesting the case on the merits there in the first place.

A practical sequence for the judgment creditor

A foreign judgment creditor, whether a foreign company with a judgment against an Indian counterparty or an NRI with a judgment against a relative or borrower, should work through a fixed sequence before committing to either route. First, confirm whether the originating court sits in a notified reciprocating territory, checking the current gazette position rather than relying on a general list. Second, obtain a certified or exemplified copy of the judgment, along with the foreign court's certificate of satisfaction where Section 44A applies, and have it apostilled or otherwise legalised and translated as the Indian court will require.

Third, locate the judgment debtor's assets in India, since both routes are only as useful as the assets available for execution once a decree exists; an Indian court will not search for assets on the creditor's behalf. Fourth, calculate the limitation position carefully against the date of the foreign judgment rather than against any later date, such as the conclusion of a foreign appeal, and file promptly rather than waiting for certainty that may never arrive. Fifth, and particularly for a foreign company rather than an individual creditor, confirm which Indian court has territorial jurisdiction over the judgment debtor or its assets before filing, since getting this wrong risks a transfer application that itself consumes months.

Sixth, build in time for authentication and translation from the outset: a judgment from a country that is itself party to the Hague Apostille Convention can usually be authenticated through a single apostille, while a judgment from a non-member country needs full consular legalisation, a materially slower process that has caught more than one judgment creditor close to the limitation deadline.

Seventh, keep a clear paper trail of every step taken toward enforcement in India, including correspondence with the foreign court registry, bank statements showing any partial recovery already made abroad, and advice taken on the Indian route chosen, since a debtor resisting enforcement years later will often probe exactly this kind of procedural history looking for a gap to exploit.

After recovery: FEMA and repatriating the money out of India

Recovering a decree in India is not the end of the exercise for a foreign judgment creditor who wants the money outside India. Remitting the recovered sum abroad raises its own separate compliance questions under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, including the authorised dealer bank's own due diligence on the source and purpose of the remittance and, depending on the amount and the creditor's own residential or corporate status, the certification requirements this site's separate guides on NRI remittance and on FEMA-compliant repatriation address in more detail. A foreign company should build this step into its planning from the outset rather than treating it as an afterthought once the Indian decree is finally in hand, since a bank's compliance queries at the remittance stage can themselves take weeks to resolve.

The authorised dealer bank processing the outward remittance will typically want to see the Indian court's decree or order releasing the funds, documentation establishing the judgment creditor's identity and, where the creditor is a foreign company rather than an individual, its registration and the underlying transaction that gave rise to the original foreign judgment, so that the bank can satisfy itself the remittance is not disguising an unrelated transfer. A creditor who anticipates this scrutiny and assembles the supporting documentation while the Indian enforcement proceeding is still under way, rather than only after a decree is obtained, generally clears the remittance stage considerably faster than one who treats it as a formality to be dealt with later.

What remains unsettled

The limitation period for a Section 44A execution petition is in fact settled: the Supreme Court held in Bank of Baroda v. Kotak Mahindra Bank Ltd (17 March 2020) that the residuary three-year period under Article 137 applies, not the twelve-year period under Article 136, and a judgment creditor should plan around that shorter period rather than assuming the longer one is available. The precise current list of reciprocating territories is also maintained through individual, scattered gazette notifications rather than one consolidated schedule, so the list given in this piece should be verified against the current Official Gazette position for the specific country concerned rather than relied upon as exhaustive or permanent.

Whether the United States or Canada will ever be added as a reciprocating territory, which would materially change the enforcement calculus for the large number of Indian-origin judgment creditors and debtors with connections to those countries, is a matter of government policy rather than settled law, and nothing in the material reviewed suggests this is currently under active consideration. A judgment creditor with a US or Canadian judgment should plan on the fresh-suit route remaining the only available procedure for the foreseeable future.

It is also worth flagging that the degree of scrutiny different Indian High Courts apply to a Section 44A execution, and the speed with which different district courts actually process such petitions, varies in practice far more than the statute's apparently mechanical language would suggest, a variation this piece cannot resolve in the abstract and that a judgment creditor should expect to encounter and plan around once a specific forum is chosen.

Which Indian court: territorial jurisdiction and the forum question

For a Section 44A execution, jurisdiction follows the judgment debtor or the debtor's property: the petition is filed in the district court within whose territorial limits the debtor resides, carries on business, or holds property that can be attached, applying the same general principles Sections 16 to 20 of the CPC use to fix jurisdiction for an ordinary suit. Where a debtor holds property or a place of business in more than one state, a creditor has a genuine choice of forum and should weigh factors such as which court is likely to move faster and where the assets actually most valuable to the recovery are located, rather than defaulting automatically to wherever the debtor's registered office happens to sit.

For a fresh Section 13 suit, the same territorial principles apply as they would to any other civil suit: the defendant's place of residence or business, or the place where the cause of action, in this context treated as having arisen where the foreign judgment debt is sought to be enforced against assets or where the defendant can be found, determines which court has jurisdiction. A foreign company planning to sue an Indian counterparty on a US or Canadian judgment should identify the Indian entity's registered office and principal place of business, and where it holds attachable assets, before filing, since an objection to territorial jurisdiction raised late in the proceeding can cost as much time as getting the underlying route wrong in the first place.

A foreign company should also check, before filing, whether the Indian counterparty's registered office or principal assets sit within a commercial division or a designated commercial court under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, where the claim value crosses that court's specified pecuniary threshold, since a money suit of commercial character above that threshold is generally required to go through that specialised track rather than an ordinary civil court, with its own case management timelines that can work in a creditor's favour.

Authentication: apostille, legalisation and translation

India acceded to the Hague Apostille Convention of 1961 on 26 October 2004, with the Convention taking effect for India on 14 July 2005, and Indian authorities have issued apostilles on outgoing Indian documents since 29 August 2007, with the Ministry of External Affairs administering the process domestically. For a judgment creditor, the practical consequence runs the other way: a certified copy of a foreign judgment, and the accompanying certificate under Section 44A where relevant, coming from a country that is itself a party to the Apostille Convention, which includes the United Kingdom, Singapore, the United States and most of the European Union, can generally be authenticated for use in India through a single apostille issued by the competent authority in the country of origin, rather than through the older and slower chain of consular legalisation.

Where the originating country is not a party to the Apostille Convention, the document instead has to go through full consular legalisation, typically involving notarisation, authentication by that country's own foreign ministry, and finally attestation by the Indian embassy or consulate in that country, a process that reliably takes longer and should be factored into any limitation calculation well before the three-year clock under Article 101 is close to running out. Either way, a judgment creditor should also expect an Indian court to require a certified translation into English or the relevant regional language, prepared by a translator the court will accept, if the original judgment was issued in any other language, and should arrange this well before the filing deadline rather than after a court registry raises the point.

A judgment creditor working through counsel in both the originating country and India should confirm, early, which of the two authentication paths actually applies to the specific document and the specific pair of countries involved, rather than assuming either path by default.

The single most consequential decision a foreign judgment creditor makes in India is also the simplest to get right: checking, before filing anything, whether the originating court sits in a country India has actually notified as a reciprocating territory under Section 44A. Get that answer first, and the rest of the planning, which Indian court to approach, whether a summary suit under Order XXXVII is available, how the three-year limitation period under Article 101 is running, follows from it in a reasonably predictable way.

What does not follow automatically, on either route, is quick money. A Section 44A execution still faces the same six Section 13 objections raised as execution defences rather than as a fresh suit, and a Section 13 suit, even run as a summary proceeding, still requires the creditor to prove the judgment was validly obtained on the merits by a competent court. Building in realistic time for either process, and for the FEMA compliance that follows once money actually changes hands, serves a judgment creditor far better than assuming a foreign judgment is self-executing anywhere outside the country that issued it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a US court judgment be enforced directly in India?

No. The United States has never been notified as a reciprocating territory under Section 44A CPC, so a US judgment cannot be executed directly in India. The judgment creditor has to file a fresh civil suit in an Indian court, using the US judgment as evidence of the debt under Section 13 CPC.

What is a reciprocating territory under Section 44A CPC?

It is a country the Government of India has specifically notified in the Official Gazette as one whose superior court decrees can be executed directly in India without a fresh suit. The United Kingdom, Singapore, Hong Kong, Malaysia, Bangladesh and the UAE are among the countries notified; the United States and Canada are not.

How long does a foreign judgment creditor have to sue in India?

A suit to enforce a foreign judgment under Section 13 CPC must generally be filed within three years of the date of the foreign judgment, under Article 101 of the Limitation Act, 1963. This period runs even if an appeal is still pending in the foreign court, so a creditor should not wait for the foreign appeal to conclude before filing in India.

Can an Indian court reopen the merits of a foreign judgment during enforcement?

Generally no. Section 13 CPC treats a foreign judgment as conclusive on matters it actually decided, unless the judgment debtor can show one of six specific exceptions, covering jurisdiction, whether the case was heard on the merits, errors of law, natural justice, fraud, or a claim founded on a breach of Indian law. An Indian court will not simply re-try the original dispute.

Is enforcing a foreign arbitral award different from enforcing a foreign court judgment in India?

Yes, they run on completely different tracks. A foreign arbitral award is enforced under Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 and the New York Convention. A foreign court judgment is enforced under Sections 13 and 44A of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, which apply a different procedure and a different, narrower set of defences.

Does a UK money judgment need a fresh trial in India?

No. Since the United Kingdom is a notified reciprocating territory, a UK money decree can generally be filed directly for execution in India under Section 44A CPC, without a fresh trial on the merits, though the judgment debtor can still raise Section 13-style objections at the execution stage.

What happens if the foreign court judgment was passed ex parte, without the defendant appearing?

An ex parte judgment is not automatically unenforceable in India. What matters is whether the defendant was properly served and given a genuine opportunity to defend under Indian standards of natural justice. A defendant who was validly served but chose not to appear generally cannot resist enforcement on that ground alone.

Can the money recovered from an Indian judgment debtor be sent abroad afterward?

It can, but remitting the recovered sum out of India is a separate step governed by the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, involving the remitting bank's own compliance checks on the source and purpose of the funds. A foreign or NRI judgment creditor should plan for this step rather than assume the money is freely transferable the moment it is recovered.
