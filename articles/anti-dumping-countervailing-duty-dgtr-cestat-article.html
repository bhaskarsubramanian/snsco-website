Anti-Dumping and Countervailing Duty Proceedings: DGTR Investigation to the CESTAT Appeal

What a foreign exporter or an Indian importer actually faces once the Directorate General of Trade Remedies opens an investigation, and why the right to appeal to the CESTAT is now itself the subject of a live jurisdictional dispute

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 23 November 2026 · Law stated as on 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A notification in the Gazette imposing anti-dumping duty on a product a company has been exporting to India for years rarely arrives as a surprise to anyone who read the questionnaire sent eighteen months earlier and chose not to respond. The Directorate General of Trade Remedies runs a quasi-judicial process with real deadlines, real consequences for staying silent, and, as of this year, a genuinely unsettled question about which parts of its findings a foreign exporter or an Indian importer can even appeal. This piece sets out how an anti-dumping, countervailing or safeguard duty investigation actually proceeds from initiation to the CESTAT, and why that last step has become harder to predict than it was even two years ago.

Key points

  • Anti-dumping duty under Section 9A and countervailing duty under Section 9 of the Customs Tariff Act, 1975 answer different questions: dumping looks at whether an exporter sold below its normal value, while a countervailing duty looks at whether the exporting government subsidised the product, and the same imported article can in principle attract both.
  • The Directorate General of Trade Remedies investigates and recommends, but the Central Government retains discretion under Section 9A(1) to accept, reject or modify that recommendation, and a failure to notify duty within three months of the final findings has been treated by the CESTAT as a deemed decision not to impose it at all.
  • Provisional duty under Section 9A(2) can be imposed from as early as sixty days after initiation, generally capped at six months and extendable to nine, and is refundable under the Anti-Dumping Rules if the final duty turns out lower or is not imposed.
  • An exporter who does not respond to the Directorate's questionnaire risks being treated as non-cooperative and assessed at the residual, generally higher, duty rate that applies to all exporters the investigation could not individually examine, a risk recent CESTAT rulings in the ophthalmic lens investigations have shown is not merely theoretical.
  • Anti-dumping and countervailing duty normally lapse after five years under Section 9A(5) and Section 9(6) respectively unless a sunset review is initiated and completed before that period runs out, and courts have held that a notification extending duty after the five years have already expired cannot revive it.
  • Section 9C gives a right of appeal to the CESTAT within ninety days of the order under challenge, with condonation available for sufficient cause, but a 2023 amendment that has not yet been brought into force, and a resulting CESTAT ruling and pending Delhi High Court challenge, have left open exactly what can be appealed: only the Directorate's own determination, or also the government's notification accepting it.
  • A new shipper or individual exporter who was not part of the original investigation can seek its own margin under the new shipper review mechanism associated with Section 9AA, rather than being stuck paying the duty rate fixed for exporters it had no involvement with.
  • Countervailing duty carries its own specificity requirement: a subsidy only attracts countervailing duty if it is contingent on export performance or on using domestic over imported inputs, or is otherwise specific to an enterprise or industry, which keeps broadly available government support schemes outside the Directorate's reach.

Three different remedies, three different questions

India's trade remedy framework under the Customs Tariff Act, 1975 gives domestic industry three distinct tools against injurious imports, and the three are frequently confused by exporters encountering the system for the first time. Anti-dumping duty under Section 9A responds to an exporter's own pricing decision: it asks whether the exporter sold the product into India at less than its normal value, meaning less than the price it charges in its own home market or a comparable third country, and whether that price gap caused or threatened injury to the domestic industry making the like article in India.

Countervailing duty under Section 9 asks a different question entirely. It does not care what price the exporter charged; it asks whether the exporting government gave the exporter a financial benefit, a subsidy, that let it sell more cheaply than it otherwise could have, and whether that subsidy was specific enough, tied to export performance, to using domestic inputs over imported ones, or otherwise targeted at a particular enterprise or industry, to fall within the countervailing duty discipline rather than being a general welfare or infrastructure measure available to the whole economy. A safeguard duty, a third and separate mechanism not dealt with at length here, asks neither question, and instead responds to a sudden surge in imports of a product, regardless of whether that surge involved dumping or subsidy at all.

An exporter can in principle face both an anti-dumping and a countervailing duty investigation over the same product at the same time, if domestic industry alleges both unfair pricing and government subsidy, and the two proceedings run largely in parallel under their own separate rules, the Anti-Dumping Rules, 1995 for the first and the Countervailing Duty Rules, 1995 for the second, both framed under their respective provisions of the Customs Tariff Act and both administered by the Directorate General of Trade Remedies.

For an importer, the practical consequence of this overlap is that a single sourcing decision can carry two separate duty exposures that have to be tracked, defended and, if necessary, appealed on their own timelines, because a finding that answers the dumping question does not automatically dispose of the subsidy question, and a company that treats the two investigations as one undifferentiated proceeding risks missing a deadline that was specific to only one of them.

How a DGTR investigation actually starts and runs

A trade remedy investigation does not begin with a notice to a specific exporter; it begins with an application from the domestic industry, or occasionally a suo motu initiation by the Directorate itself, alleging dumping or subsidisation and injury. Once the Directorate is satisfied the application is supported by a sufficient share of domestic production and discloses a prima facie case, it initiates the investigation by a public notification under Rule 5 of the Anti-Dumping Rules, 1995 or Rule 6 of the Countervailing Duty Rules, 1995, identifying the product, the countries of export under examination and the period of investigation.

From that point, known exporters, and any exporter who comes to know of the investigation through trade channels even without direct notice, are expected to file a questionnaire response within the time the Directorate prescribes, typically covering costs, home-market sales, export sales to India and, for a countervailing duty case, the specific government schemes the exporter has availed. The Directorate can, and routinely does, verify these responses through on-site visits, and it holds oral hearings before issuing its findings.

An exporter who does not respond, or whose response the Directorate finds materially deficient, is not simply excluded from the investigation; it is instead assessed on the facts available, which in practice means the residual duty rate set for all exporters the Directorate could not individually examine, a rate that is almost always higher than any cooperating exporter's individual margin. Recent CESTAT proceedings concerning anti-dumping duty on semi-finished ophthalmic lenses from China illustrate how consequential a non-cooperation finding can be and how hard it can be to reverse: the Tribunal has had to send at least one such finding back to the Directorate for fresh consideration rather than simply accepting it at face value, which shows the finding is reviewable but also shows how much delay and cost an exporter incurs correcting it after the fact.

Provisional duty: the cash-flow consequence of a preliminary finding

An investigation does not need to run to its conclusion before an exporter or importer starts paying duty. Once the Directorate reaches a preliminary determination that dumping is occurring and causing injury, Section 9A(2) of the Customs Tariff Act, read with Rules 12 and 13 of the Anti-Dumping Rules, lets the Central Government impose provisional anti-dumping duty by customs notification, generally expressed as a cash deposit or a bank guarantee an importer must furnish at the port of entry rather than an outright charge against the exporter directly.

Provisional duty is time-limited: it is generally valid for up to six months from the date of imposition, extendable to nine months in specified circumstances, and it is refundable under the Rules if the Directorate's final finding either does not support imposing duty at all or supports a lower rate than the provisional one charged. For an importer who has already built the provisional rate into its landed cost and pricing, that refund right matters less as a planning tool than as damage control after the fact, because the cash deposit itself still has to be funded for however long the investigation runs before the final finding issues.

Provisional duty has in practice been used sparingly in India relative to the scope the Rules allow, and commentary on the Directorate's recent casework has noted preliminary findings issuing well past the timelines the Rules originally contemplated, which means an importer cannot assume provisional duty will arrive on any fixed schedule once an investigation is initiated and should instead track the specific case's own procedural history.

A business importing a product under active investigation should also factor provisional duty into any supply contract being negotiated while the case is pending, since a fixed-price contract signed before a preliminary finding can leave the importer absorbing a cash deposit it never priced in, while a contract that passes the duty through to the buyer shifts the same planning problem one step down the supply chain rather than resolving it. Neither approach removes the need to actually read the preliminary finding once it issues and work out, product code by product code, whether the specific goods being imported fall within its scope, since anti-dumping notifications are drafted around tariff headings and product descriptions that do not always track commercial product names exactly.

Countervailing duty: the specificity test and the five-year default

Section 9 of the Customs Tariff Act is the countervailing duty equivalent of Section 9A, and it comes with a specificity filter that anti-dumping duty does not need, because anti-dumping duty looks only at an exporter's own pricing rather than at a government's own policy choices. A subsidy becomes countervailable only where it is specific: contingent on export performance, contingent on using domestic inputs in preference to imported ones, or otherwise limited to an enterprise, industry or group of enterprises or industries, rather than being a general measure, a broad tax concession or an infrastructure subsidy, available to the whole economy regardless of what it produces or where it sells.

Like anti-dumping duty, countervailing duty is not permanent. Section 9(6) provides that it ceases to have effect on the expiry of five years from the date of imposition unless revoked earlier, and Rule 24 of the Countervailing Duty Rules governs the review that can extend it, following broadly the same discipline that governs the anti-dumping sunset review discussed below. An importer relying on a countervailing duty notification that is approaching its fifth anniversary should check whether a review has actually been initiated before that date, rather than assuming the duty simply continues by inertia.

A single imported product can, in principle, be subject to both anti-dumping and countervailing duty investigations from the same country at the same time, and where both are eventually imposed, an importer ends up paying two separately calculated duties layered on top of ordinary customs duty, which is why the threshold procedural questions, whether a questionnaire response was adequate, whether a specificity finding on a particular subsidy scheme is sound, carry financial consequences well beyond the headline anti-dumping margin alone.

The Central Government's discretion, and what happens when it stays silent

A common misconception among exporters new to the Indian system is that the Directorate's final finding is itself the duty. It is not. Section 9A(1) uses the word may, and the Directorate's recommendation goes to the Central Government, specifically the Department of Revenue, for a decision on whether to issue the notification that actually imposes the duty, at what rate up to the margin the Directorate found, and for what duration. The government is not bound to accept the recommendation, can impose a lower rate than recommended, and has in practice sometimes declined to notify duty the Directorate had recommended.

That discretion has produced its own body of procedural law around what happens when the government simply does not act. The CESTAT has held that where the Central Government fails to issue a notification imposing anti-dumping duty within three months of the Directorate's final finding, a presumption arises that the government has decided not to impose the duty at all, and that such a deemed decision is itself something an aggrieved domestic industry can challenge under Section 9C, rather than being left with no remedy simply because no formal order of refusal was ever issued.

For an exporter monitoring an investigation's progress, this means the absence of news after a final finding is not necessarily good news in a settled sense; it can mean the government is still considering the recommendation within the three-month window, or it can mean a deemed decision not to impose duty has already crystallised and is itself now open to challenge by the domestic industry that sought the duty in the first place.

Sunset review: why five years is rarely the true end of the story

Anti-dumping duty, like countervailing duty, carries a built-in expiry under Section 9A(5): unless revoked earlier, it ceases to have effect on the expiry of five years from the date of imposition. In practice, duty on a product that genuinely continues to see injurious dumping almost never simply lapses at the five-year mark, because domestic industry has every incentive to apply for a sunset review well before that date, and the Directorate, if it initiates that review in time, can recommend continuation.

The timing discipline here has produced real litigation. Courts have held that a sunset review, and any notification continuing duty on the strength of that review, must be initiated and, in substance, completed before the original five-year period actually expires, and that neither Section 9A(1) nor Section 9A(5) permits reviving anti-dumping duty once the five years have already run out without a timely continuation. The first proviso to Section 9A(5) allows the government to extend duty for a further period of up to five years where the Directorate finds that cessation of the duty is likely to lead to continuation or recurrence of dumping and injury, while the second proviso allows a shorter interim extension of up to one year specifically to let a review that was initiated in time but could not be completed before expiry run to its conclusion.

For an exporter that has lived with an anti-dumping duty for close to five years, the approach of the expiry date is not the moment to relax; it is the moment to watch for a sunset review notification and to participate in it, because a review initiated and completed on time can extend the same duty, at the same or a revised rate, for another full five-year term, and an exporter who sat out the original investigation has a fresh opportunity, and a fresh incentive, to participate properly in the review rather than repeating the same silence.

New shipper review: a route out of someone else's duty rate

Section 9AA was introduced alongside Sections 9A, 9B and 9C as part of India's implementation of the WTO Anti-Dumping Agreement, and it addresses a specific unfairness in how anti-dumping duty would otherwise operate: an exporter who had no export trade to India during the original period of investigation, and so had no opportunity to participate in that investigation or secure an individual margin, would otherwise be stuck paying whatever residual duty rate the investigation fixed for exporters it could not individually examine, even though that exporter's own pricing was never actually tested.

The new shipper mechanism lets such an exporter, one that genuinely did not export the product to India during the original investigation period and is not related to any exporter that did, seek an individually determined margin through an expedited review, rather than being bound indefinitely by a residual rate calculated from other companies' conduct. Pending that review, the Rules contemplate that duty is not collected from the new shipper on the same footing as an established exporter already covered by the original finding, with the exporter instead furnishing security pending the outcome.

A foreign manufacturer entering the Indian market for the first time, after a residual anti-dumping duty on its product category has already been in place for some years because of other exporters' conduct, should treat a new shipper review as the default route into the market rather than simply absorbing the residual rate or routing sales through an established exporter already covered by the original finding, a workaround that invites its own scrutiny for circumvention.

Circumvention is a live concern on the Directorate's side as much as compliance is on the exporter's, and a new entrant whose supply chain, ownership or pricing pattern looks designed to piggyback on an existing exporter's lower individual margin, rather than reflecting a genuinely independent commercial relationship, should expect that structure to attract exactly the scrutiny the new shipper mechanism was designed to make unnecessary. Keeping the corporate and contractual relationship between a new entrant and any existing exporter in the same product category transparent and at arm's length is the more durable protection, even where it takes longer to establish than an informal arrangement would.

The CESTAT appeal, and the jurisdictional dispute nobody fully resolved yet

Section 9C gives any person aggrieved by an order of determination or review of margin or injury, or by a notification imposing anti-dumping duty, a right of appeal to the CESTAT, generally within ninety days of the date of the order under appeal, a period the Tribunal can extend where it is satisfied the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause from filing in time. Until 2023, this provision was read broadly enough to let an aggrieved party challenge both the Directorate's own determination and the government's notification giving effect to it.

Section 134 of the Finance Act, 2023 amended Section 9C in a way that appears narrow on its face but has turned out to carry real consequences: it omitted the words order of from Section 9C(1), and in Section 9C(2) it replaced the word order with the words determination or review. Read together, the amendment points toward confining the CESTAT's appellate jurisdiction to a challenge against the Directorate's determination or review itself, rather than against the government's separate notification accepting, rejecting or modifying that determination, which would leave a party wishing to challenge the government's own notification with no clear statutory appeal route to the Tribunal at all.

The Principal Bench of the CESTAT in New Delhi, in an anti-dumping appeal brought by an importer of semi-finished ophthalmic lenses against Customs Notification No. 32/2022-Customs (ADD), dated 27 December 2022, held in August 2025 that the appeal remained maintainable because Section 134 of the Finance Act, 2023 had not, as of that date, been brought into force by a separate notification, so the pre-amendment, broader version of Section 9C continued to govern. The Union government has since challenged that interim position before the Delhi High Court, where a Division Bench has issued notice on the question of whether the Tribunal's appellate reach is confined to the Directorate's own determination or review, or extends also to the government's notification implementing it.

Until that challenge is resolved, a party contemplating an appeal under Section 9C faces a genuine, acknowledged uncertainty about the scope of what it can actually put before the Tribunal, and should frame its appeal to challenge both the Directorate's determination and the government's notification in the alternative, rather than assuming either framing alone will survive a maintainability objection, while keeping a close watch on the Delhi High Court proceedings for a ruling that could settle the question either way.

Practical steps for a foreign exporter or Indian importer facing an investigation

An exporter that learns of an investigation, whether through direct notice from the Directorate, through a customs broker, or through trade association channels, should treat the questionnaire deadline as a genuinely hard one, because the consequence of missing it is not a modest increase in an already-calculated margin but exposure to the residual rate calculated from the facts available, typically the least favourable figures the Directorate has before it. Engaging counsel and preparing a complete, verifiable questionnaire response well before the deadline, rather than after an extension request has already been sought and possibly refused, remains the single most effective protection available at the investigation stage.

An importer should treat a provisional duty notification as an immediate cash-flow event requiring a security or bank-guarantee arrangement with its bank, not merely as a pricing adjustment to pass on to customers, and should keep its own records of the provisional payments made so that a refund claim under the Rules, if the final duty comes in lower, can be filed promptly and supported without having to reconstruct the payment history months later.

For both exporters and importers, the live uncertainty over the scope of a Section 9C appeal following the 2023 amendment is a reason to build an appeal strategy earlier in the process rather than later. An exporter unhappy with a final finding should not wait for the government's notification to decide whether to appeal, given the ninety-day clock and the unresolved question of what exactly that clock permits it to challenge, and should instead take advice on the appeal at the point the final finding itself issues, while the full range of procedural options is still realistically open.

India's trade remedy framework gives domestic industry a genuine and frequently used set of tools against dumped and subsidised imports, but it is not a process that operates on autopilot once an investigation opens. The Directorate's findings, the government's own discretion over whether to notify duty, and now the CESTAT's own appellate jurisdiction, all carry procedural choices and real deadlines that decide outcomes as much as the underlying dumping or subsidy margin does.

For a foreign exporter weighing whether to engage with an Indian investigation at all, and for an Indian importer trying to plan around a provisional duty notification, the discipline that actually protects a commercial position is procedural: meeting the questionnaire deadline, tracking the sunset review clock, and treating the current jurisdictional dispute over Section 9C as a live reason to take appeal advice earlier rather than later, not as a technicality that can be sorted out after the fact. The underlying margin calculation, whether of dumping, subsidy or injury, is ordinarily the hardest-fought part of any individual case, but it is the procedural steps surrounding that calculation, who responded to the questionnaire, when the sunset review was initiated, and what exactly a Section 9C appeal is still understood to cover, that most often decide which side gets to argue the substantive merits at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between anti-dumping duty and countervailing duty?

Anti-dumping duty under Section 9A of the Customs Tariff Act, 1975 responds to an exporter's own pricing decision, specifically selling into India below the price charged in its home market or a comparable third country. Countervailing duty under Section 9 responds instead to a financial benefit the exporting government gave the exporter, a subsidy, and only where that subsidy is specific to an enterprise, industry or export activity rather than a general measure available across the economy. The same product can in principle attract both duties at once if domestic industry establishes both dumping and a specific subsidy.

Is the Directorate General of Trade Remedies' recommendation binding on the government?

No. Section 9A(1) uses the word may, which the courts have read as giving the Central Government discretion over whether to notify the duty the Directorate recommends, at what rate up to the recommended margin, and for what period. The CESTAT has held that where the government does not notify duty within three months of the Directorate's final finding, a presumption arises that it has decided not to impose the duty, and that deemed decision is itself open to challenge.

What happens if an exporter does not respond to the Directorate's questionnaire?

An exporter that does not respond, or whose response is found materially deficient, is assessed on the facts available to the Directorate, which in practice means the residual duty rate applied to all exporters the investigation could not individually examine. That rate is almost always higher than the individual margin a cooperating exporter would receive, and recent CESTAT proceedings in the ophthalmic lens investigations show how difficult and slow it can be to reverse a non-cooperation finding after the fact.

How long does anti-dumping or countervailing duty last once imposed?

Both ordinarily cease to have effect five years after imposition, under Section 9A(5) for anti-dumping duty and Section 9(6) for countervailing duty, unless a sunset review is initiated and completed before that period expires. Courts have held that a notification extending duty cannot revive it once the five years have already lapsed, so the review has to be timed to conclude, in substance, before the original period runs out, though a limited interim extension of up to one year is available where a timely review could not be completed in time.

Can a new exporter that was not part of the original investigation get its own duty rate?

Yes, through the new shipper review mechanism associated with Section 9AA. An exporter that genuinely had no export trade to India during the original period of investigation, and is not related to any exporter that did, can seek an individually determined margin through an expedited review rather than being bound by the residual rate fixed for exporters whose conduct it had no part in.

What is the time limit to appeal an anti-dumping or countervailing duty order to the CESTAT?

Section 9C(2) sets a ninety-day limitation period from the date of the order under appeal, with the Tribunal able to condone delay where it is satisfied the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause from filing in time. Several reported appeals have sought condonation running into months or years, with mixed results depending on the explanation offered.

Why is there currently uncertainty about what can be appealed under Section 9C?

Section 134 of the Finance Act, 2023 amended Section 9C to remove the word order from the provision and replace it with determination or review, which on one reading confines the CESTAT's jurisdiction to challenging the Directorate's own determination rather than the government's separate notification giving effect to it. The CESTAT held in August 2025 that this amendment had not yet been brought into force, so the earlier, broader position continued to apply, and the Union government has since challenged that ruling before the Delhi High Court, where the point remains pending.

Is provisional anti-dumping duty refundable?

Yes. Provisional duty imposed under Section 9A(2) after a preliminary finding is refundable under the Anti-Dumping Rules, 1995 if the Directorate's final finding either does not support imposing duty at all or supports a lower rate than what was collected provisionally. An importer should keep clear records of provisional payments made so a refund claim can be filed and supported promptly once the final finding issues.
