SEBI's 2025-2026 IPO Rule Changes: What a Foreign or Institutional Pre-IPO Investor Needs to Know

Lock-ins, promoter contribution, anchor allocation and disclosure, after three rounds of ICDR amendments

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 21 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign venture fund, a family office or an institutional investor that bought into an Indian company before its IPO sits at the intersection of three sets of rules that SEBI has rewritten piece by piece since March 2025: how long shares stay locked in, whether those shares can count toward the promoter's own minimum contribution, and how much of the IPO itself a sophisticated investor can actually buy.

Key points

  • SEBI's March 2025 amendment to the ICDR Regulations tied the promoter's post-IPO lock-in to how the issue proceeds are used: a three-year lock-in where most of the fresh issue funds capital expenditure or related debt repayment, and eighteen months otherwise.
  • Regulation 17 already locked in pre-IPO shares held by investors other than promoters for six months from the date of allotment in a mainboard IPO before March 2025 (a separate one-year lock-in applies to an SME IPO under Regulation 239); the March 2025 amendment did not shorten that period, but widened the categories of shares exempt from the lock-in and changed how the promoter lock-in treats repayment of pre-existing capital expenditure loans.
  • At its June 2025 board meeting (the 210th meeting, Press Release 33/2025), SEBI widened Regulation 14's first proviso so that alternative investment funds and foreign venture capital investors, in addition to promoters, can now contribute equity shares arising from the conversion of fully paid-up compulsorily convertible securities toward the promoter's minimum contribution where post-issue promoter holding falls below 20%.
  • SEBI increased the anchor investor reservation in an IPO from 33% to 40% of the qualified institutional buyer portion, effective from late November 2025, and allowed more anchor investors for larger anchor books, subject to a minimum allotment per investor.
  • Pre-IPO transactions must now be disclosed to the stock exchanges within 24 hours, giving later investors and the market visibility into a company's pre-IPO capitalisation that did not exist in the same form before 2025.
  • A March 2026 amendment lets depositories mark pledged pre-IPO shares, other than promoters', as non-transferable for the duration of the lock-in, based on the issuer's instructions, addressing a gap in how pledged pre-IPO holdings were previously tracked.

Why a pre-IPO investor should read ICDR amendments, not just the prospectus

A foreign venture fund or family office that invests in an Indian company before its public listing typically negotiates its commercial terms, valuation and governance rights at the time of that private round, and then treats the eventual IPO as a liquidity event governed mainly by the investment bank's own process. That view understates how much the Securities and Exchange Board of India's Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements Regulations, 2018, commonly called the ICDR Regulations, actually shape what happens to a pre-IPO holding once the company files to go public.

Three rounds of amendments to those Regulations, in March 2025, June 2025 and March 2026, changed how long a pre-IPO investor's shares stay locked in, whether those shares can be used to satisfy the promoter's own minimum contribution requirement, how large the anchor book is and who can participate in it, and how quickly a pre-IPO transaction has to be disclosed to the market. None of this appears in the term sheet a foreign investor signs at the time of its original investment, which is exactly why it needs separate attention as an exit approaches.

What makes this cycle of amendments worth tracking closely, rather than leaving entirely to the company's domestic counsel at the time of listing, is how directly each change touches commercial outcomes a foreign investor cares about: how soon the investor can actually sell, how much of its holding the company can rely on to meet statutory thresholds, how large an allocation it can realistically expect if it wants to participate as an anchor in the IPO itself, and how much visibility the market gets into the investor's own pre-IPO dealings before the prospectus is even filed. A fund that revisits its documentation only once a portfolio company announces its IPO is usually revisiting it too late to use several of these changes to its advantage.

This matters particularly for funds and family offices that hold stakes across several Indian companies at different stages of the path to listing, since a holding in one portfolio company may already be subject to the post-March 2025 framework while a holding in another, closer to an IPO filed slightly earlier, may still sit under the older rules depending on exactly when each company's own IPO process began and which version of the Regulations its prospectus was prepared against. Treating the rules as a single static fact learned once, rather than as something to re-check for every portfolio company approaching listing, is the single habit most likely to cost a sophisticated investor an avoidable misstep.

Promoter lock-in: now tied to what the IPO money is actually for

Regulation 16 of the ICDR Regulations has long required the promoters' minimum contribution, the 20% stake Regulation 14 requires them to hold after listing, to stay locked in for a set period after the IPO. Before March 2025, this lock-in ran for a single period regardless of how the company intended to use the money it raised. The amendment gazetted on 8 March 2025 changed that: where a majority of the fresh issue proceeds are earmarked for capital expenditure, or for repaying debt taken for capital expenditure, the minimum contribution must now stay locked in for three years. In every other case, including an IPO used mainly to fund working capital or to provide an offer for sale exit to existing shareholders, the lock-in remains at eighteen months.

For a foreign investor negotiating board representation or exit rights ahead of an IPO, this distinction affects how quickly the promoter group itself can realistically diversify its holding after listing, which in turn affects the signal a promoter sale, or the absence of one, sends to the market in the company's first few years as a listed entity. A pre-IPO investor advising a promoter on how to frame the use of IPO proceeds in the draft prospectus should treat the choice between a capital expenditure-heavy and a working capital-heavy use of funds as carrying a direct, three-year consequence for the promoter's own liquidity, not merely a disclosure choice.

Before this change, Indian practice had generally treated the promoter lock-in as a single fixed period regardless of the company's actual capital needs, which meant a company raising money mainly to fund growth capital expenditure and a company raising money mainly to let existing shareholders exit through an offer for sale faced an identical restriction on the promoters' own shares. Linking the lock-in to the use of proceeds brings Indian practice closer to the idea that a promoter who has genuinely committed fresh capital to building the business should face a longer restriction than one whose IPO served mainly as a partial exit mechanism for the existing shareholder base.

Pre-IPO investor lock-in: the long-standing six-month period, and what March 2025 actually changed

Regulation 17 of the ICDR Regulations has for some time already locked in pre-IPO shares held by persons other than the promoters, the category that covers most foreign venture capital investors, private equity funds and family offices that invested before the IPO, for six months from the date of allotment in a mainboard public issue (a one-year lock-in applies instead to an SME IPO under Regulation 239). The March 2025 amendment did not shorten this mainboard period; it widened the categories of pre-issue shares exempt from the lock-in altogether and brought repayment of loans taken for earlier capital expenditure within the capex test used for the promoter's own lock-in. An investor should not treat the six-month figure as a recent concession, and should instead check whether its specific holding now falls within one of the widened exemption categories.

This shorter window also changes the practical calculus around selling down a pre-IPO stake through an offer for sale at the time of the IPO itself, rather than waiting out the lock-in afterward. An investor now has to weigh a smaller, immediate exit through the offer for sale against a six-month wait for a potentially larger sale at the prevailing market price, a choice that is considerably less finely balanced than it was when the wait ran a full year.

Fund managers running vehicles with their own investor-facing liquidity timelines, such as a venture fund approaching the end of its term or a family office rebalancing a concentrated position, should check their internal return models against the correct mainboard figure of six months rather than the one-year period that applies only to an SME IPO. A model that assumes a twelve-month lock-in for a mainboard listing, and that prices in a corresponding discount for illiquidity, overstates how long that capital is genuinely unavailable, and a fund relying on such a model risks reporting a more conservative liquidity position to its own investors or limited partners than the rule actually requires.

It is worth being precise about the start date here, since it is a common point of confusion. The six-month period runs from the date of allotment in the public issue itself, which for most pre-IPO holdings is the IPO listing date, rather than from whatever earlier date the investor originally subscribed to or acquired its pre-IPO shares. An investor that bought into the company across more than one funding round still has every tranche of its pre-IPO holding released on the same date, six months after listing, rather than on a staggered schedule keyed to each round's original closing date.

Counting toward the promoter's minimum contribution: a door now open to AIFs and FVCIs

Regulation 14 requires the promoters to collectively hold at least 20% of the company's post-issue capital, described in the Regulations as the minimum promoters' contribution. Where the promoters cannot meet that threshold on their own, the first proviso to Regulation 14 has long allowed specific categories of shareholder, including alternative investment funds, foreign venture capital investors and scheduled commercial banks, to make up the shortfall. Until SEBI's board approved a change at its 210th meeting on 18 June 2025 (Press Release 33/2025), this route covered ordinary equity holdings but not equity shares that arose from the conversion of fully paid-up compulsorily convertible securities, a structure commonly used in venture and growth-stage financing rounds in India.

The June 2025 decision extended the Regulation 14 proviso so that alternative investment funds and foreign venture capital investors can now contribute equity shares obtained through the conversion of such compulsorily convertible securities toward the promoter's minimum contribution, putting them on the same footing as promoters for this specific purpose. A foreign fund that invested through compulsorily convertible preference shares or compulsorily convertible debentures, and whose holding converts to equity ahead of listing, should raise this point specifically with the company's counsel when the promoter group's post-issue shareholding looks likely to fall below 20%, since the fund's converted holding may now be exactly what closes that gap, where before 2025 it generally could not.

This change matters most for companies where the promoters themselves have been diluted below 20% through successive funding rounds, a pattern now common among venture-backed Indian companies that raised several rounds of growth capital before listing. Before June 2025, such a company's IPO could face a genuine structural obstacle if the promoters alone could not reach the 20% threshold using only their own shares, since the earlier version of the proviso did not recognise converted CCS holdings from AIFs and FVCIs as eligible to help close that gap. The amendment removes that obstacle for companies whose cap table includes these categories of investor holding convertible instruments, which is a meaningful change for exactly the kind of foreign and institutional backers this article is written for.

An investor whose holding is used this way should also understand what it is agreeing to: shares contributed toward the minimum promoters' contribution are subject to the same lock-in as the promoters' own minimum contribution shares, which, as the previous section describes, can now run as long as three years depending on how the issue proceeds are used. An investor should weigh the commercial benefit of helping the company satisfy Regulation 14, which can materially ease the path to listing, against the longer lock-in that its own shares may then carry as a result.

Anchor investors: a bigger book, and more seats at the table

SEBI increased the reservation available to anchor investors, the qualified institutional buyers who commit to an allocation a day before the public issue opens at a price the book-running lead managers set, from 33% to 40% of the qualified institutional buyer portion of an issue, with the change reported to take effect from around the end of November 2025. The same change raised the number of anchor investors a large IPO can accommodate, allowing additional investors for every further block of anchor allocation above a threshold, subject to a minimum allotment per investor, so that a very large anchor book no longer has to be concentrated among as few institutions as the earlier rule effectively required.

For a foreign institutional investor or a sizeable family office fund that wants anchor exposure to a large Indian IPO, this widened book means a realistic chance of inclusion that did not exist to the same degree when only ten anchor slots were available regardless of deal size. SEBI's own September 2025 consultation paper noted, as a point still under discussion, that pension funds do not currently receive a dedicated reservation within the anchor portion despite being long-term investors, which is worth watching for a further change aimed specifically at that category.

Anchor allocation still comes with its own distinct lock-in treatment, separate from the pre-IPO lock-in discussed earlier in this article: anchor investors generally face a lock-in running from the date of allotment in the anchor book, split between a shorter period for part of the allocation and a longer period for the remainder, a structure intended specifically to discourage anchor investors from exiting the market the moment trading opens on listing day. A foreign investor weighing anchor participation against simply buying into the public issue once trading begins should price this lock-in into its decision, since the certainty of an anchor allocation at a set price comes bundled with a restriction ordinary public issue buyers do not face.

Practically, an investor interested in anchor participation should approach the book-running lead managers well ahead of the anchor bidding date, since anchor allocations are decided through a book-building process the managers run with their own relationships and allocation preferences, and a wider formal reservation under the ICDR Regulations does not by itself guarantee any particular investor a seat in a specific deal's anchor book. A larger reserved pool simply means the managers have more room to accommodate additional investors than before, not that every investor who asks will necessarily be included.

Mandatory 24-hour disclosure of pre-IPO transactions

SEBI's March 2025 reforms also made disclosure of pre-IPO transactions mandatory to the stock exchanges within 24 hours of the transaction, a requirement that applies once a company is in the IPO process rather than only from the date the prospectus is filed. The practical effect is that a secondary sale of pre-IPO shares, a fresh round completed shortly before listing, or an arrangement under which a promoter waives rights in favour of a particular investor, all become visible to the market, and to competing bidders in the same company, considerably sooner than under the earlier disclosure regime.

An investor negotiating a pre-IPO transaction in the window shortly before a company files to go public should factor this visibility into deal structuring, since terms that might previously have stayed within the company's own records until the prospectus was filed will now reach the exchanges, and through them the market, within a day. Counsel advising on such a transaction should confirm with the company's compliance team, at the time of signing, whether the 24-hour disclosure clock has already started to run.

This disclosure requirement also changes the negotiating dynamic between a late-stage pre-IPO investor and the company. Where a round priced shortly before listing at a valuation meaningfully different from what the company expects the IPO to achieve, that difference becomes visible to prospective public market investors, and to the underwriters pricing the issue, almost immediately, rather than surfacing only through the prospectus's own disclosure of recent share transactions. A late-stage investor negotiating price and terms in this window should assume the terms will become public well before the IPO itself, and should structure any protective provisions, such as anti-dilution or most-favoured-investor clauses, with that visibility in mind.

Companies and their counsel should also build a specific internal process for this requirement, assigning clear responsibility for identifying a reportable pre-IPO transaction and meeting the 24-hour window, since the obligation sits with the company and a missed disclosure becomes a compliance lapse of the kind that can attract SEBI's attention at exactly the point when the company can least afford regulatory friction, in the run-up to its own listing. An investor negotiating a pre-IPO transaction has a direct interest in the company getting this right, since a disclosure lapse discovered during SEBI's review of the draft prospectus can delay the entire listing timetable, which in turn delays the investor's own path to the liquidity the lock-in changes described earlier in this article are meant to shorten.

The 'Specific Investor' route: promoters waiving rights in favour of a chosen investor

Alongside the disclosure change, SEBI's March 2025 amendment introduced a mechanism letting promoters or the promoter group waive specific rights or benefits in favour of particular investors, a route commentary has described as the 'Specific Investor' concept, and a related amendment allowed issuers to assign unsold rights-issue shares to particular investors rather than letting them lapse automatically. The precise contours of which investors can be designated this way, and what rights can be waived in their favour, are still comparatively new and have not yet been tested through extensive market practice or litigation.

A foreign or institutional investor negotiating a pre-IPO round should ask explicitly whether the promoters intend to use this mechanism in the investor's favour, and should have the specific waiver documented in the transaction papers rather than left as an informal understanding, given how directly it can affect the investor's practical position once the company moves toward listing.

The rights-issue assignment limb of this change is narrower in application but can matter to an investor holding a pre-IPO stake through a rights issue carried out ahead of listing. Where other shareholders choose not to subscribe to their full rights entitlement, the issuer can now assign the unsubscribed shares to particular investors rather than letting the entitlement lapse or go through a renunciation process open to all shareholders equally, which gives an investor willing to take up additional shares in a rights round a more direct route to doing so than existed before this amendment.

Because this mechanism is new and has not yet generated a significant body of SEBI guidance, enforcement action or litigation, an investor relying on it should treat the documentation around any waiver or assignment with particular care, including a clear record of how the promoters' decision to designate a specific investor was reached and approved internally, since that record is likely to matter if the arrangement is ever questioned by a rival bidder, a minority shareholder, or SEBI itself. Until a settled body of practice develops around this mechanism, a cautious foreign investor should also ask whether the designation could be read as giving it an advantage over other shareholders in a way that invites a minority shareholder challenge after listing, and should be prepared to justify the commercial rationale for the waiver on terms that would hold up to that kind of scrutiny.

Pledged pre-IPO shares: the March 2026 fix

One gap that remained unaddressed until March 2026 concerned pre-IPO shares, other than promoters' own holdings, that had been pledged as security for a loan or other obligation during the lock-in period. The amendment notified around 16 March 2026 lets depositories mark such pledged shares as non-transferable for the remainder of the lock-in, based on instructions from the issuer, closing a gap in which a pledge could, in practice, create a route to effectively transfer a locked-in holding before the lock-in had actually run its course.

An investor that has pledged, or is considering pledging, pre-IPO shares as collateral for financing should confirm with its lender and with the company how this mechanism is being implemented for the specific holding, since a lender that is unaware of the updated depository treatment may structure a facility on assumptions about transferability that the March 2026 amendment has since overtaken. This question is worth asking even where the investor itself has not pledged any shares, since a co-investor's pledged and encumbered holdings in the same company can affect how the market and the underwriters read the overall quality of the pre-IPO shareholder base ahead of listing.

Pledging pre-IPO shares is a common way for a founder, an early employee, or indeed a fund itself, to raise liquidity against an illiquid holding without waiting for the IPO or the lock-in to run its course, and the gap this amendment addresses arose because the depository systems that record and enforce lock-ins had not previously been built to flag a pledge created during the lock-in period in the same way they flag an outright transfer. Before this fix, a lender taking a pledge over locked-in pre-IPO shares, and the investor granting it, both carried some uncertainty about whether the pledge itself, or any enforcement of it during the lock-in, could be treated as effectively defeating the lock-in's purpose.

The practical takeaway for a foreign fund or family office using pre-IPO shares as collateral is to ask the lender's counsel directly, before the facility is finalised, how the depository will treat the pledged shares during the remaining lock-in period under the post-March 2026 framework, rather than relying on how similar facilities were structured before this amendment existed.

What this means for the draft red herring prospectus and diligence

Every one of the changes described in this article eventually has to be reflected correctly in the company's draft red herring prospectus and, later, the final prospectus, since SEBI reviews these filings specifically for compliance with the ICDR Regulations as they stand on the date of filing, not as they stood when the company first began preparing for its IPO. A pre-IPO investor's legal team should treat the prospectus disclosure on lock-in, promoter contribution and pre-IPO transactions as a document to check line by line against the current Regulations, rather than assuming the company's listed-market counsel has already built in every recent amendment correctly, particularly where the IPO preparation process began well before March 2025 and has simply carried forward assumptions from that earlier period.

A foreign investor's own diligence team, reviewing the company's IPO readiness ahead of an anchor commitment or a decision on whether to sell down through an offer for sale, should specifically ask the company's counsel for a dated note confirming which version of the lock-in and promoter contribution rules applies to this particular issue, including the use-of-proceeds classification that determines whether the promoter lock-in runs for eighteen months or three years. That single question, asked early, resolves a surprising number of the practical uncertainties this article otherwise has to describe in general terms.

Common mistakes foreign and institutional pre-IPO investors make

Confusing the one-year SME IPO lock-in under Regulation 239 with the six-month mainboard lock-in under Regulation 17 is a frequent mistake, particularly for an investor in a portfolio company that could list on either platform. An investor should confirm, at the time the company actually files for listing, which platform it is listing on and which of the two lock-in periods therefore applies, rather than assuming the mainboard six-month figure is a new or temporary concession.

Treating the promoter's minimum contribution requirement as purely the promoter's problem is a second mistake. An investor holding compulsorily convertible securities that will convert to equity ahead of listing may now be able to help the promoter meet that 20% threshold under the widened Regulation 14 proviso, and failing to raise this with the company's counsel can mean losing a point of commercial leverage in structuring the final pre-IPO round, since a company genuinely short of promoter contribution has real reason to value an investor willing to have its own shares classified this way, and that willingness can be a bargaining chip worth something in the final negotiation.

Overlooking the 24-hour pre-IPO disclosure requirement when structuring a transaction close to the listing process is a third mistake, since a transaction intended to remain commercially confidential until the prospectus stage may now become visible to the market, and to rival bidders, far sooner than the parties expect. Parties sometimes discover this only when a term they considered sensitive, such as a valuation struck at a discount to an earlier round, appears in a market report within days of signing, by which point there is no way to undo the disclosure.

Assuming anchor allocation works the same way it did before the November 2025 expansion is a fourth mistake. An investor that was told, in an earlier deal, that anchor participation was effectively capped at a small number of large institutions should revisit that assumption for a current IPO, since the widened book and additional investor slots may now make participation realistically available where it was not before.

Relying on a single lawyer's memory of 'how pre-IPO deals work in India', built up over several years of practice, without checking the current Regulations is a fifth and surprisingly common mistake, precisely because this is an area where the rules have moved three times in roughly twelve months. A foreign fund's own in-house counsel, however experienced with Indian transactions generally, should treat a pre-IPO or anchor investment made before 2025 as a poor template for one being structured now, and should ask Indian counsel for a fresh, dated summary of the applicable lock-in, disclosure and promoter-contribution rules for every new transaction rather than reusing an older checklist.

A practical checklist before committing to a pre-IPO or anchor allocation

Confirm, at the time the company actually files to go public rather than at the time of the original investment, which lock-in period applies to the specific holding, since the rules have changed more than once since March 2025, and treat an earlier portfolio company's experience as a starting point for questions rather than as a reliable answer in itself.

Ask the company's counsel whether the investor's holding, particularly where it arises from convertible securities, can be used to help satisfy the promoter's minimum contribution under the widened Regulation 14 proviso, and document the answer in the transaction papers rather than leaving it to be resolved later, since the lock-in consequence that follows from that answer is significant enough to want settled well before the draft prospectus is finalised.

Build the 24-hour pre-IPO disclosure requirement into the timing of any transaction close to the listing process, and confirm with the company's compliance team exactly when that disclosure clock starts running for the specific transaction, rather than assuming the company will flag this automatically on the investor's behalf.

Where a pre-IPO holding is pledged as collateral, confirm with the lender and the company how the March 2026 depository mechanism for pledged shares is being applied, so that the lender's own assumptions about transferability match the current rule, and raise the point proactively rather than waiting for the lender to ask.

Ask the book-running lead managers, well before the anchor allocation date, whether the expanded anchor book and increased investor count make a meaningful anchor allocation realistically available for this specific issue, rather than assuming the older, narrower anchor rules still apply, and get that answer early enough to still influence the fund's own bidding strategy.

Request a dated note from the company's Indian counsel, addressed to the specific issue in question, confirming the use-of-proceeds classification that determines the promoter lock-in length and the version of the ICDR Regulations the draft prospectus has actually been prepared against, rather than relying on counsel's general familiarity with the Regulations as they stood in an earlier year.

Where the investor's holding includes compulsorily convertible securities that will convert ahead of listing, model both outcomes before the IPO is finalised: the scenario where that converted holding is used to help satisfy the promoter's minimum contribution, which brings the longer lock-in with it, and the scenario where it is not, so that the investor goes into the listing process understanding which lock-in length actually applies to its own shares rather than discovering the answer only once the prospectus is filed. Share the result of that modelling with the fund's or family office's own investment committee before the final terms are settled, so the liquidity consequence is approved at the right level internally rather than surfacing as a surprise after the fact.

SEBI's 2025 and 2026 amendments to the ICDR Regulations moved in a direction that, on balance, favours a pre-IPO investor: a shorter lock-in, a wider route to help satisfy the promoter's minimum contribution, a larger anchor book, and clearer treatment of pledged holdings. None of this is automatic, and each of these changes needs to be checked against the specific transaction and the specific date on which a company actually files to list, rather than assumed from a deal structured, or a lock-in period quoted, before March 2025.

A foreign venture fund, family office or institutional investor that revisits its pre-IPO documentation against this sequence of changes, well before a portfolio company's listing process actually begins, is in a considerably stronger position to use these changes than one that discovers them only once the prospectus has already been filed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is the lock-in period for a pre-IPO investor's shares in India now?

Under Regulation 17 of the ICDR Regulations, pre-IPO shares held by investors other than promoters are locked in for six months from the date of allotment in a mainboard public issue (a separate one-year lock-in applies to an SME IPO under Regulation 239). This six-month mainboard period predates the March 2025 amendment, which instead widened the categories of shares exempt from the lock-in and changed how loan repayment counts toward the promoter lock-in's capex test.

Does the promoter lock-in period always stay the same length?

No. Since the March 2025 amendment, the promoters' minimum contribution is locked in for three years where a majority of the fresh issue proceeds are used for capital expenditure or related debt repayment, and for eighteen months in other cases, such as an issue used mainly for working capital or an offer for sale.

Can a foreign venture capital investor help a promoter meet the minimum promoters' contribution requirement?

Since SEBI's board decision on 18 June 2025, alternative investment funds and foreign venture capital investors can contribute equity shares arising from the conversion of fully paid-up compulsorily convertible securities toward the promoter's minimum contribution under the first proviso to Regulation 14 of the ICDR Regulations, where the promoters' own post-issue holding falls below 20%.

How big is the anchor investor portion of an Indian IPO now?

SEBI increased the anchor investor reservation from 33% to 40% of the qualified institutional buyer portion, with the change reported to take effect from around the end of November 2025, and also increased the number of anchor investors permitted in larger anchor books.

Do pre-IPO transactions need to be disclosed before the IPO prospectus is filed?

Yes. Since the March 2025 amendment, pre-IPO transactions must be disclosed to the stock exchanges within 24 hours of the transaction, once the company has entered the IPO process, well before the prospectus itself is filed.

What happens to pre-IPO shares that have been pledged as collateral during the lock-in period?

A SEBI amendment notified around 16 March 2026 lets depositories mark such pledged pre-IPO shares, other than promoters' own holdings, as non-transferable for the remainder of the lock-in period, based on instructions from the issuer.

What is the 'Specific Investor' mechanism under the ICDR Regulations?

It refers to a route, introduced through SEBI's March 2025 amendment, allowing promoters or the promoter group to waive specific rights or benefits in favour of particular investors, and a related change allowing issuers to assign unsold rights-issue shares to particular investors rather than letting them lapse.
