Coastal Regulation Zone Clearance: What a Port, Power Project or Coastal Developer Must Get Right

How the CRZ Notification 2019 classifies India's coastline into four zones, who actually grants clearance, and what the Great Nicobar litigation shows about how the National Green Tribunal reviews a coastal infrastructure project once it is challenged

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 23 November 2026 · Law stated as on 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A port operator, a power generator sizing a coastal plant for imported coal or LNG, and a developer marketing sea-facing villas to a buyer with no intention of ever visiting the site before purchase are, in one important respect, in exactly the same position: each is building on land the Coastal Regulation Zone framework treats as a shared, limited resource, not an ordinary parcel the owner can develop at will. The 2019 notification is more permissive than its predecessors in several places, but it has not made coastal development a formality, and the National Green Tribunal's handling of the Great Nicobar port project this year shows both how seriously a challenge can be taken and how far a project can still go once the evidentiary record supports it.

Key points

  • The CRZ Notification 2019 divides India's coastline into four zones, CRZ-I for ecologically sensitive and intertidal areas, CRZ-II for already developed urban coastal land, CRZ-III for rural and undeveloped coastal land, and CRZ-IV for the water area itself, with materially different rules on what can be built and how close to the high tide line.
  • CRZ-III is itself split by population density under the 2011 Census: CRZ-IIIA areas above 2,161 persons per square kilometre get a reduced 50 metre No Development Zone once the local Coastal Zone Management Plan is approved, while CRZ-IIIB areas below that density keep the older 200 metre No Development Zone.
  • Ports, harbours, jetties, quays and similar waterfront-dependent facilities are among the activities the notification itself permits even within the otherwise restrictive CRZ-I classification, because they cannot function anywhere except at the water's edge.
  • Clearance authority is split by zone and project scale: the National Coastal Zone Management Authority, acting through the Ministry's Expert Appraisal Committee, generally handles CRZ-I and CRZ-IV proposals and larger projects, while the State or Union Territory Coastal Zone Management Authority handles CRZ-II and CRZ-III proposals after a district-level committee's recommendation, with applications filed through the PARIVESH portal.
  • The Supreme Court's ruling in S. Jagannath v. Union of India remains the leading word on how narrowly an exemption from coastal protection should be read, having held that intensive shrimp farming did not qualify as an activity genuinely requiring waterfront access, a reasoning that still disciplines how the authorities and the Tribunal assess any project claiming to fall within a listed exception.
  • The Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep fall under a separate instrument, the Island Coastal Regulation Zone Notification 2019, not the mainland CRZ Notification, with its own, generally more permissive, zoning and development limits.
  • An appeal against a CRZ clearance, or against a refusal, goes to the National Green Tribunal under Section 16 of the NGT Act, 2010, within thirty days of the order, extendable by the Tribunal for a further period of up to sixty days on sufficient cause, for an outer limit most benches have treated as a hard ninety days.
  • The National Green Tribunal's February 2026 ruling upholding the environmental clearance for the Great Nicobar port and township project shows the Tribunal will rely on ground-truthing by technical bodies such as the National Centre for Sustainable Coastal Management, and on a High-Powered Committee's findings, rather than simply accepting either side's assertions about whether a project actually sits within a protected CRZ-IA area.

Why the coastline is regulated differently from every other category of land

The Coastal Regulation Zone framework exists because India's coastline carries ecological functions, mangroves, coral reefs, turtle nesting beaches, fish breeding grounds, sand dune systems that buffer storm surge, that an ordinary land-use or building-byelaw regime was never designed to protect, and because a structure built too close to the sea faces a flood and erosion risk that inland construction does not. The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 gives the Central Government the power to declare coastal stretches as regulated zones and to restrict what can be built within them, and the Coastal Regulation Zone Notification, 2019 is the current instrument exercising that power for mainland India.

What makes this framework distinct from an ordinary building permission is that it operates on top of, not instead of, every other approval a coastal project needs. A port still needs its environmental clearance under the Environment Impact Assessment Notification, 2006, a real estate project still needs its building plan sanction from the local municipal or development authority, and a power plant still needs its own sectoral clearances, but a CRZ clearance is a distinct, additional gate that a coastal project cannot simply fold into those other processes, even where the applications are filed and processed together administratively.

A developer, lender or investor evaluating a coastal project for the first time should treat the CRZ status of the specific site, not the project's broader regulatory profile, as the first fact to establish, because the zone a parcel falls within decides what can be built, which authority has to approve it, and how long that approval realistically takes to obtain.

The four zones, and the arithmetic that actually decides what you can build

CRZ-I covers ecologically sensitive areas and the intertidal zone, and it is itself split into two parts that are regulated very differently. CRZ-IA covers the genuinely sensitive features themselves, mangroves, coral reefs, sand dunes, turtle nesting grounds and similar ecosystems, where construction is barred outright except for a narrow list of activities the notification specifically permits. CRZ-IB covers the intertidal zone, the stretch between the high tide line and the low tide line, where the same general prohibition applies but the permitted-activity list is somewhat wider, built around activities that genuinely need direct access to the water.

CRZ-II covers land that is already developed and falls within an existing municipal or urban area, built up to or close to the shoreline. The 2019 notification eased the floor space index restrictions that had earlier constrained redevelopment in these areas, recognising that an already-built urban coastal stretch is a different proposition, in both ecological and regulatory terms, from an undeveloped one, and allowing denser redevelopment on the landward side of existing authorised roads or structures.

CRZ-III covers rural and otherwise undeveloped coastal land, and it carries the No Development Zone restriction that drives the most coastal real estate and infrastructure litigation. The 2019 notification split CRZ-III by population density measured against the 2011 Census: CRZ-IIIA, where density exceeds 2,161 persons per square kilometre, gets a reduced 50 metre No Development Zone from the high tide line, but only once the local authority's Coastal Zone Management Plan has actually been approved under the current notification, while CRZ-IIIB, the lower-density stretches, keeps the older 200 metre No Development Zone. A site's CRZ-III sub-classification, and whether its governing Coastal Zone Management Plan has actually been updated and approved, therefore decides whether a given parcel sits 50 metres or 200 metres away from where construction can begin, a difference that can determine whether a coastal plot is developable at all.

CRZ-IV covers the water area itself, the territorial waters out to twelve nautical miles and the water area of tidal rivers, creeks, backwaters and similar water bodies influenced by tidal action, and it is the zone within which a port's berths, a power plant's intake and outfall structures, or a marina's moorings actually sit, even though the land-side facilities supporting them may fall under a different zone entirely.

A single large project routinely straddles more than one of these four zones at once, a port's administrative building and warehousing sitting in CRZ-II, its approach road and storage yards crossing a CRZ-III stretch, its berths and breakwater reaching into CRZ-IV, and its dredged channel brushing against a CRZ-IB intertidal area, and the practical consequence is that a single proposal can require sign-off calibrated to several different standards within the same application rather than one uniform test applied end to end. Mapping a project's footprint against the zone boundaries early, parcel by parcel and structure by structure, rather than treating the site as a single undifferentiated CRZ category, is usually what separates an application that moves through the authorities in a predictable sequence from one that gets sent back for reclassification midway through review.

Why ports and power projects are not simply barred from the most protected zones

A common misconception is that CRZ-I's strict prohibition rules out port and power infrastructure altogether, since both activities necessarily require a location at or very near the water's edge. The notification itself answers this by listing waterfront-dependent and foreshore-requiring activities, ports, harbours, jetties, quays, wharves and similar facilities, as permissible within CRZ-IB specifically, on the logic that these activities cannot function anywhere else and are therefore treated differently from construction that has no genuine need to sit at the water's edge. Activities permitted within CRZ-IB are, generally, also permissible within CRZ-II, so a port or similar facility bridging a CRZ-IB foreshore and an adjoining CRZ-II developed area does not automatically fall foul of the more protective classification simply because part of its footprint touches CRZ-I.

This distinction, between an activity that genuinely needs waterfront access and one that does not, is also the one the Supreme Court used to decide the opposite kind of case. In S. Jagannath v. Union of India, reported at (1997) 2 SCC 87 and decided on 11 December 1996 by a bench of Kuldip Singh and S. Saghir Ahmad JJ, the Court held that intensive shrimp farming did not qualify as an activity directly related to the waterfront or directly requiring foreshore facilities, rejecting the industry's argument that aquaculture needed the same exemption that a port or jetty would genuinely need. The Court went on to direct the Central Government to constitute a dedicated authority under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 to deal with coastal aquaculture, which led to the Coastal Aquaculture Authority Act, 2005.

The lesson for a port developer, power generator or any project claiming a waterfront-dependent exemption is that the exemption is read narrowly and functionally: the question the authorities and, on challenge, the Tribunal will ask is not whether the activity is commercially more profitable near the coast, but whether it is genuinely incapable of being located anywhere else. A project that can make a clear, factual case that its core function, berthing vessels, drawing seawater for cooling, loading and unloading cargo, cannot be relocated inland is in a materially stronger position than one relying on a general commercial convenience argument dressed up in the language of the permitted-activity list.

Who actually grants a CRZ clearance, and how the file moves

CRZ clearance authority is split by zone and, in practice, by project scale and category. Proposals falling within CRZ-I and CRZ-IV, and larger projects generally, are examined by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change through its Expert Appraisal Committee, with the National Coastal Zone Management Authority exercising the apex coordinating and policy role at the national level. Proposals falling within CRZ-II and CRZ-III, which cover the great majority of ordinary coastal real estate and smaller commercial development, are handled instead by the concerned State or Union Territory Coastal Zone Management Authority.

The application itself typically starts at the district level, where a District Coastal Zone Management Authority or equivalent district-level committee scrutinises the proposal against the approved Coastal Zone Management Plan for that stretch of coast and forwards its recommendation to the State or Union Territory authority. The State authority then takes up the file at its own meetings, where a technical committee first reviews the application before it is placed before the full authority for a decision, and the entire process is now meant to run through the Ministry's PARIVESH online portal rather than through paper filings with each authority separately.

A practical complication that has shown up repeatedly in recent practice is that a State or Union Territory authority can itself become non-functional between reconstitution cycles, leaving applications with nowhere to go until a fresh authority is notified. Where that has happened, the national authority has in at least one instance directed the state's own environment department, as custodian of the Coastal Zone Management Plan, to continue processing applications in the interim, which means a developer facing an unexplained delay should check whether the relevant state authority is actually sitting before assuming the delay reflects anything about the merits of the application itself.

A proponent should also budget for the fact that a State or Union Territory authority meets periodically rather than continuously, so a file that misses the cut-off for one meeting's agenda can sit for weeks before the next sitting takes it up, and a technical committee's own preliminary queries, typically asking for additional survey data, a revised site plan, or clarification on how the proposal squares with the approved Coastal Zone Management Plan, routinely add a further cycle before the matter reaches the full authority for a substantive decision. Treating the published meeting calendar of the relevant authority as part of the project timeline, rather than as an administrative detail to be chased only once a deadline is already missed, is a more realistic way to plan a coastal project's overall schedule than assuming the clearance will track the pace of the project's other, unrelated approvals.

Islands are a separate regime, not a relaxed version of the mainland rules

A developer or investor looking at a project in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands or Lakshadweep should not assume the mainland CRZ Notification 2019 applies at all. Those island groups are instead governed by the Island Coastal Regulation Zone Notification, 2019, a separate instrument that superseded the earlier Island Protection Zone Notification, 2011, and that sets its own zoning and No Development Zone framework rather than simply importing the mainland rules.

The island notification is, in several respects, more permissive than the mainland framework, reflecting the different development pressures and the smaller available land base on island territories, including narrower No Development Zone distances for specified categories of island and specified uses such as eco-tourism. The precise distances and categories vary enough across sources and across individual island groupings that a developer should treat the applicable figure as something to confirm against the current gazetted notification and the island's own approved Coastal Zone Management Plan for that specific project, rather than relying on a single rule of thumb carried over from mainland practice.

The Great Nicobar litigation: what it shows about how a coastal infrastructure challenge is actually decided

The most instructive recent illustration of how coastal infrastructure clearance actually gets tested is the litigation over the Great Nicobar project, a township, power plant and port development on Great Nicobar Island that has drawn sustained challenge since its environmental clearance was first granted. In April 2023, the National Green Tribunal largely upheld that clearance but, rather than simply dismissing the challenge, constituted a High-Powered Committee to specifically examine three contested issues: whether coral reefs had been adequately protected, whether the environmental impact assessment's reliance on a single season of field data was adequate, and whether part of the project actually sat within the strictly protected CRZ-IA classification.

That committee's work, and the ground-truthing exercise the National Centre for Sustainable Coastal Management carried out on its behalf, concluded that no part of the project area actually fell within a CRZ-IA classification, and that single-season, non-monsoon field data was, per the guidelines applicable to port projects specifically, an adequate basis for the assessment. On 16 February 2026, the Tribunal relied on those findings to uphold the clearance in full, subject to strict and continuing compliance with the clearance's environmental conditions, and rejected the argument that the High-Powered Committee's own mandate had been drawn too narrowly, noting that the challenger had not identified any further substantial issue the committee ought to have examined.

For a developer or investor assessing litigation risk on a coastal infrastructure project, the Great Nicobar sequence shows two things worth planning around. First, the Tribunal does not resolve a disputed factual question, such as whether a site actually falls within CRZ-IA, by weighing competing affidavits alone; it has shown a willingness to direct an independent technical body to physically verify the position on the ground, which means a developer should expect its own site classification to be tested against exactly that kind of independent verification rather than accepted on the strength of its own environmental impact assessment. Second, a clearance that survives this kind of scrutiny is not necessarily final relief from compliance risk, since the Tribunal conditioned its approval on continuing, strict adherence to the clearance's environmental conditions, which means the project's obligations do not end once litigation concludes but continue through construction and operation.

Appealing a CRZ clearance, or a refusal, to the National Green Tribunal

A person aggrieved by a CRZ clearance, by a condition attached to one, or by a refusal to grant one, can appeal to the National Green Tribunal under Section 16 of the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010. The limitation period is thirty days from the date of communication of the order under challenge, and the proviso to Section 16 lets the Tribunal condone a further delay of up to sixty days where the appellant shows sufficient cause for not filing within the original thirty days, for a combined outer limit of ninety days that most benches have treated as a hard cap rather than a guideline, declining to go further even where a creditable explanation for the additional delay is offered.

A developer that has obtained a CRZ clearance should treat this ninety-day window, running from whenever a third party is taken to have had notice of the clearance, as a real and finite period of exposure rather than an open-ended risk, and should keep clear records of when and how the clearance was published or otherwise communicated, since the starting point for the limitation clock is frequently itself a contested issue in these appeals. Conversely, a project proponent or an affected resident wishing to challenge a clearance should not assume that time spent pursuing a parallel remedy, such as a writ petition before a High Court, will automatically be excluded from the Tribunal's limitation calculation, since that argument depends on provisions of the Limitation Act, 1963 that the authorities applying Section 16 have not uniformly treated as available in every case.

Practical due diligence for a developer, lender or HNI buyer

Anyone acquiring an interest in coastal land, whether a port developer assembling a site, a lender taking security over a coastal hotel, or an individual buyer purchasing a sea-facing plot or villa, should start due diligence with the site's actual CRZ classification under the current, approved Coastal Zone Management Plan for that stretch of coast, not with the seller's or broker's representation about what can be built. A plot's classification, CRZ-II, CRZ-IIIA or CRZ-IIIB, and whether the governing Coastal Zone Management Plan has actually been updated to reflect the 2019 notification, decide whether the applicable No Development Zone is a matter of tens of metres or hundreds, which in turn decides whether a buildable footprint exists on the parcel at all.

A second, equally important check is whether any existing structure on the site was itself constructed with a valid CRZ clearance, since a structure built without one remains exposed to demolition regardless of how long it has stood or how many times it has since changed hands, and an innocent purchaser inherits that exposure along with the property. Obtaining the original clearance file, or at minimum written confirmation from the relevant Coastal Zone Management Authority that the existing structure is compliant, is a more reliable protection than relying on the property having been openly used for years without incident, since coastal enforcement action in India has repeatedly shown that age alone does not cure an unauthorised structure's underlying defect.

A lender financing a coastal acquisition or a coastal hospitality project should build the same check into its own security review, because a mortgage over a structure that later turns out to have been built without CRZ clearance does not protect the lender from the underlying demolition risk, and a loan sized against the asset's current market value can quickly become under-secured if the structure itself is later found non-compliant and ordered removed. Conditioning disbursement on a specific, documented CRZ compliance confirmation for every existing structure on the security property, rather than a general environmental representation in the loan agreement, is the more defensible position if the question is ever tested.

For a port or power project specifically, the practical sequencing question is usually whether to pursue environmental clearance and CRZ clearance in parallel or sequentially, and while the two processes are administratively distinct, a proponent is generally better served preparing both applications together from a single, consistent factual record, since a discrepancy between what the environmental impact assessment says about a site's coastal classification and what the CRZ application separately asserts is exactly the kind of inconsistency that invites the sort of independent ground-truthing the Great Nicobar litigation shows the Tribunal is prepared to order.

The Coastal Regulation Zone framework gives India's coastline a layer of protection that operates independently of, and in addition to, every other clearance a coastal project needs, and the 2019 notification's relaxations, in CRZ-II redevelopment, in the CRZ-IIIA density-based No Development Zone reduction, and in the island-specific regime, have not changed that basic structure. A project's zone classification, the state of its governing Coastal Zone Management Plan, and whether its core activity genuinely needs waterfront access remain the three facts that decide both what can be built and how a challenge, if one comes, is likely to be resolved, and getting any one of those three facts wrong at the outset tends to surface later, and more expensively, rather than simply disappearing.

The Great Nicobar litigation is a useful marker of where the law stands today precisely because it shows a clearance surviving sustained challenge, not because it shows clearances being rubber-stamped. A technical body physically verified the contested facts, a High-Powered Committee tested the environmental assessment's methodology, and the Tribunal conditioned its approval on continuing compliance rather than treating the litigation's end as the end of the project's obligations. A coastal developer, lender or buyer planning around that precedent should expect the same level of scrutiny applied to its own project, and should build its application, and its due diligence, on a factual record that can survive exactly that kind of independent verification, since the cost of assembling that record properly at the outset is consistently smaller than the cost of reconstructing it years later in front of a tribunal that has already shown it will send a disputed fact out for independent ground-truthing rather than take either side's word for it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the four CRZ zones under the 2019 notification?

CRZ-I covers ecologically sensitive areas and the intertidal zone, split into the strictly protected CRZ-IA and the more workable CRZ-IB. CRZ-II covers already developed, built-up urban coastal land. CRZ-III covers rural and undeveloped coastal land, split by population density into CRZ-IIIA, with a reduced 50 metre No Development Zone where the Coastal Zone Management Plan is approved, and CRZ-IIIB, with a 200 metre No Development Zone. CRZ-IV covers the water area itself, including territorial waters and tidal water bodies.

Can a port or power plant be built within CRZ-I?

Yes, where the activity genuinely needs waterfront or foreshore access. The notification lists ports, harbours, jetties, quays and similar waterfront-dependent facilities as permissible within CRZ-IB specifically, on the reasoning that these activities cannot function away from the water's edge. The Supreme Court's reasoning in S. Jagannath v. Union of India, which denied the same kind of exemption to intensive shrimp farming, means this exception is read narrowly and tested against whether the activity is genuinely incapable of being located elsewhere, not merely more convenient or profitable near the coast.

Which authority grants CRZ clearance?

Clearance for CRZ-I and CRZ-IV proposals, and generally for larger projects, is examined by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change's Expert Appraisal Committee, with the National Coastal Zone Management Authority playing the apex coordinating role. CRZ-II and CRZ-III proposals, covering most ordinary coastal real estate and smaller commercial development, are handled by the State or Union Territory Coastal Zone Management Authority after a district-level committee's recommendation, with applications filed through the PARIVESH portal.

Do the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep follow the same CRZ rules as the mainland?

No. Those island territories are governed by the separate Island Coastal Regulation Zone Notification, 2019, which superseded the earlier Island Protection Zone Notification, 2011, and sets its own zoning and development limits rather than applying the mainland Coastal Regulation Zone Notification. A project in these islands should be assessed against the island-specific notification and the island's own approved Coastal Zone Management Plan, not against mainland CRZ rules.

What is the time limit to appeal a CRZ clearance to the National Green Tribunal?

Section 16 of the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 sets a thirty-day limitation period from the date the order is communicated, with a further delay of up to sixty days condonable on sufficient cause, for a combined outer limit most benches have treated as a hard ninety days. Whether time spent in a parallel High Court writ petition can be excluded from this calculation has not been applied consistently across cases, so a party relying on that argument should not treat it as a guaranteed extension.

What did the National Green Tribunal actually decide in the Great Nicobar case?

In April 2023, the Tribunal largely upheld the project's environmental clearance but set up a High-Powered Committee to examine coral protection, the adequacy of single-season environmental data, and whether part of the project sat within the strictly protected CRZ-IA zone. On 16 February 2026, relying on ground-truthing by the National Centre for Sustainable Coastal Management and the committee's findings, the Tribunal upheld the clearance in full, subject to strict and continuing compliance with its environmental conditions, after finding no part of the project actually fell within CRZ-IA.

How does the CRZ-IIIA 50 metre No Development Zone actually apply?

It applies only where the area has a population density above 2,161 persons per square kilometre under the 2011 Census and, critically, only once the local Coastal Zone Management Plan has been updated and approved under the current notification. Until that plan is approved for a given stretch, the older 200 metre No Development Zone continues to apply even in a high-density area, so a buyer or developer should confirm the plan's actual status for the specific site rather than assuming the reduced distance applies simply because the area is densely populated.

What should a buyer check before purchasing coastal property?

Confirm the plot's CRZ classification and No Development Zone distance under the current, approved Coastal Zone Management Plan, and separately confirm that any existing structure on the site was built with a valid CRZ clearance. A structure built without clearance remains exposed to demolition regardless of its age or how many times it has changed hands, so written confirmation from the relevant Coastal Zone Management Authority is a more reliable protection than the property's history of undisturbed use.
