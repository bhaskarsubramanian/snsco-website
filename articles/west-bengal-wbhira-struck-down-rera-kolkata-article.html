Why West Bengal Has No HIRA Anymore: WBHIRA's Fall and What Applies to Kolkata Buyers Today

West Bengal tried to run real estate regulation under its own statute instead of the central RERA Act, the Supreme Court struck that statute down in 2021, and Kolkata homebuyers are still dealing with the aftermath of a regulator that took years to properly stand up.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 13 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Ask a Kolkata homebuyer which law protects them against a defaulting promoter and many will still say HIRA, the state's own real estate statute. That statute no longer exists in any operative sense. The Supreme Court struck it down in 2021 for duplicating and diluting the central Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016, and the state then took nearly two years to get a working RERA regime of its own in place. What follows is where that history leaves a Kolkata buyer or promoter now, including a regulator that is, once again, short-staffed as of this year.

Key points

  • West Bengal enacted its own West Bengal Housing Industry Regulation Act, 2017 instead of implementing the central RERA Act, repealing the older West Bengal (Regulation of Promotion of Construction and Transfer by Promoters) Act, 1993 in the process.
  • The Supreme Court struck down WBHIRA as unconstitutional in Forum for People's Collective Efforts v. State of West Bengal, decided 4 May 2021, holding it repugnant to the central RERA Act and passed without the Presidential assent Article 254(2) would have required.
  • The repealed 1993 Act was not revived by that judgment, so West Bengal was left, for a period, without any state-level real estate statute in operation at all beyond the central Act itself.
  • The West Bengal Real Estate Regulatory Authority did not become properly operational until roughly a year and a half after the judgment, with its online portal reported live only by early 2023.
  • Projects registered under the former WBHIRA regime are not required to re-register with WBRERA, but they remain subject to ongoing reporting obligations and to limits on how far legacy registrations can be extended.
  • A pending Calcutta High Court public interest litigation has, as recently as September 2026, pressed the state government for a timeline to fill vacant posts at WBRERA and its Appellate Tribunal.
  • Kolkata buyers retain a choice between WBRERA and the consumer forum for many claims, but West Bengal's own Appellate Tribunal has shown it will dismiss a RERA complaint where the same grievance has already been argued before the State Consumer Commission.

A name that still confuses people

Mention RERA to a homebuyer in Pune or Gurugram and the reference lands immediately. Mention it in Kolkata and a surprising number of people, including some who bought flats years after the central Act came into force, will correct you to HIRA, the West Bengal Housing Industry Regulation Act, 2017. That confusion is not a quirk of local vocabulary. It reflects a real and unusual piece of legal history: West Bengal is the one large state that tried, for several years, to run its own parallel regime instead of implementing the national one, and the Supreme Court eventually put a stop to it.

This piece sets out what WBHIRA was, why the Supreme Court struck it down, how long it took West Bengal to build a working RERA regime after that, and what a Kolkata buyer or promoter deals with today, including continuing staffing problems at the state's regulatory bodies that a Calcutta High Court petition was still pressing on as recently as September 2026.

How West Bengal ended up with its own statute

Before the central Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 came into force, West Bengal already regulated promoters under the West Bengal (Regulation of Promotion of Construction and Transfer by Promoters) Act, 1993, an older and narrower statute. Rather than simply transitioning to the central Act once it commenced, the state legislature repealed the 1993 Act and replaced it with the West Bengal Housing Industry Regulation Act, 2017, a separate state law covering much of the same ground as the central Act, registration of projects, obligations of promoters, remedies for allottees, but with its own definitions, its own procedure and, on several points, materially different protections for buyers.

The state's reasoning was never fully tested in open argument before the Supreme Court decided the matter on constitutional grounds instead, but the practical effect was clear enough: a buyer in Kolkata and a buyer in almost every other major Indian city were, for several years, operating under genuinely different statutory regimes for what was, in substance, the same transaction.

The petitioners who eventually challenged WBHIRA argued that the state statute had, in effect, watered down protections the central Act gave buyers elsewhere, and that this dilution had caused real, ongoing loss to homebuyers rather than a merely technical or administrative difference. This piece does not attempt a clause-by-clause comparison between WBHIRA and the central Act, since the secondary sources available for this research describe the overlap and conflict in general terms rather than setting out a verified, side-by-side list of every differing provision, and the Supreme Court's own judgment, rather than a commentary on it, is the right place to look for that level of detail.

The Supreme Court strikes down WBHIRA

A homebuyer advocacy group, the Forum for People's Collective Efforts, challenged WBHIRA directly before the Supreme Court under Article 32, arguing that the state law had caused real and ongoing harm to buyers by displacing stronger central protections with weaker state ones. The case, Forum for People's Collective Efforts v. State of West Bengal, Writ Petition (Civil) No. 116 of 2019, was decided on 4 May 2021 by a bench of Justices D.Y. Chandrachud and M.R. Shah, and reported as (2021) 8 SCC 599 and 2021 INSC 283.

The Court's central reasoning rested on repugnancy. Real estate regulation of this kind falls within a Concurrent List entry that both Parliament and state legislatures can legislate on, and where a state law covers the same field as a central law and conflicts with it, Article 254 of the Constitution generally gives the central law precedence unless the state law has received Presidential assent specifically preserving it. West Bengal had not obtained that assent for WBHIRA. Having found substantial overlap between WBHIRA and the central Act, and having concluded that WBHIRA operated as a parallel regime in direct conflict with it rather than as a complementary local scheme, the Court held WBHIRA unconstitutional and void for repugnancy.

One consequence of the ruling is easy to miss. Striking down WBHIRA did not revive the older 1993 Act that WBHIRA had repealed; the Court's order left that repeal in place, so the practical effect of the judgment, for a short and awkward period, was that West Bengal had no state-specific real estate statute in force at all, only the central Act itself, pending the state actually implementing it properly.

The judgment also left an open practical question that the Supreme Court itself was not asked to answer in detail: what happens to registrations already granted, and complaints already decided, under a statute that no longer exists. That question did not get resolved on the day of the judgment. It took the state's own subsequent implementation steps, discussed further below, to settle how legacy WBHIRA projects would be treated once WBRERA actually started functioning, and the gap between the ruling and that settled practice is itself part of why the transition took as long as it did.

The gap between the judgment and a working regulator

A Supreme Court judgment striking down a statute does not, by itself, staff an office or launch a website, and West Bengal's experience after May 2021 illustrates that gap starkly. The state did take an initial step relatively promptly, notifying rules under Section 84 of the central Act to begin the process of implementation, but a homebuyers' body went back to the Supreme Court within the following year complaining that the Authority's Chairperson and Members had still not been appointed and that no functioning online portal existed for filing complaints.

Reports place the West Bengal Real Estate Regulatory Authority as finally becoming operational only around December 2022, close to a year and a half after the judgment that was supposed to clear the way for it, with the authority reported at that point to be headed by a former bureaucrat, Sandipan Mukherjee, sitting with two other members. A separate account places the online complaint portal itself going live only by February 2023, meaning that even after the Authority existed on paper, a Kolkata buyer could not necessarily file a complaint through it for some further months.

None of this delay is unique to West Bengal in the abstract; implementation lag has dogged RERA rollout in several states. What makes West Bengal's version notable is that it followed directly from the state having spent its first several years under the Act pursuing an entirely different statutory path, so the delay was not merely administrative friction but the tail end of an unsuccessful attempt to avoid the central regime altogether.

For a buyer whose cause of action arose during this transitional window, roughly May 2021 through early 2023, the practical lesson is to check carefully which body actually had jurisdiction and was functioning on the date a complaint needed to be filed, rather than assuming WBRERA could simply be approached from the moment the Supreme Court's judgment was announced. A complaint that could not practically be filed because no functioning portal or constituted Authority yet existed is a fact worth recording contemporaneously, since it may become relevant later to any argument about delay in bringing the claim.

Filing a complaint with WBRERA today

The current route for a Kolkata buyer is the West Bengal Real Estate Regulatory Authority's own portal, at rera.wb.gov.in, which the Authority's site describes as open to any aggrieved person wishing to complain of a violation or contravention of the Act by a promoter, developer, agent or allottee. Coverage extends to the disputes a buyer actually experiences most often: delayed possession, construction defects and misleading advertising about a project's specifications or amenities.

Fee reporting is inconsistent across sources in a way worth flagging rather than glossing over. At least one account of the current WBRERA process puts the complaint fee at Rs 1,000, while other, apparently older guides still describe a Rs 5,000 fee tied to the earlier WBHIRA-era process, filed at an office in New Town rather than through the current online route. Given that WBHIRA itself no longer has legal force, the Rs 5,000 figure and the New Town filing address should be treated as superseded rather than current, but a complainant should still confirm the exact fee on WBRERA's own portal before filing rather than relying on any secondary source, including this one.

A separate West Bengal Real Estate Appellate Tribunal ruling from 2025 is a useful caution on forum selection. The Tribunal dismissed a RERA complaint on the ground that the same parties had already filed an essentially identical case before the State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, treating the earlier filing as foreclosing a second attempt at the same relief through RERA. A Kolkata buyer weighing which forum to use should decide once, on the basis of which forum suits the relief actually wanted, rather than treating a first filing as a trial run that can be abandoned for a second one later.

One further source of confusion is worth flagging on its own. A web domain carrying the old HIRA name remains online and reachable, adding to the mix-up this piece opened with: a search for West Bengal real estate regulation can easily surface material connected to the defunct authority alongside material from the current one. Treat any filing instructions, fee schedule or office address that traces back to the old HIRA domain as historical rather than current, and use WBRERA's own portal, rera.wb.gov.in, for anything filed from this point onward.

What happens to projects registered under the old WBHIRA regime

A fair number of ongoing Kolkata projects were originally registered under WBHIRA before the Supreme Court struck that statute down, and the transition to WBRERA raised an immediate practical question: did those projects need to register all over again. The available material indicates they do not. Projects already registered under the erstwhile WBHIRA regime continue to be recognised as registered, and only genuinely new projects need to register with WBRERA directly, sparing promoters and buyers alike a wholesale re-registration exercise that would have added further delay on top of what had already occurred.

That continuity is not the same as being left alone, however. WBRERA has continued to impose reporting obligations on these legacy projects, including a quarterly update requirement for registered projects, erstwhile WBHIRA registrations among them, reinforced by an Authority order issued in December 2024 directing promoters to comply. Extensions of a legacy registration's validity are also being scrutinised rather than granted automatically: in one 2026 case before the West Bengal Real Estate Appellate Tribunal, a promoter seeking an extension of its WBHIRA-era registration through to the end of December 2026 instead had that extension cut back by the Tribunal to 31 March 2026, a reminder that the regulator, once functioning, has not simply waved legacy projects through on their old terms.

A regulator short-staffed again, as of this year

West Bengal's staffing troubles did not end once WBRERA finally stood up in 2022 and 2023. A public interest litigation now before the Calcutta High Court alleges prolonged vacancies in key posts at both WBRERA and the West Bengal Real Estate Appellate Tribunal, and seeks disclosure of exactly how many posts are vacant and how far hearings and adjudication are actually proceeding despite them.

The timeline through this September has been active. On 3 September 2026 the High Court directed the state's counsel to obtain instructions on the vacancies, and at a further hearing on 15 September, counsel still had no instructions on the specific question of who, if anyone, had been appointed as Chairperson of the Appellate Tribunal. A Division Bench of Justices Arijit Banerjee and Reetobroto Kumar Mitra then directed the office of the Chief Secretary, West Bengal, to file a report on the status of appointments to the posts of REAT Chairperson, a REAT Judicial Member and a WBRERA Member, and to state a time limit within which those vacancies would be filled, allowing the petitioner to add the Chief Secretary as a party to the proceeding. The matter was listed to be heard again on 29 September 2026, and this research did not find a report of what the Chief Secretary's office actually filed or what happened at that later hearing, so the current staffing position should be treated as unresolved rather than settled as of the date of this piece.

A recruitment notice already on the Calcutta High Court's own website, posted in February 2026, sought applications for a Judicial Member of the Appellate Tribunal and a Member of WBRERA, with a selection committee made up of the Chief Justice of the High Court or a nominee, the state's Housing Secretary and its Law Secretary. Taken together with the September proceedings, the clear picture is of a regulator that, having finally become functional after the WBHIRA episode, has struggled since to keep its senior posts filled on a continuous basis.

None of this means WBRERA has stopped functioning altogether while posts sit vacant; the Tribunal's own 2025 and 2026 orders discussed elsewhere in this piece show it was deciding matters during the same general period these vacancies were being litigated. What the Calcutta High Court petition captures is less a regulator standing still than one running with persistent gaps in its senior ranks, a pattern that began with the Authority's own slow start after 2021 and has not fully resolved five years on.

Building a complaint: what a Kolkata buyer needs on file

The documentary record that decides a WBRERA complaint is the same kind of record that decides one anywhere else in India: the allotment letter or agreement for sale, a complete set of payment receipts tied to the construction-linked or time-linked schedule actually agreed, and the written correspondence in which the promoter addressed delay, whether by acknowledging it, blaming external causes, or simply failing to respond. Buyers who registered originally under WBHIRA and are now dealing with a promoter under the WBRERA regime should keep both sets of documents together, since a dispute over a project's registration history, legacy status or extension can turn on exactly when and under which statute a given representation was made.

A promoter's response in West Bengal is likely to raise the same categories of defence seen elsewhere: force majeure arguments tied to the pandemic years, delay attributed to municipal or state clearances rather than to the promoter's own default, and claims that the complainant is behind on payments. Kolkata buyers should also expect, given the Tribunal's own 2025 ruling on parallel proceedings, a possible objection that the claim has already been pursued, or should have been pursued, before the State Consumer Commission, so keeping a clear record of exactly what relief has been sought in which forum, and when, is worth doing from the outset rather than reconstructing it later under pressure.

Getting an order enforced once you have won

A favourable order from WBRERA or its Adjudicating Officer is not self-executing. Section 40 of the central Act allows an amount due under such an order to be recovered as an arrear of land revenue, through a recovery certificate issued to the District Magistrate or Collector with jurisdiction over the promoter's assets, the same enforcement mechanism this firm has described in more detail elsewhere for other states. A Kolkata complainant who has obtained an order against a promoter that does not pay voluntarily should expect to take this further step rather than treating the original order as the end of the process, particularly given how recently West Bengal's own regulatory machinery has settled into regular operation.

Where the central Act's usual rules still apply

None of this history changes the substance of the rights a Kolkata buyer has under the central Act once a complaint is actually filed. Delay compensation and refund claims proceed under Section 18 in broadly the same way they would in any other state, structural defect liability runs for the same statutory period under Section 14(3), and the same two-track split between the Authority's jurisdiction over refund and possession and the Adjudicating Officer's separate jurisdiction over compensation applies in West Bengal exactly as it does elsewhere. The Appellate Tribunal route, with its pre-deposit condition for a promoter's appeal under Section 43(5), and the possibility of a further challenge to the High Court, follow the national framework this firm has set out in detail for Delhi's version of the same process, and little of that changes simply because the project is in Kolkata rather than in the capital.

What West Bengal's history does change is the degree of confidence a buyer or promoter should place in settled local practice. A regulator that only became functional in 2022 and 2023, and that is still fighting staffing vacancies through 2026, has had less time and continuity than most of its counterparts to build up the kind of consistent order-writing and procedural practice that lawyers in longer-established jurisdictions can rely on. Treat any secondary guide describing WBRERA's current fees, forms or sitting pattern as provisional, and confirm directly with the Authority before relying on it.

Where this leaves Kolkata homebuyers and promoters

A Kolkata buyer today files with WBRERA, not with any surviving HIRA office, and should expect the same broad procedural shape, Authority or Adjudicating Officer at first instance, appeal to the Appellate Tribunal, a further option to approach the High Court, that applies under the central Act nationally. The genuinely state-specific risk is not a different set of substantive rights but a thinner, more recently assembled institutional structure behind those rights, one that has needed repeated judicial prodding, first from the Supreme Court in 2021 and now from the Calcutta High Court in 2026, simply to stay staffed.

A promoter in Kolkata faces a parallel point on the other side of the same coin. Legacy WBHIRA registrations carry forward without a fresh filing, but they do not carry forward on autopilot: quarterly reporting obligations continue, and the Tribunal has shown it will cut back an extension request rather than grant it as asked. Anyone managing a project that began life under WBHIRA should treat its current WBRERA status as something to actively monitor rather than something settled once and for all when the original registration was granted.

How Kolkata's experience compares with the rest of India

West Bengal is not the only state where RERA's rollout has been uneven, but the shape of its difficulty is distinctive. Elsewhere, delay has typically come from ordinary administrative slowness in setting up an Authority the state always intended to create under the central Act. In West Bengal, the delay came after years spent trying a different statutory path altogether, so the usual start-up friction was layered on top of the aftermath of a Supreme Court judgment unwinding an entire parallel regime, rather than simply following the central Act's commencement in the normal course.

A promoter or investor comparing a Kolkata project against one in Delhi, Noida or Mumbai should factor in this institutional history alongside the identical statutory text. The substantive rights under Sections 12, 14, 18 and 19 of the central Act do not vary by state, but the maturity of the body applying them does, and West Bengal's regulator, Authority and Tribunal both, has simply had less uninterrupted time in operation than its counterparts in states that moved to the central Act without first detouring through a state-specific alternative.

West Bengal's experiment with its own real estate statute lasted roughly four years before the Supreme Court ended it, and the state then took almost as long again to build a central-Act regulator that actually functioned. For a Kolkata homebuyer weighing a complaint today, the practical takeaway is straightforward: file with WBRERA under the central Act, expect the same substantive rights the Act gives buyers anywhere in India, and do not assume the word HIRA still means anything beyond a legacy label attached to older project registrations.

The less straightforward takeaway is about institutional reliability rather than substantive law. A regulator whose own senior posts were still the subject of a live Calcutta High Court petition as of September 2026 is not yet the kind of settled, predictable forum that buyers and promoters in states with a longer unbroken RERA history can generally assume they are dealing with. Building a case or a compliance strategy around WBRERA at present means checking its current staffing and practice at the time a complaint or appeal is actually filed, not relying on how the Authority was described in a guide, including this one, written even a few months earlier.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does HIRA still apply to real estate in West Bengal?

No. The Supreme Court struck down the West Bengal Housing Industry Regulation Act, 2017 as unconstitutional in May 2021, and the state's older 1993 promoter law was not revived by that judgment. The operative law today is the central Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016, administered in West Bengal through WBRERA.

Why did the Supreme Court strike down WBHIRA?

The Court held that WBHIRA covered much the same ground as the central RERA Act, that the two laws conflicted rather than complemented each other, and that West Bengal had not obtained the Presidential assent under Article 254(2) of the Constitution that would have been needed to let a conflicting state law prevail. On that basis it found WBHIRA repugnant to the central Act and void.

Do flats registered under the old WBHIRA need to be re-registered with WBRERA?

No. Projects already registered under the former WBHIRA regime continue to be treated as registered and are not required to register afresh with WBRERA. They remain subject to continuing obligations, including periodic reporting requirements, and extensions of their original registration period are being scrutinised by WBRERA and the Appellate Tribunal rather than granted automatically.

Where does a Kolkata homebuyer file a complaint now?

Through the West Bengal Real Estate Regulatory Authority's own portal at rera.wb.gov.in, not through any surviving WBHIRA office. Reported complaint fees vary across sources, so confirm the current fee on the portal itself before filing rather than relying on an older guide describing the earlier WBHIRA-era process.

Is WBRERA fully staffed and functioning smoothly today?

Not consistently. A public interest litigation before the Calcutta High Court, with hearings through September 2026, has pressed the state government for a timeline on filling vacant posts, including the Appellate Tribunal's Chairperson and Judicial Member and a WBRERA Member, after the government's own counsel was unable to confirm the appointment position at a hearing that month. Confirm the current composition of both bodies before assuming either is fully staffed when your matter is likely to be heard.

Can a Kolkata buyer choose between WBRERA and the consumer forum?

In principle, yes, for many delay and defect claims, since RERA has generally not been read to oust consumer forum jurisdiction. In practice, West Bengal's own Appellate Tribunal has dismissed a RERA complaint where the same grievance had already been argued before the State Consumer Commission, so the sensible course is to pick one forum and pursue the claim there rather than attempting both in sequence.

Did the fall of WBHIRA give West Bengal buyers weaker protection than before?

The Supreme Court's own reasoning pointed the other way: the petitioners who brought the challenge argued WBHIRA had diluted buyer protections compared to the central Act, and the Court's finding of repugnancy rested in part on that overlap and conflict. The practical difficulty for buyers was less about the substance of their rights under the central Act, once it finally applied, and more about the delay before a working regulator existed to enforce them.

How does a RERA appeal in West Bengal differ from one in Delhi or Uttar Pradesh?

The statutory steps are the same everywhere, since all three states apply the same central Act: an appeal to the Appellate Tribunal within sixty days, a mandatory pre-deposit for a promoter's appeal, and a further route to the High Court. The practical difference is institutional rather than legal, West Bengal's Authority and Tribunal became functional only in 2022 and 2023 and have faced repeated staffing gaps since, a continuity problem this firm has not found reported to the same degree in Delhi or Uttar Pradesh's own RERA structures.
