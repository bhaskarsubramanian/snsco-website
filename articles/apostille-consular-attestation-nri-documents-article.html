Apostille or Consular Attestation: Getting an NRI's Documents Accepted in India

A power of attorney, an affidavit or a marriage certificate signed abroad needs a specific chain of authentication before an Indian court, bank or registry will act on it, and the chain depends entirely on which country it came from

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Every NRI client we have advised on a property sale, a probate petition, or a pending suit in India eventually asks the same practical question: why will the bank, the sub-registrar or the court not simply accept a power of attorney or an affidavit signed and notarised abroad. The answer is that Indian law treats a document executed outside India as needing a specific authentication chain before it carries any weight here, and that chain is not the same for every country. This piece sets out the two tracks, apostille and consular attestation, which countries fall into each, and the particular trap the Gulf countries, home to a large part of the NRI population, present because they sit outside the apostille system altogether.

Key points

  • India has been a member of the Hague Apostille Convention, 1961 since 2005, and a document apostilled by the competent authority of another member country needs no further embassy attestation to be used in India; the apostille alone is sufficient under the Convention's reciprocal scheme.
  • A document coming from, or going to, a country that has not joined the Convention follows a different and longer chain: notarisation in that country, authentication by that country's own foreign ministry, and then legalisation by the Indian embassy or high commission there, or, for an Indian document headed to a non-Hague country, notarisation, state-level authentication, Ministry of External Affairs attestation, and finally that country's own embassy in India.
  • The United Arab Emirates and Qatar, home to a very large share of the NRI population, are not parties to the Hague Apostille Convention, so a power of attorney or affidavit executed there for use in India, or an Indian document headed there, must go through consular attestation rather than apostille, regardless of how routine the document itself is. Saudi Arabia, by contrast, acceded to the Convention with effect from 7 December 2022 and is now a member, so a document executed there can be apostilled instead.
  • A power of attorney executed abroad gets the benefit of Section 85 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (the provision that replaced Section 85 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872), a presumption of due execution, only where it has been properly authenticated, and Indian courts have held that this presumption for a foreign-notarised power of attorney depends on the executing country having reciprocal status recognised under the Notaries Act, 1952, not on notarisation alone.
  • Section 33 of the Registration Act, 1908 separately requires that a power of attorney executed outside India be authenticated, by a notary, an Indian consular officer, or a representative of the Central Government, before it can be accepted for registering a document in India, which is a distinct requirement from the apostille or attestation chain and has to be satisfied alongside it.
  • A power of attorney authenticated by an Indian consular or diplomatic officer abroad must generally be stamped under the Indian Stamp Act within three months of its receipt in India to remain valid for that purpose, a deadline NRI clients routinely miss by assuming the document is ready to use the moment it lands.
  • Within the Hague system itself, inbound and outbound apostilles use different officials: a document originating in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia or Singapore is apostilled by that country's own designated competent authority, while an Indian document headed to any Hague member country is apostilled by India's Ministry of External Affairs, generally after state-level pre-authentication for personal documents.

Why a document signed abroad is not automatically good in India

A document executed outside India, a power of attorney authorising a relative to sell a flat, an affidavit for a court proceeding, a marriage certificate needed for a visa or a passport application, carries no automatic weight before an Indian court, bank or government office merely because it was signed and notarised wherever the NRI happens to live. Indian law treats the signature and the notarisation abroad as something that itself needs to be verified through a recognised chain before the document can be relied upon here, precisely because an Indian authority has no independent way of knowing whether a foreign notary's seal is genuine.

Two separate systems exist to supply that verification, and which one applies to a given document turns entirely on whether the country where the document was executed, or the country it is headed to, has joined the Hague Apostille Convention. Confusing the two, or assuming the simpler of the two always applies, is the single most common reason NRI clients arrive at our office with a document that a bank or registrar has already refused to accept.

The consequence of getting this step wrong is rarely a simple delay that sorts itself out with a follow-up call. A power of attorney that a sub-registrar refuses to act on means a property sale cannot close on the date the parties agreed to, with the usual knock-on consequences for an agreement to sell that fixes a closing date, and an affidavit a court declines to take on record because it was not properly authenticated can mean an NRI litigant's evidence is simply unavailable at the hearing it was meant to support. Treating the authentication chain as a genuine procedural prerequisite, to be settled before the underlying transaction's own timeline is fixed, rather than as paperwork that can be sorted out once a problem actually arises, is the single habit that avoids most of the difficulties this piece describes.

Apostille: the single-step route among member countries

India has been a party to the Hague Convention of 5 October 1961 Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents, commonly called the Apostille Convention, since 2005. Among member countries, the Convention replaces the older multi-step legalisation chain with a single certificate, the apostille, issued by a designated competent authority in the country where the document originates, and that certificate alone is sufficient for the document to be accepted in any other member country without further attestation by an embassy or consulate.

For a document originating in a Hague member country and headed to India, the apostille is issued by that country's own designated authority, the US Department of State or the relevant Secretary of State's office for a document from the United States, the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office for one from the United Kingdom, Global Affairs Canada, or the equivalent body in Australia or Singapore, each of which is itself a Convention member. Once apostilled there, the Ministry of External Affairs' own guidance confirms that no further attestation or legalisation is required for the document to be used in India.

For an Indian-origin document headed the other way, to be used in a Hague member country, the Ministry of External Affairs is India's own designated competent authority, and it issues the apostille, generally after the document has first been authenticated at the state level for personal documents such as birth, death and marriage certificates, affidavits and powers of attorney. The Ministry does not accept documents directly from individual applicants; applications are routed through its designated outsourced service centres, a point that catches out NRIs who assume they can simply walk into a Ministry office and collect an apostille the same day.

The Ministry's own published fee for the apostille itself is modest, a small per-document charge, and the real cost and time an applicant should budget for comes instead from the state-level pre-authentication step and the outsourced service centre's own processing and courier arrangements, both of which can vary noticeably between states and between the specific personal document involved. A birth certificate issued by one municipal corporation, a marriage certificate registered with a different authority, and a notarised affidavit each sometimes need to go through a slightly different state department for that first authentication, which is why confirming the exact pre-authentication route for the specific document and the specific issuing state, rather than assuming one state's process mirrors another's, is worth doing before an NRI family starts the clock on a transaction that depends on the apostille being ready by a particular date.

Consular attestation: the longer chain for non-member countries

Where either the country of execution or the destination country has not joined the Apostille Convention, the simpler apostille route is unavailable, and the older legalisation chain applies instead. For a document executed in a non-Hague country and intended for use in India, that chain runs through notarisation in the country of execution, authentication by that country's own foreign ministry, and finally attestation by the Indian embassy or high commission there, each step confirming the authenticity of the one before it.

For an Indian document headed to a non-Hague country, the direction reverses but the number of steps stays broadly the same: notarisation in India, authentication at the state level, attestation by the Ministry of External Affairs, and then legalisation by that destination country's own embassy or consulate in India, since the destination country's mission is the body that, in the end, certifies the document is acceptable for use on its own territory. This is a materially longer and slower process than apostille, often running to several weeks once each authority's own processing time is added up, and it is the route that catches NRI clients from the Gulf off guard most often, since many assume every Indian document abroad goes through the same apostille process regardless of destination.

Each link in this longer chain exists to verify the one before it, which is why skipping a step, or getting the order wrong, usually means the whole document has to go back to an earlier stage rather than simply picking up where it left off. An Indian embassy abroad attesting a document will generally expect to see the destination country's own foreign ministry stamp already on it, and will not substitute its own attestation for a step that properly belongs to that country's authorities; similarly, the Ministry of External Affairs in Delhi will not attest a personal document that has not already cleared the relevant state authority. Families managing this chain on behalf of an NRI relative abroad do better treating each stage as a genuine prerequisite for the next, confirming each office's specific stamp or certificate is actually in place, rather than sending the document onward and hoping a later authority will simply work around a missing earlier step.

The Gulf exception: why the UAE and Qatar are different, and why Saudi Arabia no longer is

A very large share of the NRI population lives and works in the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia and Qatar. Of the three, the UAE and Qatar are not parties to the Hague Apostille Convention, so a power of attorney or an affidavit executed in Dubai, Abu Dhabi or Doha for use in India cannot be apostilled at all; it has to go through the full consular attestation chain, typically notarisation in the Emirate or country concerned, authentication by that country's own Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and attestation by the Indian embassy or consulate there, before the document is ready to use in India. Saudi Arabia is different: since acceding to the Convention with effect from 7 December 2022, a document executed in Riyadh or elsewhere in the Kingdom for use in India can instead be apostilled by the Saudi competent authority, and a family should confirm the current position given how recently it changed.

This matters in practice because the same NRI client often has family or business connections in both a Hague country and a Gulf country, and the correct chain for a document genuinely depends on exactly where it was signed, not on the client's general sense of where NRI paperwork usually comes from. A power of attorney signed during a visit to India itself, rather than abroad, avoids this question altogether since it is then simply an Indian document executed in India, which is a reason some of our Gulf-based clients prefer to execute time-sensitive documents on a visit home rather than through the attestation chain from abroad, where that is practically possible.

Gulf and Middle Eastern jurisdictions do not all follow the same pattern: Bahrain and Oman, like Saudi Arabia, are Convention members, while the UAE and Qatar are not, so a client relocating within the region should not assume that a neighbouring country's status mirrors the one they are used to; the Hague Conference's own published list of member states is the only reliable way to confirm a given country's status, and it is worth checking afresh for the specific country in question rather than relying on general impressions about the Gulf as a whole.

Commercial documents from the Gulf, a board resolution for an Indian subsidiary of a Dubai company, or a power of attorney with commercial content rather than purely personal content, sometimes attract a different, higher fee and a separate processing track from that country's own foreign ministry, since several Gulf authorities classify a document as commercial once it relates to a business transaction rather than a personal matter such as a property sale on the NRI's own behalf. Confirming which category a given document falls into, and budgeting for the fee and timeline that category actually carries, is worth doing before submission rather than discovering the distinction only after the document is returned for reclassification.

Power of attorney specifically: Section 85, Section 33, and the reciprocity trap

A power of attorney executed abroad raises questions beyond the apostille or attestation chain itself. Section 85 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, the provision that took over from Section 85 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 when the new evidence law came into force, gives a presumption that a power of attorney executed before, and authenticated by, a notary public, or any court, judge, magistrate, Indian consul or vice-consul, or a representative of the Central Government, was properly executed, sparing the party relying on it from having to separately prove execution through ordinary evidence.

Courts have held that this presumption for a foreign-notarised power of attorney does not arise automatically merely because a notary abroad has put a seal on it; it depends on the executing country having been notified as a reciprocating country for this purpose under the Notaries Act, 1952, and the corresponding rules. A power of attorney notarised in a country that has not been so notified does not get the benefit of the presumption in the same automatic way, which can leave a party having to prove the document's execution through other evidence if its validity is challenged, a real risk in contested property or succession matters.

Section 33 of the Registration Act, 1908 adds a separate and independent requirement specifically for a power of attorney meant to be used for registering a document, a sale deed or a gift deed, for instance. It requires the power of attorney to have been authenticated by an Indian consular officer, a notary, or specified categories of officers, and the sub-registrar is entitled to insist on this authentication quite apart from whatever apostille or embassy attestation the document may separately carry. The two requirements, Section 85 BSA's presumption and Section 33's authentication for registration purposes, are often satisfied through overlapping but not identical paperwork, which is why a power of attorney meant for an Indian property transaction should be prepared with both provisions in mind from the outset, not just whichever one the executing notary abroad happens to be familiar with.

A further wrinkle worth flagging is that some sub-registrars, as a matter of local practice rather than a uniform statutory rule, also expect a power of attorney executed abroad for a property sale to be adjudicated and stamped, not merely authenticated, before they will act on it to register the sale deed, treating the power of attorney itself as an instrument chargeable to stamp duty under the relevant state's stamp legislation. Whether this is required, and at what rate, depends on the state where the property and the registration office are located, since stamp duty on instruments is a state subject and individual states have adopted differing schedules and differing practice on powers of attorney specifically. Checking the specific sub-registrar's current practice, rather than relying on what a different state's office required for a similar transaction, avoids a last-minute surprise at the registration counter itself, and a quick confirmatory call to the registration office before the document is finalised abroad costs very little against the alternative of discovering the gap only once the family is already at the counter.

The three-month stamping deadline that catches NRIs out

A power of attorney authenticated by an Indian consular or diplomatic officer abroad, rather than executed before a foreign notary, is treated under the Indian Stamp Act as requiring stamp duty to be paid, and adjudicated or stamped, within three months of its receipt in India, failing which it can lose its character as a validly stamped instrument for the purpose it was meant to serve. NRI clients who have the power of attorney authenticated well in advance of when they actually need it, then let it sit in a drawer for months before a family member in India gets around to using it, risk discovering that the window to stamp it properly has already closed by the time it matters.

The practical fix is straightforward but easy to overlook: treat the three-month clock as starting the moment the document physically arrives in India, not the date it was signed or authenticated abroad, and arrange for stamping promptly once it does. A family handling a property transaction or a court matter on an NRI's behalf should build this step into the timeline from the start, rather than treating stamping as an afterthought to be dealt with whenever the document is finally needed.

Proving exactly when the document arrived in India is itself worth attending to, since the three-month clock runs from receipt, not execution, and a family that cannot show a clear date of arrival, through a courier record, an email confirming receipt, or a dated acknowledgment, leaves itself with little to point to if a sub-registrar or a court later questions whether the stamping was done within time. Keeping the courier's tracking record or the envelope itself, rather than discarding it once the document is unpacked, is a small step that can matter considerably if the timing is ever challenged.

Affidavits, educational and civil documents: the same chain, different uses

An affidavit sworn abroad for use in an Indian court proceeding, a probate petition, a matrimonial case, or a civil suit where the NRI party cannot appear in person, follows the same apostille-or-attestation logic as a power of attorney, apostille where the country of execution is a Hague member, consular attestation through the Indian embassy where it is not. Courts generally expect the affidavit to show, on its face, that it was sworn before a notary or other competent authority recognised in that country, with the apostille or attestation certificate physically attached or endorsed, before accepting it as properly proved.

Marriage certificates, birth certificates, educational degrees and similar civil documents follow the identical authentication logic whenever they are to be used officially in India, registering a marriage, applying for a document that depends on proof of date of birth, or having a foreign degree recognised, though the state-level pre-authentication step for an Indian document headed abroad, and the specific department that handles it, the General Administration Department, the Home Department, or the Sub-Divisional Magistrate's office depending on the state, can differ from one Indian state to another. A document issued in one Indian state but needed for an NRI formality tied to another state's procedure should be checked against the specific requirements of the department actually handling the pre-authentication, rather than assumed to be uniform across India.

A marriage certificate presents its own particular wrinkle for an NRI couple who married abroad and now need the marriage recognised in India, for a spouse visa, a name change on a passport, or a later succession matter. The certificate issued by the foreign registrar generally needs the same apostille or attestation treatment as any other civil document before an Indian authority will rely on it, and a couple who married in a Hague member country can expect the single apostille to suffice, while a couple married in a non-member country faces the fuller consular chain. Separately, and quite apart from the authentication question, the couple may also need to register the marriage under the Foreign Marriage Act, 1969 or the relevant state marriage registration law if they intend to rely on Indian recognition of the marriage itself rather than merely producing the foreign certificate as evidence, a distinct step our earlier piece on NRI marriage registration addresses in more detail.

A grant of probate obtained abroad: a different route from apostille altogether

Where the document in question is not a power of attorney or an affidavit but a grant of probate already obtained in a foreign court, an English, American or Canadian probate for a will that disposes of property in both that country and India, the apostille or attestation chain described in this piece does not apply at all, and treating it as if it did is a mistake we see even among families who have otherwise done their paperwork correctly. Section 5 of the Indian Succession Act makes clear that a foreign grant does not, by itself, extend to immovable property in India, so an executor cannot simply apostille an English probate and present it to an Indian sub-registrar as sufficient authority to deal with an Indian flat.

Section 228 instead lets an executor apply to the appropriate Indian court for probate of an authenticated copy of the will, where the original will has already been proved and deposited in a court of competent jurisdiction outside India, a route sometimes loosely described as recognising the foreign grant but which, in substance, is a fresh Indian probate petition supported by that authenticated copy rather than an automatic transposition of the foreign order. Section 270 fixes which Indian court has territorial jurisdiction over this petition, generally tracking the location of the deceased's property in India in the same way an ordinary probate petition would.

The authenticated copy of the foreign will and grant still needs to go through the apostille or attestation chain in its own right, since it is itself a document issued by a foreign court that an Indian court has no independent means of verifying, which means families dealing with a cross-border estate often end up running two processes side by side: authenticating the foreign grant for use as evidence, and separately pursuing the Section 228 petition in the Indian court that actually confers authority over the Indian assets. Starting the Section 228 petition only after the foreign grant is fully authenticated, rather than in parallel, is usually the more efficient sequence, since the Indian court will in any event expect the authenticated copy before it proceeds.

Corporate documents: board resolutions and powers for an NRI-run company

NRIs who sit on the board of an Indian company, or who hold shares in one, routinely need to execute a board resolution, a shareholder consent, or a specific power authorising someone in India to sign on the company's or their own behalf, while they themselves are abroad. These corporate documents follow the same underlying logic as a personal power of attorney, apostille where the document is executed in a Hague member country, consular attestation through the Indian embassy where it is not, though banks and the Registrar of Companies dealing with corporate paperwork sometimes apply their own additional internal requirements on top of the statutory minimum, a specific board resolution format, or a requirement that the signatory's authority under the company's own articles be separately demonstrated.

A director who signs a document abroad for an Indian company matter should also keep the company's own compliance calendar in mind alongside the authentication chain; a resolution authenticated weeks after the board meeting it records, or a power executed just before a filing deadline with no margin for the attestation chain's own processing time, creates an entirely avoidable compliance risk. Our earlier piece on NRIs as directors and shareholders in Indian companies sets out the broader residency and signing framework this fits into, including the DIN and digital signature requirements that sit alongside the authentication question; the attestation issue covered here is one layer within that wider picture, not a substitute for it, and a director abroad should expect to satisfy both before a filing is treated as complete.

Common errors that get a document rejected

The error we see most often is skipping the state-level pre-authentication step entirely for an Indian personal document and sending it straight to the Ministry of External Affairs for apostille, on the assumption that the Ministry handles everything in one sitting; the Ministry's own process generally requires that earlier state-level step for personal documents such as affidavits, powers of attorney, and birth, death and marriage certificates, and a document that skips it is simply returned rather than apostilled.

A second recurring error is treating a Gulf-executed document as eligible for apostille because the client has seen the apostille process work smoothly for a relative in the United States or the United Kingdom, without checking that the specific Gulf country is in fact outside the Convention. A third is letting a consular-authenticated power of attorney sit unused past the three-month stamping window discussed above, and a fourth is notarising a power of attorney in a country whose reciprocating status under the Notaries Act has not actually been confirmed, only to find the Section 85 BSA presumption unavailable if the document's validity is later challenged.

A fifth, less obvious error is assuming that a document once apostilled or attested remains valid for every subsequent use without regard to how much time has passed or whether the underlying facts have changed; a power of attorney for a specific transaction that has since fallen through, or an affidavit prepared for a proceeding that has since taken a different turn, should generally be re-executed and re-authenticated for a materially different later use, rather than reused on the assumption that the original attestation covers whatever the document is now being asked to do.

A sixth error, common enough among families who try to manage the process themselves to save time, is sending an original document through the full chain and then being unable to produce it again quickly if a second institution, a different bank branch, or a parallel court filing, needs its own original rather than a copy. Where more than one institution genuinely needs to see an original apostilled or attested document, it is usually more efficient to prepare and authenticate more than one original from the outset, each carrying its own apostille or attestation certificate, than to discover midway through a transaction that the single authenticated original is tied up with one institution while another is waiting on it.

A worked comparison: Houston and Dubai, the same power of attorney

Consider two NRIs, one based in Houston, Texas, the other in Dubai, each needing to authorise a sibling in Noida to sell an inherited flat on their behalf. The Houston NRI signs the power of attorney before a Texas notary, and because the United States is a Hague member and Texas's Secretary of State is a designated apostille authority, that single apostille is enough; the document travels to India ready for the sub-registrar to examine alongside the Section 33 Registration Act authentication the notarisation itself satisfies, and the family can expect the document to be usable within days to a couple of weeks of signing, depending mainly on the Texas apostille office's own turnaround.

The Dubai NRI signing an identical power of attorney faces a materially longer path for an identical purpose. The document first needs notarisation in the UAE, then authentication by the UAE's Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and then attestation by the Indian embassy or consulate in the UAE, three separate steps rather than one, each with its own processing queue, before the document is ready to travel to India. Where time genuinely matters, a sale closing by a fixed date, a court deadline the sibling in Noida is working against, the Dubai-based NRI is often better served planning for four to six weeks for the full chain rather than assuming it will move at the same pace as the Houston apostille, and, where the transaction allows it, timing a visit to India to execute the power of attorney in person can be the faster option once the UAE chain's realistic timeline is weighed against the cost and disruption of travel.

The underlying document, the transaction it supports, and the sub-registrar's Section 33 requirement are identical in both cases; only the authentication chain differs, and differs because of a fact about the country of execution that has nothing to do with the document's own content. This is the point families most often miss when comparing notes with relatives in different countries: a smooth experience with apostille for one NRI in the family says nothing about how long the same step will take for another NRI whose documents originate in a non-Hague country.

Sequencing the paperwork correctly

The sequence that avoids most of these errors starts with identifying, specifically, the document's country of execution and, where relevant, its intended destination, before drafting a single word of the document itself, since the correct notarial clause, the authentication chain, and the time each step will realistically take all flow from that one fact. For a document executed in a Hague member country for use in India, the sequence runs local notarisation, that country's own apostille authority, and then straight to use in India without any embassy step. For a document executed in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar or another non-member country, the sequence runs local notarisation, that country's foreign ministry, and the Indian embassy or consulate there, each step adding its own processing time that should be budgeted for well ahead of any deadline the document is actually needed for.

For an Indian-origin power of attorney or affidavit headed abroad to a non-Hague destination, the sequence runs the other way, notarisation in India, the relevant state authority, the Ministry of External Affairs, and then the destination country's own embassy in India, and families coordinating this on an NRI's behalf should expect to deal with at least three or four different offices in sequence rather than a single counter that handles the whole chain. Building in enough time for each step, and confirming the receiving institution's exact requirement before starting rather than after a document is rejected, remains the most reliable way to keep an NRI transaction, a property sale, a probate petition, or a pending suit, moving on the timeline the family actually needs.

It is worth a family keeping a simple written record of each step as it is completed, the date a document was notarised, the date it reached the state authority, the date the Ministry or the embassy stamped it, and the date it actually arrived in India, since this record is exactly what resolves a later dispute over whether the three-month stamping window was met, or whether a particular authority's step was genuinely completed before the next one was sought. A transaction that goes smoothly rarely needs this record examined, but one that does not, a sale that falls through and is later revived, or a court that questions the document's provenance, benefits considerably from a family that can show precisely when each link in the chain was forged rather than reconstructing the sequence from memory months later.

Getting an NRI's document accepted in India is rarely a question of the document's own content; it is almost always a question of whether the right authentication chain, apostille or consular attestation, was followed for the specific country it came from or is going to. Hague membership is the fact that decides which chain applies, and two of the Gulf countries with the largest NRI populations, the UAE and Qatar, sit outside that system in a way that genuinely changes the process rather than merely adding a formality; Saudi Arabia, the third, acceded to the Convention in December 2022 and no longer does.

A power of attorney carries its own additional layer, the Section 85 BSA presumption, Section 33 of the Registration Act, and the three-month stamping deadline, each operating independently of the apostille or attestation question and each capable of undoing an otherwise properly authenticated document if overlooked. Checking the specific country's status, the specific document's purpose, and the specific deadlines that apply to it, before the document is even drafted, remains the surest way to avoid the rejection and delay that an assumption about 'the usual process' so often produces.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a power of attorney signed in the USA or UK need embassy attestation to be used in India?

No. Both countries are members of the Hague Apostille Convention, as is India, so an apostille from the designated competent authority in the USA or UK is sufficient on its own. No further attestation by the Indian embassy or consulate there is required once the document carries a valid apostille.

Why can't a power of attorney signed in Dubai or Abu Dhabi be apostilled?

The United Arab Emirates is not a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, so there is no apostille authority there whose certificate India would recognise. A document executed in the UAE for use in India must instead go through consular attestation: notarisation, authentication by the UAE's own foreign ministry, and attestation by the Indian embassy or consulate in the UAE.

How long is an apostille or attestation valid once obtained?

The apostille or attestation itself does not expire in the way a licence might, but a power of attorney authenticated by an Indian consular officer abroad must generally be stamped under the Indian Stamp Act within three months of reaching India to remain valid for that purpose, and any document should in practice be reasonably current to the transaction it supports rather than years old.

Can a foreign notary's seal alone make a power of attorney valid in an Indian court?

Not reliably. Courts have held that the presumption of due execution under Section 85 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 for a foreign-notarised power of attorney depends on the executing country's reciprocating status under the Notaries Act, 1952, not on notarisation by itself, and Section 33 of the Registration Act separately requires authentication before the document can be used to register a property document in India.

Which Indian authority issues an apostille for a document going abroad?

The Ministry of External Affairs is India's designated competent authority under the Convention. For personal documents such as affidavits, powers of attorney, and birth, death or marriage certificates, the document generally needs state-level authentication first, and the Ministry does not accept applications directly from individuals, routing them instead through its designated outsourced service centres.

Is Saudi Arabia or Qatar treated the same way as the UAE for this purpose?

Only Qatar is. Qatar is not a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, so documents executed there for use in India, and Indian documents headed there, follow the same consular attestation chain described for the UAE. Saudi Arabia is different: it acceded to the Convention with effect from 7 December 2022, so a Saudi-executed document can now be apostilled rather than routed through consular attestation.

What happens if a document is sent for apostille without the required state-level authentication first?

For a personal document such as an affidavit or power of attorney, the Ministry of External Affairs generally requires prior authentication by the relevant state authority before it will apostille the document, and an application that skips this step is typically returned rather than processed, which simply adds delay rather than resolving the underlying requirement.
