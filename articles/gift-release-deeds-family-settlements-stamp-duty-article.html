Gift Deeds, Release Deeds and Family Settlements: Stamp Duty and Registration Pitfalls in West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh

Why the label on a family property document matters less than its substance, and how getting that substance wrong invites a stamp duty demand or a title dispute years later

By Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Families transferring property among themselves in Kolkata and in Noida or Greater Noida reach for whichever document sounds cheapest or most familiar: a gift deed, a release deed, or a hand-drafted family settlement. Each carries its own stamp duty treatment, its own registration requirement and its own way of failing. This piece sets out what the law, as it stands today, actually requires in West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh, and where an unregistered family arrangement can still be relied upon.

Key points

  • A gift of immovable property must be made by a registered instrument under Section 123 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882; an unregistered gift deed transfers nothing.
  • West Bengal taxes a gift to specified family members at a concessional rate on market value with a separate registration fee; Uttar Pradesh instead caps the stamp duty itself at a flat figure for blood relatives, extended in January 2026 to commercial and industrial property as well.
  • A release deed only works to enlarge the share of someone who is already a co-owner; naming a person who was never a co-owner turns the document into a gift for stamp purposes, whatever the parties call it.
  • The Delhi High Court's 2025 ruling in Ramesh Sharma confirms that siblings releasing their shares to a co-owning brother are not making a gift, but the result can flip the moment the release favours an outsider.
  • A memorandum that only records a family settlement already acted upon does not need registration under Section 17(2)(v) of the Registration Act, 1908; a document that itself creates or transfers rights for the first time does.
  • Courts look at the substance of what a document does, not its title, when deciding whether it needed registration or attracted a particular stamp duty rate.
  • A suit to set aside a gift deed, release deed or settlement on grounds such as fraud or undue influence must ordinarily be filed within three years of the plaintiff learning the facts that justify cancellation, under Article 59 of the Limitation Act, 1963.
  • Stamp duty concessions and flat-rate caps are set by state notification and are revised without much notice; the rate quoted by a deed writer last year may already be out of date.

Why the choice of instrument matters more than families assume

A father wants to move a flat in Salt Lake into his daughter's name. A set of siblings in Greater Noida want to divide an ancestral plot without the cost and delay of a partition suit. A joint family in north Kolkata wants to put on paper an arrangement they reached around a dining table years ago. In each case, someone will suggest a document: a gift deed, a release deed, or a family settlement. The instinct is to treat these as interchangeable ways of achieving the same result at the lowest possible cost. They are not.

Each of these instruments is defined differently in law, is stamped differently, and fails differently when something goes wrong. A gift deed that is not registered conveys no title at all. A release deed that names someone who was never a co-owner is reclassified as a gift and taxed accordingly, sometimes years after execution, when the Collector of Stamps or a court takes a second look. A family settlement that purports to create fresh rights in immovable property, rather than merely record an arrangement already acted upon, needs registration just as much as a sale deed does. Getting the label wrong does not make the underlying transaction illegal; it makes it vulnerable, either to a stamp duty demand with penalty, or to a successful challenge to title at the worst possible time, such as when the property is being sold or mortgaged.

This guide works through the three instruments in turn, with the stamp duty and registration position in West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh set out separately, because the two states diverge sharply on how a family gift is taxed. It then turns to the harder question practitioners in both states are asked most often: when, if ever, an unregistered family settlement will hold up in court.

The gift deed: what the Transfer of Property Act actually requires

A gift, under Section 122 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, is a transfer of existing movable or immovable property made voluntarily and without consideration, by a donor to a donee, and accepted by or on behalf of the donee. Three things follow from this definition that families routinely overlook. The property must already exist and already belong to the donor; a gift of something the donor expects to inherit or acquire later is not a gift in law. The transfer must be truly without consideration; if money, however small, changes hands for the transfer, it stops being a gift and becomes a sale or an exchange, taxed at the ordinary conveyance rate. And the donee must accept the gift during the donor's lifetime, while the donor is still capable of giving; a gift accepted after the donor's death, or after the donor has lost capacity, generally fails.

Section 123 fixes the mode of transfer for immovable property: the gift must be effected by a registered instrument signed by the donor and attested by at least two witnesses. There is no informal route for land or a flat, unlike for movable property, which can also pass by delivery. The Supreme Court has clarified that Section 123 does not make physical delivery of possession a condition of a valid gift of immovable property; registration of the instrument is what matters, and possession can follow later or not at all. A donor who executes an unregistered gift deed, however carefully drafted, has not completed a gift of land under Indian law. The document may still carry some evidentiary value, but it does not transfer title, and a subsequent purchaser or a competing heir can attack it on that ground alone.

Once validly made, a gift of immovable property is, as a rule, irrevocable. Section 126 permits revocation only in narrow circumstances: where the donor and donee had agreed, at the time of the gift, that it would be suspended or revoked on the happening of a specified event not depending on the donor's own will, or where the gift can otherwise be rescinded as if it were a contract, for instance for fraud, coercion, or undue influence. A donor who simply changes their mind, or falls out with the donee later, has no unilateral right to take the property back. Families who treat a gift deed as a flexible, reversible arrangement are working from a mistaken premise, and the consequences surface only when the relationship sours.

Gift deeds in West Bengal: the concessional family rate

West Bengal taxes a gift to a close family member at a materially lower rate than a gift to anyone else or an ordinary sale. The concessional treatment applies where the donor and donee fall within a defined family relationship, broadly covering parents, children, spouse and certain other lineal relations, and is pegged to the market value of the property rather than to a flat sum. Outside that family bracket, a gift is stamped at the same rate as a conveyance, which runs into several per cent of market value depending on whether the property is within a municipal corporation area, and an additional surcharge can apply once the market value crosses a notified threshold. Registration is charged separately, as a percentage of market value, on top of the stamp duty itself.

Two points deserve particular care in West Bengal. First, the concessional rate is tied to the specific list of relationships the state notification recognises as family for this purpose; a gift to a son-in-law, a daughter-in-law, or a more distant relative such as a niece or nephew does not automatically qualify, and deed writers sometimes assume a broader definition than the notification actually allows. Second, the figures quoted by property portals and deed-writing services change from year to year and are not always current; the only way to be certain of the rate that will actually be charged on a given deed is to check with the Directorate of Registration and Stamp Revenue or the jurisdictional Sub-Registrar's office before the deed is finalised, not after. A deed presented for registration at the wrong duty is impounded, and the deficiency, along with a penalty that can run into multiples of the shortfall, becomes payable before registration can proceed.

A gift deed of immovable property situated in Kolkata or anywhere else in West Bengal must be presented for registration within the statutory period after execution, before the Sub-Registrar having jurisdiction over the property, not over the donor's residence. Delay beyond the permitted window requires an application for condonation, with its own fee, and is not guaranteed.

Gift deeds in Uttar Pradesh: the flat-rate cap and its 2026 extension

Uttar Pradesh has taken a different route. Rather than pegging family gifts to a percentage of market value, the state introduced an administrative cap fixing stamp duty on a gift between blood relatives at a flat figure, first applied in 2022, revived by notification in 2023, and since written into statute through an amendment to the Indian Stamp Act as it applies in Uttar Pradesh. The effect, for a gift deed between parents and children, spouses, and siblings, is that the stamp duty payable is a fixed sum rather than a percentage of the property's value, regardless of whether the property is a modest plot in a village or an expensive flat in Noida or Greater Noida. Registration fee is charged separately and is not reduced by the same concession.

In January 2026, the Uttar Pradesh Cabinet extended this flat-rate treatment to gifts of commercial and industrial property between family members, which until then had been treated as outside the concession and taxed at the full ad valorem rate. For a family business looking to pass a shop, a small factory, or other commercial premises to the next generation without triggering duty calculated on the full market value of commercial property, which runs well above residential rates, this is a material change, and it is recent enough that many deed writers and even some registration counters had not updated their practice at the time of writing. Anyone relying on the flat-rate cap for a commercial or industrial gift should confirm, at the Sub-Registrar's office or on the state's online portal, that the concession has been applied correctly to their specific transaction, including the qualifying definition of blood relative used for that purpose, which is not identical across every state scheme.

The practical consequence of the difference between the two states is this: a family with property in both Kolkata and Noida cannot assume that what was paid, or what qualified for concession, in one state will carry over to the other. The rate, the definition of qualifying relative, and even the mechanism, a percentage concession in West Bengal against a flat cap in Uttar Pradesh, are entirely separate regimes, each revised by its own state notification on its own timetable.

The release deed: an instrument that only works between co-owners

A release deed, sometimes called a relinquishment deed, is used when a person who already holds a share in jointly owned property gives up that share in favour of another co-owner. The underlying legal idea is not a transfer of a fresh interest from one stranger to another; it is the extinguishment of the releasing co-owner's existing share, which has the effect of enlarging the share already held by the remaining co-owner. Because nothing new is being created or transferred to someone who held nothing before, a release between genuine co-owners has traditionally attracted a lower stamp duty than a gift or sale of the same value, and in several states, including both West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh, specific concessional treatment exists for such releases within a family.

The limit on this treatment is strict and has produced recurring litigation. A release deed can only operate between people who already hold an interest in the property as co-owners. If a document styled as a release names a person who was never a co-owner, whether a son-in-law brought in as an afterthought, a grandchild, or anyone else outside the existing ownership, the document cannot do what a release is meant to do, because there is no existing share of that person's to enlarge. Courts and stamp authorities treat such a document as a gift or a conveyance in substance, whatever its title says, and tax it accordingly, which can mean a demand for the shortfall in duty, with penalty, raised long after the deed was registered.

The Delhi High Court's Division Bench decision in Ramesh Sharma v. Government of NCT of Delhi, decided on 8 October 2025, illustrates the line on the side that favours families. In that case, a Greater Kailash property had been inherited by a brother and five sisters from their parents. The sisters executed release deeds in the brother's favour, and the Collector of Stamps impounded the deeds as gift deeds carrying deficient stamp duty, apparently applying an earlier test under which a document favouring only one of several co-owners, rather than all of them, was treated as a gift. The Division Bench disagreed and held that a relinquishment of rights by one co-owner in favour of another co-owner who already holds a share simply enlarges that co-owner's existing share and does not amount to a transfer of ownership requiring gift-deed treatment, even where the release favours only one among several co-owners rather than all of them together.

The other side of the line, where a release favours someone outside the existing ownership, has long been settled by the Supreme Court. In Kuppuswami Chettiar v. A.S.P.A. Arumugam Chettiar, decided in 1966 and reported at [1967] 1 SCR 275, the Court examined a deed styled as a release where the releasor in truth intended to transfer title to someone without consideration, and held that, on its true construction, the deed operated as a gift under the Transfer of Property Act notwithstanding its label. The lesson for drafting is straightforward: before a release deed is prepared, confirm that every person named as a releasee already holds a recorded or provable share in the property. If the intended recipient does not, a gift deed, correctly stamped as such, is the honest and safer route, even though it may cost more.

Family settlements: a different legal category altogether

A family settlement, sometimes called a family arrangement, is treated by Indian courts as a distinct legal category from a gift, a sale, or a partition in the strict sense. The underlying idea, recognised by the Supreme Court as far back as Kale v. Deputy Director of Consolidation, is that where there is a genuine dispute or possible dispute about rights in family property, and the members of the family resolve it by mutual agreement in order to preserve family peace and property, that arrangement is given effect by courts even though, strictly speaking, no member may be giving up more than what they might arguably have been entitled to claim. A family settlement need not even be in writing; an oral arrangement, acted upon by the parties, can be perfectly valid, and no registration question arises for an oral arrangement because there is no instrument to register.

The difficulty arises once the family decides to put the settlement on paper, which happens far more often than not, if only so that there is something to show a bank, a buyer, or a mutation authority later. Whether that written document needs to be registered, and stamped as a conveyance, turns on what the document itself does rather than on what the parties call it. If the writing merely records, after the fact, a settlement that the family had already reached and already acted upon, perhaps by dividing possession, paying out shares, or mutating revenue records, it falls within the exemption in Section 17(2)(v) of the Registration Act, 1908, for documents that do not themselves create, declare, assign, limit or extinguish any right in immovable property, and registration is not compulsory. If, instead, the writing is the document that for the first time creates, assigns or extinguishes rights between the family members, rather than simply recording an earlier arrangement, it falls squarely within the categories of document that Section 17 requires to be registered, and an unregistered version cannot be used to prove the transaction it records, and will often also attract conveyance-level stamp duty rather than the lower rate sometimes available for recorded family arrangements.

The Supreme Court's decision in Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur, decided in 2020, works through exactly this distinction. The question before the Court was whether a memorandum dated 10 March 1988 created rights in immovable property for the first time, or merely recorded an oral family settlement that the parties had already reached and acted upon. The Court held that the document was a mere memorandum of a past settlement, exempt from compulsory registration under Section 17(2)(v), and that the unregistered document was nonetheless admissible in evidence; further, the parties who had acted upon the settlement for years were estopped from turning around and disputing it once litigation began. The High Court in that matter had taken the opposite view, holding the memorandum compulsorily registrable, and the Supreme Court restored the First Appellate Court's decree in the family's favour.

Where a written family arrangement instead materially alters the legal rights of family members in a way the earlier arrangement, oral or otherwise, had not already settled, the position runs the other way: such a document creates new rights and is compulsorily registrable. The practical test a drafter should apply, before advising a family on whether their settlement document needs registration, is to ask squarely: has this family already divided the property between themselves, in substance, before this paper was drawn up, or is this paper the first time anyone's share is actually being fixed? The honest answer to that question, not the title on the document, decides the registration question, and a lawyer who signs off on a settlement as exempt from registration without pressing the family on this point is taking a real risk on the client's behalf.

Stamp duty on family settlements in West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh

Where a family settlement document is held to require registration because it itself creates or transfers rights, both West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh generally treat it, for stamp purposes, in line with how a partition or a release between the relevant family members would be treated, which in both states is ordinarily lower than the duty on an outright sale to a stranger, though the exact figure depends on whether the arrangement is structured as a partition among co-owners, a release, or something closer to cross-gifts between family members. Because the concessional partition and release rates, and the definitions of qualifying family relationship that unlock them, are themselves set and revised by state notification, and because the two states do not use identical mechanisms, a family settlement spanning property in both Kolkata and Noida or Greater Noida should be stamped and registered as two separate exercises, each confirmed against the current notification in that state, rather than assumed to track whatever was paid on the other side of the arrangement.

Where a settlement merely records an earlier arrangement and falls within the Section 17(2)(v) exemption, no registration is compulsory and, correspondingly, no registration fee or conveyance-style stamp duty is triggered by the recording document itself, though a nominal stamp may still be advisable for evidentiary purposes and to satisfy a bank or revenue authority that is being asked to act on it. Families sometimes choose to register such a memorandum voluntarily, even though the law does not require it, precisely because an optionally registered document is harder for a disgruntled relative to challenge later, and because mutation authorities and banks are often more comfortable acting on a registered paper even when an unregistered one would, in strict law, suffice.

Challenging any of these documents: limitation and grounds

A family member who was pressured, misled, or simply left out of a gift deed, release deed, or family settlement does have remedies, but they are time-limited, and the clock matters as much as the ground. A suit to have an instrument cancelled or set aside, whether on the ground of fraud, undue influence, lack of free consent, or the executant's incapacity, falls within Article 59 of the Limitation Act, 1963, which allows three years from the date the facts entitling the plaintiff to have the instrument cancelled first become known to them. This is a discovery-based limitation period, not one that runs automatically from the date of execution; a person who did not know a gift deed had been executed in their absence, and who only learns of it years later when they try to deal with the property, has three years from that discovery, not from the original execution date, though the burden of proving when the relevant facts actually came to light rests on the person bringing the suit, and a court will scrutinise a long gap between the deed and the suit with some suspicion if the circumstances suggest the facts were, or should have been, known earlier.

The three-year period under Article 59 merged what, under the earlier Limitation Act of 1908, had been two separate articles, and courts have read it broadly enough to cover suits to set aside a range of instruments: sale deeds, gift deeds, settlement documents and decrees that fall within its wording. Where a document is simply void from the outset, for instance because it was never validly executed or because the executant had no title to give, a suit for a bare declaration of that fact may in some circumstances fall under the longer general limitation period applicable to declaratory suits rather than Article 59's three years, and which article actually governs a given dispute is a question that repays careful legal advice rather than assumption, because getting it wrong at the drafting stage of a plaint can be fatal to the claim long before the merits are reached.

For a family weighing whether to challenge a deed at all, the practical calculation is rarely only legal. A suit to set aside a gift to a sibling, or to challenge a family settlement signed years ago, reopens a dispute the rest of the family may have considered closed, and the limitation period exists partly to protect the stability of transactions and family peace that the settlement doctrine itself was designed to encourage. Advice on whether to sue should weigh the strength of the ground, the freshness of the knowledge that triggers limitation, and the practical cost of reopening a settled family arrangement, not merely whether a technical ground for cancellation exists on paper.

Practical drafting checklist for both states

Before finalising any of these three instruments, a few checks are worth the time they take. Confirm, in writing if possible, the exact relationship between donor and donee or releasor and releasee against the current state notification defining who qualifies for a concessional rate; the list is narrower than most families assume and is not identical between West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh. Confirm the current rate or flat figure directly with the Sub-Registrar's office or the state's online calculator shortly before execution, not from a deed writer's memory of last year's figure, since both states have revised these figures by notification with little public notice. For a release deed, verify and, where possible, document that every releasee already holds a recorded or otherwise provable share in the property; where that cannot be shown, treat the transaction as a gift and stamp it accordingly rather than risk impounding later. For a family settlement, establish and record, in the document itself if appropriate, whether the writing is recording an arrangement already acted upon or is creating the division for the first time, since that single fact decides the registration question. And register every instrument, including a memorandum that the law does not strictly require to be registered, with the Sub-Registrar having jurisdiction over the property's location, keeping in mind that registration deadlines run from execution and that condonation of delay, where available at all, is neither automatic nor free.

None of the three instruments discussed here is inherently more or less legitimate than the others; each has a proper use, and each has a stamp duty and registration consequence that follows from what the document actually does rather than from what it is called. A gift deed must be registered to transfer anything at all, and the concessional family rate in West Bengal and the flat-rate cap in Uttar Pradesh operate on entirely different mechanisms that do not translate across the two states. A release deed only enlarges an existing co-owner's share and turns into a gift, for tax and for title purposes, the moment it favours someone who was never a co-owner. A family settlement can validly bypass registration altogether, but only where it genuinely records an arrangement already reached and acted upon, and a document that instead creates rights for the first time needs the same registration a sale deed would need.

The safest course for a family dealing with property across Kolkata and Noida or Greater Noida is to treat each document, and each state's requirements, as a separate exercise, confirmed against the current notification rather than against what was paid on a similar transaction last year or across the state line, and to be candid, at the drafting stage, about what the family arrangement is actually doing rather than reaching for whichever label seems to carry the lowest duty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a gift deed of a flat or house be valid without registration?

No. Under Section 123 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, a gift of immovable property must be effected by a registered instrument. An unregistered gift deed does not transfer title, whatever the parties may have intended or how clearly the document is worded.

Does a gift deed need the donee's physical possession of the property to be valid?

No. Courts have held that Section 123 does not make delivery of possession a condition of a valid gift of immovable property once the instrument is properly registered; possession can follow separately.

Can a family member who gave a gift simply revoke it later?

Only in narrow circumstances under Section 126 of the Transfer of Property Act: where the donor and donee had specifically agreed, at the time of the gift, to a condition for revocation not depending solely on the donor's will, or on grounds available to rescind an ordinary contract, such as fraud or undue influence. A simple change of mind is not a ground.

Is the stamp duty on a gift to family the same in West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh?

No. West Bengal applies a concessional percentage of market value to gifts within a defined family relationship, with registration charged separately. Uttar Pradesh instead applies a flat stamp duty figure for gifts between blood relatives, a cap recently extended, in January 2026, to commercial and industrial property as well. The two mechanisms are not interchangeable, and each state's current notification should be checked separately.

Can a release deed be used to transfer property to someone who is not already a co-owner?

No. A release deed only operates to enlarge the share of someone who already holds an interest as a co-owner. Naming a person who was never a co-owner turns the document into a gift in substance, which stamp authorities and courts can reclassify and tax accordingly, even where the deed is titled a release.

Does a family settlement have to be in writing or registered to be valid?

No. An oral family settlement, acted upon by the parties, can be valid without any writing at all. Where it is reduced to writing, registration is compulsory only if the writing itself creates, assigns or extinguishes rights in immovable property for the first time; a document that merely records a settlement already reached and acted upon is exempt from compulsory registration under Section 17(2)(v) of the Registration Act, 1908.

How long does someone have to challenge a gift deed or family settlement they disagree with?

Generally three years under Article 59 of the Limitation Act, 1963, running from when the facts entitling them to seek cancellation first became known to them, not necessarily from the date the document was executed.

Is a lower stamp duty available for a release between co-owners compared to an outright sale or gift?

Both West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh offer concessional treatment for a genuine release between existing co-owners, lower than the ordinary conveyance rate, but the exact figure and qualifying conditions are set by state notification and should be confirmed before the deed is executed.
