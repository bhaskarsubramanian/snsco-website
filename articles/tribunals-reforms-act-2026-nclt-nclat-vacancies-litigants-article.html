The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 and NCLT Vacancies: What Changes for Litigants

Why the Supreme Court struck down the 2021 law again, what the new National Tribunals Commission is meant to fix, and what to do while benches stay short-staffed

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 21 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A company or creditor with a matter pending before the National Company Law Tribunal has, for several years now, faced a second problem layered on top of the dispute itself: whether the bench hearing the case will have enough members, and whether the law governing how those members are appointed will survive the next constitutional challenge. The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 is Parliament's latest attempt to settle that second problem.

Key points

  • The Supreme Court struck down key provisions of the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 for a second time in Madras Bar Association v Union of India, 2025 INSC 1330, decided on 19 November 2025, holding that the 2021 Act simply re-enacted provisions already held unconstitutional in earlier Madras Bar Association rulings of 2020 and 2021.
  • The Court directed the Union to establish a National Tribunals Commission within four months of the judgment. That deadline passed without the Commission being set up, and Parliament instead passed the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026, which received presidential assent on 13 August 2026, roughly five months after the Court's deadline lapsed.
  • The 2026 Act creates the National Tribunals Commission as a standing body responsible for the appointment process, performance oversight and service conditions of members across sixteen tribunals, including the NCLT and NCLAT.
  • Separately from the constitutional litigation, the Supreme Court has been hearing a suo motu matter on NCLT vacancies and infrastructure that it registered in May 2026; the NCLT Bar Association's submission that at least eighteen benches were holding only half-day sittings for want of members came later, in September 2026, as a development within that already-pending case.
  • The NCLT presidency itself stood vacant from 13 February 2026, when Justice Ramalingam Sudhakar retired, until Justice (Retd.) Anupinder Singh Grewal was appointed as the regular President around the end of April 2026, with two acting Presidents holding the post in the interim.
  • A litigant before the NCLT or NCLAT today should plan around continuing vacancies and sitting pressure, not assume the 2026 Act has immediately resolved the backlog, since commentators have already questioned whether the new Act repeats features the Supreme Court previously found unconstitutional.

Why the composition of the bench is now part of every NCLT matter

A creditor filing a Section 7 or Section 9 application, a company facing an oppression and mismanagement petition, or a party to a scheme of arrangement, all assume that the tribunal hearing their matter is properly constituted and reasonably staffed. For much of the last several years, that assumption has not been safe in India. The National Company Law Tribunal has run short of judicial and technical members at various benches, the law governing how those members are appointed has been struck down by the Supreme Court more than once, and the body meant to fix the appointment process on a standing basis took years longer to arrive than the Court itself directed.

None of this changes the substantive law a litigant argues before the NCLT. What it changes is how quickly that argument gets heard, how often a bench sits, and, at the margins, whether a particular member's appointment can itself become a point of challenge. A litigant and their counsel who track this background are better placed to plan timelines and manage client expectations than one who treats tribunal administration as someone else's problem.

Tribunals were introduced across Indian commercial and regulatory law precisely because specialised, efficient adjudication was meant to be faster and more technically informed than ordinary civil courts. The NCLT, created to take over company law jurisdiction from the High Courts and the old Company Law Board, and then given the central role it now plays under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, is a case study in how that promise depends entirely on the tribunal actually being staffed and resourced to do the job Parliament gave it.

Lenders and promoters on opposite sides of the same dispute often experience this gap differently. A lender pursuing recovery through the IBC generally wants the fastest possible route to resolution, since delay erodes recoverable value as the underlying business continues to deteriorate. A promoter resisting an insolvency application, by contrast, sometimes benefits from exactly the delay a short-staffed bench produces, which means the staffing question this article describes is not a neutral administrative detail for either side but a factor that can shape negotiating leverage well before any order is actually passed.

The Madras Bar Association line: a decade of the same fight

The current round of litigation is only the latest in a line of cases going back more than a decade, in which the Madras Bar Association has repeatedly challenged how Parliament structures tribunal appointments. In 2020, the Supreme Court held that a short tenure for tribunal members was, in its words, anti-merit, and directed a minimum tenure of five years with eligibility for reappointment, and that the search-cum-selection committee responsible for recommending candidates could put forward only one name per vacancy, not a panel of names for the government to choose from.

In 2021, after the Government tried to reintroduce a four-year tenure and a minimum age of fifty years through an Ordinance, the Supreme Court struck those provisions down again, reaffirming the five-year tenure and finding the minimum age requirement arbitrary. The pattern across both rulings was the same: Parliament and the Executive proposing a shorter tenure, more control over the selection committee, and the Court holding that this compromised the independence tribunals need to function as substitutes for ordinary courts.

The search-cum-selection committee sits at the centre of this dispute because it decides, in practice, who becomes available to be appointed at all. The 2020 ruling held that the committee could recommend only one name per vacancy, not a shortlist the government could then pick from, and that the government had to complete the appointment within three months of receiving that recommendation. Both points go directly to how much discretion the Executive retains over who eventually sits on a bench deciding cases against the government's own departments, public sector lenders, and regulators, which is why the Court has treated them as going to judicial independence rather than as mere administrative detail.

It is worth being specific about why this matters for an NCLT litigant rather than treating it as an abstract separation-of-powers argument. The NCLT regularly adjudicates disputes in which a public sector bank, a government department, or a regulator such as the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India is itself a party, whether as a financial creditor pursuing recovery, as a tax authority with a claim in a resolution process, or as the body overseeing the resolution professional's conduct. A tribunal member appointment process that leaves the Executive effectively free to pick whichever candidate it prefers raises a genuine question about how independently that member can be expected to rule against the government's own interests, which is the concern each Madras Bar Association ruling has returned to.

The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 struck down a second time

Parliament's response to the 2021 ruling was the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021, which the Madras Bar Association challenged yet again. On 19 November 2025, a Supreme Court bench of the Chief Justice, B.R. Gavai, and Justice K. Vinod Chandran decided that challenge in Madras Bar Association v Union of India, reported as 2025 INSC 1330, and struck down the Act's core provisions on appointment and tenure. The Court's reasoning was direct: the 2021 Act had simply reproduced provisions from the Tribunals Reforms Ordinance that earlier Madras Bar Association rulings had already held unconstitutional, which the Court treated as an impermissible attempt to override binding precedent under Article 141 of the Constitution rather than a fresh legislative choice Parliament was entitled to make.

The ruling did more than strike the Act down. The Court directed the Union of India to establish a National Tribunals Commission within four months of the judgment, describing this as a constitutional obligation the Executive had already been told to meet after the 2020 ruling and had not met. For litigants with matters pending before the NCLT, NCLAT and other tribunals covered by the ruling, the practical question was not the constitutional reasoning itself but what would happen to appointments made, and pending, while this litigation ran its course.

A tribunal whose governing statute has just been struck down does not stop functioning the next morning. Judgments of this kind are typically accompanied by directions that protect appointments already made, so that members who were validly selected under the process the Court approved are not themselves displaced by a ruling aimed at the legislative framework rather than at any individual appointee. For a litigant, the immediate effect of a ruling like this is less about whether today's bench can keep hearing the matter and more about whether the next round of appointments, needed to fill the vacancies this article goes on to describe, will happen any faster than the last round did.

The four-month deadline, missed, and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026

That four-month window the Supreme Court gave the Union government in November 2025 ran out in March 2026 without a National Tribunals Commission being established. Parliament eventually acted several months later: the Tribunals Reforms Bill, 2026 cleared the Lok Sabha on 10 August 2026 and the Rajya Sabha the following day, and received the President's assent on 13 August 2026, roughly five months after the Court's own deadline had passed.

What the new Act actually creates is a standing body, the National Tribunals Commission, with responsibility for the appointment process, performance oversight and service conditions of members across sixteen tribunals, including the NCLT and NCLAT, rather than leaving each appointment round to be negotiated afresh between the Executive and whichever search-cum-selection committee the law of the day prescribes. A standing commission, in principle, should shorten the gap between a vacancy arising and a replacement being appointed, because the machinery for recommending candidates no longer has to be reconstituted each time a new round of appointments is needed.

At least one commentator has already questioned whether the new Act fully answers the Court's concerns, or whether it carries forward features the Court has previously treated as compromising judicial independence, given how directly its predecessor statutes were struck down for exactly that reason. A litigant should treat the 2026 Act as a significant structural change, but not as the final word, given how many earlier versions of essentially the same reform have been struck down before it, and given that a body created by statute in August 2026 still has to be staffed, funded and made operational before it can actually shorten the appointment timelines litigants care about.

Vacancies on the ground: the suo motu case and the NCLT presidency

While the constitutional litigation over how appointments should be structured was running, the NCLT's actual staffing kept falling behind its workload. The Supreme Court registered a suo motu matter in May 2026, titled around the appointment of judicial and technical members and inadequate infrastructure at the NCLT. Months later, in September 2026, the NCLT Bar Association told the Court, within that already-pending case, that at least eighteen benches were sitting only for half days because they did not have enough members to sit full time. The Court had already directed the government in September 2025 to fill tribunal vacancies on what it called a war footing, and a further direction reported in December 2025 required the government to complete the process of filling the NCLAT Chairman's post and the NCLT President's post within a further two months. By the time of a September 2026 hearing in the suo motu matter, the Court was reported to have sought a status report from the Centre specifically on infrastructure gaps, suggesting its concern by then had moved beyond the headline vacancies in the Chairman's and President's posts to the broader question of whether individual benches have the courtrooms, staff and equipment to function once a member is actually appointed to sit on them.

The NCLT presidency itself fell vacant on 13 February 2026, when Justice Ramalingam Sudhakar retired, and the post was filled only through a sequence of acting appointments, first former district judge Deep Chandra Joshi and then Bachu Venkat Balaram Das, before Justice (Retd.) Anupinder Singh Grewal was notified as the regular President around the end of April 2026, more than two and a half months after the vacancy arose. One of the acting appointments was itself challenged by a sitting technical member, first before the Delhi High Court and then before the Central Administrative Tribunal, a reminder that even the interim arrangements made to keep the tribunal functioning can themselves become a source of litigation.

According to the Centre's own explanation to the Delhi High Court, the appointment could not proceed without a recommendation from the Chief Justice of India, which had been pending since December 2025. That explanation illustrates a structural point beyond this one appointment: filling even a single, high-profile vacancy at the NCLT depends on coordination between the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, the search-cum-selection machinery in place at the time, and the judiciary, and a bottleneck at any one of those points delays the whole chain. The National Tribunals Commission is meant to tighten exactly this kind of coordination, but it had not existed at all while this particular vacancy was running.

What vacancies do to an insolvency timeline

Delay at this level has a direct cost in insolvency matters specifically. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code builds in statutory timelines at almost every stage, from the fourteen days a tribunal is meant to take admitting or rejecting an application to the overall three hundred and thirty day outer limit for completing a corporate insolvency resolution process. These timelines assume a functioning bench available to hear the matter when it is listed. A bench sitting for half a day because it lacks a member, or a matter adjourned repeatedly because the technical member assigned to a case has not yet been replaced after a transfer or retirement, pushes every one of these statutory clocks further from what the Code itself contemplates, without anyone formally extending the deadline.

Creditors and resolution applicants dealing with this reality should build it into their own planning rather than treating a missed statutory timeline as a sign that something has gone wrong with their specific matter. A committee of creditors deciding whether to extend a resolution process, or a resolution applicant deciding how long to keep a bid open, does better to ask its counsel directly how the particular bench hearing the matter is currently staffed, rather than assuming every NCLT bench in the country is equally affected.

Operational creditors pursuing a Section 9 application face a related but distinct pressure. A respondent company facing genuine financial difficulty sometimes benefits, quite apart from the merits of any dispute over the debt, simply from a listing delay that gives it more time before a resolution process can formally begin. Counsel for an operational creditor should factor this incentive into how aggressively to pursue an expedited hearing, since a debtor with every reason to welcome delay is unlikely to raise the bench's own staffing shortage as a reason for urgency.

Pre-packaged insolvency resolution for eligible micro, small and medium enterprises offers one practical route around some of this pressure, since it is designed to run largely outside the tribunal's day-to-day case management, with the tribunal's own role confined mainly to approving or rejecting a plan the debtor and its creditors have already negotiated. A small or mid-sized corporate debtor eligible for this route, and a creditor dealing with such a debtor, should weigh it specifically against the ordinary corporate insolvency resolution process when a particular bench is known to be badly backlogged, since the pre-pack route needs far less tribunal time to reach a conclusion.

What vacancies do to ordinary company law matters

Insolvency cases attract most of the public attention, but the same bench shortages affect oppression and mismanagement petitions under Sections 241 and 242 of the Companies Act, 2013, applications for compounding of offences, scheme of arrangement approvals, and winding-up petitions that remain with the NCLT rather than the liquidator route under the Code. A family business dispute over control of a company, heard by the same understaffed bench hearing a queue of insolvency matters, can sit for months waiting for a date, not because the dispute itself is complicated, but because the bench simply has fewer sitting hours available than the docket requires.

Counsel appearing in these matters should keep a realistic record of adjournments caused by bench unavailability, separate from adjournments caused by the parties' own conduct, since that record becomes useful both in seeking expedited hearing from the tribunal itself and, in an appropriate case, in explaining delay to a higher forum reviewing the tribunal's conduct of the matter.

Scheme of arrangement approvals, which require the tribunal to convene and then sanction a scheme after creditor and shareholder meetings, add a further layer of timing risk specific to transactions. A merger or demerger that depends on tribunal sanction being obtained by a particular financial year end, or before a regulatory approval lapses, needs that dependency flagged to the deal team as early as possible, since the tribunal's own calendar, not the transaction timetable the parties have agreed among themselves, is what actually controls when the scheme can be heard and sanctioned.

Promoters defending an oppression and mismanagement petition, and minority shareholders bringing one, both have reason to want this kind of delay understood correctly. A promoter facing a petition that threatens control of the company has every incentive to let administrative delay run its course, while a minority shareholder seeking urgent interim relief against a specific act of oppression needs the tribunal's attention precisely when the bench may be least able to give it. Framing an interim application around the tribunal's own power to grant relief quickly, rather than around the full merits of the underlying petition, gives a minority shareholder the better chance of being heard despite a backlog that affects the final hearing date far more than it affects an urgent interim application.

NCLAT: the same problem one level up

An appeal from the NCLT goes to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal, which has faced its own version of the same staffing and appointment difficulties, including the delay in filling the Chairman's post that the Supreme Court's December 2025 direction specifically addressed alongside the NCLT presidency. A litigant who successfully clears a long wait at the NCLT stage, only to find the appeal queue at the NCLAT equally affected, should factor the appellate stage into overall case planning from the outset, rather than assuming that reaching a first-instance order resolves the timing problem.

Where a matter genuinely needs urgent appellate attention, for example a stay application against an NCLT order with immediate commercial consequences, counsel should be direct with the NCLAT registry about the urgency and should be prepared to move the jurisdictional High Court under Article 226, or in an appropriate case the Supreme Court, where the NCLAT's own capacity cannot accommodate the timeline the matter genuinely requires.

The IBC's own thirty-day limitation period for filing an appeal from an NCLT order, extendable by a further fifteen days only on sufficient cause, adds pressure of a different kind. A party does not get more time to file an appeal because the NCLAT bench that will eventually hear it is short-staffed, so the filing deadline has to be met on its own terms even while every later stage of the appeal may move slowly. Counsel should treat the filing deadline and the hearing timeline as two entirely separate problems, meeting the first strictly on schedule regardless of how the second is likely to unfold.

Will the 2026 Act actually end this, or is a sixth round coming

Every reform in this area since 2010 has followed roughly the same pattern: Parliament or the Executive adjusts the tenure, age or selection process for tribunal members, the Madras Bar Association or a similar petitioner challenges the change, and the Supreme Court strikes down some or all of it for compromising judicial independence, usually citing the same handful of earlier judgments each time. The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 is the latest entry in that sequence, and it arrives with early commentary already questioning whether it answers the Court's concerns fully or whether some of its provisions will face the same objections that sank its predecessors.

A litigant cannot wait for this question to be settled before deciding how to run an active matter, and should not assume that the mere existence of the National Tribunals Commission, as a body now created by statute, means every future appointment under it is immune from challenge. The safer assumption, based on the pattern of the last decade, is that the structure around tribunal appointments will keep evolving, and that a prudent litigant plans for continuing uncertainty rather than for a fixed and final settlement.

There is also a harder, less legal point buried in this history. Each round of litigation over tenure, age and selection has taken one to two years to resolve at the Supreme Court, and each legislative response has taken several more months to draft and enact after that. Measured against that cadence, a business with a live dispute before the NCLT today should expect the appointment framework itself to remain unsettled for at least the life of that dispute, which argues for building flexibility into litigation strategy rather than waiting for a stable system that experience suggests will arrive, if at all, only after the current matter has concluded.

Alternatives while the tribunal catches up: arbitration, settlement and mediation

Not every dispute that could go to the NCLT has to go there. A shareholders' agreement or a joint venture agreement with a properly drafted arbitration clause can take a dispute between shareholders out of the oppression and mismanagement route altogether, at least where the dispute is genuinely contractual rather than one that depends on the NCLT's statutory powers, such as winding up or specific company law remedies that only the tribunal can grant. Parties negotiating new shareholder or joint venture documentation in the current environment have good reason to think carefully about where disputes will actually be resolved, rather than assuming company law disputes always belong before the NCLT by default.

Mediation and negotiated settlement, including through the pre-litigation mediation mechanisms available under the Companies Act and more generally under the Mediation Act, 2023, offer a similar escape from bench-driven delay for disputes between family members or business partners who retain some interest in resolving the matter without a prolonged tribunal fight. Unlike a tribunal hearing, a mediation session can be scheduled within days rather than months, which is precisely the advantage a backlogged system cannot currently offer, even where the underlying dispute is exactly the kind the NCLT would otherwise be asked to decide. A family business considering litigation over control or succession should weigh the realistic timeline for a contested NCLT proceeding against the time a structured mediation might take, particularly where the relationship between the parties needs to continue in some form after the dispute is resolved.

For insolvency matters specifically, the Code's own emphasis on a committee of creditors reaching a commercial resolution, rather than litigating every point before the tribunal, remains available regardless of how the bench is staffed. Creditors who can agree a resolution plan or a one-time settlement without repeated tribunal intervention reduce their own exposure to the delay this article describes, even though they cannot avoid the tribunal's role in approving the final plan. Section 12A withdrawal, where the applicant and the corporate debtor settle after an insolvency application has already been admitted, follows the same logic: it still needs the tribunal's approval, but the substantive negotiation that actually resolves the dispute happens entirely outside the tribunal's own crowded calendar.

Common mistakes litigants and counsel make around tribunal delay

Treating every adjournment as routine, without asking whether the bench is short-staffed, is the most common mistake, because it means a litigant only discovers the real reason for delay well after the fact, at a point when raising it with the tribunal or a higher court carries less weight than it would have earlier in the matter.

Assuming that a matter's urgency alone will move it ahead of a backlog is a second mistake. A tribunal genuinely short of members cannot create sitting hours that do not exist, however urgent any individual matter may be, and a party is usually better served by a specific, well-supported application for expedited hearing than by repeated informal requests at each listing.

Challenging the validity of a particular member's appointment as a litigation tactic, rather than on a genuine and specific defect, is a third and riskier mistake. Given how closely the Supreme Court has already scrutinised the appointment process at a systemic level, a tribunal is unlikely to look favourably on a collateral challenge raised simply to delay a matter on its merits, and such a challenge can invite costs where it is found to lack substance. The protection the Supreme Court has consistently extended to appointments validly made before a statute was struck down means a party looking for a technical defect in a sitting member's appointment is usually starting from a weak position, not a strong one.

Failing to document delay caused by tribunal administration, as distinct from delay caused by the parties, is a fourth mistake that becomes apparent only when a party later needs to explain, before an appellate forum or in a limitation argument, why a matter took as long as it did to reach a hearing.

Assuming that the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 has already resolved the staffing shortage described in this article is a fifth mistake, and perhaps the easiest one to fall into, simply because a new law with a reassuring title has been enacted. A statute creating a commission is not the same thing as that commission functioning with the staff, budget and settled procedure needed to clear a backlog of vacancies that built up over several years, and a litigant who assumes otherwise risks being caught off guard by delays that persist well after the headlines about the new Act have faded. Clients, in particular, tend to read a reported legislative fix as meaning their own matter will now move faster, and counsel do their clients a service by correcting that impression early rather than letting it sit uncorrected until the next adjournment.

A practical checklist for a matter pending, or about to be filed, before the NCLT

Ask counsel to confirm, before filing, how the specific bench the matter will likely be listed before is currently staffed, rather than assuming every bench across the country is in the same position.

Build realistic internal timelines for insolvency matters that account for the possibility of bench-driven delay, and communicate that possibility clearly to clients, committees of creditors and resolution applicants before it becomes an unwelcome surprise partway through an already difficult process.

Keep a contemporaneous record of adjournments attributable to bench unavailability, distinct from adjournments sought by either party, since this record is useful both for an expedited hearing application made to the tribunal itself and for any later argument about delay raised before an appellate forum.

Where an appeal is likely, plan for the NCLAT stage from the outset rather than only after a first-instance order is passed, and identify in advance whether an urgent matter might need to go to the jurisdictional High Court or the Supreme Court if the appellate tribunal's own capacity cannot meet the timeline.

Track whether the National Tribunals Commission is actually functioning, rather than assuming its creation by statute in August 2026 means appointments are already running on the new system, since the gap between a law being enacted and a body being made operational has, on this subject, repeatedly run to months.

Revisit dispute resolution clauses in new shareholder agreements, joint venture documents and family settlements being drafted now, and consider, with counsel, whether arbitration or a structured mediation mechanism should sit alongside or instead of an NCLT-dependent remedy for disputes that do not strictly require the tribunal's statutory powers.

Where a matter is genuinely urgent and the relevant bench cannot accommodate it, discuss with counsel at an early stage whether an application before the jurisdictional High Court, or in an appropriate case the Supreme Court, is realistically available, rather than treating the NCLT or NCLAT as the only possible forum regardless of how pressing the relief sought may be.

Review this list again whenever a significant development is reported, whether a new notification under the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026, a fresh order in the suo motu vacancy matter, or a further round of appointments, since the practical position for litigants is likely to keep shifting for some time after this article is published.

The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 and the National Tribunals Commission it creates are a genuine attempt to end a cycle of litigation that has run, in one form or another, since at least 2020. Whether the attempt succeeds is not yet something a litigant can rely on, given how many earlier versions of the same reform were struck down, and given how long the gap has repeatedly run between a legal change being announced and a tribunal actually being adequately staffed on the ground.

A party with a matter before the NCLT or NCLAT today manages this uncertainty best by treating bench staffing as a factor in its own case strategy, not as background noise that will resolve itself, and by keeping the kind of contemporaneous record that makes a delay-related argument credible if it is ever needed before a higher forum.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did the Supreme Court strike down the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 a second time?

In Madras Bar Association v Union of India, 2025 INSC 1330 (19 November 2025), the Court held that the 2021 Act's provisions on tenure and appointment simply reproduced provisions already held unconstitutional in earlier Madras Bar Association rulings of 2020 and 2021, which it treated as an impermissible attempt to override binding precedent.

What is the National Tribunals Commission?

It is a standing body created by the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026, which received presidential assent on 13 August 2026, responsible for the appointment process, performance oversight and service conditions of members across sixteen tribunals, including the NCLT and NCLAT.

Did the government meet the Supreme Court's deadline to set up the Commission?

No. The Court gave the Union four months from its 19 November 2025 judgment, a deadline that fell in March 2026. The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 was not enacted until August 2026, roughly five months after that deadline had passed.

Why have NCLT hearings been taking longer than expected?

The NCLT has faced member shortages serious enough that the NCLT Bar Association told the Supreme Court at least eighteen benches were sitting only for half days. A suo motu Supreme Court matter on NCLT vacancies and infrastructure has been running since May 2026, alongside the litigation over how tribunal members are appointed.

Was the NCLT without a president for a period in 2026?

Yes. The post fell vacant on 13 February 2026 when Justice Ramalingam Sudhakar retired, and was filled through two acting appointments before Justice (Retd.) Anupinder Singh Grewal was appointed as the regular President around the end of April 2026.

Does a short-staffed bench extend the statutory timelines under the IBC?

No, not formally. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code's timelines, such as the outer limit for completing a corporate insolvency resolution process, remain as written even when a bench sits only part of the time it would need to meet them, so delay caused by staffing is not automatically treated as extending those deadlines.

Can a litigant challenge an NCLT order on the ground that a tribunal member's appointment was invalid?

This is possible in principle where there is a genuine, specific defect in an appointment, but tribunals and appellate courts are likely to view a challenge raised mainly to delay a matter on its merits unfavourably, particularly given how closely the appointment process has already been scrutinised at a systemic level.
