Alternative Investment Funds in India: A Working Guide for HNI and NRI Investors

AIFs have become the default route for serious private capital in India, Category I, II and III, each taxed differently, each open to NRIs on different terms, and almost none of this is explained to an investor before the subscription papers land on their desk.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 16 November 2026 · Law stated as on 15 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An HNI who has exhausted the obvious mutual fund and portfolio management routes, or an NRI being pitched a private credit or real estate fund by a relationship manager, is usually handed a private placement memorandum running to a hundred pages and told the fund is SEBI-registered, as if that single fact answers every question. It answers very few of them. This piece sets out how Alternative Investment Funds actually work under Indian law, what each category means for tax and lock-in, and what an NRI investor specifically needs to check before signing.

Key points

  • An AIF is a privately pooled investment vehicle registered with SEBI under the SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012, falling into Category I, II or III depending on strategy.
  • The general minimum commitment is Rs 1 crore per investor across all three categories, though the fund's own scheme documents can set a higher figure.
  • Category I (venture capital, SME, infrastructure, social impact and similar funds) and Category II (private equity, real estate, debt and other funds not in Category I or III) get statutory tax pass-through under Section 115UB of the Income Tax Act.
  • Category III funds, which can use leverage and complex trading strategies, are generally taxed at the fund level rather than passed through to investors, with investors receiving post-tax distributions.
  • NRIs can invest in Indian AIFs, generally through NRE or NRO accounts and subject to FEMA compliance, at the same Rs 1 crore ticket size as resident investors; distributions into an NRO account are subject to the usual FEMA repatriation limits.
  • GIFT City (IFSC) funds offer NRIs and foreign investors an alternative structure with their own regulatory and repatriation framework, distinct from a mainland AIF.
  • SEBI has been actively amending the AIF framework, including changes to angel fund investor thresholds in 2025, so figures that were accurate two years ago should be rechecked against the current Regulations before relying on them for a specific investment.

Why AIFs have displaced the older private investment routes

A decade ago, an Indian HNI looking for returns beyond listed equity and bank fixed deposits had limited formal options: direct unlisted investments, portfolio management services, or informal partnerships with promoters. The growth of the Alternative Investment Fund as a regulated category has given that same investor a pooled, SEBI-registered vehicle for private equity, real estate, structured credit, and hedge-fund style strategies, run by a professional manager under a documented mandate rather than a handshake.

For an NRI, AIFs solve a different problem. Direct investment in unlisted Indian companies or real estate from abroad involves its own FEMA compliance, valuation requirements and reporting, often asset by asset. Investing through an AIF puts a regulated manager between the NRI and each underlying deal, with the FEMA and reporting questions handled once, at the fund level and at the point of subscription, rather than repeated for every portfolio company or property the fund later invests in.

None of this makes an AIF a safe or simple product. It is a privately placed, generally illiquid, long-horizon commitment, and the category label tells an investor more about the regulatory box the fund sits in than about whether the specific fund is well run. The rest of this piece works through what that regulatory box actually requires, so an investor can ask the right questions of the specific fund in front of them.

The SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012 and the three categories

An AIF is defined and regulated under the SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012, which require a fund that pools money from investors (other than family members of the sponsor and beyond the limits set for public offers) to register with SEBI in one of three categories before it can raise or deploy capital. The categorisation exists because SEBI treats the categories as carrying different levels of risk and systemic relevance, and regulates them with correspondingly different conditions on leverage, investment concentration and governance.

Category I covers funds SEBI considers to have positive spillover effects on the economy, for which the government, SEBI, or other regulators may consider providing incentives: venture capital funds, SME funds, social venture funds, infrastructure funds, and angel funds fall here. Category II is, in practice, the largest bucket by assets under management and is defined residually as funds that do not fall under Category I or III and do not undertake leverage except for meeting day-to-day operational requirements: private equity funds, real estate funds, debt funds and most other closed-ended private pools sit in this category. Category III covers funds that employ diverse or complex trading strategies and may employ leverage, including through investment in listed or unlisted derivatives, and this is where most domestic hedge-fund style strategies are registered.

The category a fund registers under is not a marketing choice; it determines which conditions in the Regulations apply to it, including, as discussed below, how the fund and its investors are taxed. A fund manager pitching a strategy should be able to state the category plainly and explain why that strategy fits it, and an investor should treat hesitation on that specific question as a warning sign.

Category I: the funds SEBI wants to encourage

Category I funds are built around sectors or stages of business that the regulatory framework treats as deserving of a lighter or more supportive touch: early-stage ventures, small and medium enterprises, infrastructure projects, and social enterprises. Venture capital funds under this category typically invest in unlisted startups at various stages; infrastructure funds invest in infrastructure-sector projects and companies; social venture funds target enterprises with a social or environmental objective alongside financial return.

Angel funds are a specific sub-category within Category I, built for pooled early-stage investment by angel investors into startups, and SEBI has amended the framework governing angel funds more than once in recent years, including changes in 2025 affecting who can invest in them and at what per-company ticket size. Because this sub-category has been actively reworked, an investor looking specifically at an angel fund should confirm the current eligibility and investment-size rules against SEBI's current regulations rather than rely on older commentary, including the general figures discussed later in this piece for mainstream AIFs, which do not necessarily carry over unchanged to angel funds.

For an HNI or family office, Category I exposure usually means a long holding period, often seven to ten years for a venture or infrastructure strategy, illiquidity until the fund begins exiting portfolio companies, and a return profile that depends heavily on a small number of successful outcomes offsetting a larger number of write-downs. This is not a substitute for a fixed-income allocation; it is a different kind of risk entirely.

Category II: the workhorse category

Category II is defined by what it is not: it is not Category I, and it does not take on leverage beyond what is needed for day-to-day operations. In practice, this residual definition has become the home for the bulk of India's private equity funds, real estate funds, and private credit or structured debt funds, precisely because those strategies do not fit the specific sectoral mandates of Category I and do not rely on the leveraged, trading-oriented strategies that define Category III.

A private equity fund buying minority or controlling stakes in unlisted or pre-IPO companies, a real estate fund financing residential or commercial development, and a performing-credit fund lending to mid-market businesses are all typically structured as Category II AIFs. The common thread across these strategies is a closed-ended structure with a fixed term, capital called down over time rather than invested in one tranche, and returns realised through exits, asset sales or loan repayments rather than through daily liquidity.

This is also the category where an HNI or NRI investor is most likely to encounter a private credit fund marketed as offering a fixed or near-fixed return, often in the mid-teens annually. That marketing framing understates the point that the return is a function of the underlying borrower's creditworthiness and the fund's recovery mechanics if a borrower defaults, not a guaranteed coupon, and the scheme documents, not the pitch deck, are where that risk is actually described.

Category III: leverage, complexity, and a different tax treatment entirely

Category III AIFs are permitted to use strategies that Category I and II funds are not: leverage, derivatives, and trading approaches that can be long-short, event-driven, or systematic rather than simply buy-and-hold. This category is where India's domestic hedge-fund industry is housed, and the strategies on offer range from long-only equity funds with modest leverage to highly active derivative-based strategies.

The leverage and trading flexibility come with a correspondingly different tax position, discussed in more detail in the next section, and also with closer regulatory attention to risk limits, since a fund that can use derivatives and borrowing carries a different systemic profile than a closed-ended private equity vehicle. Category III funds can be open-ended or close-ended, unlike the typically closed-ended structure of most Category I and II funds, which also changes the liquidity an investor can expect.

An investor evaluating a Category III fund should ask specifically about the fund's use of leverage, its drawdown history during past market stress, and how redemptions (for an open-ended structure) are actually processed when markets move quickly, because the flexibility that produces attractive returns in calm markets is the same flexibility that can produce sharp losses or gated redemptions in stressed ones.

The Rs 1 crore ticket, and who is exempt from it

Across all three categories, the SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012 set a general minimum investment amount of Rs 1 crore per investor, a figure deliberately set high enough that AIFs are treated as a sophisticated-investor product rather than a retail one. A lower threshold has generally applied to employees and directors of the fund manager investing in the fund's own schemes, reflecting the different information position of an insider relative to an outside investor, though an investor relying on a specific concessional figure for this category should confirm it against the current Regulations, since SEBI has periodically reviewed these thresholds.

SEBI has also, more recently, signalled an openness to revisiting the headline Rs 1 crore figure itself, on the basis that it was fixed in 2012 and has not moved since, while the broader investor base and market context have changed considerably. As of the date of this piece, the Rs 1 crore figure remains the operative general threshold, and any investor should check the current position directly with the fund or against SEBI's latest circulars before assuming either that figure or any lower one quoted informally.

Separately, each AIF scheme is required to have a minimum corpus, meaning the fund as a whole must raise a baseline amount of committed capital before it can proceed, which is a different number from the per-investor minimum and protects against a fund being registered and then failing to reach viable scale.

Taxation: pass-through for Category I and II, fund-level for Category III

Section 115UB of the Income Tax Act, 1961 gives Category I and Category II AIFs a pass-through tax treatment: income earned by the fund (other than business income, which is taxed at the fund level) is treated as if it had accrued directly to the investor, in the same proportion and character as it arose to the fund, and is taxed in the investor's hands rather than at the fund level. The Income Tax Department's own guidance describes this as pass-through income, and it means an investor's tax liability on AIF income depends on the investor's own tax status and the character of the income (capital gains, interest, dividend, and so on), not on a flat fund-level rate.

Category III AIFs sit outside this statutory pass-through regime. In most fund structures, a Category III AIF is taxed at the fund level on its income, under the general provisions of the Income Tax Act applicable to the vehicle (commonly a trust), and investors receive distributions after that tax has already been paid, without being taxed again in India on the same income. This produces a materially different net-of-tax comparison between a Category II private credit fund (pass-through, taxed at the investor's own rate) and a Category III fund pursuing a similar gross return (taxed at the fund level, at a rate that may be higher or lower than the specific investor's own marginal rate, depending on who the investor is).

This distinction is not a minor technical footnote; it changes the actual after-tax return two funds in different categories can deliver even where their headline gross returns look similar, and it is one of the first questions an HNI's own tax adviser, not the fund's distributor, should model before a significant commitment.

Business income earned by a Category I or II AIF is a specific exception within the pass-through structure: it is taxed at the fund level rather than passed through, and is exempt in the hands of the unit holder under a corresponding exemption in the Income Tax Act, so that the same income is not taxed twice. An investor comparing funds should ask whether the strategy is likely to generate business income (as some real estate and structured finance strategies can) rather than assume every Category II fund produces the same character of income.

The practical effect of pass-through treatment is that an investor's annual tax filing becomes more involved than it would be for a mutual fund unit, because the fund issues a statement breaking the investor's share of income down by character, capital gains, interest, dividend, business income and so on, and each category is then taxed according to the rules that would apply had the investor earned that income directly. A salaried HNI investor used to a single consolidated capital gains statement from a mutual fund should budget for a more detailed annual reconciliation exercise with their chartered accountant once they hold a Category I or II AIF unit, rather than expect the same simplicity.

Can an NRI invest, and how does the money move?

NRIs are permitted to invest in Indian AIFs, generally subject to the same Rs 1 crore minimum ticket that applies to resident investors, with no separate concessional threshold simply because the investor happens to be an NRI. Investment is typically funded from an NRE or NRO account, and the fund's subscription documents will usually require FEMA-related declarations confirming the source of funds and the investor's status.

Distributions back to the investor generally flow into the account the subscription was funded from, which matters for an NRI funding from an NRO account, because repatriation out of India from an NRO account is subject to the standard FEMA ceiling, commonly understood to allow remittance of up to USD 1 million in a financial year (net of applicable taxes) on production of the required certifications, rather than being freely and fully repatriable without limit. An NRI funding the same investment from an NRE account, by contrast, generally retains fuller repatriability, which makes the choice of funding account a genuine planning decision, not a formality.

Tax withholding on distributions is a further practical point an NRI investor should raise before subscribing rather than after the first distribution lands short of expectation. Because a Category I or II AIF's income is treated as accruing directly to the investor under the pass-through regime, the fund is generally required to withhold tax at source on the portion of income attributable to an NRI investor, and the applicable withholding rate for a non-resident can differ, and in some cases run higher, than the rate that would apply to an equivalent resident investor, before accounting for any relief available under India's tax treaty with the investor's country of residence. An NRI should ask the fund administrator directly what withholding rate will be applied to their specific unit holding and income type, and should discuss with their own tax adviser whether a lower treaty rate can be claimed and, if so, what documentation the fund needs to apply it.

A separate 2024 change affecting fund managers deserves a mention for NRI and OCI investors specifically: where the controlling shareholder or partner of an AIF's investment manager or sponsor is an OCI, that manager or sponsor is now treated as a domestic entity for this purpose, which keeps the fund from being automatically pushed into foreign-investment sectoral caps purely because its manager happens to be OCI-controlled. This is a fund-structuring point rather than an investor-facing one, but it affects which funds with OCI-linked managers remain straightforwardly open to resident and NRI investors alike.

GIFT City funds as an alternative structure

Funds set up in India's International Financial Services Centre at GIFT City operate under a separate regulatory and tax framework from mainland SEBI-registered AIFs, and are commonly used specifically to attract NRI and foreign investor capital, with features such as freely repatriable structures and a tax regime designed around that cross-border investor base. For an NRI comparing a mainland AIF with a GIFT City fund pursuing a broadly similar strategy, the practical differences, in currency of subscription, repatriation mechanics, and the specific tax treatment applicable to a non-resident investor, can matter as much as the underlying investment strategy itself.

This piece does not attempt a full comparison of mainland AIF and GIFT City fund taxation, which depends on the investor's own residency and tax position and deserves dedicated advice; the point worth taking away here is that an NRI evaluating a private fund opportunity should ask, as a first question, whether the specific vehicle on offer is a mainland AIF or a GIFT City fund, because the answer changes several of the other questions that follow.

Due diligence: what the scheme documents should actually show

The private placement memorandum is the starting document, but it is written by the fund's own counsel to describe the fund in the most favourable accurate light the law permits, and an investor's own review should go further. Confirm the fund's SEBI registration number and category directly against SEBI's own records, not merely against the fund's representation in the memorandum. Read the fee structure in full, distinguishing a management fee charged on committed capital from one charged on invested capital, and understand how carried interest or performance fees are calculated and at what hurdle rate.

Ask specifically about the fund's drawdown mechanics (how and when capital calls are made), the consequences of a default on a capital call, the term of the fund and any extension provisions, and the exit or redemption mechanics appropriate to the category (liquidity windows for an open-ended Category III fund, or the expected exit timeline for a closed-ended Category I or II fund). Side letters, under which some investors negotiate better terms than the standard scheme documents provide, are common in larger AIFs, and an investor committing a significant sum should ask whether side letters exist for other investors and, where the fund permits, seek most-favoured-investor treatment.

Finally, check the manager's own track record on a fund-by-fund basis, not on a blended or marketing-selected basis, and ask how prior funds in the same strategy performed net of fees, including any funds that underperformed or wound down early. A manager's willingness to discuss a disappointing prior fund candidly is itself useful diligence information.

Ask also to see the fund's valuation policy for illiquid holdings, since this is the document that decides how a Category II real estate or private credit fund marks an unlisted asset between formal exit events, and it is often the single biggest source of dispute between investors and managers when a fund's reported NAV diverges sharply from what an eventual exit actually realises. A valuation policy that relies heavily on the manager's own internal judgment, with limited independent third-party input, deserves more scrutiny than one anchored to regular independent valuations, particularly for an investor who may need to rely on reported NAV for their own tax or reporting purposes in the interim.

When an AIF investment turns into a dispute

Most AIF investor grievances concern delayed distributions, disputed valuations of illiquid portfolio holdings, or disagreement over whether a manager has acted within the strategy and limits described in the scheme documents. SEBI has a regulatory and grievance-redressal role over registered AIFs and their managers, and persistent or serious non-compliance can attract SEBI action against the manager, but an individual investor's claim for money or specific performance against the fund typically has to be pursued separately, under the fund's own constitutional documents.

AIF scheme documents commonly include an arbitration clause governing disputes between the investor and the fund or its trustee, which means an aggrieved investor's practical route is often arbitration rather than a civil suit, and the specific clause in that investor's own subscription agreement, not a general assumption about Indian civil procedure, governs where and how that dispute is actually resolved.

An HNI or NRI investor negotiating a significant commitment should read the dispute-resolution clause with the same attention given to the fee clause, because the seat of arbitration, the institutional rules chosen, and any jurisdiction clause in the subscription agreement will determine, years later if a dispute arises, where and under what procedure that dispute actually gets resolved.

Co-investment rights and feeder structures

Larger HNI investors and family offices frequently negotiate co-investment rights alongside their commitment to the main fund, meaning the right to invest directly, alongside the AIF, in a specific portfolio deal that the fund has sourced, typically on a no-fee or reduced-fee basis compared to the main fund's economics. SEBI has, in recent years, formalised a framework for co-investment vehicles operating alongside Category I and II AIFs, which gives this practice a clearer regulatory structure than the informal side arrangements that were more common earlier.

A co-investment right is attractive because it lets a sophisticated investor increase exposure to a specific deal they have independently diligenced, without paying a second layer of management fee and carry on that slice of capital. It is also a genuine additional diligence burden, because the investor is now assessing a single company or asset directly, not relying on the fund manager's portfolio-level risk management, and an HNI who wants this right should budget the time and advice needed to actually use it well rather than negotiate for it as a status marker and then default to the manager's judgment regardless.

Feeder structures, where a domestic or offshore vehicle pools several smaller investors' commitments into a single line item in the underlying AIF, are common for NRI and foreign investors who want exposure below the fund's direct minimum ticket, or who want the feeder's own jurisdiction and currency conventions rather than dealing with the Indian AIF directly. A feeder adds a layer of documents, and sometimes a layer of fees, between the investor and the underlying fund, and an investor using this route should read the feeder's own constitutional documents as carefully as the underlying AIF's, rather than assume the feeder simply passes through the AIF's terms unchanged.

A practical checklist before committing

Before signing a subscription agreement, confirm five things in writing, not merely as a verbal assurance from the relationship manager presenting the fund: the fund's exact SEBI registration category and number, checked independently; the all-in fee structure across management fee, carried interest and any transaction or monitoring fees charged to portfolio companies; the fund's term, including extension provisions and what happens if the manager wants to extend beyond the stated term; the tax treatment applicable to the specific investor given their residency status, cross-checked with their own tax adviser rather than the fund's marketing material; and the dispute-resolution clause governing the investor's own subscription agreement.

For an NRI, add two further checks specific to cross-border status: which account the investment will be funded from and what that implies for repatriation of distributions, and whether a GIFT City or other offshore-facing structure offered for a comparable strategy might serve the same investment thesis on better repatriation or tax terms. Neither of these is a reason to avoid a good mainland AIF opportunity, but both deserve a specific answer before capital moves, not a general assurance that 'NRIs invest in this fund all the time.'

Finally, treat the private placement memorandum as the floor of the diligence exercise, not the ceiling. The memorandum discloses what the Regulations require it to disclose; it does not substitute for independent legal and tax review of the specific subscription agreement an HNI or NRI investor is actually being asked to sign, and the cost of that review is modest against the size of commitment the Rs 1 crore threshold already implies.

A single SEBI registration certificate covers three genuinely different products. Category I, II and III differ in strategy, liquidity, leverage, and, critically, in how the income is taxed in the investor's hands, and an HNI or NRI investor who treats 'SEBI-registered AIF' as sufficient due diligence is skipping the questions that actually determine the after-tax, after-fee return.

For an NRI specifically, the additional questions are the funding account, the repatriation path for distributions, and whether a GIFT City structure might serve the same strategy on better cross-border terms. None of these questions is difficult to ask. The difficulty is remembering to ask them before signing, rather than after the first capital call.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the minimum amount needed to invest in an AIF in India?

The general rule under the SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012 sets a minimum investment of Rs 1 crore per investor across Category I, II and III funds. A lower threshold has generally applied to employees and directors of the fund manager, though specific figures and any sub-category exceptions, such as for angel funds, should be checked against the current Regulations before relying on them.

Can an NRI invest in an Indian Alternative Investment Fund?

Yes. NRIs can invest in Indian AIFs, typically through an NRE or NRO account and subject to FEMA compliance, generally at the same Rs 1 crore minimum ticket that applies to resident investors. There is no separate lower threshold simply by virtue of being an NRI.

Which AIF category gives the best tax treatment to investors?

It depends on the investor's own tax position, not on one category being universally better. Category I and II AIFs get statutory pass-through treatment under Section 115UB of the Income Tax Act, so income is taxed in the investor's own hands at their own applicable rate. Category III AIFs are generally taxed at the fund level instead, which can be more or less favourable than pass-through depending on the specific investor's tax bracket and the character of income involved.

If I invest in an AIF from my NRO account, can I bring the returns back out of India freely?

Not without limit. Distributions paid into an NRO account are subject to the standard FEMA repatriation ceiling, commonly understood to permit remittance up to USD 1 million in a financial year net of applicable taxes, on production of the required certifications. Funding the investment from an NRE account instead generally preserves fuller repatriability, which is worth considering before choosing the funding account.

What is the difference between a Category I, Category II and Category III AIF?

Category I covers funds in sectors SEBI wants to encourage, such as venture capital, SME, infrastructure, social venture and angel funds. Category II is a residual category covering private equity, real estate, debt and similar funds that do not use leverage beyond operational needs. Category III covers funds that may use leverage and complex or diverse trading strategies, including through derivatives, and is where most domestic hedge-fund style strategies are registered.

Is a GIFT City fund the same as a regular Indian AIF?

No. A GIFT City (IFSC) fund operates under a separate regulatory and tax framework designed partly to attract NRI and foreign investor capital, with its own rules on repatriation and taxation, distinct from a mainland SEBI-registered AIF. An NRI should ask specifically which structure a fund being offered to them actually is.

What happens if an AIF manager mismanages the fund or delays distributions?

SEBI has a regulatory oversight role over AIF managers and can act against serious or persistent non-compliance, but an individual investor's own claim for money or specific relief is usually pursued separately under the fund's constitutional documents, often through an arbitration clause in the subscription agreement rather than through a civil suit.

Do I have to pay tax in India on AIF income if I already pay tax on it in my country of residence?

That depends on India's tax treaty, if any, with your country of residence, and on how that treaty allocates taxing rights over the specific category of income involved. Pass-through AIF income is taxed in India in the investor's hands first; relief against double taxation, where available, is then claimed in the country of residence or through treaty provisions, and an NRI should get this checked against the specific treaty rather than assume either country automatically defers to the other.
