Family Court or Civil Court? Where a Property Dispute Between In-Laws Actually Belongs

A Delhi High Court ruling in May 2026 confirms that a daughter-in-law's property fight with her in-laws is not automatically a matrimonial matter, however much it looks like one from inside the family

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 26 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A suit over who owns a house, filed by a daughter-in-law against her in-laws or by in-laws against a daughter-in-law, feels like a family dispute to everyone involved in it. The Delhi High Court held in May 2026 that feeling does not decide which court gets to hear it, and the answer turns on a test the Family Courts Act has carried since 1984.

Key points

  • The Family Courts Act, 1984 gives Family Courts exclusive jurisdiction only over the specific categories of suits and proceedings listed in Section 7, which centre on matrimonial relief, maintenance, custody and guardianship, and, under an Explanation to Section 7(1), a suit or proceeding 'between the parties to a marriage' with respect to their property.
  • In Anjali Jayant @ Laxmi v. Kusum Singh & Anr., decided on 20 May 2026, the Delhi High Court held that a property dispute between a daughter-in-law and her parents-in-law is not within the Family Court's exclusive jurisdiction, because it is not a dispute between the parties to the marriage itself.
  • The test the Court applied, drawn from the Division Bench ruling in Geeta Anand, asks what the 'foundation' of the proceeding actually is: a claim over immovable property is not treated as integral to the matrimonial relationship merely because the parties happen to be connected by marriage, and such a claim can exist against anyone, a daughter-in-law included.
  • The Court rejected the argument that calling a house a 'shared household' converts an ownership dispute into a matrimonial one; shared household status affects a wife's right of residence under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, which is a separate question from who owns the property.
  • The practical consequence is that a civil court, not the Family Court, is usually the right forum for a suit about title, possession or partition of property even where the parties are connected through a marriage, unless the claim itself is substantively about rights arising from the marriage.
  • Filing in the wrong forum is not a mere technicality. A suit wrongly filed in, or wrongly resisted as belonging to, the Family Court can be delayed for months while an Order VII Rule 11 application, a transfer application, or a supervisory petition under Article 227 is argued and decided, time that could otherwise go toward resolving the actual dispute.
  • This question sits alongside, but is distinct from, a daughter-in-law's separate statutory right of residence in a shared household, which several other 2026 Delhi High Court rulings have continued to define narrowly where the property is exclusively owned by the in-laws and alternative accommodation is available.

Why this question keeps coming up in Delhi

A property dispute inside a family, between a daughter-in-law and her husband's parents, between siblings-in-law, or between a wife and her in-laws after a marriage has broken down, almost always gets framed by the parties themselves as a matrimonial problem. The relationship that put the parties in the same house in the first place was a marriage, the dispute often surfaces at the same time as, or soon after, a marital breakdown, and the emotional weight of the fight makes it feel inseparable from the marriage itself.

Indian procedural law does not organise its courts around how a dispute feels. It organises them around defined categories of subject matter, and the Family Courts Act, 1984, which created a specialised forum for certain kinds of family disputes, drew its jurisdiction narrowly rather than broadly. Where a dispute falls outside the specific categories the Act lists, the ordinary civil court retains jurisdiction over it, however closely the dispute is tied up with a marriage in the minds of the people involved.

The Delhi High Court has had to revisit this boundary repeatedly in 2026, generating a small but consistent line of rulings on where exactly a property dispute involving in-laws should be filed, and on a related but separate question, how far a daughter-in-law's right to live in a shared household extends against property her in-laws exclusively own. Anjali Jayant @ Laxmi v. Kusum Singh & Anr. is the clearest statement of the forum question specifically.

Section 7 of the Family Courts Act: a deliberately narrow list

Section 7(1) of the Family Courts Act, 1984 gives a Family Court the jurisdiction exercisable by a district court or any subordinate civil court under specified categories of suits and proceedings, which include a suit or proceeding between the parties to a marriage for a decree of restitution of conjugal rights, judicial separation, nullity, divorce, declaration about marital status, or a decree for maintenance between the parties to a marriage, along with proceedings for custody, guardianship, or maintenance of children. Section 7(2) separately confers jurisdiction over maintenance claims under Chapter IX of the Code of Criminal Procedure (now its successor under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita) and over guardianship and custody matters under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890.

The provision most relevant to a property fight is the Explanation to Section 7(1)(a), which brings within the Family Court's jurisdiction a suit or proceeding 'between the parties to a marriage with respect to the property of the parties or of either of them.' The operative phrase, read narrowly by the courts, is 'between the parties to a marriage'. On its face, this covers a property dispute between a husband and wife, not a property dispute in which one of the parties is a daughter-in-law and the other is her parents-in-law, her sister-in-law, or some other relative who was never married to her.

Section 8 of the Act then bars an ordinary civil court from entertaining a suit or proceeding of a kind referred to in Section 7, once a Family Court has been established for the area, making the Family Court's jurisdiction over those specific categories exclusive rather than merely concurrent. This exclusivity is precisely why the forum question matters: if a suit genuinely falls within Section 7, a civil court has no jurisdiction to hear it at all, and if it does not, the Family Court has none, so getting the characterisation wrong does not simply inconvenience the parties, it can mean the suit was filed before a court with no power to decide it.

The 'foundation of the proceeding' test

Delhi High Court authority on this question, including the Division Bench ruling in Geeta Anand that the Court in Anjali Jayant relied on, has settled on asking what the foundation of the proceeding actually is, rather than looking only at who the parties happen to be or what relationship connects them. A dispute is treated as falling within the Family Court's property jurisdiction only where the claim is genuinely founded on, and inseparable from, the marital relationship between the parties to that marriage, such as a claim to stridhan, to jointly accumulated matrimonial assets, or to maintenance calculated by reference to the marriage.

A claim to title or ownership of specific immovable property, by contrast, is generally treated as a free-standing property right that exists independently of the marriage and that could, in principle, be asserted against or by anyone, a stranger to the marriage included. The test asks whether the marriage is the source of the right being claimed, or merely the reason the parties happen to be connected and litigating against each other at all. Where the property right would exist, in the same form, even if the marriage had never taken place, which is normally true of a straightforward ownership or title dispute, the marriage is incidental to the claim rather than its foundation.

This foundation test explains why a suit between spouses over who owns the matrimonial home can sit inside Section 7's property jurisdiction, while a structurally similar-looking suit between a daughter-in-law and her parents-in-law over the same house typically does not. The spouses' claim can plausibly be said to arise from and be shaped by the marriage between them; the daughter-in-law's claim against her parents-in-law is a claim against people she was never married to, resting on ordinary principles of property law, title, gift, or inheritance, that would apply to the dispute in exactly the same way whether or not the daughter-in-law had ever married into the family at all.

Anjali Jayant @ Laxmi v. Kusum Singh & Anr.: the May 2026 ruling

The Delhi High Court decided Anjali Jayant @ Laxmi v. Kusum Singh & Anr. on 20 May 2026, in a petition filed under Article 227 of the Constitution challenging a trial court order of 26 July 2023. The daughter-in-law had applied to the civil court under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure to have her mother-in-law's property suit rejected, or in the alternative transferred to the Family Court, arguing that the dispute fell within Section 7(1)(d) and Section 8 of the Family Courts Act because, in her submission, the cause of action arose out of the marital relationship between the parties.

The trial court rejected both the Order VII Rule 11 application and the request to transfer the suit to the Family Court, and the daughter-in-law challenged that order before the High Court under its supervisory jurisdiction. Justice Amit Sharma dismissed the petition, holding that the suit over the self-acquired property remained properly before the civil court. The judgment relied on the Division Bench's foundation-of-the-proceeding test from Geeta Anand, holding that a claim over immovable property is not integral to the matrimonial relationship simply because the litigants are connected through a marriage, since such a claim can be brought against, or by, anyone, including a daughter-in-law, without reference to the marriage at all.

The daughter-in-law's further argument, that the property's status as a shared household under domestic violence law made it a matrimonial matter, was also rejected. The Court drew a clear line between a right of residence in a shared household, which is a statutory protection under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 and does not depend on ownership, and a claim to title or ownership of the property itself, which is governed by ordinary property law and does not become a matrimonial question merely because the same house happens to also be a shared household for other purposes. The neutral citation recorded for the decision, per contemporaneous reporting, is 2026:DHC:4527.

Shared household is a different question, decided by different rules

The shared household argument rejected in Anjali Jayant deserves its own explanation, because it is one of the most common ways a property dispute involving a daughter-in-law gets reframed as a matrimonial issue, and because the law in this specific area has itself been actively developing through 2026. Section 2(s) of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 defines a shared household broadly enough to include a household in which the aggrieved woman lives, or has lived, in a domestic relationship, whether or not she or her husband has any right, title or interest in it. The Supreme Court settled some years ago that this definition is not limited to property the husband owns, and extends to a household owned by his relatives where the woman has lived in a domestic relationship.

What a shared household gives a woman is a statutory right of residence, enforceable through a protection order or residence order under the Act, not an ownership interest in the property. Other Delhi High Court rulings reported around the same period as Anjali Jayant have continued to draw this line carefully: one May 2026 ruling held that a daughter-in-law's shared-household claim did not extend to insisting on residence in one specific property her in-laws exclusively owned, particularly once alternative accommodation was offered, and a separate ruling upheld eviction of a daughter-in-law from her in-laws' property to protect the in-laws' own right to peaceful residence, again treating the shared-household and ownership questions as analytically distinct.

Taken together, this body of 2026 Delhi High Court authority treats a daughter-in-law's position in her in-laws' property as resting on two genuinely separate legal foundations that happen to arise from the same set of facts: a possibly strong right of residence under domestic violence law, which does not require her to own anything, and, usually, no ownership claim at all unless she can independently establish title through gift, inheritance, purchase or some other recognised legal route. Conflating the two, as the daughter-in-law's argument in Anjali Jayant tried to do, does not strengthen either claim; it simply invites a court to separate them again, as happened there.

Choosing the right forum before filing, not after

A family considering litigation over property that touches an in-law relationship should work through the foundation test before deciding where to file, rather than filing wherever seems procedurally convenient and litigating the jurisdiction question later. The central question is simple to state even where it is not always simple to answer on particular facts: does the claim being made rest on rights that exist only because of the marriage, such as a share of matrimonial assets or maintenance calculated against the marriage, or does it rest on rights that would exist in exactly the same form regardless of whether any marriage had ever taken place, such as ownership, a gift, an inheritance, or a contractual entitlement.

Claims of the first kind belong in the Family Court, if they are between the parties to the marriage itself, and a civil court has no jurisdiction to hear them once a Family Court exists for the area. Claims of the second kind belong in the ordinary civil court, the commercial court if the value and nature of the dispute qualify it as a commercial dispute, or whichever specialised forum would hear that category of property claim regardless of who the parties happen to be, and the Family Court has no jurisdiction over them even if the same people who would otherwise be litigating a matrimonial dispute are also the parties to this property suit.

Mixed cases, where a single dispute genuinely combines a matrimonial claim between spouses with a separate property claim against in-laws who were never married to the claimant, are the hardest to structure cleanly, and often need to be split into separate proceedings in separate forums rather than forced into a single suit that neither court has full jurisdiction to decide. A family that anticipates this kind of mixed dispute is well served getting the forum question assessed early, before either suit is drafted, rather than discovering months into litigation that part of the claim was filed in a court that cannot actually grant the relief being sought. Advisors handling such a dispute should map out, claim by claim, exactly which relief is sought against which defendant and on what legal basis, because that mapping exercise is usually what reveals whether a single composite suit can genuinely work or whether the matrimonial and property elements need to be pursued separately from the outset.

What happens when the forum is wrong: delay, not dismissal on the merits

Getting the forum wrong rarely ends a claim outright, but it reliably delays it, and the delay can be substantial. A defendant who believes a suit has been filed in the wrong forum typically raises the point through an application under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, seeking rejection of the plaint, or through an application to transfer the suit to what the defendant says is the correct forum. Either route adds a contested, often appealable, preliminary round of litigation before the substantive dispute is even reached, exactly the pattern that played out in Anjali Jayant, where the daughter-in-law's forum objection was argued and rejected at the trial court, then carried further on a supervisory petition to the High Court, consuming the better part of three years before the underlying property suit could resume on its own merits.

This delay cuts both ways depending on which side benefits from slower proceedings. A party who genuinely prefers delay, whether to preserve possession of disputed property, to wait out a limitation period on a related claim, or simply to exhaust the other side's resources, has an incentive to raise a forum objection even where it has a comparatively weak chance of succeeding, because the objection itself buys time regardless of its ultimate outcome. Courts have become more alert to this pattern over time, and an objection that is raised late, inconsistently, or only after the suit has already proceeded some distance on its merits, tends to be viewed with correspondingly more scepticism than one raised promptly and in good faith at the outset.

The practical lesson for a party bringing a property suit that touches an in-law relationship is to anticipate the forum objection before it is raised, by pleading plainly, in the suit itself, why the claim is a property claim rather than a matrimonial one, including, where relevant, that the parties to the suit were never married to each other at all. A plaint that makes this basis explicit from the outset gives a court considering an Order VII Rule 11 application far less room to find ambiguity than one that leaves the characterisation to be inferred.

A note for families outside Delhi

The Family Courts Act, 1984 is a central enactment, and Section 7's categories, together with the Explanation defining the scope of the property jurisdiction, apply in every state where Family Courts have been established under the Act, in every such state, Delhi included. The foundation-of-the-proceeding test the Delhi High Court applied in Anjali Jayant draws on reasoning that other High Courts have developed in broadly similar terms when construing the same statutory language, since the underlying question, whether a property claim is genuinely a matrimonial matter or merely connected to one through the parties' relationship, does not change from state to state even where the specific precedents courts cite in different jurisdictions do.

Families and practitioners outside Delhi dealing with a comparable dispute should still check their own jurisdiction's High Court authority directly rather than assuming the Delhi position transfers automatically, since the precise boundary a particular High Court has drawn around the foundation test, and how it has treated specific fact patterns such as a shared household argument, can differ in emphasis even where the underlying statutory text and the general approach are the same. What travels reliably across jurisdictions is the basic structure of the analysis: identify what right is actually being claimed, ask whether that right depends on the existence of the marriage or would exist regardless of it, and let that answer, rather than the family relationship between the litigants, decide where the suit belongs.

Drafting the plaint to avoid the forum fight altogether

A plaint that anticipates the forum objection, rather than leaving a court to infer the correct characterisation from the facts pleaded, does more to avoid years of preliminary litigation than any amount of argument once the objection is actually raised. Where a property suit involves a daughter-in-law, a mother-in-law, or other relatives connected only through someone else's marriage, the plaint should state plainly, early and in its own terms, that the claim rests on title, inheritance, a gift, or a specific transaction, and that it does not depend in any way on the marital relationship between the claimant and anyone else involved in the family.

This is not a matter of using particular magic words. Courts applying the foundation test look at the substance of what relief is actually sought and what facts are actually pleaded to support it, not at whether the plaint happens to recite that the dispute is or is not matrimonial. A plaint that pleads a clear chain of title, a specific gift deed, or a specific inheritance, and that seeks relief, such as declaration of title, possession, or partition, that tracks ordinary property law rather than matrimonial relief, gives a court very little room to treat the suit as anything other than a property dispute, regardless of which family members happen to be named as parties.

Where the suit genuinely does touch both kinds of claim, for instance where a wife is suing her husband over jointly accumulated matrimonial property while also seeking relief against his parents over a separate transaction involving different property, the cleaner approach is almost always to split the claims into separate proceedings rather than attempt a single composite suit. A composite suit of this kind risks exactly the jurisdictional confusion Anjali Jayant had to resolve, since a court asked to decide both kinds of claim in one proceeding must still work out, claim by claim, which parts belong in the Family Court and which in the civil court, and a plaint that does that separation work itself, before filing, avoids forcing the court to do it under contest.

What a defendant should do when served with a suit in the wrong court

A party served with a property suit that genuinely does belong in the Family Court, filed instead in an ordinary civil court, should raise the objection at the earliest possible stage of the proceedings rather than waiting to see how the suit develops. Delay in raising a jurisdictional objection does not by itself cure a genuine lack of jurisdiction, since a court that has no power to hear a particular category of dispute cannot acquire that power merely because neither party objected promptly, but a late objection is, as a practical matter, viewed with more scepticism by a court weighing whether the objection is a genuine jurisdictional point or a tactical manoeuvre introduced once the suit has not been going the defendant's way on the merits.

Conversely, a party on the receiving end of a forum objection that looks tactical, raised only once the substantive proceedings have turned against the objecting party, is entitled to point to that timing as part of the argument that the objection should fail, as happened in substance in Anjali Jayant, where the daughter-in-law's objection was considered and rejected on its merits by both the trial court and the High Court rather than succeeding on the strength of the delay point alone. The timing of an objection does not decide a genuine jurisdictional question, which turns on the correct legal characterisation of the claim regardless of when the point is raised, but it does shape how a court is likely to approach an otherwise close case.

A defendant who believes the suit is wrongly filed should also consider, before filing an Order VII Rule 11 application seeking outright rejection, whether a transfer application might achieve the same practical result with less risk. Rejection under Order VII Rule 11 is a blunt remedy that can end the suit entirely if granted, inviting the plaintiff simply to refile in the forum the defendant says is correct, which achieves little beyond delay if the underlying claim is otherwise sound; a transfer application, by contrast, keeps the same suit alive while moving it to what the defendant says is the right court, which is often the more proportionate response where the real complaint is about forum rather than about the merits of the claim itself. In Anjali Jayant itself, the daughter-in-law pursued both the rejection route and the transfer route in the same application, and the trial court's rejection of both, upheld by the High Court, illustrates that raising every available procedural argument together does not improve the odds of success where the underlying characterisation of the claim is against the party raising them.

How this connects to the senior citizens eviction route

A related but procedurally quite different route exists where the parties on the property side of the dispute are senior citizens rather than, or in addition to, a question of title. The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 gives a senior citizen a summary mechanism, through the District Magistrate under the Delhi rules or through the Maintenance Tribunal, to secure eviction of a child or other relative, a daughter-in-law included in some circumstances, from the senior citizen's own property, without having to bring an ordinary civil suit for possession at all.

This route exists alongside, not instead of, the civil court's jurisdiction over title disputes, and the two are not interchangeable. The Senior Citizens Act mechanism is available only where the applicant genuinely is a senior citizen within the Act's definition and is seeking to protect their own right to maintenance and a peaceful life in property they own or are otherwise entitled to use, and courts have been clear, including in the pattern of 2026 Delhi High Court rulings on this Act, that the remedy is not to be used as a shortcut to resolve what is really a disputed question of title or inheritance better suited to a civil suit.

A family facing a dispute that could be framed either way, as a Senior Citizens Act eviction application or as an ordinary civil suit for possession or declaration of title, should consider which forum actually fits the real nature of the grievance before choosing, since a Senior Citizens Act application decided summarily does not have the same evidentiary depth as a civil trial, and a party whose real dispute is about title, rather than about an elderly parent's immediate need for a peaceful home, both risks the Senior Citizens Act forum declining jurisdiction over what is in substance a title dispute and risks the later civil proceedings, if any, having to unpick a summary order that was never meant to settle ownership conclusively in the first place.

A step back: why the line between the two forums is drawn where it is

It is worth asking, briefly, why Parliament chose to confine the Family Court's jurisdiction as narrowly as Section 7 does, rather than giving it a general mandate over any dispute that happens to arise within a family. The Family Courts Act's own stated purpose was to create a forum with procedures better suited to the particular character of matrimonial and child-related disputes, including an emphasis on conciliation, informal procedure, and specialised counselling support, rather than the adversarial, evidence-heavy process an ordinary civil trial typically involves.

An ownership or title dispute does not usually benefit from that specialised procedure in the same way a custody or maintenance dispute does. Establishing who owns a house turns on documents, a chain of transactions, and sometimes decades-old facts that need to be proved with the same rigour a civil court would apply to any other title dispute, conciliation-oriented procedure notwithstanding. Keeping these disputes in the ordinary civil court, rather than routing them through a forum designed around a different kind of case, is consistent with the Act's own underlying purpose rather than a gap or oversight in its drafting.

This also explains why the foundation test looks past the relationship between the parties to the substance of what is actually being claimed. A forum chosen for its suitability to conciliated, relationship-centred disputes is simply not well matched to a straightforward contest over documentary title, however much that contest happens to involve people who are related to each other by marriage. The Delhi High Court's repeated application of this test across 2026, in Anjali Jayant and in the related shared-household rulings decided around the same period, reflects a reasonably settled judicial view that forcing every family-adjacent dispute into the Family Court would stretch that forum well past what it was actually built to do, and would, in the process, give the Family Court a far heavier and more varied caseload than its specialised procedure was ever designed to carry.

Practical takeaways for a family weighing this question now

A family currently facing, or anticipating, a property dispute that touches an in-law relationship should treat the forum question as something to resolve with proper advice before any suit is drafted, not as a technicality to be sorted out once litigation is already under way. The cost of getting this wrong, measured in Anjali Jayant's own nearly three-year path from the original trial court order to the High Court's final word on the point, is high enough that the time spent getting the characterisation right at the outset is almost always worth it.

Where the dispute is clearly and only about property, title, a gift, or an inheritance, and the parties involved were never married to each other, the ordinary civil court, or the commercial court where the value and nature of the claim qualify it as a commercial dispute, is almost certainly the right forum, and a plaint drafted to make that basis explicit will withstand a forum challenge far more easily than one that leaves the point to be inferred. Where the dispute is clearly between spouses over property connected to their own marriage, the Family Court's jurisdiction under the Explanation to Section 7(1)(a) applies instead, and a civil court filing would be the one at risk of challenge.

A third category, sitting somewhere in between and combining elements of both, is the hardest to structure. The safer course there is almost always to separate the claims rather than gamble on a single forum resolving the whole dispute cleanly, since a court asked to untangle a composite claim after the fact, as the trial court and then the High Court effectively had to do in Anjali Jayant, adds years to a case that a clearer initial pleading could have avoided. None of this removes the underlying family conflict that gave rise to the dispute in the first place, but getting the forum right at least ensures that the time and cost spent litigating goes toward resolving that conflict on its merits, rather than toward arguing, year after year, about which court should even be allowed to hear it in the first place.

Anjali Jayant @ Laxmi v. Kusum Singh & Anr. does not change the Family Courts Act's text. It applies, clearly and in a recurring fact pattern, a test the Delhi High Court had already been developing: a property dispute belongs in the Family Court only where the marriage itself is the foundation of the claim, not merely the reason the litigants happen to know each other. A daughter-in-law's fight with her in-laws over a house, however tangled it is with the state of a marriage, is ordinarily a civil court's business.

The separate question of a daughter-in-law's right to live in a shared household, under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, is governed by its own rules and should be argued on its own terms, not folded into the ownership dispute in the hope that one strengthens the other. Keeping the two questions, and the two forums, distinct from the outset is what let the civil court in Anjali Jayant reach the merits of the title dispute in front of it at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a daughter-in-law be sued for property in a civil court rather than the Family Court?

Yes. The Delhi High Court held in Anjali Jayant @ Laxmi v. Kusum Singh & Anr. (20 May 2026) that a property dispute between a daughter-in-law and her parents-in-law is not within the Family Court's exclusive jurisdiction under Section 7 of the Family Courts Act, 1984, because it is not a dispute between the parties to a marriage, and the ordinary civil court is the correct forum.

What test decides whether a property dispute belongs in the Family Court?

Courts ask what the foundation of the proceeding actually is: whether the claimed right depends on, and is inseparable from, the marital relationship, or whether it is an ordinary property right, such as title or ownership, that would exist in the same form regardless of the marriage. Only the former falls within the Family Court's property jurisdiction under the Explanation to Section 7(1)(a).

Does calling a house a 'shared household' make a property dispute a matrimonial matter?

No. The Delhi High Court held that a property's status as a shared household under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 affects a woman's statutory right of residence, which does not depend on ownership, and does not convert a separate ownership or title dispute into a matrimonial matter decided by the Family Court.

What happens if a property suit is filed in the wrong court?

It is rarely dismissed outright on that basis alone, but the forum objection, raised through an Order VII Rule 11 application or a transfer application, typically creates a significant preliminary delay while the jurisdiction question is argued and decided, sometimes running into years, before the substantive dispute can proceed.

Does a shared household right of residence give a daughter-in-law any ownership interest in the property?

No. A shared household right under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 is a right of residence, enforceable through a protection or residence order, not a right of ownership. Several 2026 Delhi High Court rulings have confirmed that this residence right does not extend to insisting on a specific property where the in-laws exclusively own it and reasonable alternative accommodation exists.

Is a property dispute between spouses themselves also heard in a civil court?

Not necessarily. A suit or proceeding between the parties to a marriage itself, with respect to their property, falls within the Family Court's jurisdiction under the Explanation to Section 7(1)(a) of the Family Courts Act, 1984, and a civil court has no jurisdiction over it once a Family Court exists for the area, which is the opposite position from a dispute involving in-laws who were never married to the claimant.

Does this ruling apply outside Delhi as well?

The Family Courts Act, 1984 and its Section 7 categories apply nationally wherever Family Courts are established, and the foundation-of-the-proceeding approach reflects reasoning other High Courts have used on similar facts, but the precise application of that test can vary by jurisdiction, so the position in a particular state's High Court should be checked directly rather than assumed from the Delhi ruling alone.
