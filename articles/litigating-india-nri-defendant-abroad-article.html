Litigating in India as an NRI or Against a Defendant Abroad: Service, Evidence and Appearance

How Indian civil procedure actually handles a party who lives outside India, from serving the first summons to appearing by power of attorney and giving evidence from overseas

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An NRI suing a relative over inherited property, or a business sued by an Indian counterparty while living in Toronto, London or Dubai, runs into the same set of practical questions almost immediately: how will the other side actually be served, can the case proceed while one party never sets foot in an Indian courtroom, and does living abroad change the clock the law gives for filing or responding. This piece works through the Code of Civil Procedure's answers to each of those questions as they stand today, from serving a defendant outside India under Order V, to recording evidence from abroad, to the real limits on what a power of attorney holder can do in an Indian courtroom on an NRI's behalf.

Key points

  • Order V Rule 25 lets a court serve a defendant outside India by post where no agent is empowered to accept service, but the Hague Service Convention complicates this for defendants in countries, including several common NRI destinations, that have objected to postal service under Article 10 of the Convention.
  • A 2024 Kerala High Court Full Bench ruling (Charuvila Philippose v. P.N. Sivadasan) held the Hague Service Convention has domestic effect in India through executive accession alone, but held that postal service under Order V Rule 25 is not excluded by the Convention and remains available where the destination country has not itself objected to postal service under Article 10, overruling the stricter position taken in the earlier Division Bench ruling in Mollykutty v. Nicey Jacob.
  • Evidence from a witness abroad can be taken on commission under Order XXVI, or increasingly by video conference under principles the Supreme Court laid down in State of Maharashtra v. Dr. Praful B. Desai and refined through later guidance requiring the parties' consent.
  • A power of attorney holder can depose only on matters within the holder's own knowledge, or on acts done under the power itself; the Supreme Court in Janki Vashdeo Bhojwani held a POA holder cannot depose on the principal's personal knowledge, though some High Courts have taken a more permissive view.
  • Order XXV lets a court order security for costs from a plaintiff who resides outside India and has no sufficient immovable property in India beyond the suit property, and courts are divided on whether that power is mandatory once the conditions are met or remains discretionary.
  • The Limitation Act excludes a defendant's time spent absent from India when computing limitation under Section 15(5), but there is no equivalent exclusion for a plaintiff who was themselves absent from India during the limitation period.
  • Letters rogatory remain the formal channel for one country's court to ask another's for judicial assistance, operating through Order XXVI, Section 78 CPC and the Hague Evidence Convention, and India's own reservation under Article 23 of that Convention has recently been read by the Madras High Court as a real limit on what a foreign court's request can extract through Indian courts.
  • Appearing at hearings themselves by video link has become routine in most Indian courts since the Supreme Court e-Committee's 2020 Model Rules for Video Conferencing, going well beyond the narrower question of recording evidence, though a vakalatnama executed abroad should still be checked against the specific court's own registry practice before a case is filed.

Why this is one connected problem, not four separate ones

A litigant with one foot outside India tends to encounter the problems this piece covers in sequence rather than all at once, but they are genuinely connected. How a defendant is served decides whether a decree against them can later be defended as void for improper service. Whether evidence can be taken from abroad, by commission or by video conference, decides whether a case can move forward at all without every witness travelling to India. What a power of attorney holder can and cannot do in court decides how much of the litigation an NRI can actually run without being physically present. And limitation decides whether any of this matters, because a suit or an application filed too late never reaches the merits regardless of how well the service, evidence or appearance questions are handled.

This piece takes them in that order, starting with service because it is usually the first point at which an NRI's case either gets properly underway or runs into early, avoidable trouble. Two scenarios run through the discussion that follows and are worth distinguishing from the outset: an NRI who is the plaintiff, suing a relative or counterparty in India while living abroad, and an NRI or foreign resident who is the defendant, being sued in India while living outside the country. The service, evidence and appearance questions apply differently depending on which side of the case the NRI is on, and this piece flags that distinction wherever it matters rather than assuming the reader is always on one particular side of the dispute.

Serving a defendant who lives outside India

Order V of the Code of Civil Procedure sets out how summons is served, and Rule 25 is the provision most often used where the defendant resides outside India and has no agent in India empowered to accept service on their behalf: the court may send the summons by post, and where the envelope is not returned undelivered, the Act treats it as sufficient for the court to presume service. A separate route under Rule 26A contemplates sending the summons to a political agent or a court in the foreign territory notified for this purpose, but commentary from more than one High Court, including a Punjab and Haryana High Court handbook and a 2025 Kerala High Court discussion, describes this route as effectively dormant, since no political agent or foreign court appears to have ever been notified under it. For practical purposes, Rule 25's postal route, used alongside Section 29 of the Code on recognition of foreign process generally, has been the one actually relied on by Indian courts for decades.

The complication is that India has also acceded to the Hague Service Convention of 1965, which sets up an agreed mechanism, through each contracting state's designated Central Authority, for transmitting judicial documents between member countries, and Article 10 of that Convention lets a contracting state object to informal channels of service, including direct postal service, being used against persons within its territory. India has lodged such an objection of its own. The resulting question, which does not have a single settled answer across all High Courts, is whether an Indian court relying on Order V Rule 25's postal route to serve a defendant in a country that has itself objected to postal service under Article 10 is actually serving that defendant validly under the Convention, or whether the court should instead be routing the summons through the Convention's own Central Authority mechanism.

A 2024 Kerala High Court Full Bench decision, reported as Charuvila Philippose v. P.N. Sivadasan (2024:KER:84933), addressed this directly, holding that the Hague Service Convention has domestic effect in India even without a dedicated enabling statute, reasoning that India's accession through executive steps, including establishing a Central Authority, is enough to give the Convention effect domestically. On the postal-service question itself, the Full Bench held that postal service under Order V Rule 25 is not foreclosed by the Convention and remains available where the destination state has not objected to postal service under Article 10, and in reaching that result it overruled the earlier Division Bench ruling in Mollykutty v. Nicey Jacob, which had held that a defendant in a Hague Convention contracting state could only be served through the Convention's own channel and that direct postal service was barred. This account treats the citation and precise reasoning of the 2024 ruling as resting on secondary reporting rather than the judgment text itself, and a litigant relying on it for a filing should pull the actual order before citing it.

For a defendant who does have an agent in India already empowered to accept service on their behalf, none of the Rule 25 or Convention analysis above is even reached, since Order V's ordinary domestic service provisions apply to that agent directly, which is one reason an NRI defendant who expects to be drawn into Indian litigation sometimes finds it useful to have a family member or representative in India formally empowered in advance to accept service, rather than leaving every future proceeding to be served abroad under the more complicated route this section describes. Setting this up in advance is also one of the few points in this piece within an NRI's control before any dispute even arises, unlike the service and evidence questions that follow, which mostly have to be worked out reactively once litigation has already started.

Separate commentary on the same area confirms this reading, stating that Indian courts have affirmed postal service under Order V Rule 25 as valid under the Convention provided the destination country has not itself lodged an Article 10 objection to that mode. In practice this means a defendant in a country that has not objected to postal service under Article 10 may still be validly served by post, while a defendant in a country that has objected is a different matter, and this is why a litigant serving an NRI or foreign defendant should check the specific Article 10 declaration of the country where the defendant actually resides, published and periodically updated by the Hague Conference on Private International Law, rather than assume either that postal service always works or that it never does. Separate bilateral arrangements can displace the general analysis further still; a mutual legal assistance arrangement between India and the United States is reported to specify its own procedure for serving notices or summons on individuals there, which should be checked specifically for a defendant resident in that country rather than treated as covered by the general Convention discussion above.

Letters rogatory and the limits of what a foreign request can extract

Separately from service, letters rogatory are the formal, government-to-government channel through which one country's court asks another's court for judicial assistance, most often for taking evidence rather than for service itself. Indian courts execute incoming letters rogatory for evidence through Order XXVI Rules 19 to 22 read with Section 78 of the Code, under which a High Court can appoint a commissioner to record oral or documentary evidence sought by a foreign court, and India's own courts can send letters rogatory outward to a foreign court in the same way when evidence located abroad is needed for an Indian proceeding.

The Madras High Court's handling of Softgel Healthcare v. Pfizer, arising from letters rogatory the United States District Court for Delaware issued in May 2024 seeking documents and testimony from a non-party Chennai manufacturer for US patent litigation, is a useful recent marker of how far a foreign court's request can actually reach through this channel, though its two stages point in different directions. A single judge, in January 2025, allowed execution and appointed a local commissioner to collect the evidence sought. A Division Bench, on appeal, reversed that order in November 2025 and dismissed the enforcement applications, with reports describing the reversal as resting on India's sovereignty interest and on limits on what a non-party can be compelled to produce, including India's declaration under Article 23 of the Hague Evidence Convention of 1970, which lets a contracting state decline to execute letters of request seeking what is commonly called pre-trial document discovery. Pfizer has since appealed to the Supreme Court, reported as still hearing the matter, so the final word on how far a foreign request can reach through this channel has not yet been given.

For an NRI litigant or a party litigating against one, the practical lesson is that a letter of request seeking evidence from India, or an Indian request seeking evidence from abroad, is not a formality that simply produces whatever is asked for. A request framed narrowly, around specific, identified evidence rather than a broad trawl through a party's documents, is far more likely to be executed without a satellite dispute over its own scope, and a party expecting to use this mechanism should budget time for exactly that kind of preliminary argument over scope before any actual evidence is produced.

Taking evidence from a witness who will not, or cannot, come to India

Where a witness relevant to an Indian proceeding is outside India, Order XXVI Rule 5 lets a court issue a commission to examine that witness abroad if satisfied the evidence is necessary, and the commissioner appointed for this purpose records the evidence and returns it to the court with a signed written report under the general scheme of the Order, with the commissioner's own conduct of the examination bounded by what Rule 16 permits and objections to particular questions handled under Rule 16-A. Where the request for evidence originates from a foreign court rather than from the Indian proceeding itself, Rules 19 to 22 govern how the High Court executes it, a route that overlaps with the letters rogatory discussion above and, separately, with requests made under the Hague Evidence Convention of 1970 where the country concerned is a party to it.

Video conferencing has become a real alternative to a formal commission for many NRI witnesses, and the foundational authority remains the Supreme Court's 2003 decision in State of Maharashtra v. Dr. Praful B. Desai, which held that evidence of a witness located abroad can be recorded by video conference, reasoning that such evidence, recorded with the accused or the opposing party and their counsel present at the court end, satisfies the legal requirement that evidence be taken in the presence of the parties. Reports on the later 2017 ruling in Santhini v. Vijaya Venketesh, in the context of a transfer petition in matrimonial proceedings, are not fully consistent with each other, with some describing the Court as restricting video-conference evidence in matrimonial cases and others describing it as permitting the transferee court to record evidence of witnesses unable to attend in person by that very means; this account does not resolve that inconsistency and instead flags it, since the exact scope of any matrimonial-specific limitation on video-conference evidence should be checked against the judgment itself before being relied on.

A clearer and more recent marker is the Supreme Court's own 2020 guidelines on court functioning through video conferencing, issued during the pandemic, which left detailed modalities to individual High Courts but held that evidence should not be recorded by video conference without the consent of the parties. That consent requirement is the practical safeguard an NRI witness, or a party wanting to examine one, should rely on: raising the request for video-conference evidence early, and seeking the opposing party's consent or the court's direction on it well before the evidence stage is reached, rather than assuming the facility will simply be available on the day because a commission under Order XXVI would otherwise be the only alternative.

Choosing between a formal commission and a video-conference examination is itself a practical decision an NRI party and their counsel should make deliberately rather than by default. A commission under Order XXVI produces a written record taken by a commissioner, which can be useful where the evidence is technical, lengthy or likely to be disputed in detail, but it takes longer to arrange, since a commissioner has to be identified and appointed and the process typically runs over more than one sitting. Video-conference evidence is usually quicker to schedule and lets the trial judge observe the witness directly rather than reading a commissioner's report, which can matter where credibility is likely to be contested, but it depends on both sides' consent under the 2020 guidelines and on the receiving court's own technical arrangements being adequate for a hearing of that length and complexity.

Appearing through a power of attorney holder, and where that stops

Order III, Rules 1 and 2 of the Code let a party appear, apply or act through a recognised agent, which in practice usually means a power of attorney holder, rather than only in person or through a pleader, and this is the provision that lets an NRI run a great deal of Indian litigation without being physically present for every step. What a power of attorney holder cannot do is step into the principal's shoes as a witness on matters within the principal's own personal knowledge. The Supreme Court's decision in Janki Vashdeo Bhojwani v. IndusInd Bank Ltd., reported at (2005) 2 SCC 217, held that the word acts in Order III confines what a power of attorney holder may do under that provision to acts actually done by the holder in exercise of the power granted by the instrument, and that a power of attorney holder cannot depose on behalf of the principal in respect of matters that are within the principal's own personal knowledge.

This distinction matters a great deal in practice. A power of attorney holder can usually verify pleadings, sign and file documents, give instructions to counsel, and depose about steps the holder personally took under the power, such as negotiations conducted or payments made on the principal's behalf. What the holder generally cannot do, at least on the Janki Vashdeo line of authority, is give evidence in place of the principal about events the principal personally witnessed or participated in before the power of attorney was ever granted, such as the circumstances of an original property transaction or a conversation the principal had years earlier. An NRI relying entirely on a power of attorney holder to litigate a dispute should identify, early in the case, which facts genuinely need the principal's own evidence and plan for the principal's deposition, whether by a personal visit, evidence on commission, or video conference, rather than assuming the attorney holder can simply give that evidence as a substitute.

It should be said plainly that this reading is not universally followed. At least one High Court, in a decision from Floriano Armando Luis, is reported to have taken a dissenting view, holding that Order III Rule 2 cannot be read to disentitle a power of attorney holder from deposing on behalf of the principal, and later decisions from other High Courts are reported to reflect similarly divergent positions. An NRI or counsel relying on a power of attorney holder's evidence should therefore check the current position in the specific High Court where the litigation is pending, rather than assuming the Supreme Court's Janki Vashdeo position is applied uniformly everywhere, since the reported divergence suggests it is not. Until that position is clarified for the particular court involved, the safer course for an NRI with evidence of their own personal knowledge to give is to plan for giving it directly, whether by a visit timed to the hearing, a commission, or video conference, rather than relying on an attorney holder's testimony to cover ground the Janki Vashdeo line of authority may not actually permit.

Executing a vakalatnama and other papers from abroad

Before any of the appearance or evidence questions in this piece arise, an NRI has to get a lawyer properly instructed in the first place, and that starts with a vakalatnama, the document authorising an advocate to act. Forum discussion among practitioners and a handful of law-firm guides on this point are not fully consistent with each other: some describe notarisation before a local notary public in the country of residence as commonly accepted, with consular or embassy attestation needed only where a particular court insists on it, while others describe embassy or consular attestation, or an apostille under the Hague Convention where the country concerned is a party to it, as the safer or even necessary route. What is more firmly documented, at least for proceedings before the Supreme Court of India itself, is that its own rules allow a vakalatnama to be executed before a notary or an advocate and then accepted by an Advocate-on-Record, who must separately endorse that it was duly executed, rather than treating the foreign execution alone as sufficient without that endorsement.

Because registry practice on this point is reported to vary between courts, and because this research did not find a single consolidated rule that applies uniformly across every High Court and district court in India, an NRI should raise the question of how the vakalatnama and any supporting affidavits will be executed with the instructed advocate before relying on a format that worked in a different case or a different court. Embassy or consular attestation, while sometimes described as unnecessary, is rarely wrong to obtain and is the more conservative choice where a registry's own practice is not already known to the advocate handling the filing.

The same caution applies to the power of attorney itself, where it is being used to let someone in India act on the NRI's behalf more broadly than a vakalatnama alone would allow. This piece does not repeat the detailed treatment of executing and using a power of attorney from abroad, since that ground is covered at length elsewhere on this site; the point to carry into litigation specifically is that whatever execution and attestation method is chosen for the power of attorney should be checked against what the particular court hearing the matter will actually accept, for exactly the same reason that applies to the vakalatnama.

Appearing at hearings themselves by video link, beyond just giving evidence

Separately from recording evidence by video conference, discussed earlier in connection with witnesses abroad, an NRI party or their counsel may also want to appear at ordinary hearings, arguments and case-management dates by video link rather than travelling to India for each one. The infrastructure for this expanded considerably from 2020 onward: the Supreme Court's e-Committee launched a dedicated website in August 2020 carrying Model Rules for Video Conferencing and Model Rules for e-Filing for adoption by High Courts and subordinate courts, and later e-Committee newsletters tracked, state by state, which High Courts had actually brought those rules into effect. Press reporting citing e-Committee figures put the scale of adoption at around one crore cases handled through video courts between March 2020 and May 2021, rising to a reported figure beyond one crore cases across High Courts and subordinate courts by August 2021.

What this means practically is that video-conference appearance for hearings, as distinct from the evidence-specific question addressed earlier, is now routine infrastructure in most Indian courts rather than an exceptional accommodation that has to be specially argued for, though the precise local rules, the platform used, and whether a given judge's own practice directions permit it for a particular kind of hearing still vary by court and should be confirmed with the instructed advocate before an NRI party relies on being able to appear this way for a specific date. A separate but related development, the e-Committee's 2021 draft model rules on live-streaming and recording of court proceedings, is a reminder that India's courts have been moving toward greater remote accessibility generally over this period, which should make it easier, not harder, for an NRI party to stay engaged with a case without being physically present in India throughout.

None of this removes the earlier points in this piece about evidence specifically, where the consent requirement from the Supreme Court's 2020 guidelines still applies, or about appearance by a power of attorney holder, where the Janki Vashdeo limits on what the holder can depose to still apply regardless of whether the hearing itself happens in person or by video link. Video-conference appearance makes an NRI's physical presence in an Indian courtroom less necessary for routine hearings; it does not change what a power of attorney holder is legally permitted to do once in that courtroom, whether attending in person or by screen.

Security for costs: a plaintiff abroad can be asked to put up money first

Order XXV of the Code lets a court require a plaintiff to furnish security for the defendant's costs, and the provision has a specific proviso for exactly the situation this piece is concerned with: where a sole plaintiff, or every plaintiff if there are several, resides outside India and does not possess sufficient immovable property within India other than the property that is the subject matter of the suit. Failing to furnish security ordered under this provision can lead to dismissal of the suit under Order XXV Rule 2, though that dismissal cannot itself be set aside without notice to the defendant, which gives the plaintiff at least some procedural protection against an ex parte dismissal on this ground alone.

Whether a court must order security once those conditions are met, or retains discretion not to, is not settled uniformly across the reported decisions found for this piece. One line of authority treats the proviso as leaving the court with no real option once the conditions, residence abroad and no sufficient immovable property in India beyond the suit property, are established, while another line treats the power as discretionary, to be exercised having regard to the facts and circumstances of the particular case. A Delhi High Court reference is reported to have framed this exact question, whether ordering security is mandatory or discretionary, for consideration by a larger bench, and this research did not find the outcome of that reference, so the point should be treated as genuinely open rather than settled in either direction.

An NRI plaintiff planning to sue in India should, as a matter of practical case preparation, identify early whether they hold immovable property in India beyond whatever is the subject of the suit itself, since that fact alone can decide whether a security-for-costs application against them succeeds, and should not assume residence abroad by itself will automatically trigger an order to furnish security, given that at least one line of authority treats the court's power as discretionary rather than automatic.

Limitation: absence from India helps a defendant, but not usually a plaintiff

Section 15(5) of the Limitation Act, 1963 excludes, in computing the limitation period for a suit, the time during which the defendant has been absent from India, which operates in a plaintiff's favour where a defendant has been outside the country for part of the period that would otherwise have run out the claim. Older authority has held that this exclusion presupposes the defendant was at some point present in India and later became absent, so a defendant who was never in India at all during the relevant period cannot claim the benefit of having been absent from it, a distinction that matters for a claim against someone who has, for instance, lived abroad since before the cause of action even arose.

There is no equivalent exclusion running the other way for a plaintiff who was themselves absent from India during the limitation period. Commentary on this point is consistent in stating that the Limitation Act contains no corresponding provision excluding the time during which a plaintiff was absent from India, so an NRI plaintiff cannot generally argue that their own residence abroad should toll the limitation clock on a claim they wish to bring. An older Calcutta Full Bench view, decided under the predecessor 1877 Act, reasoned that it would be unjust to extend the defendant's-absence exclusion to a plaintiff who could just as easily have sued from abroad during the same period, and nothing in the sources gathered for this piece suggests that reasoning has been displaced under the current Act.

For an NRI considering litigation in India, the practical consequence is straightforward even if it is not always intuitive: living abroad does not buy extra time to file a suit, and an NRI plaintiff should calendar the ordinary limitation period for the claim in question just as a resident plaintiff would, rather than assuming that distance or the practical difficulty of instructing counsel and gathering evidence from overseas will be treated by a court as extending the deadline.

Putting it together: a sequence for a cross-border Indian suit

A party starting or defending litigation in India where one side is abroad should work through these questions roughly in the order this piece has set them out. Limitation comes first in practical terms even though it is discussed last above, since nothing else matters if the suit or application itself is out of time, and an NRI plaintiff should not count on residence abroad to extend that clock. Service comes next for whichever side is initiating proceedings against a defendant outside India: the country of the defendant's residence, its status as a Hague Convention contracting state, and its specific Article 10 declaration should all be checked before choosing between Order V Rule 25 postal service and routing the summons through the Convention's Central Authority channel, since getting this wrong risks a later challenge to any resulting decree as void for improper service.

Once the suit is properly on foot, the evidence question should be planned early rather than left until the trial stage is reached: a witness abroad, including the NRI party personally where their own evidence is needed on matters a power of attorney holder cannot give on their behalf under the Janki Vashdeo line of authority, should have a planned route, whether a commission under Order XXVI, video conference with the opposing party's consent, or an in-person visit timed to the hearing, rather than being left to improvisation once the case reaches that stage. A defendant resisting an NRI plaintiff's suit should also consider, early, whether the facts support an Order XXV application for security for costs, given the real, if contested, possibility of dismissal for non-compliance if such an order is made and not complied with.

None of these mechanisms, service abroad, evidence on commission or by video conference, appearance through a power of attorney holder, or security for costs, is unique to any one kind of dispute; they recur across property suits between relatives, commercial claims against NRI counterparties, and family disputes with a party abroad alike. Getting each of them right from the outset is usually far cheaper, in both time and money, than discovering a gap in service or evidence years into litigation, when the only remedies left are an appeal or a fresh round of proceedings to fix what should have been addressed at the start.

An NRI who is the defendant in Indian proceedings, rather than the plaintiff, should read the sequence above with the roles reversed but the same checklist in mind. Checking that service was actually valid under whichever of Rule 25 or the Hague Convention's own channel applied is the first line of defence against a decree obtained in the defendant's absence, and should be raised at the earliest opportunity rather than after a decree has already been passed, since an application to set aside an ex parte decree on grounds of improper service is a harder, slower route than objecting to defective service before judgment. A defendant abroad who does intend to contest the claim should also move quickly to instruct counsel and execute a vakalatnama, for the reasons set out earlier, so that an appearance can be entered and the case defended on the merits rather than being left to proceed ex parte while the defendant's own instructions and documentation are still being arranged.

None of the procedural questions this piece has covered, service abroad, evidence from overseas, appearance by power of attorney, security for costs or limitation, has a single, universally applied answer across every Indian court, and that is the most important practical point to take from this piece rather than any one rule in isolation. Country-specific Article 10 declarations, divergent High Court views on a power of attorney holder's evidentiary role, and an unresolved reference on whether security for costs is mandatory or discretionary all mean that an NRI or a party facing one has to check the current position in the specific court and, where relevant, the specific foreign jurisdiction involved, rather than relying on a general rule of thumb.

Where this piece has flagged a point as unsettled, reported inconsistently, or resting on secondary commentary rather than a judgment this research could independently verify, that reflects a genuine state of the law and the sources available, not an oversight, and a reader with a live matter raising any of these questions should treat it as a reason to seek specific advice rather than to proceed on an assumption.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an Indian court serve a defendant abroad simply by sending the summons by post?

Often, but not always. Order V Rule 25 allows postal service on a defendant outside India, but where the defendant lives in a country that has objected to postal service under Article 10 of the Hague Service Convention, courts have increasingly required service through the Convention's own Central Authority channel instead. The defendant's specific country of residence and its Article 10 declaration should be checked before choosing a method.

What is the difference between letters rogatory and the Hague Evidence Convention route?

Letters rogatory are the general government-to-government channel through which one country's court asks another's for judicial assistance, executed in India under Order XXVI Rules 19 to 22 read with Section 78 of the Code. The Hague Evidence Convention of 1970 provides its own, more structured mechanism for member countries, and India's reservation under Article 23 of that Convention has been read by at least one High Court as limiting how far a foreign request for broad document discovery can be executed through Indian courts.

Can my evidence be taken by video conference if I cannot travel to India?

Indian courts have permitted evidence by video conference since the Supreme Court's 2003 ruling in Praful Desai, provided the proceeding is conducted with the opposing party and counsel present at the court end. Supreme Court guidelines from 2020 add that evidence should not be recorded by video conference without the consent of the parties, so this should be raised and agreed, or directed by the court, well before the evidence stage is reached.

Can my power of attorney holder give evidence in my place in an Indian court?

Only to a limited extent. The Supreme Court in Janki Vashdeo Bhojwani held that a power of attorney holder cannot depose on matters within the principal's own personal knowledge, confining the holder's evidence to acts the holder personally carried out under the power. Some High Courts have taken a more permissive view, so the position in the specific court hearing the matter should be checked.

Can a defendant ask an NRI plaintiff to deposit security for costs before the case proceeds?

Possibly. Order XXV lets a court order security for costs where the plaintiff resides outside India and lacks sufficient immovable property in India beyond the property in dispute. Courts are divided on whether an order is mandatory once those conditions are met or remains discretionary, with the point reported as referred to a larger bench in at least one Delhi High Court matter without a confirmed outcome found in this research.

Does living abroad give me more time to file a suit in India?

No. The Limitation Act excludes a defendant's time spent absent from India when computing limitation under Section 15(5), but there is no equivalent exclusion for a plaintiff. An NRI plaintiff should calendar the ordinary limitation period for the claim just as a resident plaintiff would.

What happens if a decree is passed against a defendant who was never properly served abroad?

A decree passed on defective service can be challenged later as void for want of proper service, which can unravel years of litigation. This is precisely why getting the service method right at the outset, including checking the defendant's country's Hague Convention and Article 10 position, matters far more than it might first appear.

Is the political agent or foreign court route under Order V Rule 26A actually used?

In practice, no. Courts and procedural handbooks describe this route as dormant, since no political agent or foreign court appears to have been notified under it, leaving Order V Rule 25's postal route, and increasingly the Hague Service Convention's own channel where applicable, as the methods actually in use.
