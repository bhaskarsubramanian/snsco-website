Cross-Border Commercial Mediation in India Under the Mediation Act, 2023

What a foreign company gains from India's new mediation law, what it does not, and why the Singapore Convention still will not help it enforce a settlement reached abroad

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 28 November 2026 · Law stated as on 28 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company with a commercial dispute in Delhi, Noida or Kolkata will almost certainly meet the word mediation before it meets a judge. Two different statutes use that word for two different things, and a party that confuses them can lose months, or lose the case before it is even argued.

Key points

  • Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 makes pre-institution mediation mandatory for most commercial suits, and the Supreme Court in Patil Automation has held that filing without it invites rejection of the suit itself.
  • The Mediation Act, 2023 runs alongside, not instead of, Section 12A. Its own pre-litigation mediation under Section 5 is voluntary, and a party cannot be compelled into it.
  • An 'international mediation' under Section 3(g) of the Mediation Act is defined by a party's foreign nationality or incorporation, but Section 2 of the Act limits its application, including the international mediation chapter, to mediations conducted in India, so a settlement talk held in Singapore or Dubai between an Indian party and a foreign one falls outside the Act altogether.
  • A mediated settlement agreement is meant to be enforceable as a decree under Section 27, but at least one 2026 High Court order has held that Section 27 itself is not yet in force, so a foreign party should not assume automatic decree-status without checking the record in its own case.
  • India signed the Singapore Convention on Mediation in August 2019 and has not ratified it as of late November 2026, so a settlement reached outside India still cannot be enforced here the way the New York Convention lets an arbitral award be enforced.
  • Registration with the Mediation Council of India is not compulsory, so a foreign party does not lose the right to pick its own mediator merely because that mediator is unregistered.
  • Urgent interim relief remains the one real exit from mandatory pre-institution mediation, and courts are now actively testing whether a plaint's urgency claim is genuine or a device to dodge Section 12A.

A foreign company's first encounter with mediation in an Indian dispute

A supply contract goes wrong, an Indian distributor stops paying, or a joint venture partner in Noida refuses to honour a buyout clause, and the foreign party's first instinct is to instruct litigation counsel and file a suit. The first thing Indian counsel will usually say back is that the suit cannot simply be filed. If the claim is a commercial dispute of the kind the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 recognises and crosses the specified value threshold that the relevant High Court has notified for its commercial division, the plaintiff must first attempt mediation under Section 12A of that Act, and only then approach the court.

This surprises a foreign client more often than any other single procedural step in Indian litigation, because nothing like it exists in most common law systems the client has dealt with before. It is not a case management direction a judge gives after the suit is filed. It is a precondition to filing, enforced by the Supreme Court with real teeth, and a plaintiff who ignores it can watch the suit rejected before a single hearing on the merits.

Confusing matters further, 2023 brought a second statute that also uses the word mediation: the Mediation Act, 2023. The two laws are not competitors and they do not replace each other. A foreign party dealing with an Indian counterparty needs to know which one applies to its dispute, when, and what each one actually promises.

The pattern repeats across the kinds of disputes a foreign company most often brings to an Indian court: a distribution agreement terminated without the notice the contract required, a technology licence where royalty payments have stopped, a joint venture where one promoter has frozen the other out of board decisions, or a construction or supply contract where delay penalties are disputed. Each of these will usually qualify as a commercial dispute under Section 2(1)(c) of the Commercial Courts Act, which means Section 12A sits in the plaintiff's path before the suit can be filed at all.

Two statutes, two mediations: Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act and Section 5 of the Mediation Act

Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, inserted by the 2018 amendment, requires a plaintiff in a commercial suit not seeking urgent interim relief to attempt pre-institution mediation through the authority notified for that purpose, ordinarily a mediation cell attached to the relevant district judiciary or High Court legal services authority. This is the route a foreign company's lawyers will actually use for most contract, supply, franchise and shareholder disputes that qualify as commercial disputes of specified value.

The Mediation Act, 2023 runs on a separate track. Its Section 5 lets parties, whether or not a mediation agreement already exists between them, voluntarily and by mutual consent attempt pre-litigation mediation of a civil or commercial dispute. Commentary on the Act is consistent that commercial disputes of specified value continue to be routed through Section 12A rather than through Section 5, so the newer Act does not quietly replace the older mechanism a foreign party is more likely to encounter.

The practical difference is consent. Section 12A can force a reluctant defendant to the table, at least for the plaintiff's side of the exercise, because the suit cannot be filed without it. Section 5 cannot force anyone anywhere. A foreign party should not assume that invoking the Mediation Act gives it the same leverage that Section 12A gives a commercial plaintiff; the two provisions were not built for the same purpose.

Patil Automation: why skipping Section 12A can get a commercial suit thrown out

The Supreme Court settled the mandatory character of Section 12A in Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd. (2022). The Court held that Section 12A is mandatory, not directory, and that a commercial suit instituted without the plaintiff first attempting the pre-institution mediation it requires is liable to be rejected, unless the plaint genuinely contemplates urgent interim relief. This is not a technicality a defendant can waive by staying silent about it. Commercial courts have treated the omission as going to the maintainability of the suit itself.

For a foreign plaintiff this changes the sequencing of an Indian claim. Before instructing a plaint to be drafted, counsel in Delhi, Noida or Kolkata will first assess whether the claim crosses the specified-value threshold for a commercial dispute, and if it does, will file the mediation reference with the authority notified under the Act and wait out the statutory window before the suit itself goes in. Clients used to filing first and talking settlement later should build this lead time into any litigation timeline against an Indian counterparty.

That lead time is not open-ended. Section 12A(3) requires the mediation to be completed within three months of the application being received, extendable by a further two months but only with the consent of both the applicant and the opposite party, and the rules framed under the Act direct the mediator to report a failure to settle once that window closes without an agreement. A foreign plaintiff can treat this as a ceiling on how long an unwilling defendant can use the process to delay the suit, and the second proviso to the same sub-section keeps the plaintiff's limitation period from running out while the mediation window is open, so time spent in mediation is not held against the claim later.

Patil Automation also made clear that this requirement is prospective, applying to suits instituted after the judgment, a point the Supreme Court addressed further in later orders dealing with suits that straddled the date of the ruling. A foreign party should ask its Indian counsel, at the point a dispute first turns litigious, whether the specific suit it intends to file sits inside or outside that prospective line.

The urgent interim relief exception, and why courts are now scrutinising it

The one clean exit from mandatory pre-institution mediation is a plaint that genuinely contemplates urgent interim relief, such as an injunction to stop a competitor from using confidential pricing data or to freeze a bank account before funds leave the jurisdiction. Patil Automation itself cautioned that an application for interim relief should not be used as a disguise to defeat Section 12A, and later High Court rulings have taken that warning seriously.

Commercial courts now look past the label a plaintiff puts on its prayer and ask whether the underlying facts actually support urgency, which has turned the urgent interim relief exception into a live point of contest rather than a box a drafter can tick. A money claim dressed up with a token injunction prayer is the pattern courts have grown suspicious of, and at least one recent Delhi ruling has pointed to exactly that pattern as the kind of case better suited to mediation than to an emergency hearing.

A foreign company that genuinely needs urgent relief, for instance to stop the dissipation of assets by an Indian joint venture partner mid-dispute, should instruct its lawyers to plead the urgency with real particulars from the outset: dates, amounts, and the specific harm that will occur if the court does not act before mediation can even begin. A thin urgency claim invites the opposite of what it was meant to achieve, which is a suit thrown out for want of Section 12A compliance.

Voluntary pre-litigation mediation under Section 5 of the Mediation Act

Where Section 12A does not apply, because the dispute is not a commercial dispute of specified value, or because it is a dispute the parties would simply rather resolve without going near a court at all, Section 5 of the Mediation Act, 2023 offers a standing invitation rather than a gate. Either party can propose it, and the other is free to decline.

Many cross-border contracts already contain a multi-tier dispute resolution clause requiring negotiation, then mediation, then arbitration or litigation in sequence. Section 5 gives such a clause a statutory anchor even where the contract is silent, since the Act allows the parties to attempt pre-litigation mediation irrespective of whether a mediation agreement already exists between them. Foreign counsel drafting a contract with an Indian party should still write the clause out expressly rather than rely on the Act to fill the gap, because a court asked to decide whether a suit filed without attempting mediation was premature will look first at what the parties actually promised each other.

For a foreign company facing a dispute that falls outside Section 12A's mandatory net, proposing Section 5 mediation early costs little and can preserve a commercial relationship that litigation would end for good. It is a genuinely useful tool precisely because nobody is forced to use it, which tends to keep the process cooperative rather than adversarial from the first session.

What makes a mediation 'international' under Section 3(g)

The Mediation Act defines international mediation in Section 3(g) as a mediation relating to a commercial dispute arising out of a legal relationship under Indian law, where at least one party is an individual who is a national of, or habitually resident in, a country other than India, or is a foreign body corporate, association or body of individuals. On its face this looks built for exactly the kind of dispute a foreign company would bring.

The qualification that matters is where the mediation is conducted. Commentary on the Act, including analysis from Singapore's own mediation institute, is consistent that the Mediation Act applies to international mediation only where the mediation itself takes place in India, not merely where the underlying contract or one of the parties has an Indian connection. A settlement conference held in Singapore or London between an Indian company and its foreign counterparty, even over a dispute governed by Indian law, sits outside the Act's international mediation chapter.

This narrows the Act's usefulness for a foreign company that would rather mediate somewhere other than India, a preference common enough when one party suspects, rightly or wrongly, that a domestic process will favour the local party. If genuine neutrality of venue matters to a foreign client, that has to be negotiated into the contract's dispute resolution clause rather than assumed from the statute, because the statute will not follow the mediation outside India's borders.

The enforceability promise in Section 27, and the doubt one High Court has cast on it

Section 27(2) of the Mediation Act provides that a mediated settlement agreement is enforced in accordance with the provisions of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, as if it were a judgment or decree passed by a court, and the agreement is stated to be final and binding on the parties and on persons claiming under them once signed and authenticated by the mediator. This is the single most attractive feature of the Act for a party weighing mediation against litigation or arbitration, because a decree can be executed directly rather than sued upon afresh.

The caveat a foreign party needs before relying on this is that commencement of the Act's provisions has not followed a single, uniform date across every section. A 2026 Manipur High Court ruling recorded that neither Section 19(1) nor Section 27 had in fact been brought into force in that matter, so the court proceeded to pass its own order and draw up a decree rather than treat the settlement as self-executing under Section 27. Other reported orders in the same period have proceeded on the footing that a mediated settlement does carry decree-like force.

Until this is settled, a foreign company should treat Section 27's promise as directional rather than guaranteed, and should ask its Indian counsel, before relying on a mediated settlement as an enforceable decree without further court process, to confirm the notification status of Section 27 as it stands on the date the settlement is signed. Where there is doubt, the safer course is to have the settlement recorded by the court itself and a decree drawn in the ordinary way, which forecloses the argument entirely.

Challenging a mediated settlement under Section 28

A settlement under the Mediation Act is not immune from challenge, but the grounds are narrow by design. Commentary on Section 28 describes the available grounds as fraud, corruption, impersonation, or mediation of a matter that was not fit for mediation, and nothing resembling the broader merits review available in an ordinary civil appeal. The ninety-day style window commonly used for this kind of challenge in Indian procedure makes it important for a foreign party to flag any genuine fraud or impersonation concern quickly rather than let it surface months later.

This narrow review is, in substance, the same bargain Indian arbitration law strikes with an arbitral award: finality is protected, and a losing party cannot reopen the merits simply because it now regrets the number it agreed to. A foreign company should negotiate the mediated settlement with that finality firmly in mind, because walking away from the table assuming a court will later rescue a bad bargain on the merits is not a plan that this statute supports.

The Mediation Council of India and why registration is not compulsory

The Mediation Act establishes the Mediation Council of India, with its head office at Delhi, to prescribe standards for the education, certification and conduct of mediators and to regulate their registration. The government has since notified the Council's formal establishment, though the exact date reported across sources is not entirely consistent, so a party should confirm the current notification on the Ministry of Law and Justice's own gazette records rather than rely on secondary reports for that detail alone.

What matters more to a foreign party choosing a mediator is that registration with the Council has not been made mandatory. Reported commentary on the Act's framing records that the legislature deliberately preserved party autonomy to appoint a mediator of their own choice, registered or not. A foreign company that already has a preferred international mediator, or wants to use an institution such as a chamber of commerce's own panel, is not obliged to pick someone off a government-maintained register to get a mediation under this Act off the ground.

Why the Singapore Convention still does not help a foreign party

The United Nations Convention on International Settlement Agreements Resulting from Mediation, known as the Singapore Convention, does for mediated settlements something like what the New York Convention does for arbitral awards: it lets a settlement reached in one signatory state be enforced directly in another, without relitigating the dispute. India signed the Convention in August 2019.

India has not ratified it as of late November 2026. Ratification would require enabling domestic legislation, and the Mediation Act, 2023, unlike the Arbitration and Conciliation Act's incorporation of the New York Convention, does not itself give effect to the Singapore Convention. A September 2026 industry commentary on India's push to position itself as a global arbitration hub also recorded continuing diplomatic pressure, including from Singapore's own delegation at a mid-2026 bilateral meeting, to move ratification forward, but no deposit of an instrument of ratification has been reported.

The practical consequence for a foreign company is straightforward and worth stating plainly: a settlement agreement signed abroad, even one that would qualify for Singapore Convention enforcement in a country that has ratified it, cannot be enforced in India the way a New York Convention award can. Any cross-border settlement a foreign party expects to enforce against Indian assets should either be mediated in India under a process that can be converted into a decree, or structured as a contract whose breach can be sued upon or, better still, arbitrated under a clause that does carry New York Convention protection.

Drafting a mediation clause for a cross-border commercial contract with an Indian counterparty

Given the gaps described above, a foreign company negotiating a fresh contract with an Indian counterparty gains more from a carefully drafted dispute resolution clause than from assuming either statute will fill in the blanks. A workable clause typically specifies the seat and language of any mediation, the institution or panel from which a mediator will be drawn, a time limit after which either party may walk away and proceed to arbitration or litigation, and whether the parties intend the mediated outcome to be recorded as a consent award or consent decree rather than left to rely on Section 27 alone.

Multi-tier clauses of this kind, requiring negotiation and then mediation before arbitration, are generally enforceable in Indian courts provided the steps are objectively ascertainable and not left so vague that a court cannot tell whether the precondition has actually been satisfied. A clause that simply says the parties shall first attempt amicable settlement, without any mechanism for determining when that attempt has failed, invites exactly the kind of satellite litigation the clause was meant to avoid.

Where the contract is itself a commercial contract likely to generate a commercial dispute of specified value, the clause should also acknowledge Section 12A's mandatory pre-institution mediation rather than pretend a separate contractual mediation clause displaces it. The two can run together: a contractual mediation attempt can often double as, or feed directly into, the statutory process, provided counsel on both sides keep records that will satisfy a commercial court that the statutory step was genuinely attempted.

One point that is easy to miss when a contract also contains an arbitration clause is that naming a mediation venue outside India, say Singapore or London, while leaving the arbitration seat inside India, does not create a conflict by itself, but it does mean the mediation phase sits outside the Mediation Act's reach even though the arbitration that follows it remains fully governed by Indian arbitration law. Where a foreign party wants the eventual award to enjoy New York Convention enforcement in India, the arbitration seat and the governing law clause matter far more than where the earlier mediation conversation happened, and the two choices should be made deliberately rather than by default.

Common mistakes foreign counsel make when mediation enters an Indian dispute

The most frequent error is treating mediation as optional case management rather than a jurisdictional precondition, and discovering only after a suit is filed that the omission can sink it. A close second is assuming that a settlement mediated anywhere, by anyone, converts automatically into an enforceable Indian decree; Section 27's own uncertain commencement status makes that assumption risky even for a mediation conducted in India.

A third mistake is pursuing mediation in a venue outside India under the belief that the Mediation Act's international mediation provisions will travel with the parties. They will not, because Section 3(g) is tied to mediations conducted in India. A fourth is underestimating how narrow the grounds to challenge a signed settlement are under Section 28, and signing an agreement in the expectation that a court will later reopen its commercial terms on appeal.

Foreign in-house counsel instructing Indian lawyers for the first time on a mediation step should ask three direct questions before anything is filed: does Section 12A apply to this dispute at all, has Section 27 been notified for the forum where the settlement will be recorded, and is the mediator being proposed acceptable to both sides regardless of Council registration. Getting clear answers to these three questions before the first session avoids most of the disputes this section describes.

A practical sequence for a foreign company facing a commercial dispute in Delhi, Noida or Kolkata

Start by classifying the dispute. If it is a commercial dispute of specified value under the Commercial Courts Act and the relevant High Court's notified threshold, Section 12A applies and pre-institution mediation must be attempted before any suit is filed, subject only to a genuine urgent interim relief claim. If it falls outside that definition, the parties remain free to use Section 5 of the Mediation Act or a contractual mediation clause, but neither side can be compelled.

Next, decide where the mediation will actually sit. Conducting it in India keeps the Mediation Act's international mediation provisions potentially available and keeps open the possibility of Section 27 enforcement once its commencement status is confirmed for the relevant court. Conducting it abroad may suit a foreign party's comfort level but forecloses both of those statutory routes, leaving the parties dependent on the settlement being recorded as an enforceable contract or converted into an arbitral consent award instead.

Finally, record everything. Keep the mediation reference, the authority's report of failure where mediation does not succeed, and, where it does succeed, a settlement document signed and authenticated in the form the forum expects. Indian commercial courts have shown they will look closely at whether the statutory steps were genuinely taken, not merely gestured at, and a foreign party that can produce a clean paper trail is in a far stronger position whichever direction the dispute eventually goes.

A foreign in-house legal team managing this from outside India does not need to attend the mediation session itself in most cases, since Indian counsel or an authorised representative can appear on the company's behalf, but someone with real settlement authority should remain reachable during the statutory window. A mediator or opposite party asking for instructions and receiving silence from a distant head office is one of the more common reasons an otherwise promising mediation ends in a reported failure rather than a signed settlement.

Mediation in India now comes in two statutory flavours, one that can block a suit from being filed and one that nobody can be forced into, and a foreign company that mixes them up will lose time it did not need to lose. The underlying policy, encouraging settlement before litigation, is sound and consistent with how most commercial parties would rather resolve a dispute anyway.

What a foreign party still cannot rely on is a smooth, Singapore Convention style passage from a settlement reached anywhere in the world to an enforceable Indian decree. Until India ratifies that Convention and Section 27's commencement position is settled across every forum, the safer course remains to mediate the dispute inside India, in a process built to end in a decree the local courts will recognise without argument.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is pre-institution mediation compulsory for every civil dispute in India?

No. Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 makes it compulsory only for commercial suits that do not seek urgent interim relief and that meet the specified value threshold notified for the relevant High Court's commercial division. Disputes outside that definition are governed instead by the voluntary mediation provisions of the Mediation Act, 2023, or by whatever the parties' own contract provides.

Can a foreign company be forced to mediate in India before an arbitration or a lawsuit begins?

A foreign defendant cannot itself be compelled to agree to a settlement, but where the claim against it is a commercial dispute of specified value, the Indian plaintiff must attempt pre-institution mediation under Section 12A before filing suit, and failing to do so can get the suit rejected. Outside that specific category, mediation under the Mediation Act, 2023 remains voluntary for both sides.

Does a settlement reached through mediation become automatically enforceable as a court decree?

Section 27(2) of the Mediation Act, 2023 says it should be enforced as if it were a judgment or decree, but at least one 2026 High Court ruling has held that Section 27 was not yet in force in that case. Confirm the current notification status for the relevant forum, and where there is doubt, have the settlement recorded by the court itself.

Does the Mediation Act, 2023 apply to a mediation conducted outside India between an Indian company and a foreign one?

Generally no. The Act's international mediation provisions under Section 3(g) are understood to apply only where the mediation itself is conducted in India, not merely where one party is foreign or the contract is governed by Indian law. A settlement conference held abroad falls outside this chapter of the Act.

Can a mediated settlement agreement be challenged later in court?

Only on narrow grounds. Section 28 of the Mediation Act limits challenge to matters such as fraud, corruption, impersonation of a party, or the mediation of a dispute not fit for mediation, and does not permit a general reopening of the settlement's commercial terms.

Has India ratified the Singapore Convention on Mediation?

No. India signed the Convention in August 2019 but had not ratified it as of late November 2026. Ratification would require enabling domestic legislation, which has not yet been enacted, so a foreign mediated settlement reached outside India cannot currently be enforced here under that Convention.

Must a mediator be registered with the Mediation Council of India to conduct a valid mediation?

No. The Mediation Act establishes the Mediation Council of India to regulate standards and registration, but registration has not been made compulsory, and parties remain free to choose an unregistered mediator of their own preference.

What happens if a plaintiff files a commercial suit in India without attempting Section 12A mediation?

Following the Supreme Court's ruling in Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd. (2022), a commercial suit filed without a genuine prior attempt at pre-institution mediation, and without a bona fide claim for urgent interim relief, is liable to be rejected as not maintainable.
