Sovereign Immunity and an Arbitral Award Against the Government of India

Winning an arbitration against the Indian state, or against a public sector undertaking tied to it, is frequently the easier half of the dispute; collecting on that award, in India or abroad, runs into a body of sovereign immunity law that the Cairn Energy and Devas-Antrix sagas have tested more thoroughly than almost any other Indian dispute

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign investor or company that arbitrates against the Government of India, or against a public sector undertaking the government controls, and wins, still has to collect on that award. Two long-running disputes, Cairn Energy's retrospective tax award and the Devas-Antrix satellite award, show exactly how far sovereign immunity can stand between a valid award and an actual recovery, both inside India and in the courts of the United Kingdom, Australia and elsewhere.

Key points

  • Sovereign immunity is not one rule but at least two distinct questions: whether India can be sued or an award enforced against it at all (immunity from jurisdiction), and, separately, whether a specific asset of India or an India-linked entity can actually be attached once a judgment or award exists (immunity from execution).
  • Inside India, the Government of India and State Governments can be sued like any other litigant for a commercial or contractual dispute; Sections 79 to 82 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 set out the procedure, including a mandatory notice before suit and a three-month embargo on executing a decree against the government once one is obtained.
  • The harder immunity questions arise when an award creditor tries to enforce against India's assets abroad, where the enforcing country's own sovereign immunity statute, not Indian law, decides whether India's property there can be attached.
  • Cairn Energy's attempt to enforce its roughly USD 1.2 billion retrospective tax award against Air India and other Indian sovereign assets in the United States, France and elsewhere showed how far an award creditor can push an alter-ego argument against a state-owned enterprise, and the dispute was resolved, in the end, through the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 rather than through a final ruling on that argument.
  • The Devas-Antrix dispute, now well into its second decade, shows the opposite difficulty: in 2026, both the High Court of Australia and the England and Wales Court of Appeal held that India's ratification of the New York Convention, by itself, does not waive its sovereign immunity from enforcement, leaving the Devas award creditors to search for other grounds of waiver or other jurisdictions in which to proceed.
  • Indian courts take a markedly more pro-enforcement line when a foreign state, rather than India itself, is the award debtor before them: the Delhi High Court's 2021 ruling in KLA Const Technologies held that a foreign state cannot invoke sovereign immunity to resist enforcement of an award arising from an ordinary commercial transaction, and that the Central Government's consent under Section 86(3) of the CPC is not a precondition to that enforcement.
  • A foreign investor or company contracting with the Government of India or an Indian PSU should treat enforcement risk, not merely merits risk, as a factor in structuring the contract and choosing the seat of arbitration, because a clean award against the Indian state is frequently the easier half of the dispute, not the end of it.

Two different questions that get run together

Conversations about 'sovereign immunity and India' tend to blur two genuinely separate legal questions into one. The first is whether a foreign state can be sued or have an award enforced against it in a particular court at all, a question of immunity from jurisdiction or from adjudication. The second, which only arises once the first question has been answered against the state, is whether a specific piece of property belonging to that state, or to an entity closely linked to it, can actually be seized or attached to satisfy a judgment or award, a question of immunity from execution.

The two questions do not always travel together, and a court can quite consistently hold that it has jurisdiction to recognise an award against a state while still refusing to let the award creditor touch any particular asset, because that asset is found to be devoted to a sovereign rather than a commercial function, or because the enforcing state's law simply requires a separate and more demanding showing before execution is permitted even where jurisdiction exists.

This article works through both questions as they apply specifically to the Government of India and public sector undertakings closely tied to it, first within India's own courts under the Code of Civil Procedure, then in the harder and more contested setting of enforcement against India's assets abroad, using the Cairn Energy and Devas-Antrix disputes as the two most thoroughly litigated examples of how the problem actually plays out.

Suing the Government of India at home: a procedural hurdle, not an immunity

Within India, there is no general immunity that shields the Union or a State Government from being sued over an ordinary commercial or contractual dispute. Article 300 of the Constitution expressly contemplates suits by and against the Government of India and State Governments, and the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 supplies the procedural machinery for how such a suit proceeds, in Sections 79 to 82, rather than any rule barring the suit outright.

Section 79 identifies who is to be named: the Union of India in a suit by or against the Central Government, and the particular State in a suit by or against a State Government. Section 80 imposes a condition precedent, a written notice of the intended suit delivered to the appropriate government office at least two months before filing, with the plaint liable to rejection if this notice requirement is skipped, though the court can permit a suit for urgent interim relief to proceed without waiting out the notice period where leave is sought and granted under Section 80(2). Section 81 extends a public officer sued for an act done in an official capacity a measure of protection from arrest and from attachment of personal property, though not from the suit itself.

Section 82 becomes relevant at the execution stage rather than at the stage of filing: a decree against the government, or against a public officer in respect of an official act, cannot be executed until it has remained unsatisfied for three months after the date of the decree, giving the government a defined window to arrange payment voluntarily before coercive execution measures can begin. None of this amounts to sovereign immunity in the sense the term is used internationally; it is a set of procedural conditions on an otherwise ordinary suit, and an arbitral award against the Government of India is, once it reaches the execution stage under Section 36 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, treated as a decree for exactly this purpose.

Executing against government and PSU assets inside India

Once the Section 82 waiting period has passed, execution against the government proceeds, in principle, through the ordinary machinery of Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, which governs attachment and sale of a judgment debtor's property generally. In practice, award and decree holders find that identifying specific, attachable government property is considerably harder against the state than against a private commercial debtor, because so much of what the government holds is either devoted to a recognised public purpose, held in a form (such as consolidated fund balances) that execution law does not treat as ordinarily attachable property, or tied up in a public sector undertaking's own separate corporate structure.

A public sector undertaking incorporated as a company is not, as a matter of basic company law, the same legal person as the Government of India, and an award or decree against the government does not automatically authorise attachment of a PSU's own assets, nor does an award against a PSU automatically expose the government's own treasury, except in the narrow circumstances where a court is persuaded to look behind the corporate form, an argument examined in more detail below in the context of the Cairn Energy dispute with Air India.

The practical reality for most award creditors enforcing within India against the government or a PSU is that voluntary payment, prompted by the reputational and political cost of a public refusal to honour a final award, does far more work than coercive attachment ever does. Litigants who assume execution against the Indian state will unfold in the same way as execution against an ordinary corporate debtor are usually disappointed by how much slower and more negotiated the actual process tends to be, even where the underlying award itself is entirely uncontested on the merits.

The other direction: when a foreign state is the debtor in an Indian court

India's own courts have had to work out the opposite scenario, where a foreign state or its instrumentality is the award debtor and an Indian award creditor seeks enforcement here, and the resulting case law is worth setting out because it shows how restrictively Indian courts themselves read sovereign immunity when they are the enforcing forum rather than the state being sued.

The Delhi High Court decided KLA Const Technologies Pvt. Ltd. v. The Embassy of Islamic Republic of Afghanistan, together with a parallel enforcement petition against Ethiopia's Ministry of Education, in June 2021. KLA had been awarded a contract connected to Afghanistan's embassy premises in New Delhi, a dispute arose, arbitration followed, and the resulting award went unchallenged and became final. When KLA sought to enforce the award and Afghanistan did not appear to contest enforcement, the Court considered, on its own examination, whether sovereign immunity or Section 86 of the CPC stood in the way.

The Court held that a foreign state cannot claim sovereign immunity against enforcement of an arbitral award arising out of an ordinary commercial transaction, following the broader international shift away from an absolute theory of sovereign immunity toward a restrictive one that withholds immunity specifically for a state's commercial, as opposed to its sovereign or governmental, activities. The Court further held that prior consent of the Central Government under Section 86(3) of the CPC, which bars executing a decree against a foreign ruler's property without such consent, is not a precondition to enforcing an arbitral award against a foreign state, reasoning that an award arising from a commercial contract sits outside the kind of claim Section 86 was designed to protect.

Commentators have since flagged a genuine analytical gap in this reasoning: immunity from jurisdiction (whether the claim can be heard at all) and immunity from execution (whether a specific asset can be attached once a judgment exists) are treated by most legal systems as distinct questions requiring separate justification, and a ruling that a foreign state has no immunity from the enforcement proceeding itself does not, without more, establish that any particular asset found in India is actually available for attachment. The KLA ruling is nonetheless the clearest and most often cited Indian authority on the subject, and it signals that an Indian court asked to enforce a commercial arbitral award against a foreign state is likely to lean toward enforcement rather than toward deference to the foreign state's immunity claim.

Cairn Energy: an alter-ego argument against Air India

The clearest illustration of what happens when the direction is reversed, a private claimant trying to enforce against India's own assets, comes from Cairn Energy's long dispute over India's retrospective application of indirect transfer tax rules to a 2006 internal corporate reorganisation. An investment treaty tribunal awarded Cairn roughly USD 1.2 billion (reported variously as higher once interest is included) against India in December 2020, and India did not pay voluntarily.

In May 2021, Cairn filed suit in the Southern District of New York seeking to treat Air India, India's state-owned carrier at the time, as an alter ego of the Indian government for the specific purpose of attaching its assets, including aircraft, to satisfy the award. The argument drew on the framework American courts use for piercing the separateness of a state-owned enterprise from its sovereign parent, under which a presumption of separateness can be overcome where the enterprise is shown to be so dominated by the state, or so used for the state's own purposes, that treating it as a distinct entity would work a fraud or injustice on the creditor.

Cairn pursued parallel enforcement steps outside the United States as well; reporting from mid-2021 describes Indian government-linked assets in Paris, including residential properties used by Indian government personnel, being attached as part of the same enforcement campaign, which is widely credited, alongside Vodafone's own long-running dispute over the same retrospective tax provision, with helping to push the Indian government toward a legislative resolution rather than a continued fight asset by asset across multiple jurisdictions.

That resolution came through the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021, which received presidential assent on 13 August 2021 and withdrew the retrospective effect of the indirect transfer tax provisions that had given rise to the Cairn and Vodafone disputes in the first place. The relief it offered was conditional: a taxpayer accepting the Act's terms had to withdraw all pending litigation, arbitration and enforcement proceedings worldwide and give an undertaking not to pursue damages claims against the Indian government. Cairn accepted these terms, received a refund reported at roughly Rs 7,900 crore, and formally withdrew its global enforcement proceedings, including the Air India suit, during the latter part of 2021. Because the matter settled on these terms, no American court ever issued a final ruling on whether Air India's alter-ego status would have supported attachment of its assets, leaving that specific question technically open even today for a future case with similar facts and a less cooperative sovereign defendant.

Devas-Antrix: the award that keeps finding new jurisdictions and losing in most of them

Where the Cairn dispute settled before any court had to rule definitively on piercing a PSU's corporate veil, the Devas-Antrix dispute has gone the opposite route, generating final, reasoned rulings in multiple jurisdictions over more than a decade, almost all of them against the award creditors on the specific question of immunity from execution, even as the underlying awards themselves remain formally valid and unpaid.

The dispute traces back to a 2005 agreement under which Devas Multimedia, an Indian company whose investors included entities organised in Mauritius and elsewhere, was to lease S-band spectrum capacity from Antrix Corporation Limited, the commercial arm of India's space agency. The Indian government annulled the agreement in 2011 on security grounds, and the Devas-linked investors pursued both a commercial arbitration against Antrix and an investment treaty arbitration against India under the India-Mauritius bilateral investment treaty, obtaining separate awards that were later assigned in part to a group of claimants including CCDM Holdings LLC and affiliated entities.

Those award holders have since sought recognition and enforcement in multiple jurisdictions, with starkly different procedural postures reported across them: Canadian proceedings in Québec reportedly found, as of a late 2024 appellate ruling, that India was not immune from the jurisdiction of the Québec courts for the purpose of recognising the award there, a result favourable to the Devas side on the jurisdictional question even if it left open, for a later stage, what assets, if any, could actually be reached in execution. The Supreme Court of Canada has since refused India's application for leave to appeal that ruling (reported in 2025), leaving the Quebec Court of Appeal's finding as the final word in that jurisdiction on the immunity-from-jurisdiction question, though execution against specific assets remains a separate and still-contested matter there.

The position in Australia and the United Kingdom has gone the other way, and on a narrower, specific legal point that is worth stating precisely. In CCDM Holdings LLC v. Republic of India, decided 8 April 2026, the High Court of Australia unanimously dismissed the Devas-linked claimants' appeal, holding that India did not waive its immunity under Australia's Foreign States Immunities Act 1985 merely by becoming a party to the 1958 New York Convention, applying the standard the same Court had set in 2023 that a treaty-based waiver of sovereign immunity must be clear and unmistakeable, and rejecting the analogy the claimants drew to the different enforcement regime applicable to ICSID awards. The England and Wales Court of Appeal reached the same conclusion on the same narrow point in CC/Devas (Mauritius) Ltd & Ors v. The Republic of India, decided 24 June 2026, expressly following the Australian court's reasoning and holding that India's ratification of the New York Convention is not, without something more, a submission to the jurisdiction of the English courts or a waiver of immunity under the UK's State Immunity Act 1978. The claimants have since obtained permission from the UK Supreme Court to appeal that ruling further, a permission granted on 2 November 2026, with no hearing date yet reported.

Two things follow from reading the Australian and English rulings together with the Québec result rather than any one of them in isolation. First, the specific argument that mere ratification of the New York Convention amounts to an implied waiver of sovereign immunity has now failed, on reasoned judgments, in two separate common-law jurisdictions, and claimants relying on that particular argument elsewhere should treat it as a weak one going forward rather than a promising route. Second, immunity from jurisdiction and immunity from execution genuinely are separate battles that can go differently in different courts even on materially similar facts, since a jurisdiction can accept that it may hear and recognise the claim (as Québec's courts reportedly did) while a different jurisdiction's courts, applying a different immunity statute, decline even that first step on the specific waiver theory advanced (as Australia's and England's courts did).

What the restrictive immunity doctrine actually distinguishes

Running through the Cairn, Devas and KLA disputes is the same underlying doctrinal move, the shift most of the world's major legal systems have made away from treating a sovereign state as absolutely immune from suit and execution in any circumstance, toward a restrictive doctrine that withholds immunity specifically where the state has acted in a commercial capacity, as a private trader might, rather than in the exercise of a distinctly governmental or sovereign function.

The commercial-activity distinction does real work at the jurisdiction stage, where India itself has successfully argued, and the Delhi High Court has independently held with respect to other states appearing before it, that a state entering an ordinary commercial contract (a satellite spectrum lease, a construction contract, an investment protected by a bilateral treaty) has stepped outside whatever immunity its sovereign status might otherwise confer for that specific transaction. It does considerably less work at the execution stage, where most immunity statutes, the US Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, the UK State Immunity Act 1978 and Australia's Foreign States Immunities Act 1985 among them, impose an additional and more demanding requirement before a specific asset can actually be attached, commonly that the asset itself is used for commercial purposes in the enforcing jurisdiction, separate from and independent of whether the underlying claim arose from commercial activity.

This is precisely the gap the Cairn dispute never had to resolve (because it settled) and the Devas dispute keeps running into in a different form (whether a treaty ratification, rather than conduct connected to a specific asset, can waive immunity at all). A sovereign or PSU asset can be entirely uncontroversial in its commercial character and still be protected from execution if the particular immunity statute in play demands a showing the award creditor has not yet made, or asks a different question entirely, such as whether the state waived immunity through its own prior conduct, that the commercial character of the underlying transaction does not by itself answer.

What this means for a foreign investor negotiating with the Indian state

A foreign company or investor entering a contract with the Government of India, a state government, or a public sector undertaking, whether an infrastructure concession, a spectrum or resource lease, a supply contract, or an investment covered by a bilateral treaty, should treat enforcement risk as a distinct item of commercial diligence from the start, not as a question to address only once a dispute has already arisen and an award has already been won.

Seat selection carries real consequences here beyond the usual considerations of neutrality and procedural efficiency: an award rendered at a seat whose courts have already shown themselves willing to pierce a state-owned enterprise's corporate separateness, or to find a clear waiver of immunity on relatively modest facts, is a meaningfully more valuable asset to hold against the Indian state than one rendered at a seat whose courts take the narrower view the Australian and English courts have now both taken on the New York Convention waiver argument specifically.

A party negotiating the underlying contract can also do a certain amount of groundwork that pure litigation strategy cannot fix after the fact: identifying, at the negotiation stage, whether the counterparty is the government directly, a PSU with meaningful operational and financial independence, or a PSU that functions, in practical terms, as little more than a government department in corporate dress, and adjusting the commercial terms, security arrangements or guarantee structure accordingly, rather than assuming that any award eventually obtained will be equally easy to collect regardless of which of these three the actual counterparty turns out to be.

Structuring the investment through a treaty-protected vehicle, as the Devas investors did through their Mauritius entities, buys access to an investor-state arbitration route and the India-Mauritius treaty's own substantive protections, but it does not, on the evidence of the Devas litigation itself, buy an easier path to collecting on the resulting award once obtained. The treaty route and the enforcement route are separate problems, decided under separate bodies of law, and a well-structured investment can still end up holding an award that is extremely difficult to convert into an actual payment, for reasons that have nothing to do with how carefully the original investment structure was put together.

Where the law remains unsettled

Several questions this body of law raises are genuinely open rather than merely under-litigated. Whether an Indian court would itself apply the Bancec-style alter-ego test American courts use, or some different standard altogether, to decide when a PSU's assets can be reached for a liability that is formally the Government of India's own, was never finally tested in the Cairn dispute because that dispute settled, and no later Indian ruling squarely on this point has been reported.

Whether the Devas-linked claimants can identify some basis for waiver other than mere ratification of the New York Convention, in a jurisdiction that has not yet ruled on the question, also remains open, and the pending UK Supreme Court appeal may add a further, more considered layer of reasoning to what the Court of Appeal decided in June 2026 without necessarily resolving every jurisdiction's position in the same direction. Readers tracking either saga should treat any summary of 'where things stand', including this one, as accurate only as of the date given, given how often new rulings in new jurisdictions have continued to appear across both disputes.

What is settled, across every jurisdiction this piece has described, is the basic structural point that a sovereign immunity defence and a commercial-activity rebuttal to that defence are argued and decided separately at the jurisdiction stage and the execution stage, and that a claimant who wins decisively at one stage can still lose decisively at the other. Any party assessing the practical value of an award against the Government of India or an Indian PSU, or against a foreign state in an Indian court, should keep that two-stage structure in view rather than treating 'sovereign immunity' as a single question with a single answer.

Sovereign immunity law, as applied to the Government of India and the public sector undertakings tied to it, splits cleanly into a domestic question that Indian procedural law answers with comparative ease, the state can be sued and a decree or award against it can eventually be executed, subject to defined procedural steps, and an international question that has produced years of litigation across multiple jurisdictions with genuinely mixed results, as the Cairn Energy and Devas-Antrix disputes both show in their own, different ways.

A foreign party contracting with the Indian state, or arbitrating against it, should treat the gap between winning an award and actually collecting on it as a real and separate risk to be managed from the outset, through seat selection, contract structuring and realistic diligence on the counterparty's own degree of independence from the government, rather than as a formality that a favourable award on the merits will automatically resolve.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the Government of India be sued like any other party in a commercial dispute?

Yes. There is no general immunity shielding the Union or a State Government from suit over a commercial or contractual dispute within India; Sections 79 to 82 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 set out the applicable procedure, including a mandatory pre-suit notice and a three-month embargo on executing any decree obtained against the government.

Is enforcing an arbitral award against the Government of India the same as enforcing it against a private company?

Not in practice. The legal machinery (execution under the Code of Civil Procedure once the award is treated as a decree under Section 36 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996) is largely the same, but identifying specific attachable government property is considerably harder than against a private debtor, and voluntary payment, prompted by reputational and political pressure, resolves far more cases than coercive attachment does.

Can an award creditor attach the assets of an Indian public sector undertaking to satisfy a debt owed by the Government of India itself?

Only in narrow circumstances, and this remains a genuinely unsettled question under Indian law. A PSU incorporated as a company is a distinct legal person from the government, and a creditor would ordinarily have to show the PSU is effectively an alter ego of the state, an argument Cairn Energy raised against Air India in US courts in 2021 that was never finally decided because the underlying dispute settled.

What happened to Cairn Energy's attempt to seize Air India's assets?

Cairn filed suit in the United States in May 2021 to attach Air India's assets as India's alter ego, and separately pursued attachments against Indian government-linked property in France, but the dispute was resolved through the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021, under which Cairn accepted a refund of its retrospective tax payment and withdrew all its global enforcement proceedings, so no court ever ruled definitively on the alter-ego argument.

Did India waive its sovereign immunity by signing the New York Convention?

No, according to both the High Court of Australia (CCDM Holdings v. Republic of India, April 2026) and the England and Wales Court of Appeal (CC/Devas v. Republic of India, June 2026), each holding that mere ratification of the Convention does not amount to a clear and unmistakeable waiver of sovereign immunity under their respective immunity statutes. The claimants in the English case have since been granted permission to appeal to the UK Supreme Court.

Can a foreign state claim sovereign immunity to resist enforcement of an award in an Indian court?

Generally not for an award arising from a commercial transaction. The Delhi High Court held in KLA Const Technologies v. Embassy of the Islamic Republic of Afghanistan (June 2021) that a foreign state cannot invoke sovereign immunity against enforcement of such an award, and that prior Central Government consent under Section 86(3) of the Code of Civil Procedure is not required for that enforcement.

Does winning an arbitration against the Government of India guarantee eventual payment?

Not automatically. A valid, final award is a necessary step, but collecting on it, whether through voluntary payment, domestic execution, or enforcement against Indian assets abroad, is a separate and often significantly harder process, as both the Cairn Energy and Devas-Antrix disputes illustrate over their many years of related litigation.
