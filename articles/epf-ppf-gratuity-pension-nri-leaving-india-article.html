EPF, PPF, Gratuity and Pension When You Leave India: What an NRI Actually Needs to Do

Four separate schemes, four separate sets of rules, and a residency change that does not treat any of them the same way.

By Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Someone who has worked in India for a decade and then takes up a job abroad usually leaves behind four different pots of money: an Employees' Provident Fund balance, perhaps a Public Provident Fund account opened years earlier at a post office or bank, a gratuity payable from the last employer, and an entitlement, or a near-entitlement, to a pension under the Employees' Pension Scheme. Each of these sits under a different law, with a different test for what happens once the person is no longer resident in India, and the four tests do not line up with each other at all. A rule that is generous for EPF withdrawal has no equivalent in PPF. A restriction aimed at a narrow category of foreign workers gets applied in casual conversation to ordinary emigrating Indians who are not covered by it. This piece works through what actually changes, scheme by scheme, for someone becoming an NRI, and what a family needs to do when the account holder has since died.

Key points

  • Paragraph 69 of the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme, 1952 allows full withdrawal of the EPF balance on migration from India for permanent settlement or employment abroad, a separate and more direct route than the two-month unemployment wait that applies to an ordinary resignation claim.
  • The EPFO's 'International Worker' category, with its own restrictions including a withdrawal age of 58 for workers from countries without a Social Security Agreement with India, is a narrow, specifically defined classification. It does not automatically catch an ordinary Indian employee who resigns from an Indian job and emigrates to work for a foreign employer in a non-agreement country such as the United States, the United Kingdom or Australia.
  • EPF withdrawal remains tax-free only after five years of continuous service, with Section 10(12) carving out an exception where employment ended for reasons beyond the employee's control; a withdrawal before five years for an ordinary voluntary resignation to emigrate does not fall within that exception and is taxable.
  • A Public Provident Fund account cannot be opened by an NRI, but an account opened while the holder was still resident can run to its original 15-year maturity. Since 1 October 2024, such an account earns no interest at all for the period it is held as an NRI account, and it cannot be extended beyond that maturity in the way a resident's account can be.
  • Gratuity under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 is not affected by the employee's residency status at all; what changes is the tax exemption calculation and the TDS position once the recipient's residential status for tax purposes changes.
  • A pension under the Employees' Pension Scheme requires ten years of pensionable service; an NRI who leaves with less either takes a one-time withdrawal benefit or holds a scheme certificate to preserve service for a later claim, and the position does not become simpler merely because the member now lives abroad.
  • On a member's death, EPF, EPS pension and gratuity all route first through a registered nominee, with the legal heir route available only where no valid nomination exists, and each of the three benefits needs its own claim form and its own supporting documents even where a single composite form covers more than one.

Four schemes that do not share a definition of 'leaving'

A person who worked in India, contributed to the Employees' Provident Fund through an employer, perhaps opened a Public Provident Fund account at a nationalised bank or post office, and built up a decade or more of service toward a pension, is not dealing with one retirement benefit when they move abroad. They are dealing with four, each created by a different statute or scheme, each administered by a different authority, and each with its own idea of what residency even means for its purposes. The Employees' Provident Fund Scheme asks whether the member has migrated for permanent settlement or employment abroad. The Public Provident Fund scheme asks whether the account holder's residential status under the Foreign Exchange Management Act has changed. The Payment of Gratuity Act does not ask about residency at all, though the Income Tax Act does, once the gratuity is actually paid. The Employees' Pension Scheme cares only about years of contributory service, wherever the member now lives.

This matters because advice that is correct for one of the four schemes is routinely applied, wrongly, to another. A person told, correctly, that EPF allows immediate full withdrawal on emigration often assumes the same logic extends to PPF, where it does not, or to EPS pension eligibility, where years of service rather than residency is the only question that matters. The sections that follow take each scheme in turn, not because the four are unrelated, but because treating them as a single combined 'provident fund problem' is the single most common source of the mistakes families actually make.

EPF withdrawal on leaving India: the emigration route and the resignation route

An ordinary EPF member who resigns from a job without any plan to emigrate has to wait out a period of unemployment, commonly described as two months, before the account qualifies for a full withdrawal on the basis of unemployment under the standard provisions of Paragraph 69 of the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme, 1952. A member who is migrating from India for permanent settlement abroad, or to take up employment abroad, has access to a separate, more direct basis for full withdrawal under the same Paragraph 69, without needing to sit out that waiting period first. The practical effect is that someone who has accepted a job abroad and is leaving India for that reason, rather than someone who has simply resigned and has not yet found the next position, can generally file the claim on the emigration basis once the departure is confirmed, rather than waiting.

The claim itself is filed online through the EPFO member portal using the member's Universal Account Number, selecting the relevant reason code for the withdrawal, and is subject to the usual requirement that the account be seeded with a valid Aadhaar number and bank account for the transfer to go through. None of this requires the member to have already left India; it is common, and generally sensible, to file the claim before departure while Indian mobile numbers, email access and physical documents are still easy to produce if the claim is queried.

One recurring point of confusion deserves to be stated plainly. The emigration-based withdrawal under Paragraph 69 is a route available to an ordinary Indian EPF member leaving India, and it should not be confused with the separate set of rules that apply to a defined category called an 'International Worker', which is addressed next. The two are often run together in casual commentary, and the restrictions that genuinely apply to international workers get wrongly assumed to apply to every NRI, which is not correct.

The 'International Worker' category, and why most emigrating Indians are not in it

The EPF Scheme, through Paragraph 83, defines a specific category called an International Worker, and the age-58 withdrawal restriction that is often mentioned in connection with NRIs and provident fund belongs to this category, not to ordinary emigration under Paragraph 69. An International Worker is either a foreign national working in India for an establishment covered by the EPF Act, or an Indian employee who has worked, or is going to work, in a foreign country with which India has signed a Social Security Agreement, and who is eligible for that country's social security benefits by virtue of that agreement. India currently has such agreements with somewhere in the range of eighteen to twenty countries, a list that includes Germany, Belgium, Switzerland, France, the Netherlands, Japan, Australia and Canada among others, but does not include the United States or the United Kingdom, the two destinations where the largest numbers of Indian professionals actually move.

An Indian employee who resigns from an Indian job and takes up employment with a new employer in the United States or the United Kingdom is, on this definition, simply an ordinary EPF member leaving India, governed by Paragraph 69's emigration provisions, not an International Worker under Paragraph 83. The age-58 withdrawal restriction, and the separate requirement that the worker have ceased employment with an EPF-covered establishment before claiming, apply to international workers from countries without a Social Security Agreement with India, a category built around foreign nationals working in India rather than around Indian citizens emigrating to work abroad. Someone who has read that restriction somewhere and is worried it applies to their own move to a non-agreement country should, in the ordinary case, find that it does not, though the specific facts of unusual postings, secondments or multi-country careers can complicate this and are worth checking individually rather than assumed from a general rule.

The practical consequence is that the vast majority of Indians who leave for work in the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia or similar destinations can use the ordinary emigration route under Paragraph 69 without running into the International Worker restrictions at all. Someone posted abroad by an Indian employer to a Social Security Agreement country, continuing to contribute to the Indian scheme under a Certificate of Coverage, sits in a genuinely different position and should take advice specific to that posting rather than rely on anything in this piece written with the ordinary emigrating employee in mind.

Interest, dormancy and what happens if the claim is delayed

An EPF account that stops receiving contributions does not close itself or forfeit the balance, but it does eventually stop earning interest if it remains inoperative, broadly once a meaningful period has passed without contributions and without the member reaching retirement age, though the exact treatment of 'inoperative' accounts has itself been the subject of periodic EPFO clarification and has shifted over the years in the member's favour on some points. The safer course for anyone emigrating is not to rely on the account continuing to earn interest indefinitely while the withdrawal claim is deferred, but to file the claim once eligible, since there is little practical benefit in leaving the balance with EPFO once emigration has made it withdrawable and some risk in letting the account become harder to trace years later.

A related and frequently underestimated problem is that an EPF claim filed from abroad, with an Indian bank account that has since been converted to NRO status, a changed mobile number, and documents that may need re-verification, tends to take noticeably longer to process than the same claim filed just before departure. Filing early, while the member's details on the EPFO portal still match an active Indian phone number and a bank account the employer's HR team can confirm, avoids a meaningful share of the delay that otherwise shows up in the family's own experience of the process.

Tax on the EPF withdrawal: the five-year line

Section 10(12) of the Income Tax Act, and its restated position under the Income-tax Act, 2025, exempts a recognised provident fund withdrawal from tax only where the employee has completed five years of continuous service. An employee who resigns before five years and withdraws the balance, including on the basis of emigration, does not get the exemption merely because the withdrawal reason is emigration rather than an ordinary job change; emigration is a basis for withdrawing without the usual unemployment wait, not a basis for tax exemption in its own right.

There is a genuine exception, but it is narrower than many withdrawal guides suggest. Where employment ends for reasons beyond the employee's control, such as the employee's ill health, discontinuance or contraction of the employer's business, or completion of the specific project for which the employee was engaged, the withdrawal can remain exempt even before five years of service. An employee who voluntarily resigns in order to take up a better opportunity abroad does not fall within this exception merely because the decision to leave was the employee's own considered choice to emigrate; the exception is aimed at terminations the employee did not choose, not at voluntary departures for personal or career reasons, however sound those reasons are.

Where the withdrawal is taxable, tax is deducted at source on payment if the balance exceeds the prescribed threshold, currently described in most current guidance as fifty thousand rupees, and the deduction can be at a materially higher rate where the claimant has not furnished a PAN or is treated as a non-resident without the appropriate declarations in place. Anyone planning to emigrate with less than five years of service completed should budget for this tax exposure rather than assume the EPF balance will arrive in full, and should keep Form 15G or 15H considerations in mind only to the extent they remain relevant after residency has changed, since these declarations are generally aimed at resident taxpayers below the taxable threshold rather than at NRIs.

EPS pension: ten years is the only number that matters

The Employees' Pension Scheme, 1995 sets a flat rule that has nothing to do with where the member currently lives: a member needs ten years of pensionable service to become eligible for a monthly pension at all. A member who leaves India, with or without emigration, having completed ten years or more of pensionable service retains that eligibility and can claim the pension on reaching the eligible age, with payment arranged into whatever account, including an appropriate NRI account, the member designates, though the practical mechanics of receiving a modest monthly pension into a foreign bank account from India are not always straightforward and are worth confirming with the regional EPFO office handling the specific case.

A member who leaves with less than ten years of pensionable service has two options rather than a pension claim: a one-time withdrawal benefit, filed on Form 10C, calculated on a fixed table rather than as a return of the member's own pension contributions, or a scheme certificate, which preserves the service period on record so that it can later be combined with a further period of pensionable service, for instance after returning to India and re-joining EPF-covered employment, to eventually cross the ten-year threshold. Someone who is confident they will not return to EPF-covered employment in India has little reason to hold a scheme certificate rather than take the withdrawal benefit, but someone who sees even a modest chance of returning to Indian employment in the future should weigh the scheme certificate option carefully before closing it off by taking the withdrawal benefit instead, since the two are mutually exclusive choices at the time of leaving.

Nothing about EPS eligibility changes because the member has become an NRI, and residency status does not appear anywhere in the ten-year test. Where confusion arises in practice, it is almost always because the member or the family has conflated the EPF balance, which is withdrawable on emigration, with the EPS entitlement, which depends only on service length and is not accelerated or altered by emigration at all.

PPF: the account an NRI cannot open but can keep

The Public Provident Fund scheme has barred new accounts by non-resident Indians since a notification issued in 2003. This does not disturb an account that was validly opened while the holder was still a resident Indian; such an account continues and can run to its original maturity, which is fifteen years from the end of the financial year in which it was opened, even after the holder's residency status changes. What an NRI loses, compared with a resident account holder, is the right to extend the account in blocks of five years once it matures, an option available to residents but not, under the current position, to NRIs, whose accounts must be closed at the original fifteen-year maturity rather than rolled forward.

Account holders are required to notify the bank or post office handling the account once their residential status changes, within a defined period after the change, and the practical experience of many NRIs is that this notification does not always happen promptly, either because the holder did not realise the obligation existed or because changing a form at a branch from abroad is simply inconvenient. The consequence of not notifying is addressed in the next section, and it has become considerably more significant since a change that took effect in October 2024.

The October 2024 change: why an undisclosed NRI account now earns nothing

A set of rules issued in 2024 by the Department of Economic Affairs, taking effect from 1 October 2024, specifically addressed PPF accounts, among other small savings instruments, held by account holders whose residency status had changed to non-resident without being disclosed or updated with the account-holding institution. Before that date, an NRI-held PPF account that had not been flagged as such continued, in practice, to earn the ordinary post office savings account rate rather than the full PPF rate, a position that was itself the product of an earlier, long-running dispute over what rate such accounts should earn after an initial 2017 notification suggested they should earn no interest at all once the account became an NRI account.

From 1 October 2024, the position changed again, and this time in a direction that applies regardless of whether the account was ever disclosed: a PPF account held by an NRI earns no interest at all for any period during which it is held in NRI status, continuing only as a holding vehicle for the principal until the original fifteen-year maturity is reached, at which point it must be closed. This is a materially different outcome from simply losing a higher rate in favour of a lower one; it is the complete loss of return on funds that may sit in the account for a period of years before maturity. Anyone with an existing PPF account who is emigrating, or has already emigrated without updating their status, should treat this as a strong reason to plan the account's closure and the transfer of funds elsewhere as soon as the facts allow, rather than leaving a materially larger sum parked in an account earning nothing for the remainder of its term.

Because this is a relatively recent change to a scheme with a history of shifting positions on exactly this question, anyone relying on an older description of PPF-for-NRI interest rates, including some commentary and bank literature that still describes the pre-2024 position, should treat that description as outdated and confirm the current position with the account-holding bank or post office before making any decision based on it.

Moving PPF and EPF proceeds out of India

Proceeds from either a matured or prematurely closed PPF account, or from an EPF withdrawal, are paid into an Indian bank account, and for an NRI that generally means the Non-Resident Ordinary, or NRO, account rather than directly into a foreign account or an NRE account, since the funds originate from income or savings accumulated while the person was still resident in India. From the NRO account, the holder can remit the funds abroad subject to the usual Foreign Exchange Management Act limits and the Reserve Bank of India's current remittance scheme, which generally permits remittance up to the prescribed annual limit for an NRO account holder on production of the required chartered accountant certification confirming the source and tax position of the funds, typically Form 15CA and, where applicable, Form 15CB.

This step is often treated as an afterthought once the EPF or PPF claim itself has been settled, but the certification and documentation requirements for moving the money abroad can take as long as the underlying withdrawal claim itself, particularly where the chartered accountant preparing the certificate needs to establish the tax treatment of a withdrawal that occurred years before the remittance is actually requested. Keeping the withdrawal statement, the TDS certificate if any tax was deducted, and a clear record of which part of the amount is principal and which is interest or growth, from the point the claim is settled rather than reconstructing it later, makes this later step considerably easier.

Gratuity: the one benefit residency simply does not touch

Of the four schemes covered in this piece, gratuity under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 is the most straightforward from a pure entitlement standpoint. The Act conditions eligibility on continuous service, ordinarily five years except where the termination is by death or disablement, and on separation from service, whether by resignation, retirement, or termination; nothing in the Act makes residency, citizenship or the location of the former employee relevant to whether gratuity is payable at all. An employee who completes the qualifying service and then resigns to emigrate is entitled to gratuity on exactly the same basis as an employee who resigns to join another employer within India.

What does change, once the recipient's tax residential status shifts, is the calculation of the tax exemption on the gratuity amount and the applicable TDS position at the time of payment. The exemption under Section 10(10) of the Income Tax Act caps the tax-free amount at the lower of the actual gratuity received, a statutory ceiling that currently stands at twenty lakh rupees for employees covered by the Payment of Gratuity Act, and a formula based on last drawn salary and years of service; amounts beyond that ceiling are taxable in the recipient's hands according to the applicable slab rates for the relevant assessment year, and the employer's obligation to withhold tax at source on the taxable portion does not disappear simply because the payment is being made to someone who has, by the time of payment, already left India.

A separate point worth flagging for employers rather than only for departing employees: an employer paying out gratuity to someone whose address of record and bank account have already shifted to an NRO account should confirm the correct withholding treatment before release, since getting this wrong creates a compliance exposure for the employer that is entirely separate from, and in addition to, any dispute the departing employee might have about the amount calculated.

Claims after death: nomination comes first, legal heirs come second

Where the account holder or member has died, whether before or after emigrating, each of these benefits is claimed through broadly the same structure, though the forms differ. EPF dues are claimed on Form 20 by the person entitled, which in the first instance is whoever the member named as nominee in Form 2 filed with the employer or EPFO; where no valid nomination was made, EPFO pays the amount to the family members defined under the EPF scheme itself, generally the spouse, children and certain dependents, and a legal heir claiming without a nomination in place is typically asked to produce a legal heir certificate or succession certificate to establish entitlement, particularly where more than one person could plausibly claim.

A monthly pension under the Employees' Pension Scheme, where the member had completed ten years of pensionable service and had either started drawing the pension or died while still in eligible service, converts on death into a family pension payable to the surviving spouse for life and, separately, to eligible children below a specified age for a defined number of years, claimed on Form 10D with the death certificate, marriage certificate and children's birth certificates as supporting documents; EPFO's own Employees' Deposit Linked Insurance cover, a separate benefit most members do not think about until a death claim arises, is claimed on Form 5IF and can add a meaningful lump sum on top of the EPF and pension claims.

Gratuity on death is payable to the nominee under the employer's gratuity nomination, or, absent a valid nomination, to the legal heirs, under the same Payment of Gratuity Act framework that governs an ordinary gratuity claim, which means a family pursuing a gratuity claim after a death should expect the same kind of documentary requirement, proof of relationship, death certificate, and in the absence of nomination a legal heir certificate, that applies to the EPF and pension claims running in parallel. Where the deceased was an NRI at the time of death, none of this changes the entitlement itself; it adds the practical complication of dealing with Indian claim forms, certificates and sometimes court-issued succession documents from abroad, usually through a power of attorney holder in India rather than in person, a process our existing guidance on using a power of attorney from abroad and on NRI succession more generally covers in more detail than is useful to repeat here.

Pulling the threads together: a practical sequence

For someone who knows they are emigrating in the near term, the sequence that avoids most of the difficulties described above starts well before departure. The EPF claim on the emigration basis should be filed once the new employment or settlement plan is confirmed and the EPFO portal details, mobile number and bank account are still current, rather than deferred until after arrival abroad. Anyone with fewer than five years of EPF service should separately confirm the likely tax treatment of the withdrawal before relying on the full amount for relocation expenses. An existing PPF account should be reviewed specifically in light of the October 2024 zero-interest position, with a considered decision about whether to let it run to maturity earning nothing or to look at the premature closure route, which is generally available after five completed years from account opening subject to an interest penalty, rather than assumed to be a harmless asset left running in the background.

A pending EPS entitlement with less than ten years of service calls for a deliberate choice between the withdrawal benefit and a scheme certificate, made with some honest assessment of whether a return to Indian employment is realistically on the cards, since the choice cannot easily be reversed once made. Gratuity, by contrast, needs comparatively little advance planning beyond confirming the employer's calculation and keeping the documentation for the later tax exemption and TDS position in order. None of these four steps depends on any of the others, which is precisely why they are so often handled inconsistently, with the one that happens to come up in conversation with a colleague or a bank relationship manager getting attention while the rest wait.

The four schemes covered here were never designed as a single package, and treating them as one leads to the recurring mistakes this piece has tried to separate out: assuming EPF's emigration-friendly withdrawal rule extends to PPF, assuming a restriction aimed at a narrow category of international workers applies to an ordinary emigrating employee, assuming EPS pension eligibility turns on residency rather than years of service, and assuming gratuity entitlement itself is affected by where the former employee now lives when only its tax treatment actually is.

Where this piece has had to hedge, on the exact current treatment of long-dormant EPF accounts, on whether premature PPF closure is uniformly available to NRIs across every bank and post office branch, and on the precise mechanics of remitting pension payments to a foreign account, that reflects genuine inconsistency in the secondary material available rather than an attempt to avoid giving a clear answer; those points are worth confirming directly with EPFO, the specific bank or post office, and a chartered accountant before a family relies on them for a particular claim.

Frequently Asked Questions

I am resigning from my Indian job to take up employment in the United States. Does the age-58 restriction on international workers apply to my EPF withdrawal?

Generally, no. That restriction applies to a defined category called an International Worker under Paragraph 83 of the EPF Scheme, which for an outbound Indian employee requires that the destination country have a Social Security Agreement with India. The United States does not have such an agreement with India, so an ordinary employee emigrating there for work is not an International Worker on that basis, and can use the ordinary emigration withdrawal route under Paragraph 69 instead.

Will my EPF withdrawal be tax-free if I am leaving because I got a good job offer abroad?

Only if you have completed five years of continuous service. The exception for terminations due to reasons beyond the employee's control, such as ill health or discontinuance of the employer's business, does not generally cover a voluntary resignation taken in order to pursue better employment abroad, however sound that decision is. Below five years of service, expect the withdrawal to be taxable, with TDS deducted at source depending on the amount and your documentation.

Can I keep contributing to my PPF account after I become an NRI?

No. NRIs have not been permitted to open new PPF accounts since 2003, and an existing account cannot be meaningfully continued in the sense of ongoing favourable returns once residency changes. The account can run to its original fifteen-year maturity, but since 1 October 2024 it earns no interest at all while held in NRI status, and it cannot be extended beyond maturity the way a resident's account can.

I have an old PPF account and never told the bank I had moved abroad. What happens now?

You remain obligated to disclose the change of residential status, and the current rules apply the zero-interest position from 1 October 2024 regardless of whether the change was disclosed. Continuing not to disclose does not preserve a better interest rate; it mainly creates a compliance gap that complicates the account's eventual closure and the transfer of funds abroad.

I only have seven years of EPF service when I am leaving India for good. What happens to my pension?

Since the Employees' Pension Scheme requires ten years of pensionable service for a monthly pension, seven years is not enough on its own. You can take a one-time withdrawal benefit calculated on a fixed table, or hold a scheme certificate that preserves the service period in case you later return to EPF-covered employment in India and can add further service toward the ten-year threshold. The two options are mutually exclusive at the time you leave.

Does my gratuity entitlement get reduced or affected because I am resigning to move abroad rather than retiring in India?

No, the entitlement itself under the Payment of Gratuity Act does not depend on why you are leaving or where you are going, only on your qualifying service and the fact of separation. What does change is the tax treatment once you are paid, since the usual Section 10(10) exemption limits and TDS rules apply based on the amount and your residential status at the time of payment, not a different set of rules because you are emigrating.

My father passed away after moving abroad, and we are trying to claim his EPF, pension and gratuity from India. Where do we start?

Start by checking whether he left a registered nominee on EPF Form 2 and under his employer's gratuity nomination, since a valid nominee is paid ahead of other family members. If there was no nomination, or you are not the nominee, you will generally need a legal heir certificate or succession certificate. EPF dues are claimed on Form 20, the pension on Form 10D if he had ten or more years of pensionable service, and the EDLI insurance benefit separately on Form 5IF, each with its own supporting documents.

Should I close my PPF account before it matures, given the new zero-interest rule for NRIs?

That depends on how long remains until the original fifteen-year maturity and on the premature closure terms your specific bank or post office applies, which generally allow closure after five completed years from account opening subject to an interest reduction penalty. Where several years remain before maturity, closing earlier and moving the funds to an NRO account or another investment is usually worth comparing against simply letting a large sum sit earning nothing until maturity.
