NRIs as Directors, Shareholders and Founders in India: Residency Rules, DIN, Signing and Banking From Abroad

An NRI can sit on an Indian board, hold shares in an Indian company, or run an LLP, without ever setting foot in the country for the transaction itself. What actually trips founders up is not the headline rule everyone has heard of, but the three or four adjoining ones that get confused with it.

By Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 5 November 2026 · Law stated as on 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

There is no bar on a non-resident Indian, or for that matter a foreign national, being a director, shareholder or designated partner of an Indian company or LLP. What the law does impose is a short list of residency, filing and reporting conditions that attach to the entity rather than to the individual, and founders who get one of these conditions confused with another end up either non-compliant without realising it or, just as often, paying for compliance steps they never needed. This guide sets out which rule applies where, and treats the detailed mechanics of FEMA and FDI reporting and of the annual DIR-3 KYC filing as settled ground covered elsewhere, linked below, rather than repeating them here.

Key points

  • Section 149(3) of the Companies Act, 2013 requires every company, not every NRI-owned company specifically, to have at least one director who has stayed in India for a total of at least 182 days during the financial year, with a pro-rated figure for a company's first, part-year, financial year. An all-NRI board is not permitted; at least one resident director is mandatory even in a wholly NRI-owned or foreign-owned company.
  • The equivalent rule for a Limited Liability Partnership is a different threshold under a different statute, and the two are routinely confused. Since a 2021-22 amendment to the LLP Act, 2008, the test for a designated partner's residence in India is a stay of at least 120 days during the financial year, not 182 days; at least one of the LLP's designated partners must meet it.
  • Every director, resident or not, needs a Director Identification Number, and the Designated Partner Identification Number for LLPs has been merged into the same DIN system. A DIN, once allotted, has to be kept current through the annual DIR-3 KYC filing, a separate, personal obligation on each director that is unaffected by where they are based.
  • A foreign director does not need to travel to India to obtain a Digital Signature Certificate; most licensed Certifying Authorities now offer video-based identity verification for applicants abroad, though a passport and, in practice, an apostilled or otherwise authenticated supporting document are typically required since Aadhaar-based verification is not available to a non-resident.
  • Board meetings can be, and routinely are, held and resolutions passed by video conferencing. The 2021 omission of Rule 4 of the Companies (Meetings of Board and its Powers) Rules, 2014 removed the earlier list of matters, approving financial statements and a Board's report among them, that could not be dealt with that way, so there is now no category of board business that must be transacted only in a physical room.
  • Money an NRI puts into an Indian company's shares has to come in through a permitted banking channel, generally an NRE or FCNR(B) account or an inward remittance, not an NRO account for a fresh subscription, and the company then has 30 days from allotment to report that investment to the Reserve Bank through the FIRMS portal. The entry-route, sectoral-cap and FC-GPR reporting mechanics themselves are covered in full in our existing guides, linked below, and are only summarised here.
  • A bank in India will usually open a current account for an NRI-promoted company with an NRI or foreign national as an authorised signatory, but expect materially longer processing, often several weeks rather than days, and a KYC trail that follows the shareholding structure upward until it reaches natural persons, with every foreign document in that chain typically needing apostille or consular attestation.
  • A lapsed resident director is not a dormant risk, with the Registrar of Companies actively adjudicating Section 149(3) defaults under Section 172 and reported penalties running into several lakh rupees; by contrast, running the board largely from abroad does not itself put the company's Indian tax residence at risk, since an India-incorporated company is always an Indian tax resident under Section 6(3)(i) of the Income Tax Act, 1961, regardless of where its directors sit.

The question founders actually need answered

Almost every call that starts with "can an NRI be a director of an Indian company" is really three or four separate questions bundled together: can a non-resident hold the position at all, does someone still need to be physically in India, how does that person sign anything if they are not, and how does the money get in and get reported. The first question has a short answer, yes, with no nationality or residency bar on who can be a director, shareholder or designated partner. The remaining questions are where founders either over-comply, flying a director in for a board meeting that could have been held by video call, or under-comply, assuming a foreign-owned company can run entirely without a resident director.

This guide works through those adjoining questions in the order a founder or a family office structuring an NRI-backed venture usually meets them: the resident director and designated partner rules themselves, how DIN and digital signatures actually work for someone based abroad, what can and cannot be done by video conferencing, and what a bank will expect before it opens the company's first account. It treats the FEMA entry-route and FDI reporting rules, and the mechanics of the annual DIR-3 KYC filing, as already covered ground; both are the subject of separate, dedicated guides on this site, linked at the end, and this article only summarises what a founder needs to know about them in passing.

The resident director rule, precisely stated

Section 149(3) of the Companies Act, 2013 requires every company to have at least one director who has stayed in India for a total period of not less than 182 days during the financial year. This applies to every company incorporated in India, not only to one with NRI or foreign shareholders; a wholly Indian-owned, wholly Indian-resident-run company has to satisfy it as much as a foreign subsidiary does. What changes for an NRI-promoted company is only that this director usually cannot simply be one of the promoters, since by definition the promoters spend most of the year outside India, and the company instead has to identify someone else, a local relative, a professional nominee director, or a resident co-founder, who genuinely meets the 182-day test.

The provision was amended by the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017, with effect from 7 May 2018, to measure the 182 days against the company's financial year rather than the previous calendar year, and to add a proportionate test for a company's first financial year: where a company is newly incorporated partway through a financial year, the 182-day requirement is applied proportionately to the period remaining in that year, so a company incorporated on 1 October would need roughly half of 182 days, in the region of 90 to 92 days, of residence from its designated resident director before the end of that first financial year. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs granted a temporary relaxation of this requirement for the financial year most affected by COVID-19 travel restrictions; that relaxation was time-bound to that year, and the 182-day rule applies in the ordinary way for every financial year since.

Failing to maintain a qualifying resident director is a compliance default on the company and its officers, not merely a technicality, and the practical fix once a company realises it has fallen out of compliance, typically because a nominee resident director has resigned or a sole India-based promoter has relocated, is to appoint a new resident director promptly rather than wait for the position to be tested at a filing deadline. Many NRI-promoted companies address this at incorporation by appointing a professional nominee resident director on a service agreement specifically for this purpose, which is a common and legitimate structure provided the nominee's role and limited authority are documented clearly in the company's board resolutions and any nominee agreement.

A different threshold for LLPs, and why the confusion persists

Founders choosing between a private limited company and an LLP for an NRI-backed venture often assume the residency rule carries across unchanged. It does not. Section 7 of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 requires every LLP to have at least two designated partners, at least one of whom must be resident in India, but the Explanation defining "resident in India" for this purpose was amended, with the current threshold reported as taking effect from 1 April 2022 (financial year 2022-23), to a stay of not less than 120 days during the financial year, not the 182 days that applies to a company's resident director under the Companies Act. A considerable amount of commercial compliance material online still quotes the older 182-day figure for LLPs, carried over by analogy from the company law rule, and founders and even some service providers continue to apply the wrong number as a result.

The practical consequence is a genuine, if narrow, planning point: an LLP structure gives a resident designated partner roughly two months a year of additional flexibility, 245 days potentially spent outside India against 183 for a company's resident director, which can matter for a promoter who splits time between India and abroad but does not clear the higher bar. It does not change anything else about the choice between a company and an LLP, which still turns primarily on whether outside equity investment, employee stock options and a conventional exit route are contemplated, considerations an LLP structure serves less well regardless of residency.

The Designated Partner Identification Number that LLPs once required separately has been folded into the same Director Identification Number system used for companies; a person who already holds a DIN does not need a fresh number to become a designated partner of an LLP, and the application process, where a DIN does not already exist, now runs through the same Central Government mechanism either way.

DIN, and the annual filing that keeps it alive

Every individual proposed as a director, including a foreign national or an NRI, needs a Director Identification Number before appointment, obtained either through the integrated SPICe+ incorporation form for a brand-new company or through the standalone DIR-3 process for an appointment to an existing company or LLP. None of this requires physical presence in India; the application is supported by identity and address proof, which for a non-resident applicant is typically the passport and an overseas address proof, each authenticated in the form the Ministry of Corporate Affairs currently prescribes for foreign documents.

Once allotted, a DIN has to be kept active through an annual DIR-3 KYC filing, due by 30 September each year, a personal obligation that falls on the individual director regardless of nationality or residence and is unaffected by how many companies or LLPs that person serves. We have covered the mechanics and the current filing cycle for this in a dedicated guide, linked below, and do not repeat it in detail here; the point worth flagging for an NRI director specifically is only that missing the deadline deactivates the DIN for every company the director serves, not only the one that prompted the lapse, and reactivation generally requires the full e-form rather than the simplified web form, with a late fee, so the obligation is worth calendaring independently of whichever company's compliance officer happens to send the reminder.

A foreign director's digital signature certificate is a separate credential from the DIN and is needed to actually sign electronic filings. Several Indian Certifying Authorities now offer video-based identity verification for applicants who cannot appear in person, which in practice means a foreign or NRI director can usually complete this entirely from abroad, though Aadhaar-based verification, the fastest route for a resident applicant, is not available to them, and a passport along with an authenticated address document is generally required instead. Processing for a foreign applicant commonly runs a little longer than for a resident one because of the additional document checks and time-zone coordination for the video call, and it is worth building a few extra working days into any signing timeline on that account.

Board meetings, resolutions and signing without being in the room

A board that includes one or more NRI or foreign directors can hold its meetings, and transact essentially all of its business, by video conferencing. Rule 4 of the Companies (Meetings of Board and its Powers) Rules, 2014 used to list categories of business, approval of annual financial statements and the Board's report, approval of a prospectus, matters relating to amalgamation, merger or takeover, and certain audit committee business, that could not be transacted at a meeting held through video conferencing or other audio-visual means and had to be taken up at a physical meeting instead. That rule was omitted with effect from a Ministry of Corporate Affairs notification in June 2021, so there is now no category of board business that is barred from being conducted by video conferencing, provided the meeting otherwise follows the procedure separately prescribed for a meeting held this way, recording of attendance, and preservation of the proceedings.

This matters directly for an NRI-promoted company because it removes what was, until 2021, a genuine obstacle: a board with every director abroad previously still had to convene certain categories of meeting physically in India for specific agenda items. That constraint no longer exists, and a board can now approve financial statements, a merger, or a prospectus, exactly as it can approve routine business, entirely by video conferencing if it chooses to.

Signing itself still needs either a physical signature on a document couriered for execution, or a valid digital signature certificate for anything filed electronically with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs or another authority that accepts that format. For routine board resolutions, minutes and most contracts, a digital signature or, where the counterparty accepts it, a scanned wet signature exchanged electronically and followed by a later physical original if required, is now standard practice and does not require an NRI director to be in India at all for the signing itself.

Getting the NRI's own money into the company

Where the NRI is investing their own funds into the company's shares, rather than simply serving as a director, the funds generally need to come in through an NRE or FCNR(B) account, or by direct inward remittance from abroad through normal banking channels; using balances already held in an NRO account to subscribe for fresh shares is treated as the doubtful route, and most practitioners avoid it for a new subscription for that reason. Pricing for a subscription to the company's memorandum, as opposed to a later transfer of existing shares, can generally be at face value, subject to the applicable entry route and sectoral conditions being met; a later share transfer instead needs a fair valuation, usually certified by a chartered accountant or a SEBI-registered merchant banker, under the pricing guidelines that apply to a transaction between a resident and a non-resident.

Once shares are allotted, the company has to report that allotment to the Reserve Bank of India through the Single Master Form on the FIRMS portal, specifically Form FC-GPR, within 30 days of the date of allotment, a timeline that runs from allotment itself rather than from the date the funds were received; separately, shares generally have to be allotted within 60 days of the funds being received, so a company that delays incorporation formalities after receiving an NRI's investment can find itself out of time on the allotment deadline before it even reaches the reporting one. A late filing is not fatal, but it has to be regularised through the Reserve Bank's Late Submission Fee mechanism rather than simply filed late without consequence.

The entry routes available for different sectors, the sectoral caps that apply, and the full reporting architecture under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, including the distinction between the automatic and government approval routes, are the subject of our existing, dedicated guide on foreign investment into India, and the FC-GPR and FC-TRS filing mechanics specifically are covered in our separate guide on FEMA reporting for foreign investment, both linked below. This section exists only to flag the points in that framework that are most often missed specifically where the investor is an NRI rather than a foreign institutional or corporate investor: the NRE or FCNR(B) funding route rather than NRO, and the interaction between the 60-day allotment window and the 30-day reporting window that follows it.

Opening the company's bank account

A newly incorporated, NRI-promoted company needs its own current account in India; an NRO account, which is a personal account in the NRI's own name, is not a substitute for the company's banking relationship, and a branch office structure, which is a different entity type from a subsidiary company, needs a Special Non-Resident Rupee account rather than an ordinary current account, a distinction worth getting right at the outset since banks will not simply convert one account type into the other later.

Banks generally do permit an NRI or a foreign national to be an authorised signatory on the company's account, but they will ask for that signatory's passport and address proof, typically apostilled or attested by the Indian consulate in the signatory's country of residence, alongside the company's own incorporation documents, PAN, and the board resolution naming the signatory. Where the company's own shareholding traces up to a foreign holding company, the bank will generally ask for that holding company's KYC documents as well, and will continue tracing ownership upward until it reaches natural persons, consistent with the beneficial ownership disclosure now expected under anti-money laundering rules generally.

The practical point worth planning around is timing, not possibility: banks report materially longer processing for an NRI or foreign-promoted company's account opening, commonly several weeks rather than the few days a purely domestic account might take, because of this enhanced documentation and the internal compliance review it triggers. A founder planning an India launch around a fixed date should start the account-opening process, and the document authentication it depends on, well before that date rather than treating it as a final, quick step once incorporation is complete.

What actually happens if the resident director requirement lapses

Non-compliance with Section 149(3) is not a paperwork irregularity that quietly sits on file; it is enforced through adjudication under Section 172 of the Companies Act, 2013, which applies to a company and to every officer in default where no specific penalty is prescribed elsewhere for the particular contravention, and the Registrar of Companies has in recent years actively adjudicated this specific default. Reported orders describe penalties running into several lakh rupees, calculated on a per-company and per-director basis with an additional continuing-default component for each day the company remains without a qualifying resident director, and the Registrar of Companies, Bangalore levied a penalty of Rs 3 lakh on the company itself, with further separate penalties on each of its directors taking the reported total to roughly Rs 6 lakh, in a 2026 adjudication after the company had gone without a resident director for 2,297 days, a case the company itself brought to the Registrar's attention through a suo motu application once the lapse was discovered internally.

Two things stand out in how these cases tend to arise. First, the default is almost never deliberate; it typically follows a nominee resident director's resignation, death, or relocation out of India, which the company's own management does not notice for months or years because no one owns the specific task of monitoring that one director's day-count. Second, voluntarily disclosing the lapse through a suo motu adjudication application, once discovered, has generally been treated more favourably than waiting for the Registrar to find it through a routine filing review, though it does not avoid the penalty altogether. The practical lesson for an NRI-promoted company is to assign the day-count monitoring for its resident director, whether a nominee or a resident co-founder, to a specific person, usually the company secretary or the compliance retainer, as a standing, calendared task, rather than leaving it to be noticed only when a filing or a bank asks for confirmation that the board is properly constituted.

Why running the board from abroad does not make the company a foreign tax resident

A worry that surfaces often enough to be worth addressing directly: if every director and every substantive board decision is, in practice, made from the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom, does the Indian company risk being treated as something other than an Indian tax resident. It does not, and the reason is a feature of the Income Tax Act, 1961 that is often confused with a separate rule aimed at a different kind of company. Under Section 6(3)(i), a company incorporated in India is always resident in India for tax purposes, regardless of where its control and management, or its board's effective decision-making, actually sits. This is an unconditional test tied to the place of incorporation, not to the residence of directors or shareholders.

The rule that NRI founders are usually, understandably, thinking of, the Place of Effective Management test under Section 6(3)(ii), does something different: it is the test used to determine whether a company incorporated outside India should nonetheless be treated as an Indian tax resident, because the key management and commercial decisions necessary for conducting its business as a whole are, in substance, made from India. It has no application to a company that is already incorporated in India; that company's Indian tax residence is settled by incorporation alone and is not disturbed by where its directors happen to be sitting when they make decisions. Where POEM genuinely becomes relevant for an NRI family is the reverse structure, a holding company incorporated abroad whose real decision-making is run out of India, which raises its own, separate set of questions outside the scope of a company already incorporated here.

This does not mean residence from abroad has no tax consequences at all; a director's own personal tax residence, and the treatment of director's fees or remuneration paid to a non-resident, are separate questions governed by the director's individual residential status and any applicable double taxation treaty, not by the company's residence. The point worth taking away here is narrower and purely reassuring: an NRI-promoted company does not put its own corporate tax residence at risk merely by being run, in practice, largely from outside India, provided it remains an Indian-incorporated entity.

Points founders and family offices most often get wrong

The single most common structuring error is assuming that because every shareholder and every original promoter lives abroad, the company can simply operate without any India-resident director at all; it cannot, and the fix, usually a professional nominee resident director engaged on a clearly documented, limited-authority basis, needs to be built into the structure from incorporation rather than discovered as a gap during a later compliance review.

The second most common error is applying the company law's 182-day figure to an LLP's designated partner, or vice versa, when the two statutes now set different thresholds, 182 days for a company's resident director and 120 days for an LLP's resident designated partner, following the LLP Act amendment. Getting this backwards either overstates the burden on a promoter choosing the LLP route or, worse, understates it for a company, leaving the entity non-compliant without anyone noticing until a filing is rejected or queried.

A third, quieter error is treating the DIR-3 KYC filing as the company's responsibility rather than the individual director's. Because the obligation attaches to the person, not the entity, an NRI director who sits on several Indian boards can let this lapse on the strength of one company's compliance team forgetting to remind them, and find every DIN-linked filing across every company frozen as a result, a disproportionate consequence for what is, procedurally, a routine annual form.

None of the individual rules covered here are especially onerous on their own: one resident director or designated partner, a DIN kept current through an annual filing, a digital signature obtained by video call, a board that can meet and resolve almost anything by video conferencing, and investment money that has to travel through the right account and get reported within 30 days of allotment. What causes real difficulty is treating these as one undifferentiated "NRI compliance" bundle rather than as several distinct obligations sitting in different statutes, with different thresholds and different people responsible for meeting them.

Structured correctly from incorporation, an NRI-promoted company or LLP runs with very little friction attributable to the promoters' residence; structured on assumptions carried over from a different statute, or from general compliance material that does not distinguish companies from LLPs, it tends to surface its gaps at the worst possible moment, a rejected filing, a frozen DIN, or a bank account that takes two months longer to open than the business plan assumed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an Indian company or LLP have a board or partnership made up entirely of NRIs, with no one resident in India?

No. A company must have at least one director who has stayed in India for at least 182 days in the financial year under Section 149(3) of the Companies Act, 2013, and an LLP must have at least one designated partner who meets the separate 120-day residency test under the LLP Act, 2008. Many NRI-promoted structures meet this through a professional nominee resident director or designated partner engaged specifically for the purpose, with their limited role documented in a board resolution or partner agreement.

Is the residency test for an LLP's designated partner the same 182 days as for a company's director?

No, and this is one of the most common mistakes in practice. Since the LLP Act's residency Explanation was amended, the threshold for an LLP designated partner is a stay of at least 120 days during the financial year, lower than the 182-day test that applies to a company's resident director under the Companies Act. A good deal of compliance material online still quotes 182 days for LLPs by analogy with the company rule, which is outdated.

Does an NRI director need to travel to India to get a DIN or a digital signature certificate?

No. A Director Identification Number can be obtained through an application supported by authenticated identity and address documents without the applicant being physically present in India, and most licensed Certifying Authorities now verify foreign and NRI applicants for a digital signature certificate through a video call rather than requiring an in-person appearance. Expect the process to take somewhat longer than for a resident applicant because of the additional document checks involved.

Can a board with NRI or foreign directors approve financial statements, a merger or a prospectus by video conference, or does that still need a physical meeting in India?

It can be done entirely by video conference. Until 2021, a specific rule barred certain categories of board business, including approval of financial statements, the Board's report, a prospectus and matters relating to mergers and amalgamations, from being transacted at a video-conference meeting. That rule was omitted by a 2021 Ministry of Corporate Affairs notification, and there is now no category of board business restricted to a physical meeting.

Can I pay for my shares in the Indian company out of my NRO account?

This is generally treated as the doubtful route for a fresh subscription to shares, and most practitioners avoid it. The accepted funding routes are an NRE or FCNR(B) account, or a direct inward remittance from abroad through normal banking channels. An NRO account holds income already earned in India and is treated differently for this purpose; confirm the current position with your bank or adviser before relying on it for a new investment.

How quickly does the company have to report my share allotment to the Reserve Bank?

Within 30 days of the date of allotment, through Form FC-GPR on the Single Master Form on the RBI's FIRMS portal; this runs from the allotment date, not the date your funds arrived. Separately, the company generally has to allot the shares within 60 days of receiving the funds, so delays in completing the allotment paperwork can eat into the time available before the reporting deadline follows. A late filing can be regularised through the Reserve Bank's Late Submission Fee mechanism, but it is not simply overlooked.

Will an Indian bank let an NRI or foreign national be a signatory on the company's bank account?

Generally yes, but expect to provide apostilled or consulate-attested passport and address documents for that signatory, and expect the account-opening process to take materially longer, commonly several weeks, than it would for a purely domestic company, because of the enhanced know-your-customer review banks apply where the ownership or signatory chain extends abroad.
