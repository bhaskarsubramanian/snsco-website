Buying Property in India Without Visiting It: The Title Due Diligence a Broker's Reassurance Cannot Replace

An NRI in Houston, Toronto or Leicester usually sees the property on a video call, trusts a relative or a broker to check the papers, and transfers the money once that person says it looks fine. Most of the title disputes that reach a courtroom years later begin exactly there. This is the due diligence that has to happen instead, and why it looks different in Delhi NCR, Punjab, Haryana and Kolkata.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 5 November 2026 · Law stated as on 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

There is already a reliable guide on this site to the FEMA rules that decide whether an NRI may buy a given property at all, which account the money must come from, and which categories of land are off limits. This article assumes that question is answered and starts where most families actually run into trouble: establishing that the seller genuinely owns what is being sold, that nobody else has a claim on it, and that the money reaches the seller in a way that will hold up if the transaction is ever questioned. None of this is exotic law. It is a sequence of checks that a buyer physically present in the city would do as a matter of course, and that a buyer watching the property on a phone screen has to do more deliberately, because nobody is going to do it for them by default.

Key points

  • A registered sale deed, not a mutation entry in the revenue or municipal record, is what transfers title. The Supreme Court has said repeatedly, most recently in a December 2025 decision, that a mutation entry confers no right, title or interest and exists only for fiscal purposes; a seller's name appearing in the jamabandi, khatian or property tax register is not proof of ownership by itself.
  • A sale, GPA and will combination without a registered conveyance does not transfer ownership. The Supreme Court's 2011 decision in Suraj Lamp and Industries v. State of Haryana, still the governing authority, held that only a registered deed of sale under Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act passes title to immovable property worth more than Rs 100, which in practice means almost all of it.
  • An encumbrance certificate only tells you what is registered against the property for the specific years you asked the Sub-Registrar to search. A certificate covering eight years on a property built in 1978 says nothing about the other forty years, and there is no single statutory minimum period; the only real protection is asking for as long a window as the Sub-Registrar's records allow, as a rule of thumb fifteen to thirty years where the records go back that far.
  • A pending court case over the property, a lis pendens under Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act, will not show up on an encumbrance certificate at all. It has to be searched separately on the district court and relevant High Court e-filing systems, and against a company seller, before the National Company Law Tribunal as well.
  • Delhi NCR and Noida, Punjab and Haryana, and Kolkata each keep land records on different systems with different gaps: Noida and Greater Noida Authority leasehold allotments, Punjab and Haryana's jamabandi and fard records that still run largely through the local patwari despite digitisation, and West Bengal's dual mutation, one for the land record and, inside Kolkata Municipal Corporation limits, a separate one for the municipal holding and property tax register.
  • The purchase price has to move through an NRE, FCNR(B) or NRO account by bank transfer; cash, traveller's cheques and foreign currency notes are not permitted payment routes under the RBI's rules for property purchase by an NRI, and a clean, bank-traceable payment record is itself part of protecting the title, not a separate compliance exercise.

A purchase decided from three time zones away

The pattern repeats often enough that it is worth describing plainly. A family in the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom decides to buy a flat, a plot or a floor in India, usually in the city they or their parents came from. The shortlisting happens over video calls and photographs. A local relative, a broker, or occasionally the builder's own sales team is asked to confirm that the papers are in order. Money is sent, the registration happens with a power of attorney standing in for the buyer, and everyone moves on. In most cases nothing goes wrong. In a meaningful minority of cases, something does, and it is usually discovered years later, when the NRI tries to sell the property, mortgage it, or finds a stranger occupying part of it with a document of their own.

This article is about the specific checks that prevent that outcome, aimed at someone buying from the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom who cannot personally walk into the Sub-Registrar's office, the revenue office or the local court. It does not repeat the FEMA eligibility rules for NRI property purchase, which account the funds must come from, or which land categories an NRI cannot buy; our dedicated guide on that subject covers it and is linked below. What follows assumes the property is one an NRI is legally entitled to buy, and asks a narrower question: how do you establish, from abroad, that the person selling it to you actually owns it, free of anyone else's claim, and that your payment itself does not create a new problem.

What a registered sale deed proves, and what a mutation entry does not

Under Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, a sale of immovable property worth more than Rs 100, which covers almost every transaction a buyer will encounter, can only be made by a registered instrument. Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908 makes registration compulsory for exactly this category of document, and Section 49 of the same Act provides that an instrument required to be registered, if it is not, cannot affect the property or be used as evidence of the transaction at all, subject to a narrow proviso allowing it as collateral evidence for an unconnected purpose. Put simply: the chain of title a buyer is checking has to be a chain of registered sale deeds, going back from the current seller through each previous owner, not a chain of agreements, receipts or family understanding.

This is where mutation, the entry in the revenue record (jamabandi in Punjab and Haryana, khatian in West Bengal, the property register in most urban areas) or the municipal property tax register showing a particular name as the owner, is routinely misunderstood. The Supreme Court held as far back as Balwant Singh v. Daulat Singh in 1997, and has repeated the point many times since, including in Suraj Bhan v. Financial Commissioner in 2007 and again in a December 2025 decision, Tarachandra v. Bhawarlal, that a mutation entry neither creates nor extinguishes title and carries no presumptive value on ownership; it exists only to decide who pays land revenue or property tax. A seller who can show their name on the jamabandi or the municipal tax bill has shown that the authorities currently bill them for the property. They have not, by that fact alone, shown that they own it.

For a buyer checking this from abroad, the practical consequence is that a mutation record or a tax receipt in the seller's name is a useful starting point for locating the property and its earlier owners, never a substitute for examining the registered deed by which the seller actually acquired title, and the unbroken chain of such deeds behind it.

Verifying who is actually selling, and whether they are entitled to

A separate check, distinct from the title chain itself, is confirming that the person signing as seller is who they claim to be and is actually entitled to sell. This starts with comparing the seller's passport, Aadhaar and PAN against the name recorded in the registered deed under which they hold the property; a mismatch, a recently changed name, or identity documents that look inconsistent with the age of the deed are all reasons to ask more questions before proceeding, not reasons to assume an innocent clerical difference.

Where the seller holds the property as an heir of a deceased owner rather than as the original registered buyer, a death certificate and a family claim of inheritance are not, by themselves, sufficient. The buyer should ask for the succession certificate, letters of administration, or probate of a will, whichever applies on the facts, and should confirm whether the deceased left more than one heir. If there were other heirs, every one of them generally needs to join the sale deed or give a registered, documented consent or release; a sale by only one of several heirs leaves the door open for the others to later assert a claim to their share, against the buyer as much as against the selling heir. Where the seller is a company, the equivalent check is a board resolution specifically authorising the sale and the particular signatory, and a search on the Ministry of Corporate Affairs portal for any registered charge against the company that could extend to the property being sold.

This check matters more, not less, for property where the true owner lives abroad and has left day-to-day dealings to a relative or a power of attorney holder in India, precisely the pattern behind the Punjab, Haryana and Gurugram fraud cases already discussed. A buyer who can reach the actual title holder directly, by phone or video call, to confirm they intend to sell and have authorised the specific transaction in front of them, adds a layer of protection that no document search by itself provides, and costs nothing beyond the time to make the call.

The encumbrance certificate: genuinely useful, and only for the years you ask for

An encumbrance certificate (EC), issued by the Sub-Registrar, lists registered transactions, mortgages, charges and similar encumbrances against a property for a period the applicant specifies. If something is found, the Sub-Registrar issues Form 15; if nothing is found for that period, a nil certificate is issued on Form 16. The certificate is only as good as the period searched, and this is the single most common way an EC gives false comfort: a nil certificate for the last eight years on a property that has existed since 1978 says nothing at all about what happened in the other four decades.

There is no uniform statutory minimum period that every Sub-Registrar's office in India applies, and practice differs between lending institutions and prudent buyers. Banks financing a purchase commonly ask for an EC covering roughly thirteen to fifteen years; a buyer protecting their own interest, rather than a lender's, is generally better served asking for fifteen to thirty years, or for the full period the local Sub-Registrar's digitised records can search, whichever is available. Where the office cannot search back that far on its own systems, certified copies of the earlier registered deeds in the chain, obtained directly from the registry, serve the same purpose.

An EC also only covers what was actually registered at that Sub-Registrar. It will not show an unregistered agreement, a family settlement never registered, or, as the next section covers, litigation pending in a court rather than recorded against the property at the registry. Treating a clean, long-period EC as confirmation that the title is clear of registered charges, and nothing more than that, is the right way to read it.

The litigation search the registry will not do for you

Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act codifies the doctrine of lis pendens: once a suit is pending in which a right to specific immovable property is directly in question, a party to that suit cannot transfer the property during the litigation in a way that prejudices the rights of the other side under whatever decree the court eventually passes. Courts have extended this beyond ordinary title suits to a money suit where the debt is secured by a mortgage over the property. A transfer made in breach of Section 52 is not automatically void, but it is subject to the outcome of the pending case, which is precisely the risk a buyer wants to avoid taking on unknowingly.

The difficulty is that a pending suit of this kind generally does not appear on the property's encumbrance certificate at all, because the EC records what was registered at the Sub-Registrar, not what is pending in a court. A separate litigation search is therefore a distinct step, not a subset of the EC check. In practice this means searching the relevant district court's records and the e-Courts portal by both the seller's name and the property's description, checking the relevant High Court's cause list and case search facility, and, where the seller is a company, checking the National Company Law Tribunal as well, since a company seller can be the subject of insolvency or other proceedings that affect what it can sell.

For an NRI buyer working through counsel in India, this search is one of the more straightforward items to delegate, provided it is actually done and a written confirmation obtained, rather than a verbal assurance that there is 'nothing pending', which is the single phrase most likely to precede a later dispute.

Some States have gone a step further and require a notice of pending litigation to itself be registered against the property, so that a search of the registry picks up at least some pending suits rather than relying solely on a court records search. This is a State-level variation, not a uniform national rule, and a buyer or their counsel should confirm whether the State in question has such a requirement rather than assuming either that it exists everywhere or that it exists nowhere. Where it does exist, it supplements the court search; it does not replace it, since a suit filed but not yet noted against the property at the registry will still only surface through the court records themselves.

The GPA, agreement to sell and will combination: still the most common trap

A disproportionate share of title disputes trace back to a transaction built around a general power of attorney (GPA), an agreement to sell, and sometimes a will, in place of a registered sale deed. The Supreme Court's 2011 decision in Suraj Lamp and Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana addressed exactly this practice and held, consistent with Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, that such arrangements do not transfer ownership of immovable property; only a duly executed and registered deed of conveyance does that. The judgment was not presented as a change in the law so much as a reminder of what Section 54 had always required, and it remains the controlling authority today.

The practical risk for an NRI buyer is twofold. First, a seller offering to convey through an existing GPA, rather than executing a fresh registered sale deed in the buyer's favour, should be treated as a signal to examine the entire chain, not an acceptable shortcut; a genuine, narrowly drafted and properly registered power of attorney used to authorise someone to sign a registered sale deed on an owner's behalf is a different matter from a GPA being used as a substitute for that deed. Second, and this is the pattern behind several reported NRI cases, a forged or misused power of attorney has repeatedly been used to sell an NRI's own property out from under them while they were abroad and the property was left unattended; a separate, widely reported Rs 10.7 crore fraud involving a plot in Gurugram followed the same absentee-owner pattern but was carried out through a forged gift deed and forged identity documents rather than a power of attorney. The Punjab and Haryana High Court has held, in line with Suraj Lamp, that a power of attorney is not itself an instrument that transfers any right, title or interest in immovable property.

Both risks point the same way for a buyer: insist on a registered sale deed executed directly by the current owner, verified against their identity and the earlier registered deed by which they acquired the property, rather than accepting an intermediary document as a stand-in.

Delhi NCR and Noida: leasehold allotments and builder title

A large share of the organised housing stock in Noida and Greater Noida sits on land allotted by the Noida or Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority on a leasehold basis, not sold outright in freehold. For a plot or an independent allotment, the chain to verify includes the original lease deed from the Authority, the allotment letter, any permission the Authority granted for transfer or sub-lease (a step often skipped in informal resale chains), and a current no-dues certificate from the Authority confirming there is no outstanding lease rent or other charge. A buyer who checks only the private resale agreement and the registered conveyance between private parties, without going back to the Authority's own allotment and transfer-permission record, can end up with a title that is defective at its root, regardless of how many private hands the property passed through afterwards.

For a flat bought from a builder, whether under construction or newly completed, the project's registration with the Uttar Pradesh Real Estate Regulatory Authority should be checked directly on the UP RERA portal, which discloses the promoter's title documents, the sanctioned plan, and the project's registration status. Where the project is still under construction, Section 4(2)(l)(D) of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 requires the promoter to declare that seventy per cent of the amounts realised from allottees will be deposited in a separate escrow account, to be withdrawn only in proportion to construction progress and on certification by an engineer, architect and chartered accountant. Several States have diluted aspects of this requirement in their own rules, and Haryana's regulator has in practice taken enforcement action against lenders who withdrew funds from such an account in breach of the provision, which is itself a reminder that the safeguard is real but depends on active compliance rather than existing automatically.

Punjab and Haryana: jamabandi, fard and the patwari who still matters

Both States maintain their land records on digitised portals, jamabandi.punjab.gov.in for Punjab and the Haryana revenue department's jamabandi portal for Haryana, which let a user search by owner name or khewat number and view the current and historical jamabandi for a holding. A fard is a certified extract of the jamabandi for a specific khasra number, and the two are not interchangeable; a transaction document should generally be checked against a current certified fard, not merely a printout from the portal.

Mutation in both States is processed through an online Intkal facility layered on top of these portals, but on-the-ground practice in Punjab in particular still runs substantially through the village-level patwari, whose physical records and local knowledge the digitised system has not fully displaced. For a buyer verifying remotely, this matters practically: a clean-looking online jamabandi extract is a necessary check, not a sufficient one, and a local advocate or a trusted representative confirming the position with the patwari's record, and obtaining a certified fard rather than relying only on the portal printout, remains worthwhile in both States.

This is also a region where NRI-owned land, left unattended for years while the owner lives abroad, has been a specific and reported target for impersonation and forged-document fraud, which is a reason for an NRI buyer to look closely at how recently and under what circumstances the seller's own ownership was established, particularly for agricultural or semi-urban land that may not have changed hands in a formal sale for a long period.

Kolkata and West Bengal: two mutations, not one

West Bengal's land record system is built around the khatian, the State's equivalent of the jamabandi, recorded under either the Revisional Survey (RS) or the more current Land Reforms (LR) series, and searchable on the Banglarbhumi portal by khatian number, plot number or owner name. For rural and semi-urban land outside municipal limits, mutation is processed through the Block Land and Land Reforms Office (BLRO) and updates the khatian itself.

A property within the limits of the Kolkata Municipal Corporation sits under a separate layer again: the municipal mutation that updates the KMC's own holding and property tax records is a distinct process from the BLRO land-record mutation, and a seller who has completed one is not automatically shown to have completed the other. A buyer verifying a Kolkata property should check both the land-record position on Banglarbhumi and the municipal holding record and property tax payment history directly with the Kolkata Municipal Corporation, rather than assuming that an in-order khatian also means the municipal record is current, or the other way round.

As with every record-of-rights discussed in this article, an entry in either system, revenue or municipal, remains fiscal in character under the general principle the Supreme Court has repeatedly stated; it is useful for tracing the property and its recent history, not a substitute for the registered sale deed chain that actually establishes ownership.

Benami risk and the temptation to buy in someone else's name

The Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988, substantially strengthened by the 2016 amendment that came into force on 1 November 2016, prohibits holding property in the name of a person who did not actually provide the funds for it, with limited statutory exceptions, and allows such property to be confiscated. The practice sometimes suggested to an NRI buyer, routing the purchase through a resident relative's name for convenience, since that relative is physically present to sign documents and deal with authorities, carries a real risk of falling within this prohibition if the NRI is the one actually funding the purchase and the arrangement does not fit one of the Act's own exceptions.

A safer and simpler course, in almost every case, is to buy in the NRI's own name directly, using a registered power of attorney limited to the specific steps a representative needs to carry out, execution of the sale deed, registration formalities, and similar acts, rather than structuring the purchase so that someone else's name appears as owner on paper while the NRI's money pays for it. Where a family genuinely intends joint ownership, that should be reflected as joint ownership in the sale deed itself, with each party's contribution to the price documented, rather than left to be inferred later from bank transfers if a dispute arises.

Benami risk also surfaces in a quieter form that NRI buyers sometimes overlook: a seller who themselves acquired the property benami, holding it in their own name for an undisclosed real owner, decades before the current sale. Where that earlier arrangement later unravels, through a family dispute, a tax investigation, or a claim by the undisclosed real owner's heirs, a buyer who purchased from the named benami holder can find their own title challenged, even though they dealt in good faith with the person the registry showed as owner. A thorough chain-of-title and litigation search, of the kind this article describes, is the best available protection against this risk, though it cannot eliminate it entirely, since a benami arrangement is by its nature designed not to show up in the ordinary registry or revenue records.

The payment trail: why FEMA compliance also protects your title

The RBI's rules for the acquisition of immovable property in India by an NRI require the purchase consideration to be paid out of funds received by inward remittance through normal banking channels, or by debit to an NRE, FCNR(B) or NRO account maintained in accordance with FEMA; payment by traveller's cheque or in foreign currency notes is not a permitted route, and payment in cash is treated the same way. An FCNR(B) deposit cannot be used to fund a purchase directly and generally has to be converted into an NRE or NRO account first, and funds routed through an NRO account carry their own repatriation limits, currently up to USD one million in a financial year, which is a separate consideration from the purchase itself but worth factoring in before deciding which account to fund the transaction from.

Beyond the compliance point, a clean, traceable bank transfer record from the NRI's own NRE, FCNR(B) or NRO account to the seller, matched to the consideration stated in the registered sale deed, is itself part of protecting the title. If the transaction is ever challenged, whether as benami, as undervalued, or as part of a larger family dispute over the property, a documented payment trail corroborating the registered deed is far stronger evidence of a genuine arm's length purchase than the deed standing alone. Any part of the price paid informally, in cash, or outside the documented banking channel, is not simply a tax or FEMA irregularity; it is a gap in the buyer's own evidence of having genuinely paid for and owned the property, discoverable by anyone with an interest in later disputing the sale.

Running the due diligence itself from abroad

For the verification steps themselves, rather than for signing the final sale deed, a special or limited power of attorney authorising a specific advocate or relative to collect certified copies, inspect records and correspond with the Sub-Registrar, revenue office or municipal office is generally sufficient, and is the more cautious instrument compared with a wide general power of attorney covering the entire transaction. Where the power of attorney is executed in the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom, each a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, an apostille from the designated authority in that country is generally accepted in India without further consular legalisation, though some families obtain consular attestation as well where a particular bank or registering authority prefers it; the document still needs to be stamped under the applicable State's stamp law once it reaches India, generally within the period that State's law allows. Our separate guide on powers of attorney for NRIs, linked below, covers the drafting and authentication steps in full and is not repeated here.

The verification itself should be instructed as a written title opinion from an advocate engaged specifically for that purpose, addressed to the buyer and setting out the chain of title examined, the period covered by the encumbrance search, the result of the litigation search, and any gap or qualification the advocate found, rather than a verbal assurance relayed through a broker, a builder's sales team, or a relative who is not themselves legally trained. The seller's own lawyer, where one exists, is not a substitute for this; their obligation runs to the seller, not the buyer.

It is also worth settling, before the search begins, who holds the original documents once the transaction closes. A buyer abroad should arrange for the original registered sale deed, the certified EC, and the title opinion to be kept somewhere secure in India, ideally in a bank locker or with the instructed advocate rather than only with the local relative who helped coordinate the purchase, and should keep certified or scanned copies accessible from abroad as well. This is a small administrative step that is frequently skipped once the excitement of completing the purchase has passed, and it becomes expensive to fix only when the documents are needed years later for a sale, a mortgage, or to defend the title against a challenge.

A working sequence before any money changes hands

In practice, the steps that protect a remote buyer hold together best in a fixed order, rather than being done piecemeal after an agreement to sell has already been signed and token money paid. Obtain and examine certified copies of the registered sale deed chain going back as many transfers, and as many years, as the records allow, not merely the most recent deed in the seller's name. Separately obtain an encumbrance certificate for a long period, fifteen to thirty years where available, and have it read against that same deed chain rather than taken at face value.

Run the litigation search across the relevant district court, e-Courts portal and High Court, and the NCLT if the seller is a company, before paying any token amount, not after. Check the revenue or municipal mutation and tax record for the property, understanding it as confirmation of who is currently billed, not as proof of ownership, and, for Noida or Greater Noida leasehold land, check the Authority's own allotment and transfer-permission record directly. Verify any power of attorney in the chain against the governing principle that only a registered sale deed transfers title, and be prepared to walk away, or insist the actual owner execute the deed personally, where a GPA is being offered as a substitute. Only once all of this is in hand should the purchase consideration be transferred, and then only through an NRE, FCNR(B) or NRO account by bank transfer, matched to the figure in the registered sale deed.

What due diligence can reduce, and what it cannot eliminate

No amount of documentary verification removes risk entirely from a property purchase in India, and it would be misleading to suggest otherwise. A forged document can be well made; a litigation search only catches what has already been filed in a searchable court; and an encumbrance certificate, however long the period searched, only shows what was actually registered. What the steps in this article do is close the specific gaps that most often turn into years of litigation for exactly the buyer least able to respond quickly, someone who lives on another continent, has limited ability to visit the property on short notice, and finds out about a problem only once it has already hardened into a dispute.

Where a chain of title cannot be established cleanly, where a seller resists a litigation search or a long-period encumbrance certificate, or where the only way to convey the property is through an existing power of attorney rather than a fresh registered deed from the actual owner, the right response for a remote buyer is usually to walk away or materially renegotiate, not to proceed on the strength of a relative's or a broker's reassurance that 'this is how it's always done here'. The properties that generate the Gurugram-style disputes this article has referred to were, almost without exception, bought on exactly that reassurance.

A buyer who does find a genuine defect part way through the process, a missing heir's consent, a short encumbrance search that cannot easily be extended, a mutation that has not caught up with an old sale, should treat the defect as a reason to slow down and seek it cured before paying further money, rather than as a problem to be solved after possession changes hands. Curing a defective chain of title before completion, by obtaining the missing consent, getting the earlier deed properly registered, or having the seller rectify the mutation, is almost always cheaper and faster than attempting the same correction once the buyer has already paid the full price and the seller has no remaining incentive to cooperate.

Buying property in India without being there in person is not, by itself, the source of risk. The source of risk is treating the verification steps as something someone else will handle, rather than as a defined sequence the buyer insists on seeing in writing before any money moves. A registered deed chain, a long-period encumbrance certificate, a separate litigation search, the right local land-record checks for the specific city or State, and a clean FEMA-compliant payment trail are not exotic requirements. They are the ordinary due diligence any buyer should do, made deliberate rather than assumed, because nobody local is going to insist on it for someone who is not standing in the room.

The families who avoid the worst outcomes in this area are rarely the ones who found a cleverer legal device. They are the ones who treated the sequence in this article as non-negotiable before paying anything, engaged independent counsel rather than relying on the seller's side, and were willing to walk away from a property whose papers did not hold up, however much time had already gone into the purchase.

Frequently Asked Questions

The seller's name is on the latest property tax bill and the jamabandi or khatian. Doesn't that prove they own the property?

No. The Supreme Court has held repeatedly, most recently in a December 2025 decision, that a mutation entry in the revenue record, and the same reasoning applies to a municipal property tax register, confers no title and exists only for fiscal purposes such as deciding who pays revenue or tax. It is a useful lead for tracing the property's history, not proof of ownership. Ownership is established by the chain of registered sale deeds, which has to be checked separately.

Can I accept a sale through someone holding a general power of attorney for the owner, since the owner lives abroad and cannot travel?

Be cautious. The Supreme Court's decision in Suraj Lamp and Industries v. State of Haryana held that a sale, GPA and will arrangement does not transfer ownership of immovable property; only a registered sale deed under Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act does that. A properly drafted, registered and narrowly limited power of attorney authorising someone to execute a registered sale deed on the owner's behalf is different from treating the power of attorney itself as the conveyance. Insist on seeing, and having your lawyer verify, exactly what the power of attorney authorises, and insist on a registered sale deed as the actual instrument of transfer.

How many years should an encumbrance certificate cover?

There is no single statutory minimum that applies everywhere; banks financing a purchase commonly ask for roughly thirteen to fifteen years, while a buyer protecting their own interest is generally better served asking for fifteen to thirty years, or the full period the Sub-Registrar's records can search. A nil certificate for a short period on an older property tells you very little about the years outside that window, so the period searched matters as much as the result.

Will the encumbrance certificate tell me if there is a court case pending over the property?

Generally not. An encumbrance certificate records what was registered at the Sub-Registrar; a pending suit affecting the property, governed by the doctrine of lis pendens under Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act, is a separate matter that has to be searched directly on the relevant district court and High Court systems, and before the National Company Law Tribunal if the seller is a company, rather than assumed to be covered by the encumbrance search.

Can I pay for the property in cash, or ask a relative in India to pay on my behalf and reimburse them later from abroad?

No. The RBI's rules for property purchase by an NRI require payment through normal banking channels, by debit to an NRE, FCNR(B) or NRO account, or by inward remittance; cash and foreign currency notes are not permitted. Paying through a relative and reimbursing them later also risks being read as a benami arrangement if the funds genuinely originated with you, since the Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act treats property held in one person's name but funded by another as benami, subject to narrow exceptions. Pay directly from your own NRE, FCNR(B) or NRO account by bank transfer, matched to the figure in the registered sale deed.

The property is in Noida and the seller says it is leasehold from the Authority, not freehold. Does that change what I should check?

Yes. For Authority-allotted leasehold land in Noida or Greater Noida, check the original lease deed and allotment letter from the Authority, confirm the Authority actually permitted the transfer or sub-lease in the private resale chain, and obtain a current no-dues certificate from the Authority before relying only on the private sale documents between individuals. A clean private registered deed does not cure a defect at the level of the original Authority allotment.

I'm buying a flat under construction in Delhi NCR. What should I check about how the builder is holding buyer funds?

Check the project's registration and disclosures on the relevant RERA portal (UP RERA for Noida and Greater Noida, or the applicable State authority elsewhere). Under Section 4(2)(l)(D) of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016, the promoter must deposit seventy per cent of amounts collected from allottees in a separate escrow account, drawn down only against certified construction progress. Some States have diluted this requirement through their own rules, and enforcement varies, so confirm the specific project's compliance rather than assuming the statutory protection is automatically in force.

My Kolkata property's khatian looks clean on Banglarbhumi. Is that enough?

Not on its own, for a property within Kolkata Municipal Corporation limits. West Bengal's land-record mutation, processed through the Block Land and Land Reforms Office and reflected on Banglarbhumi, is a separate process from the KMC's own municipal mutation of the holding and property tax record. Check both, since a seller can be current on one and not the other, and a clean khatian does not by itself confirm the municipal record is equally in order.
