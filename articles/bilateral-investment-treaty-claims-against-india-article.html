Bilateral Investment Treaty Claims Against India: What a Foreign Investor Can and Cannot Claim

Why White Industries, Vodafone and Cairn still shape how a foreign investor should think about treaty protection, what India's 2016 Model BIT actually offers in its place, and where an investor stands today if a dispute with the Indian state goes wrong.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 14 November 2026 · Law stated as on 13 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company negotiating a concession, a licence or a joint venture in India usually spends its legal budget on the contract itself: governing law, the arbitration clause, conditions precedent. Whether that investment also sits behind a bilateral investment treaty, and what that treaty would actually let the investor claim if the Indian state later cancelled a licence, changed a tariff formula or imposed a retrospective tax demand, is a separate question that gets asked far less often, usually only after the dispute has already arrived. India's own experience with investment treaty claims, from White Industries in 2011 through the Vodafone and Cairn tax awards in 2020, changed both India's treaty practice and what protection remains available to an investor structuring a position today. This piece sets out what a bilateral investment treaty actually adds beyond the investment contract, why India tore up most of its older treaties, what its 2016 Model BIT offers in their place, and the procedural and enforcement realities an investor needs to weigh before assuming a treaty claim is a reliable backstop.

Key points

  • A bilateral investment treaty gives a foreign investor a direct claim against the host state itself, distinct from and in addition to whatever dispute resolution clause sits inside the investment contract or a related commercial arbitration.
  • India's older generation of BITs, with broad fair and equitable treatment language and most-favoured-nation clauses, produced the White Industries award in 2011 and the Vodafone and Cairn tax awards in 2020, both decided against India.
  • Following those awards, India issued termination notices to the large majority of its roughly seventy BITs and adopted a 2016 Model BIT with a narrower standard of treatment, a mandatory period of exhausting local remedies before arbitration, and no most-favoured-nation clause.
  • New treaties signed on or close to the 2016 model, including with the UAE, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Brazil and Israel, now sit alongside a much smaller surviving set of older-generation treaties, so the protection available to a given investment depends heavily on the investor's home state and the date of the relevant treaty.
  • India has never ratified the ICSID Convention, so a BIT claim against India proceeds as ad hoc arbitration, typically under the UNCITRAL Rules, rather than before the ICSID tribunal that investors from most other capital-importing countries would use.
  • The Devas Multimedia dispute shows how a single underlying grievance can generate a commercial arbitration award, a separate investment treaty claim by the investor's shareholders, and a parallel domestic insolvency proceeding, each running on its own timetable and each capable of being used defensively by the other side.
  • Even a favourable award does not end the matter: enforcing a BIT award against the Indian state abroad has run into sovereign immunity defences, as an English court held when it ruled that India's ratification of the New York Convention was not, by itself, a waiver of its immunity from execution.

A treaty claim is not the same claim as the one in your contract

Most foreign investors structure their protection around the investment agreement itself: a concession deed with a state utility, a power purchase agreement, a shareholders' agreement for a joint venture, or a licence from a sectoral regulator. Each of these ordinarily carries its own dispute resolution clause, commonly an arbitration agreement naming a seat, an institution and a governing law. A dispute under that clause runs against the counterparty named in the contract, usually an Indian company, a public sector undertaking or a state instrumentality, and the relief available is whatever the contract and the applicable contract law allow.

A bilateral investment treaty operates on a completely different plane. It is an agreement between two sovereign states, India and the investor's home state, under which each state promises certain standards of treatment to investors and investments from the other state. Where that promise is broken, by a court, a regulator, a legislature or an executive act, the investor can bring a claim directly against the Republic of India itself, not against the counterparty to its commercial contract, and the claim is decided under international law rather than Indian contract law. No contractual arbitration clause with an Indian counterparty is needed for this route to exist; what is needed is a qualifying investment, a qualifying investor, and a BIT in force between India and the investor's state of nationality at the relevant time.

This distinction matters for structuring, not just for litigation strategy after something has gone wrong. An investor who only reads its own concession agreement's arbitration clause is looking at one layer of protection. Whether a second, treaty-based layer exists underneath it depends on facts that have nothing to do with that contract: the investor's actual nationality and corporate structure, whether a BIT between that state and India was in force when the investment was made, and whether that specific treaty's protections, which vary considerably between India's older and newer generations of BITs, actually cover the kind of state conduct the investor is worried about.

Why India's older treaties became a problem: White Industries

India signed dozens of BITs from the early 1990s onward, mostly following a fairly standard template: broad fair and equitable treatment language, full protection and security, expropriation protection, and a most-favoured-nation clause promising investors from the treaty partner no less favourable treatment than India gave investors from any other country. For roughly two decades these treaties generated little visible dispute activity. White Industries Australia Limited v. The Republic of India changed that.

White Industries had an arbitral award in its favour against Coal India arising out of a commercial contract, but spent years trying to get an Indian court to enforce that award, caught in proceedings that dragged on without resolution. It brought a claim against India under the India-Australia BIT. That treaty did not itself contain an "effective means" standard, the obligation to provide investors an effective means of asserting claims and enforcing rights. The India-Kuwait BIT did contain such a clause. White Industries invoked the India-Australia BIT's most-favoured-nation provision to import that Kuwait-treaty standard into its own claim, and the tribunal accepted the argument in its final award of 30 November 2011, holding that the delay in India's courts breached the effective-means obligation, a standard the tribunal treated as a lower threshold than the separate and more demanding test for denial of justice.

The award was a narrow legal point with a wide practical effect. It showed Indian treaty drafters, for the first time in a case that actually went to a reasoned award, that a most-favoured-nation clause in one Indian BIT could be used to borrow a more investor-friendly standard sitting in an entirely different Indian BIT with a different country, regardless of what the treaty actually being invoked said on its own terms. Commentary on the award consistently treats it as the case that pushed India toward dropping the MFN clause from its later treaty model altogether, a change that shows up directly in the 2016 Model BIT discussed below.

Vodafone and Cairn: when a tax dispute becomes a treaty claim

The next and far larger wave of claims arose not from court delay but from India's 2012 retrospective amendment to the Income-tax Act, which reached back to tax indirect transfers of Indian assets through offshore holding structures, including transactions completed years before the amendment itself. Vodafone and Cairn Energy, both facing large tax demands under that retrospective provision, each invoked the investment treaty their respective home jurisdictions had with India: the Netherlands for Vodafone, the United Kingdom for Cairn.

In September 2020, a Permanent Court of Arbitration tribunal ruled that India's roughly Rs 22,000 crore demand against Vodafone, pursued using the retrospective legislation, breached the fair and equitable treatment guarantee under the India-Netherlands investment treaty. In December 2020, a separate PCA tribunal ruled against India in Cairn's case, rejecting India's position that the matter was purely a sovereign tax question outside the tribunal's jurisdiction, and awarding Cairn damages reported in the region of 1.2 to 1.4 billion US dollars plus interest and costs, on the basis that the funds India had seized, through attachment of dividends, a tax refund and the sale of Cairn's residual shares, should be returned.

Both awards turned on the same underlying point: a BIT's fair and equitable treatment standard can be breached by the state's own tax measures, applied retrospectively against a settled transaction, and is not confined to expropriation of physical assets or discriminatory regulation in the narrower sense. That conclusion, reached twice by two different tribunals against the same respondent state within a few months, is what eventually forced India's hand on the underlying tax law itself, discussed in the next section, and not merely on its treaty policy.

The Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021: unwinding the dispute, not just the award

India did not simply pay the Vodafone and Cairn awards and move on. Parliament instead passed the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021, which received presidential assent on 13 August 2021 and withdrew the retrospective application of the 2012 amendment for transactions undertaken before 28 May 2012, the date the original 2012 Finance Act provision had come into force. The withdrawal was not unconditional: a taxpayer seeking its benefit had to withdraw any pending appeal, writ petition, arbitration, mediation or conciliation proceeding it had brought over the retrospective demand, and give an undertaking not to pursue further claims, including claims for costs, interest or damages, arising from the dispute.

Cairn Energy accepted those terms, withdrew its international enforcement proceedings against Indian assets abroad, and received a refund reported at roughly Rs 7,900 crore of the amount that had been collected from it. The amendment was widely reported as a direct legislative response to the Vodafone and Cairn awards, intended to close out both disputes by removing the underlying tax demand that had given rise to them, rather than by contesting the awards indefinitely in enforcement proceedings around the world.

The lesson for a foreign investor is not that a favourable BIT award guarantees payment on the tribunal's terms. It is that India has, at least once, chosen to resolve the underlying legislative cause of a BIT dispute rather than simply litigate enforcement, and that the price of taking that route was the investor's binding undertaking to walk away from every other proceeding over the same grievance. An investor weighing whether to settle on comparable terms in a future dispute should expect a similar all-or-nothing structure, not a partial settlement that leaves other claims alive.

The 2016 Model BIT: a narrower, more defensive template

In the aftermath of White Industries and with the Vodafone and Cairn claims already running, India adopted a new Model BIT in December 2016 as the template for all future investment treaty negotiations, and simultaneously issued termination notices to the overwhelming majority of its existing BITs, discussed in the next section. The 2016 model reflects a considered retreat from the broad, investor-protective language of the earlier generation, on several specific points that a foreign investor needs to understand before assuming an older-style protection is still available.

The fair and equitable treatment standard in the 2016 model is tied to the customary international law minimum standard of treatment, covering denial of justice, fundamental breach of due process, targeted discrimination on manifestly unjustified grounds and manifestly abusive treatment, rather than the free-standing and considerably more elastic "fair and equitable treatment" language that tribunals in cases like Vodafone's had applied. The model also drops the most-favoured-nation clause entirely, closing off the treaty-shopping route that White Industries had exploited, and excludes taxation measures from the treaty's substantive protections in most circumstances, directly addressing the kind of claim Vodafone and Cairn had brought.

Perhaps the single change with the most practical bite is Article 15's requirement that an investor exhaust available domestic remedies for a minimum period, generally five years under the 2016 model, before bringing an international claim, subject to an exception where no adequate or effective remedy exists domestically. Newer treaties concluded closer to the present, including with the UAE in 2024 and reportedly with Israel, have cut that window down to around three years, suggesting India's own negotiating position on this point continues to move, but the basic structure, requiring the investor to litigate in India first, is now a fixture of India's treaty practice rather than a one-off feature of the 2016 text.

Mass termination and what has replaced the old treaties

Alongside adopting the new model, India issued termination notices covering the large majority of its existing BITs and invited the other states to renegotiate on the 2016 template. The scale of the retreat is substantial by any account: India's treaties in force are reported to have fallen from the seventies in the mid-2010s down to single digits by around 2021, a level that persisted for several years while renegotiation talks with individual states proceeded slowly.

Most of India's terminated BITs, like most BITs generally, carried a survival or sunset clause protecting investments made before termination for a further period, typically ten to fifteen years depending on the specific treaty's text, even after the treaty itself ceased to be in force for new investments. An investment made under an older treaty before its termination notice may therefore still carry that older treaty's broader protections for a defined window running from the termination date, a point that has to be checked treaty by treaty and is easy to get wrong by assuming termination ended protection immediately.

The renegotiation process has picked up pace more recently. India's BIT with the United Arab Emirates entered into force in 2024, treaties with Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan and Brazil followed in 2025 broadly on the 2016 template, and the India-Israel investment agreement entered into force on 4 July 2026, reportedly extending protection to portfolio investments as well as direct investment. India is also reported to be working on a further revision of the Model BIT itself, with a shorter local-remedies window under discussion, and is said to be pursuing new treaties with the European Union, Saudi Arabia, Switzerland, Oman and the Maldives, though none of this had produced a public draft text or a concluded treaty with those parties as of this writing. An investor from a state without any BIT currently in force with India, which today includes a number of major capital-exporting countries given how much of the older network was torn up, has no treaty-based claim available at all and depends entirely on its contract and on ordinary Indian law.

What protection actually survives for a new investment today

An investor making a new investment in India today, from a state that does have a current BIT with India, is working with a materially thinner set of protections than an investor relying on one of the older-generation treaties still within its survival window. Expropriation protection remains a consistent feature across both generations: direct expropriation without compensation, due process and a public purpose is prohibited, and indirect expropriation, through regulatory measures that effectively deprive the investor of the benefit of its investment, is covered in principle, though the newer model treaties carve out non-discriminatory regulatory measures taken for legitimate public welfare objectives, including environmental, health and public order measures, as not amounting to indirect expropriation except in rare circumstances.

Fair and equitable treatment survives in name under the new model but, as described above, is pinned to a customary-international-law floor rather than the broader standard tribunals applied in Vodafone and Cairn. National treatment, promising a foreign investor no worse treatment than a domestic investor in like circumstances, remains available in most of the newer treaties but is typically subject to exceptions for specified sectors and for measures necessary to protect essential security interests, public order or public health, exceptions that tribunals have historically read with real deference to the state's own characterisation of the measure.

What has not survived into the new generation is the most-favoured-nation clause and, with it, the treaty-shopping route White Industries used. An investor cannot borrow a better standard from whatever other BIT India happens to have signed with a third country; it is confined to whatever its own treaty actually says. Umbrella clauses, which in some older treaties elevated a breach of the underlying investment contract itself into a treaty breach, are also generally absent from the 2016 model, meaning a straightforward contractual default by an Indian counterparty, without more, is unlikely on its own to support a treaty claim under a newer-generation BIT.

The five-year clock: exhausting local remedies before arbitration

Article 15 of the 2016 Model BIT requires an investor to pursue available domestic judicial and administrative remedies for a minimum continuous period, generally stated as five years, before submitting a claim to international arbitration, unless it can show that no domestic remedy exists that could reasonably be expected to provide effective relief. This provision inverts the practical sequencing an investor from an older-treaty jurisdiction might expect: rather than treating international arbitration as an alternative to litigating in India, the newer treaties treat it as a last resort available only after India's own courts and tribunals have had a genuine opportunity to resolve the dispute.

In practice this means an investor whose licence is cancelled or whose concession is terminated by a state instrumentality today, under a treaty on the 2016 template, needs to commit to pursuing writ proceedings, civil suits or sectoral tribunal remedies in India as a precondition to any later treaty claim, not as a parallel or optional track. The five-year figure itself appears to be under revision in India's own ongoing negotiations, with newer treaties such as the UAE's 2024 agreement and reportedly Israel's cutting the window closer to three years, so the exact length of the exhaustion period has to be checked against the specific treaty in force for the investor's home state rather than assumed from the 2016 template alone.

The exception for cases where no domestic remedy could provide effective relief exists in the treaty text, but an investor should not assume a tribunal will readily find that exception satisfied merely because Indian litigation is likely to be slow or because the investor doubts it will succeed on the merits in an Indian court. The exception is aimed at genuine absence of an available remedy, not at the ordinary delay and uncertainty that litigating in India, like litigating in most jurisdictions, inevitably carries.

Devas Multimedia: one grievance, three parallel battles

The Antrix-Devas dispute illustrates, better than any single textbook example, how a treaty claim interacts with other proceedings arising from the same underlying facts rather than replacing them. Antrix Corporation, the commercial arm of India's space agency, entered into a satellite spectrum agreement with Devas Multimedia, a private Indian company, which the Indian government later cancelled on grounds connected to the spectrum's strategic and defence uses. Devas brought a commercial arbitration against Antrix under an ICC tribunal, which issued a final award in Devas's favour in September 2015 running into several hundred million dollars, an award commentary describes as having grown to well over a billion dollars with accrued interest by the time enforcement proceedings reached the United States appellate courts.

India's response went beyond resisting that commercial award. In May 2021, the National Company Law Tribunal ordered Devas Multimedia's compulsory winding up on findings that the company had been incorporated for a fraudulent purpose, a ruling the Supreme Court of India upheld in January 2022. The government has used that domestic insolvency finding as part of its defence against enforcement of the Devas award in courts around the world, while separately contesting the award's validity through set-aside proceedings in Indian courts, which led to the award's annulment domestically, a ruling whose effect on enforcement proceedings already under way abroad remains a live and unresolved question in the reported US litigation.

Running alongside both the commercial award and the domestic insolvency proceedings is a third, separate track: an investment treaty claim brought by Devas's Mauritius-based shareholders under the India-Mauritius BIT, asserting that India's conduct, including the spectrum cancellation and the subsequent liquidation, breached the treaty's investment protections independently of whatever the ICC tribunal found on the underlying commercial contract. That treaty claim was no longer pending by 2026: a 2016 award on jurisdiction and merits found India liable under the India-Mauritius BIT, followed by an award on quantum on 13 October 2020. What remains contested is enforcing that award against Indian state assets abroad, where India has raised sovereign immunity defences, discussed further below. For a foreign investor, the lesson is that a single adverse state action can generate a commercial claim, a treaty claim and a domestic proceeding working in different directions at once, each on its own clock, and that a state defending against all three has shown itself willing to use findings in one proceeding, including its own domestic court rulings, as ammunition in the others.

Structuring around treaty protection, and its limits

Because treaty protection depends on the investor's nationality rather than on where the economic activity actually takes place, investors have long structured investments through intermediate holding companies in jurisdictions with a favourable BIT with the host state, a practice commonly called treaty shopping. Mauritius, the Netherlands and Singapore have historically been common choices for routing investment into India for this reason, among others connected to tax treaties rather than investment treaties alone.

India's newer-generation treaties push back against this directly. Many include a denial-of-benefits clause, allowing India to refuse treaty protection to an investor that is itself owned or controlled by nationals of a third state with no BIT with India, or that has no substantial business activity in its claimed home jurisdiction beyond the bare fact of incorporation there. A shell holding company with no employees, no office and no real operations in the treaty partner state is increasingly vulnerable to having its treaty claim dismissed at the jurisdictional stage on exactly this ground, a line of argument tribunals in other investor-state disputes worldwide have accepted with increasing frequency in recent years.

For an investor planning a new structure specifically to capture treaty protection, the practical implication is that the holding jurisdiction chosen needs to reflect genuine commercial substance, not simply the existence of a favourable treaty text, and that the choice should be made before the investment is made and the dispute has already arisen, since restructuring after a dispute is visible is far more likely to be challenged as an attempt to manufacture jurisdiction after the fact, a pattern several tribunals have specifically scrutinised and rejected in unrelated disputes.

None of this means treaty-conscious structuring is improper in itself. Choosing to invest through a jurisdiction with a BIT in force, a reasonably short local-remedies window and no history of denial-of-benefits disputes with India is an ordinary part of cross-border deal planning, comparable to choosing a tax-efficient holding jurisdiction, provided the chosen entity actually carries on real activity there. What tribunals have pushed back against is the thinner version of the practice, an empty holding company inserted purely to acquire a treaty that would not otherwise apply, assembled only once a dispute with the host state already looks likely.

Where claims actually arise: infrastructure, power and natural resources

Reported investor-state disputes involving India, and investment treaty disputes generally across capital-importing states, cluster heavily in sectors where the state itself is a counterparty or a regulator with direct control over the investment's commercial viability: power generation and distribution, mining and natural resource concessions, telecommunications spectrum, and infrastructure built under long-term concession or PPP structures. These are precisely the sectors where a change in a tariff order, a licence condition, a royalty formula or a spectrum allocation can destroy an investment's economics without any single identifiable expropriatory act, making them fertile ground for fair-and-equitable-treatment and indirect-expropriation arguments specifically.

A foreign investor in one of these sectors should treat its underlying concession or licence dispute, which this site's separate guides on power sector change-in-law claims and on infrastructure and PPP termination disputes address from the contractual and regulatory side, as the first and usually the only readily available remedy, with a BIT claim, where one is actually available on the facts, functioning as a longer-term and more expensive fallback rather than a parallel first option. The five-year local remedies requirement discussed above reinforces this sequencing even for investors who do have treaty protection: the domestic dispute has to be pursued seriously in any event, so the treaty claim should be planned as the next step after that domestic process, not abandoned in favour of it.

A further complication specific to these sectors is that the state counterparty is often not the Union government itself but a state-level utility, a sector regulator or a central public sector undertaking, each with its own degree of separation from the sovereign for treaty purposes. Tribunals in disputes against other capital-importing states have had to work through whether a given entity's conduct can be attributed to the state at all under customary international law rules on attribution, and an investor dealing with, say, a state electricity distribution company or a state mining corporation should not assume attribution is automatic simply because the entity is publicly owned.

Bringing a claim: forum, rules and the ICSID gap

India has never ratified the ICSID Convention, the multilateral treaty that establishes the World Bank-affiliated International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes and provides its own self-contained enforcement regime in all member states. Because of this, a BIT claim against India cannot be brought before an ICSID tribunal regardless of what the underlying BIT says, and India's treaties, including the 2016 model, instead designate ad hoc arbitration under the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules, administered by an appointing authority such as the Permanent Court of Arbitration at The Hague, as the default forum. This is exactly the structure Vodafone and Cairn each used.

An ad hoc UNCITRAL award does not carry ICSID's automatic, treaty-based enforcement mechanism. Instead, it has to be enforced as a foreign arbitral award under the ordinary mechanism each enforcing state applies to foreign commercial awards, typically the New York Convention regime, which brings its own set of narrow public-policy and procedural defences into play, as this site's separate guide to enforcing foreign arbitral awards in India explains for the comparable question of enforcing an award in India in the other direction. For a state respondent like India, this generally makes enforcement slower and more contested than it would be under ICSID's streamlined mechanism, a reality the Vodafone and Cairn enforcement efforts, and the Devas enforcement litigation discussed above, have each illustrated in their own way.

Procedurally, an ad hoc claim under the UNCITRAL Rules still requires the investor to give India formal notice of the dispute and observe whatever negotiation or consultation period the specific treaty prescribes, commonly around six months, before a notice of arbitration can be filed, quite apart from the separate five-year local-remedies requirement discussed earlier for treaties on the 2016 model. An investor should treat these as cumulative rather than overlapping requirements: the consultation period is a short procedural formality that applies regardless of how long the underlying domestic litigation has already run, while the local-remedies period is the substantive precondition that determines whether the claim is even arbitrable in the first place.

Winning the award is not the end: sovereign immunity in enforcement

A tribunal ruling against India does not automatically translate into money in the investor's account. India, like most sovereign states, can raise sovereign immunity defences against execution of an award in the courts of the state where enforcement is sought, and has done so. An English High Court ruling in April 2025, in the same Devas Mauritius-BIT enforcement proceedings described above, held that India's ratification of the New York Convention did not, by itself, constitute a waiver of its immunity from execution against state assets in England, rejecting the argument that signing the Convention alone opens the door to seizing sovereign property; the Court of Appeal unanimously affirmed that ruling on 24 June 2026.

This does not mean a BIT award against India is unenforceable. Investors have pursued, and in some reported instances succeeded in advancing, enforcement against specific categories of Indian state or state-instrumentality assets abroad that a court is prepared to treat as commercial rather than sovereign in character, and jurisdictions differ considerably in how readily they draw that line. What it does mean is that an investor should treat a favourable award as the start of a further, potentially lengthy enforcement campaign across whichever jurisdictions hold identifiable Indian state assets, rather than as a self-executing judgment, and should factor the cost and uncertainty of that campaign into any decision about whether to accept a negotiated settlement along the lines India offered Vodafone and Cairn instead.

What remains unsettled

Several points bearing directly on a foreign investor's treaty position in India are still moving rather than settled. The precise current count of India's BITs in force is reported inconsistently across sources even for 2026, reflecting how quickly the renegotiation process is adding new treaties to a base that had shrunk to single digits; an investor needs to check the specific treaty position for its own home state directly rather than rely on a general figure. India's further revision of its own Model BIT, reportedly nearing Cabinet approval with a shorter local-remedies window under discussion, has not been published in final form as of this writing, so the exact terms that will apply to future treaties remain to be seen.

The interaction between a domestic set-aside or annulment of an award by Indian courts and that award's enforceability abroad, the specific point left open in the Devas enforcement litigation in the United States, has not been authoritatively resolved and is likely to keep generating conflicting rulings across jurisdictions for some time. And the limitation period applicable to enforcement proceedings themselves, as distinct from the underlying claim, is an area where even the comparable question for ordinary foreign money judgments, addressed in this site's separate guide to enforcing a foreign money judgment in India, has produced inconsistent judicial statements that have not been fully reconciled.

Finally, how India's upcoming treaty partners, particularly the European Union and Saudi Arabia, will actually position taxation measures, the local-remedies window and dispute settlement generally in their own negotiations with India is not yet known, and an investor from a state currently negotiating with India should treat any publicly reported draft position as provisional until the treaty is actually signed and ratified.

A bilateral investment treaty is not a background clause an investor can safely ignore while focusing on the commercial contract. It is a separate, state-to-state instrument whose existence, scope and procedural preconditions depend entirely on the investor's own nationality, corporate structure and the specific treaty, if any, in force between India and that state at the relevant time, and India's treaty network has changed so substantially since 2016 that an assumption based on the older, broader generation of BITs is now more likely to be wrong than right.

An investor in a sector exposed to regulatory or tariff risk, particularly power, mining, telecommunications and long-term infrastructure concessions, should establish early, ideally before the investment is structured rather than after a dispute has already surfaced, whether a BIT is actually available, what standard of treatment it promises, how long the local-remedies clock runs, and what enforcement realities would follow even a favourable award. The contractual arbitration clause in the investment agreement remains the first and usually the faster remedy; the treaty claim, where one exists, is the fallback worth understanding in advance rather than discovering only once the dispute has already gone wrong.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a bilateral investment treaty and how is it different from the arbitration clause in my contract?

A bilateral investment treaty is an agreement between India and another country promising certain standards of treatment to each other's investors. It lets a qualifying foreign investor bring a claim directly against the Indian state for breaches like unfair treatment or uncompensated expropriation, separately from any arbitration clause in the investor's own commercial contract with an Indian counterparty, which only governs disputes with that counterparty under that contract.

Can a foreign investor go straight to international arbitration against India without first suing in Indian courts?

Usually not under India's current treaty model. The 2016 Model BIT and treaties following it require an investor to pursue available Indian judicial and administrative remedies for a minimum period, generally around five years though some newer treaties shorten this, before bringing an international claim, unless no effective domestic remedy exists at all.

Is India a member of ICSID, and does that matter for a treaty claim?

India has never ratified the ICSID Convention, so a BIT claim against India cannot be brought before an ICSID tribunal. India's treaties instead provide for ad hoc arbitration, typically under the UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules, which carries a different and generally less streamlined enforcement mechanism than an ICSID award would have.

What actually happened with the Vodafone and Cairn tax arbitration awards?

Separate tribunals ruled against India in 2020 in both cases, over India's 2012 retrospective tax amendment. India later passed the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021, withdrawing that retrospective tax application for pre-2012 transactions on condition that the taxpayer withdraw all related proceedings; Cairn accepted those terms and received a substantial refund.

Which countries currently have a bilateral investment treaty in force with India?

The list has shrunk sharply since 2016 and is still being rebuilt through renegotiation on India's new model, with the UAE, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Brazil and Israel among the more recent additions. Many major capital-exporting countries currently have no BIT in force with India at all, so this has to be checked for the specific investor's home state rather than assumed.

Does a foreign investor's Indian subsidiary get bilateral investment treaty protection on its own?

Generally no. BIT protection runs to the foreign investor and its qualifying investment, based on the investor's own nationality, not to the Indian subsidiary itself, which is an Indian company subject to Indian law like any other. Newer treaties also scrutinise whether the claiming entity has genuine substance in its claimed home jurisdiction before allowing the claim to proceed.

If a tribunal rules against India, can the investor actually collect the award?

Not automatically. India has raised sovereign immunity defences against enforcement abroad, and an English court has held that India's ratification of the New York Convention alone does not waive that immunity. Investors typically need to identify specific commercial, non-sovereign Indian state assets abroad to enforce against, which can take years.

What should a foreign investor do before investing to preserve treaty protection?

Confirm whether a BIT is actually in force between India and the investor's genuine home jurisdiction, structure any holding company with real commercial substance rather than as a bare shell, and keep records showing the investment and its ownership chain from the outset, since restructuring only after a dispute has emerged is far more likely to be challenged as an attempt to manufacture treaty access after the fact.
