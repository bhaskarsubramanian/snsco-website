Fugitive Economic Offender Declarations, Red Corner Notices and Extradition: What Each One Actually Does

Four separate legal tools get treated as one and the same whenever a promoter goes abroad and stops answering a court's summons. They are not the same, and the difference changes what a bank, a family member or an NRI should actually do.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 17 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

When a promoter facing a bank fraud case or a large default stops responding and leaves the country, four different words start getting used about the same person as though they meant one thing: fugitive economic offender, Red Corner Notice, extradition and Look Out Circular. Each is a separate legal mechanism under a separate law, run by a separate authority, with its own test and its own limits. A lender deciding where to put its recovery effort, a family member caught in an unrelated civil suit, or an NRI stopped at an airport over a civil dispute all need to know which of the four is actually in play, because the remedy for one does nothing against the other three.

Key points

  • A person becomes a Fugitive Economic Offender only through a Special Court order under the 2018 Act, and only for offences valued at Rs 100 crore or more
  • Declaration confiscates property to the Central Government free of all encumbrances, which can override a secured lender's own claim over the same asset
  • Section 14 can also stop a connected company or LLP from pursuing or defending any civil claim, reaching well beyond the underlying fraud case
  • A Red Corner Notice is an Interpol request to locate and provisionally detain a person; the Supreme Court has held it is not an arrest warrant and cannot bypass the Extradition Act, 1962
  • Actual extradition runs through a magistrate's prima facie inquiry in India and then through the requested country's own courts, which is usually where years are lost
  • A Look Out Circular rests on executive guidelines, not a statute, and courts have struck it down where it was used to pressure a civil debtor rather than to stop a genuine flight risk
  • As of the Enforcement Directorate's own reporting, 21 people have been declared fugitive economic offenders and roughly Rs 2,178 crore confiscated since the Act came into force
  • No Supreme Court ruling has yet settled whether Section 14's bar on civil claims survives an Article 21 challenge

Why the Four Labels Get Confused, and Why That Matters

News coverage of an absconding promoter tends to use "declared a fugitive economic offender", "Red Corner Notice issued" and "extradition sought" as though they were three ways of saying the same thing. A Look Out Circular gets added to the mix as a fourth near-synonym. They are four separate legal mechanisms, created under three separate statutes and one set of executive guidelines, administered by three different authorities, and each has its own test, its own procedure and its own limit on what it can actually achieve. Treating them as interchangeable produces bad advice at the exact moment good advice is needed.

The distinction carries practical weight for people who are not the headline defendant at all. A bank or an asset reconstruction company assessing recovery against a defaulting promoter needs to know which of the four tools actually freezes an asset and which only restricts travel. A spouse or sibling who is a party to an unconnected partition or matrimonial suit needs to know whether a declaration made against a family member elsewhere in the family can derail their own case. An NRI with no criminal case against them at all can still find a Look Out Circular opened on a purely civil loan dispute, long before any court has recorded a finding against them. A foreign lender or investor pricing counterparty risk needs an honest sense of how slow and contested the full chain from declaration to actual surrender really is, because the recovery timeline implied by the first news report rarely matches reality.

The scale of what is actually happening under the principal statute is modest next to the coverage it gets. By the Enforcement Directorate's own reporting, proceedings under the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018 had been initiated against 54 individuals and declarations secured against 21 of them, with about Rs 2,178 crore in assets confiscated since the Act came into force. A separate parliamentary answer from October 2025 put the number of declared offenders at 15 at that date, including Vijay Mallya and Nirav Modi, with a combined principal loss to banks of Rs 26,645 crore. The gap between the two counts is explained by declarations made in the intervening months, not by any inconsistency in the law.

Who Can Actually Become a "Fugitive Economic Offender"

The Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018 does not create a new crime. It attaches a civil consequence to a narrow factual situation: a person against whom an Indian court has issued an arrest warrant for a Scheduled Offence has either left India to dodge prosecution, or is already abroad and has refused to return to face it. That is the two-limb definition under Section 2(1)(f). Nothing in the Act requires a conviction, or even a finding that the underlying charge will succeed, at the point of declaration.

A Scheduled Offence is not every criminal charge. Section 2(1)(m) restricts the Act to offences listed in its Schedule, drawn mainly from the Indian Penal Code, the Prevention of Money-laundering Act, the Companies Act, the SEBI Act, the Customs Act and similar legislation, and only where the total value involved is one hundred crore rupees or more. A default, a cheque bounce or a fraud below that threshold stays in the ordinary criminal and civil process; it never reaches this Act regardless of how determined the complainant is.

The forum is itself telling. Declarations are made not by an ordinary criminal court but by a Special Court designated under Section 43(1) of the Prevention of Money-laundering Act, 2002, which the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act borrows wholesale as its own "Special Court" under Section 2(1)(n). The same bench that handles money-laundering prosecutions handles fugitive declarations, and the authorities who investigate PMLA cases double as the authorities under this Act as well, under Section 4(3).

That borrowing is not an accident of drafting. In practice, almost every reported case under the 2018 Act arrives at the Special Court after the Enforcement Directorate has already registered a money-laundering case over the same facts, because large bank fraud and diversion of funds are themselves scheduled offences under the PMLA. A lender bank is rarely the applicant before the Special Court; its own recourse against the same default runs separately through SARFAESI, the Debts Recovery Tribunal or the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, while the ED pursues the fugitive declaration and confiscation on a parallel track driven by the underlying criminal case rather than by the bank's own civil claim.

How a Declaration Is Actually Made

The process starts with an application under Section 4, filed before the Special Court by a Director, or by an officer not below the rank of Deputy Director whom the Director has authorised. The officer must record in writing the reasons for believing the person is a fugitive economic offender, and the application must set out the person's last known whereabouts, an estimate of the proceeds of crime (including any portion held abroad), and a list of properties, including benami properties, owned in India or overseas.

Once the application is admitted, Section 10 requires the Special Court to issue notice to the named individual and to anyone else with an interest in the listed properties. The notice must fix a date and place of appearance that is not less than six weeks from the date the notice issues, and it must warn that non-appearance will result in a declaration and confiscation. Service is not limited to the conventional route: the Court can direct notice to the email address linked to the person's PAN application or Aadhaar enrolment, or to a foreign authority notified for service in a contracting state, which is meant to address exactly the scenario this Act is built for, a person who has already left the country.

What happens next depends on whether the person shows up. Appearing in person under Section 11(1) can end the proceeding there. Appearing only through counsel earns a week to file a reply under Section 11(2). If nobody appears, the Special Court can still proceed, but only after recording in writing that it is satisfied service was effected, or that the person deliberately evaded it despite the authorities' best efforts, under Section 11(3). A person who returns to India and submits to the court's jurisdiction before the declaration is actually made stops the process at that point; the consequence attaches to continued absence, not to the underlying charge by itself.

What the Declaration Actually Triggers

A declaration under Section 12(1) is the Special Court recording, with reasons, that it is satisfied the person meets the Act's definition. The confiscation that follows under Section 12(2) reaches two categories: proceeds of crime located in India or abroad, whoever currently holds them, and any other property or benami property in India or abroad that the offender owns, whether or not it has any connection to the underlying offence at all. Where the proceeds of crime cannot be precisely identified, the Court is required to quantify their value instead, under Sections 12(3) and 12(4).

The vesting that follows a confiscation order is immediate and absolute. Section 12(8) vests the right and title to the confiscated property in the Central Government from the date of the order, free from all encumbrances. For a secured lender, this is the clause that actually matters: a mortgage, charge or pledge that would ordinarily give a bank priority under SARFAESI or before the Debts Recovery Tribunal can be overtaken by a later confiscation order, unless the lender has already stepped in. Section 12(7) gives the Special Court discretion to carve out property in which a third party holds an interest acquired bona fide and without knowledge that it was proceeds of crime, which makes intervening in the Section 4 proceeding itself, rather than waiting for the confiscation order, the only reliable way to protect a genuine secured claim. If the Court finds, once the proceeding concludes, that the person was not a fugitive economic offender after all, Section 12(9) requires any attached or seized property to be released.

Section 14: A Bar That Can Reach Well Beyond the Fraud Case

Section 14 is the part of the Act that draws the most sustained criticism, and for a specific reason: it operates independently of the confiscation and can be invoked in litigation that has nothing to do with the original offence. Under Section 14(a), once a person is declared a fugitive economic offender, any court or tribunal in India may disallow that person from putting forward or defending any civil claim. Section 14(b) extends the same bar to a company or LLP connected to the offender, where the person bringing or defending the claim on the entity's behalf is a promoter, key managerial personnel, majority shareholder or someone with a controlling interest who has themselves been declared a fugitive economic offender.

Commentary on the Act has flagged that nothing in the text confines this bar to claims connected with the scheduled offence. On a plain reading it could in principle reach an unrelated civil suit to which the declared person happens to be a party, including matrimonial, succession or family-settlement litigation, though the word "may" in Section 14 leaves the court or tribunal discretion on whether to apply it in a given case rather than making the bar automatic. Parliament's own Legislative Brief on the Bill flagged the Article 21 concern squarely: barring a person from accessing a court to defend a civil claim sits uneasily against the Supreme Court's recognition of access to justice as part of the guarantee of personal liberty.

That constitutional question remains open as of today. No Supreme Court ruling has decided whether Section 14 survives an Article 21 challenge. The one concrete development on this front is procedural rather than substantive: in December 2025, the Bombay High Court told Vijay Mallya it would not take up his constitutional challenge to the Act's validity unless he submits to the court's jurisdiction, and ordered him to file an affidavit on when he intends to return to India; it indicated it could still consider his separate appeal against his own 2019 Special Court declaration, even in his absence, but added that the two petitions could not proceed together and one would have to be withdrawn. His counsel argued that physical presence should not be a precondition to challenging a statute's validity; the Court's position, as reported, was unmoved by that argument.

Four Different Tools, Not One Escalating Process

It helps to set out all four side by side, because none of them is a precondition for any other. A Fugitive Economic Offender declaration is a Special Court order under the 2018 Act, built to confiscate assets of someone who has fled an Indian criminal case. A Red Corner Notice is an Interpol instrument, requested by the CBI as India's National Central Bureau, asking other member countries to locate a wanted person and provisionally detain or restrict their movement pending extradition. An extradition request is a formal government-to-government process under the Extradition Act, 1962, aimed at physically securing the person's return. A Look Out Circular is an executive instruction, resting on no statute at all, that stops a named person from leaving India through an airport or seaport.

They do not need each other to exist. A person can be stopped by a Look Out Circular over a civil dispute with no criminal case, no Red Corner Notice and no FEOA proceeding anywhere near them. A Red Corner Notice can be withdrawn by Interpol's own Commission for the Control of Files, as happened with Mehul Choksi in November 2022, while the underlying extradition request to Belgium and the separate FEOA proceeding in India continued regardless; the CBI's own public position has been that a Red Notice is "neither a prerequisite nor a requirement" for extradition to proceed. And a declaration under the 2018 Act can be made against someone who was never the subject of any Interpol notice at all, since the trigger is the Special Court's own satisfaction, not an Interpol listing.

The Supreme Court settled the legal status of a Red Corner Notice as far back as 2009, in Bhavesh Jayanti Lakhani v. State of Maharashtra. The Court held that a Red Corner Notice is not itself an arrest warrant and cannot be used to bypass the statutory procedure laid down in the Extradition Act, 1962; it also rejected the view that a High Court has no power to examine how a notice is being enforced, holding that superior courts retain supervisory jurisdiction over coercive action taken under one. That holding is still the reference point whenever a notice is challenged as a substitute for due legal process rather than what it actually is, a request to locate and hold someone.

Interpol's own constitution imposes a further constraint that lenders chasing a defaulting borrower abroad often overlook. The organisation's rules bar it from any involvement in matters of a purely political, military, religious or racial character, and a request built on what is really a private commercial or matrimonial dispute, dressed up as a criminal complaint to get a Red Corner Notice issued, runs the risk of later removal once Interpol's Commission for the Control of Files reviews the underlying facts. That is close to what happened with Mehul Choksi's notice in 2022, where the Commission withdrew his name from the list and the CBI had to make a formal case for restoring it. The lesson for anyone relying on a notice as proof of guilt, or anyone trying to get one lifted, is the same: the notice records Interpol's own satisfaction that its narrow criteria are met at a point in time, and that satisfaction can be revisited and reversed independently of whatever is happening in the Indian courts or the country where the person is actually located.

How Extradition From India Actually Works

The Extradition Act, 1962 does not require a bilateral treaty before India can seek or grant extradition, though a treaty is the ordinary route. Section 3 lets the Central Government extend the Act to a treaty state by notification. For a country with no treaty, Sections 3(4), 29 and 32 together give the Central Government discretion to extend the Act's machinery by a general or special order, and Section 32 makes clear that the Central Government's power to discharge a fugitive under Section 29 applies to every foreign state regardless of treaty status, which is why extradition activity with non-treaty countries is possible but depends entirely on executive discretion in a way that treaty-based requests do not.

Where India is the requesting state, the domestic leg runs through a magistrate's inquiry under Section 7. The magistrate's role is deliberately narrow: to decide whether a prima facie case exists in support of the foreign request, not to conduct anything resembling a full trial, which is reserved for the courts of the country to which the person will eventually be sent. If no prima facie case is made out, Section 7(3) requires the magistrate to discharge the person outright. Once that inquiry is complete, the file goes to the Central Government, which has its own discretion on whether to proceed, before the matter crosses into the requested country's own legal system.

That last stage is where years are typically lost, and it is worth being precise about whose process is actually running at any given time. India's own magisterial and Central Government steps against Vijay Mallya and Nirav Modi were completed years ago. What has taken the rest of the time is the appellate and human-rights litigation inside the United Kingdom's own courts, up to and including applications to the European Court of Human Rights. Reporting in mid-2026 recorded the European Court rejecting Nirav Modi's final such application, which removed his remaining UK-side legal avenues, though the actual physical transfer still depends on administrative steps that follow a court clearing the path, and nothing in that reporting confirmed a transfer had yet occurred. Mallya's case has been described as stuck at a comparably final stage for several years, independent of the separate Bombay High Court proceedings over his FEOA declaration.

A September 2026 Kerala High Court ruling, in Rakhul Krishnan v. Union of India, is a reminder that extradition machinery also runs the other way: when a treaty state's own authorities issue an international arrest warrant and route it through Interpol to India's nodal agency, that agency cannot simply sit on it because no formal extradition request has yet followed. The Division Bench held that the warrant must be forwarded to the Central Government and the jurisdictional magistrate, and that an arrest under it still requires a magistrate's intervention rather than happening automatically on the strength of the Interpol communication alone.

Look Out Circulars: An Executive Tool With No Statute Behind It

Unlike the other three mechanisms, a Look Out Circular has no legislative basis at all. Its authority comes from Ministry of Home Affairs executive instructions, read together with the general powers under criminal and immigration law, not from a dedicated Act of Parliament. That has not stopped it from becoming the most commonly invoked of the four tools, precisely because it is the fastest to obtain and the one that most directly affects an NRI who may have no criminal case pending anywhere.

In March 2026 the Ministry revised its guidelines to curb a pattern of misuse that had drawn repeated judicial criticism: statutory bodies and investigating agencies can no longer directly instruct the Bureau of Immigration to open a circular on their own say-so, a circular can only be opened in a criminal or penal matter, and the reason for opening it must be recorded at the time it is issued. The revised guidelines also fixed a tight procedural clock for what happens once someone is actually stopped: the agency that asked for the circular has three hours to take the detained person into custody, failing which the person is handed over to the local police, and the originating agency then has a further twenty-four hours to complete formal custody.

Courts have been explicit about the test a circular has to meet to survive a challenge. A Look Out Circular can continue only where there is cogent material showing a real risk the person will evade arrest, fail to comply with a court's coercive process, or abscond to defeat ongoing proceedings. In October 2026, the Delhi High Court addressed the fact pattern that recurs most often with NRIs: a bank or creditor pushing for a circular purely to pressure repayment of a civil debt, with no criminal case and no genuine flight risk beyond the debtor living abroad. The Court held that mere suspicion that a person has opened accounts overseas, or a wish to use travel restriction as leverage for an outstanding debt, cannot by itself justify restricting the person's right to travel abroad under Article 21.

The practical point for an NRI stopped at departure is that a Look Out Circular proves nothing about Red Corner Notice status, extradition proceedings or an FEOA declaration, and none of those three other mechanisms needs to exist for a circular to have been opened. It also does not freeze any asset; it only stops a person crossing the border. The remedy sits with the jurisdictional High Court through a writ petition seeking quashing or modification of the circular, not with any appeal to Interpol, the CBI or the agency that requested it.

Practical Points for Lenders, Families and NRIs

A lender weighing recovery against an absconding promoter gains little from tracking Red Corner Notice or extradition news, since neither tool touches an asset. What actually determines priority over a specific property is whether an FEOA confiscation order has been made, because Section 12(8) vests title in the Central Government free of all encumbrances the moment that order is passed. A secured creditor with a genuine, bona fide charge should consider seeking to be heard, and seeking the Section 12(7) exemption, in the Section 4 proceeding itself, well before any confiscation order, rather than relying on a parallel SARFAESI or DRT claim to survive the vesting automatically.

A family member who is a party to an unrelated civil suit, such as a partition, matrimonial or succession matter, alongside someone who has separately been declared a fugitive economic offender should check, early, whether Section 14(b) could be invoked against a connected company or LLP in which that person held a role, and whether Section 14(a) could in principle be raised against the declared individual's own participation in the unrelated suit. Raising the Article 21 access-to-justice argument squarely before the court or tribunal hearing that suit, rather than waiting to see whether the bar is invoked at all, keeps the point live for appeal given that no Supreme Court ruling has yet resolved it either way.

An NRI stopped under a Look Out Circular with no criminal case in sight should start with the jurisdictional High Court, by writ, seeking quashing on the ground that the circular lacks the cogent material courts now require, rather than treating it as the first step in a larger extradition process it may have nothing to do with. Conversely, a person actually served with a Section 10 notice under the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act should treat the six-week clock as real: appearing, even only through counsel, preserves a week to respond under Section 11(2) and keeps the matter within a process the person can still influence, while non-appearance risks an ex parte declaration once the Special Court records that it is satisfied service was effected or deliberately evaded.

Building a writ against a Look Out Circular is largely a documentation exercise rather than an argument about broad principle. The petition needs the circular itself, obtained through an RTI request or produced by the airline or immigration authority at the point of refusal, since the originating agency rarely discloses it voluntarily; a clear statement of whether any criminal case actually names the petitioner, and if so its stage; and evidence that the petitioner has in fact complied with every summons, notice or court process issued so far, since that is precisely the absence of cogent material the Delhi High Court has pointed to in striking circulars down. Filed promptly and supported this way, such a petition is usually listed and heard within weeks rather than months, because the restriction on movement under Article 21 is treated as the kind of urgent personal-liberty matter High Courts take up out of turn.

What Remains Unsettled

Two questions sit open as on this date. The first is whether Section 14's bar on civil claims can withstand an Article 21 challenge when applied to litigation that has no connection to the underlying scheduled offence; Parliament's own pre-enactment review flagged the risk, and no apex court decision has yet tested it against a concrete fact pattern. The second, procedural rather than substantive, is whether a person can be made to return to India and submit to a court's jurisdiction before that same court will even hear a challenge to the statute's validity, which is the position the Bombay High Court has taken in Vijay Mallya's case and which his own counsel has disputed as a matter of principle.

Until either question is settled by a higher court, the safer course for anyone actually facing a Section 10 notice, or advising a company that could be caught by Section 14(b), is to raise the constitutional point at the first available opportunity before the Special Court or Tribunal actually seized of the matter, rather than hold it in reserve for an appeal that may never need to be filed if the point succeeds earlier.

A Fugitive Economic Offender declaration, a Red Corner Notice, an extradition request and a Look Out Circular answer four different questions: whether a Special Court will confiscate assets, whether Interpol will help locate someone, whether a foreign country will hand a person over, and whether that person can leave India at all right now. None of the four needs the others to exist, and confusing one for another leads people to look for a remedy, or to fear a consequence, in the wrong forum entirely.

The one feature common to all four is that the end result rarely arrives quickly. Years pass between a Section 10 notice and a confiscation order, and years more between a Red Corner Notice and an actual surrender, with most of that time spent not in India's own courts but in the requested country's domestic appeals. Anyone dealing with one of these four mechanisms, whether as a creditor, a family member, or the person named in the notice, gets further by identifying exactly which one is actually in play than by reacting to the headline that uses all four names interchangeably.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a Red Corner Notice and being declared a fugitive economic offender?

A Red Corner Notice is an Interpol request asking other countries to locate and provisionally detain a person; it does not freeze any asset and the Supreme Court has held it is not an arrest warrant. A fugitive economic offender declaration is an Indian Special Court order under the 2018 Act that confiscates property. A person can have either without the other.

Can a Look Out Circular be opened against someone with no criminal case at all?

The Ministry of Home Affairs' own March 2026 guidelines say a circular should only be opened in a criminal or penal matter, but courts have repeatedly found circulars opened purely to pressure repayment of a civil debt, with no genuine criminal case or flight risk. Such a circular is challengeable by writ before the jurisdictional High Court.

Does an Interpol Red Corner Notice amount to an arrest warrant in India?

No. The Supreme Court held in Bhavesh Jayanti Lakhani v. State of Maharashtra in 2009 that a Red Corner Notice cannot be used to bypass the procedure under the Extradition Act, 1962, and that High Courts retain the power to examine how coercive action is taken under one.

What happens to a company if its promoter is declared a fugitive economic offender?

Section 14(b) of the Act allows a court or tribunal to disallow a company or LLP from pursuing or defending a civil claim if the person acting for it, such as a promoter, key managerial person or majority shareholder, has been declared a fugitive economic offender. The bar is discretionary, not automatic, and can be contested on the facts.

Can property outside India be confiscated under the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act?

Yes. Section 12(2) extends confiscation to proceeds of crime and other property, including benami property, located either in India or abroad, provided the Special Court is satisfied it belongs to or was derived by the declared offender.

Is there a minimum amount involved before the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act applies?

Yes. The Act applies only to a Scheduled Offence where the total value involved is one hundred crore rupees or more, under Section 2(1)(m). A fraud or default below that threshold is dealt with under ordinary criminal and civil law, not this Act.

How long does extradition from India typically take?

India's own magisterial inquiry and Central Government decision are usually completed within a reasonable time, but where the requested country's courts hear repeated appeals, including human rights challenges, the overall process has stretched to the better part of a decade in the most reported cases, such as those involving Vijay Mallya and Nirav Modi.

Can a person contest an FEO declaration without returning to India?

The law does not require physical presence to file a challenge, but at least one High Court, in Vijay Mallya's case in December 2025, has declined to hear a constitutional challenge to the Act's validity until the person returns to India and submits to the court's jurisdiction, while treating a separate appeal against the declaration order itself differently. That position has not been tested before the Supreme Court.
