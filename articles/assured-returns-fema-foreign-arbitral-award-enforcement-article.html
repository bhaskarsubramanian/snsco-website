Assured Returns, FEMA and Enforcing a Foreign Arbitral Award in India: What GPE v Twarit Actually Settled

A 2025 Supreme Court order removed one obstacle foreign investors had been fighting for years; it did not remove every obstacle

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 24 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign investor who wins an arbitration arising from an Indian joint venture or share purchase agreement still has to enforce that award in India, and for years the Enforcement Directorate's and the RBI's view on assured-return structures stood in the way. The Supreme Court's August 2025 order in GPE (India) Ltd v Twarit Consultancy Services Pvt Ltd, read with its 2020 ruling in Vijay Karia v Prysmian Cavi e Sistemi, narrows that obstacle considerably. It does not remove it entirely, and a similar question is still pending before the Court in a separate matter involving Nine Rivers Capital.

Key points

  • Section 48 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 lists narrow, exhaustive grounds for refusing to enforce a foreign award, and Indian courts have been told repeatedly to read that list strictly, in the enforcing party's favour.
  • The Supreme Court held in 2020, in Vijay Karia v Prysmian Cavi e Sistemi, that a FEMA violation in the underlying transaction is not by itself a ground to refuse enforcement of a foreign award under Section 48.
  • In August 2025, in GPE (India) Ltd v Twarit Consultancy Services, the Supreme Court went further and held that paying compensatory damages under a foreign award does not need prior RBI approval, because such a payment is a current account transaction under FEMA.
  • Both rulings concerned structures that promised a fixed or guaranteed return to a foreign investor, a feature India's FEMA pricing rules for equity instruments do not allow, which is precisely why the underlying FEMA objection was raised in the first place.
  • A similar question, involving Nine Rivers Capital, was reported in 2026 as still pending before the Supreme Court, with the RBI impleaded; this piece treats that matter as open and does not predict its outcome.
  • Removing the enforcement-stage objection does not remove every FEMA consequence; the RBI can still examine the original pricing breach separately, including through compounding, and remittance of the award amount out of India still has to clear ordinary banking-channel compliance.
  • For a foreign investor negotiating an Indian joint venture or share purchase agreement today, the safer course remains to structure the return as a FEMA-compliant pricing mechanism from the outset rather than relying on a later Section 48 argument to rescue an assured-return clause.

Why a win in arbitration is not the end of the story

A foreign investor who puts money into an Indian company by way of equity, compulsorily convertible instruments or a similar route, and negotiates a shareholders' agreement promising an exit at a guaranteed price or a minimum internal rate of return, is negotiating something India's foreign exchange law does not straightforwardly allow. The Reserve Bank of India's pricing guidelines for equity and equity-linked instruments held by a person resident outside India require the exit price to be determined by an internationally accepted pricing methodology at the time of exit, not fixed in advance as a guaranteed number, because a fixed, assured return on an equity instrument looks, to the regulator, like disguised debt rather than genuine risk capital.

When the Indian counterparty defaults on that kind of promise and the matter goes to arbitration, typically seated outside India under the joint venture agreement's dispute resolution clause, the foreign investor usually wins on the contract. The harder fight often begins only after the award is in hand, when the award debtor argues before an Indian court that enforcing it would require giving effect to a transaction that itself breached FEMA, and that doing so would be contrary to India's public policy under Section 48(2)(b) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. Two Supreme Court rulings, five years apart, now answer that argument, and this piece sets out what they actually decided and what they leave open.

Section 48: a narrow and pro-enforcement gateway

Section 48 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 lists the specific grounds on which a court may refuse to enforce a foreign award, including incapacity of a party, an invalid arbitration agreement, lack of proper notice, an award exceeding the scope of the submission to arbitration, an improperly constituted tribunal, an award not yet binding or set aside in the country where it was made, and, under Section 48(2), that the subject matter of the dispute is not capable of settlement by arbitration under Indian law or that enforcement would be contrary to India's public policy. The provision deliberately mirrors the New York Convention's own narrow list, and Indian courts enforcing foreign awards are expected to treat that list as exhaustive and to construe each ground strictly against the party resisting enforcement.

The public policy ground is where FEMA objections typically land, because an award debtor will argue that an award requiring payment under, or giving effect to, a transaction that breached India's exchange control law offends the fundamental policy of Indian law, one of the three limbs Explanation 1 to Section 48(2)(b) recognises after the 2015 amendment narrowed what public policy can mean in this context. The Supreme Court in Vijay Karia v Prysmian Cavi e Sistemi SRL, decided on 13 February 2020 and reported at (2020) 11 SCC 1, confronted this argument directly in a dispute arising from a joint venture among the shareholders of Ravin Cables and an Italian cable manufacturer, where the award was made in London under the rules of the London Court of International Arbitration.

The three limbs of 'public policy' after the 2015 amendment

Explanation 1 to Section 48(2)(b), inserted by the Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Act, 2015, confines public policy for this purpose to three limbs: an award induced or affected by fraud or corruption, an award that contravenes the fundamental policy of Indian law, and an award that conflicts with the most basic notions of morality or justice. This followed a line of case law in which the Supreme Court itself had narrowed the ground considerably two years earlier. A two-judge bench had briefly widened public policy for foreign awards in Phulchand Exports Ltd v OOO Patriot in 2011, reading it as carrying the same broad meaning it had for domestic awards, including patent illegality; a three-judge bench in Shri Lal Mahal Ltd v Progetto Grano SpA, decided 3 July 2013 in Civil Appeal No. 5085 of 2013, overruled Phulchand and returned the test for foreign awards to the narrower Renusagar standard, a position Explanation 1 then set down in statutory language in 2015. An award debtor arguing a FEMA breach has to fit that argument into one of the three surviving limbs, and 'fundamental policy of Indian law' is the only one that comes close.

The Supreme Court's own gloss on 'fundamental policy of Indian law', developed in the domestic-award context in Associate Builders v Delhi Development Authority, decided 25 November 2014, and carried across to foreign awards by later decisions including Vijay Karia itself, confines the phrase to the fundamental, core principles underlying Indian statutes and judicial precedent, such as compliance with a statute intended to protect a weaker party or basic notions of fairness and a judicial, non-arbitrary approach, rather than every regulatory requirement found somewhere in Indian law. FEMA's exchange control provisions, important as they are to India's monetary and regulatory architecture, have been treated by this line of authority as falling short of that fundamental-policy threshold on their own, which is the doctrinal basis on which Vijay Karia and GPE were able to say a FEMA breach, without more, does not satisfy Explanation 1.

Vijay Karia: a FEMA breach is not, by itself, a public policy bar

The Court in Vijay Karia held that a breach of FEMA in the underlying transaction cannot, without more, be treated as a violation of the fundamental policy of Indian law for the purposes of refusing enforcement under Section 48. The reasoning draws a distinction most Indian regulatory statutes share: FEMA is a civil, remedial statute built around compounding and penalty, not a provision whose every breach automatically voids the transaction it attaches to or offends some free-standing notion of Indian public policy. Reading a FEMA breach into Section 48(2)(b) as a matter of course would let an award debtor resist almost any cross-border award by pointing to some regulatory irregularity somewhere in the underlying deal, which is precisely the kind of expansive public policy review the 2015 amendment to Section 48 was designed to shut down.

The Court also dealt with how the enforcing court should treat the fact that Section 48 says a court 'may' refuse enforcement even where a listed ground is technically made out, which commentary on the case describes as leaving the enforcing court with residual discretion to enforce an award despite a proven ground, rather than treating every proven ground as an automatic bar. Vijay Karia left open, deliberately, exactly how the Reserve Bank's own regulatory interest in a FEMA-breaching structure should be accommodated once the court declines to let that breach block enforcement outright, and that is the gap the GPE case was decided to fill five years later.

GPE v Twarit: no prior RBI approval needed to pay damages under a foreign award

GPE (India) Ltd v Twarit Consultancy Services Private Limited arose from a dispute over share purchase agreements that an arbitral tribunal found had been breached, awarding damages and interest to the foreign investor side. According to commentary consistent across several sources, the underlying investment structure had promised foreign investors a guaranteed internal rate of return reported at 24 per cent, the kind of assured-return feature the RBI's pricing guidelines for equity instruments do not permit. When the award creditor sought enforcement, the Madras High Court allowed it but made enforcement conditional on the award debtor first obtaining the Reserve Bank's approval before the compensatory damages could be paid, treating the payment as requiring specific regulatory clearance because of the underlying FEMA concern.

The Supreme Court, in an order dated 26 August 2025, set that condition aside. Deferring to the position the RBI itself took in the proceedings, the Court held that paying compensatory damages pursuant to a foreign arbitral award is a current account transaction under Section 5 of FEMA, read with the Foreign Exchange Management (Current Account Transactions) Rules, 2000, rather than a capital account transaction requiring prior approval, and concluded there was no legal impediment to enforcing the award without that approval being obtained first. In substance, the Court separated two questions that the Madras High Court's conditional order had run together: whether the award can be enforced at all, which Vijay Karia already answered in the award creditor's favour, and whether the specific payment of damages needs the RBI's prior sign-off, which GPE now answers in the negative for compensatory damages paid as a current account transaction.

Multiple reports note that a related matter, Nine Rivers Capital Limited, involving a comparable combination of assured returns and an alleged FEMA breach in a foreign award for the purchase of shares, was pending before the Supreme Court as of April 2026, with the RBI impleaded as a party. This piece has not found a reported outcome in that matter, and the question of whether Nine Rivers will simply apply GPE's reasoning or add to it remains open as on the date of this article.

What GPE did not decide

GPE answers a narrower question than it is sometimes read to answer. It holds that the remittance of compensatory damages under a foreign award is a current account transaction not requiring prior RBI approval; it does not hold that the underlying transaction's FEMA pricing breach, such as an assured-return clause that priced an equity exit as a fixed guaranteed number rather than through a permitted methodology, has become lawful or is no longer examinable by the regulator at all. The RBI retains its ordinary powers under FEMA to examine the original contravention separately, including through the compounding mechanism under Section 15 of FEMA for contraventions that can be compounded, and nothing in GPE insulates the Indian company or its promoters from that separate regulatory exposure merely because the award itself has been enforced.

Nor does GPE remove the ordinary banking-channel compliance that applies to any outward remittance from India. An authorised dealer bank processing the payment will still require the standard documentation for a current account remittance, including, typically, a chartered accountant's certificate and confirmation of the transaction's character, and the classification of the payment as a current account transaction is itself a finding the RBI supported in that specific case rather than a blanket rule that every payment made pursuant to a foreign award will automatically be so classified; a different factual structure, for instance an award ordering a share transfer at a specified price rather than an award of money damages, could raise a different FEMA question that GPE does not purport to resolve.

Why assured-return clauses keep generating this litigation

The underlying commercial pattern in both Vijay Karia and GPE is familiar to anyone who has negotiated an Indian joint venture or growth-equity investment on behalf of a foreign client. A foreign investor, often reluctant to take pure equity risk in an unfamiliar market, asks for a put option or an exit guarantee pricing their shares at a fixed return, sometimes expressed as a minimum IRR, rather than at fair value determined at the time of exit. The Indian promoter, keen to close the round, agrees. The arrangement is commercially straightforward and extremely common in cross-border venture and private equity deals generally; it is also, under India's FEMA pricing rules for equity and equity-linked instruments, a structure that treats what is formally equity as if it were debt with a guaranteed coupon, which the regulator does not permit without the instrument being priced and classified accordingly from the outset.

Both disputes reached the Supreme Court only because the Indian counterparty, having lost the commercial argument in arbitration, used the FEMA angle as a second line of defence at the enforcement stage rather than as a reason to renegotiate the structure before signing. That sequence, agree to an assured return, lose in arbitration, resist enforcement on FEMA grounds, is now considerably less likely to work after GPE, at least where the only relief sought is a money award for damages. It remains a live risk wherever the award itself orders something other than a straightforward damages payment, such as specific performance of a share transfer at a fixed price, because that kind of relief sits closer to the capital account transaction the RBI's pricing guidelines were written to police.

The procedural route: how a foreign award actually gets enforced in India

A foreign award under Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 is not first converted into a decree by a separate suit; the award creditor files an execution petition, or an application, before the relevant High Court having jurisdiction, usually where the award debtor's assets are located, asking the court to treat the award as deemed to be a decree of that court once the court is satisfied it is enforceable. The debtor then gets the opportunity to raise objections confined to the Section 48 grounds, and the court decides those objections first; once they fail, the award is executed exactly as a money decree would be, through attachment and sale of assets, garnishee proceedings against bank accounts, or other modes available under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.

This single-stage structure is itself a 2001 Supreme Court holding, in Fuerst Day Lawson Ltd v Jindal Exports Ltd, decided 4 May 2001 and reported at (2001) 6 SCC 356, which read Part II of the Act as not requiring a separate declaratory proceeding before execution could begin, a reading that materially shortens the enforcement timeline compared to what award debtors in India could otherwise draw out under a two-stage process. It is also why Vijay Karia and GPE matter as much as they do: because objections under Section 48 are decided within the execution proceeding itself, a FEMA argument accepted by the court at this stage can stall recovery for years, and an argument rejected, as both these rulings rejected it, lets the creditor move straight to attaching assets without having to relitigate the underlying dispute in an Indian court on the merits.

A practical point foreign award creditors sometimes overlook is limitation. Execution of a foreign award in India is generally treated as governed by a twelve-year period under Article 136 of the Limitation Act, 1963, by analogy with execution of a decree, rather than the much shorter three-year period that applies to filing most ordinary civil suits, though the exact limitation question has produced some divergence across High Courts and deserves a fresh check against current authority before an award creditor assumes either period applies to a specific case.

Interim relief before the enforcement fight is even won

Waiting for the enforcement petition to be decided before taking any protective step is often the wrong sequence for a foreign award creditor, particularly where the Indian award debtor or its promoters have other assets that could be moved or encumbered while the Section 48 objections are being argued. Since the 2015 amendment added a proviso to Section 2(2) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, Section 9 relief, interim measures including an order restraining the disposal of assets, is available in support of a foreign-seated arbitration, both before and after the award, unless the parties have expressly or by necessary implication agreed to exclude it. Delhi, Bombay and Calcutta High Court decisions have each applied this proviso to grant Section 9 relief in aid of foreign-seated proceedings, including after the award has already been rendered.

An assured-return dispute of the kind in Vijay Karia and GPE often involves promoters who also control other, unrelated Indian entities, and a foreign award creditor's practical leverage can depend less on winning the Section 48 argument in principle than on having frozen enough value before the debtor has time to restructure its holdings. Counsel acting for a foreign award creditor should treat the interim-relief application as a parallel track from the day the award is rendered, rather than something to consider only once an enforcement petition has been drafted.

Practical structuring for a foreign investor negotiating today

The safer route remains to avoid needing Vijay Karia or GPE at all. A foreign investor's exit protection in an Indian joint venture or investment agreement should be priced through a permitted methodology, such as a fair-value formula applied at the time of exit, rather than through a fixed guaranteed return or IRR stated as a number in the agreement, and any put or call option should be drafted with FEMA's pricing guidelines for equity instruments in mind from the first draft rather than left to be defended later as a damages claim if the deal goes wrong.

Where a foreign investor genuinely wants a fixed, debt-like return rather than equity upside, the cleaner route under FEMA is usually to instrument that return as what it actually is: a compulsorily convertible instrument priced within the permitted band, a genuine debt instrument subject to the applicable external commercial borrowing framework and its own all-in-cost ceilings, or an optionally convertible debenture structured and reported to the RBI as debt from the start, rather than dressing a guaranteed coupon up as an equity put option and hoping an arbitration clause will paper over the mismatch later. Advisers structuring the deal should also build in, as a matter of course, a clause requiring the Indian company to make the FEMA filings the investment itself triggers, such as the relevant forms under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019 and the Foreign Exchange Management (Mode of Payment and Reporting of Non-Debt Instruments) Regulations, 2019, since a failure at the reporting stage is a separate and avoidable source of later regulatory exposure even where the pricing itself is compliant.

Where a dispute has already arisen and an award is in hand, the lesson from GPE is to characterise the relief sought carefully. An award for compensatory damages, framed and quantified as money due for breach rather than as an order compelling a transfer of shares at a specific guaranteed price, sits on the more favourable side of the current account versus capital account line that the Supreme Court drew in that case. Counsel enforcing a foreign award arising from this kind of structure should also expect, and prepare for, the RBI being impleaded or making submissions in the enforcement proceeding, since its position on the transaction's classification has been the decisive factor in both Vijay Karia and GPE, and a well-prepared RBI submission on the award creditor's side can materially shorten the enforcement timeline.

Finally, enforcement under Section 48 and regulatory exposure under FEMA should be tracked as two separate workstreams rather than one. Winning on enforcement, as GPE confirms is now easier for a straightforward damages award, does not retrospectively cure a pricing breach in the original instrument, and an Indian promoter or company that structured an assured return without FEMA-compliant pricing should still consider whether the contravention needs to be regularised, including by approaching the RBI for compounding, independently of how the arbitration or the enforcement proceeding turns out.

This two-track approach matters just as much on the Indian promoter's side of the table. A promoter who signed an assured-return clause years ago, before Vijay Karia and GPE existed as precedent, cannot assume those rulings have retroactively made the clause lawful; they have only made it harder to use the clause's FEMA problem as a shield against paying what an arbitrator has already found is owed. Promoters facing this position are generally better served approaching the RBI proactively to regularise the original contravention, where compounding remains available, than waiting to raise FEMA defensively once a foreign investor has already obtained an award and moved to enforce it in an Indian court.

Where this leaves pending and future disputes

As on 24 November 2026, Vijay Karia and GPE together represent the clearest statement from the Supreme Court that FEMA concerns, by themselves, are not meant to be litigated at the enforcement stage of a foreign award, and that a straightforward damages payment under such an award is ordinarily a current account transaction not requiring prior RBI approval. Nine Rivers Capital, reported as pending with the RBI impleaded, is the matter to watch for whether the Court extends, qualifies or distinguishes that reasoning on a different factual record, and nothing in this piece should be read as predicting how that matter will be decided.

For foreign companies and investors with existing Indian joint ventures carrying assured-return or guaranteed-exit language, the practical message is not that such clauses are now safe to enforce without difficulty, but that the specific objection which used to stall enforcement for years, namely that a FEMA pricing breach bars enforcement outright or that RBI approval must come first, has narrowed considerably for an award that simply orders payment of damages. It has not disappeared for every kind of relief, and it has not changed what FEMA requires going forward for how the next joint venture agreement should be priced.

Vijay Karia v Prysmian Cavi e Sistemi SRL closed off the argument that any FEMA breach in the underlying deal is, by itself, a public policy ground to refuse enforcement of a foreign award under Section 48. GPE (India) Ltd v Twarit Consultancy Services closed a narrower, specific gap left open after Vijay Karia, holding that paying compensatory damages under such an award does not need prior RBI approval because it is a current account transaction.

Neither ruling cures the underlying FEMA pricing breach in an assured-return structure, and neither removes the RBI's separate power to examine that breach through compounding or otherwise. A foreign investor negotiating an Indian joint venture today is better served structuring the exit price to comply with FEMA's pricing guidelines from the outset than relying on Section 48 case law to rescue a guaranteed-return clause if the deal later goes to arbitration.

Whichever side of the table a client sits on, the two rulings and the still-pending Nine Rivers Capital matter are best treated as a reason to get the structuring right before signing, and to run the enforcement and the FEMA compliance questions as two separate, carefully managed workstreams, rather than as a settled answer that either side can rely on without reading the specific facts of its own deal against them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an Indian company refuse to pay a foreign arbitral award because the underlying deal breached FEMA?

Not merely on that ground. The Supreme Court held in Vijay Karia v Prysmian Cavi e Sistemi SRL (2020) 11 SCC 1 that a FEMA violation in the underlying transaction is not, by itself, a ground to refuse enforcement of a foreign award under Section 48 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996.

Does a foreign award creditor need RBI approval before receiving damages awarded against an Indian party?

Following the Supreme Court's order of 26 August 2025 in GPE (India) Ltd v Twarit Consultancy Services Pvt Ltd, paying compensatory damages under a foreign arbitral award is treated as a current account transaction under FEMA, which does not require prior RBI approval, unlike a capital account transaction.

What is an assured-return or guaranteed-return clause, and why does FEMA object to it?

It is a contractual promise to a foreign investor of a fixed exit price or a minimum rate of return on an equity instrument. The RBI's pricing guidelines for equity and equity-linked instruments require exit pricing to follow a permitted valuation methodology at the time of exit, not a number fixed in advance, because a guaranteed return on equity resembles debt rather than genuine equity risk.

Does GPE v Twarit mean assured-return clauses are now enforceable in India?

No. GPE addressed only whether paying damages under an award needs prior RBI approval; it did not hold that the original FEMA pricing breach in the assured-return structure was lawful. The RBI can still examine that breach separately, including through compounding.

Is the question settled for every kind of relief, not just money damages?

No. Both Vijay Karia and GPE involved money awards. An award ordering a different kind of relief, such as a share transfer at a fixed price, could raise a different FEMA classification question that these two rulings do not resolve, and a similar dispute involving Nine Rivers Capital was reported as still pending before the Supreme Court as of 2026.

What should a foreign investor negotiating an Indian joint venture do differently now?

Price any exit mechanism, put option or guaranteed return through a FEMA-compliant valuation methodology from the outset rather than relying on later enforcement case law to save a non-compliant clause, since winning enforcement does not retrospectively cure the underlying pricing breach.

What does Section 48 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 actually allow a court to refuse?

A narrow, exhaustive list of grounds mirroring the New York Convention, including incapacity, an invalid arbitration agreement, lack of proper notice, an award beyond the scope of submission, an improperly constituted tribunal, an award set aside where it was made, non-arbitrability of the subject matter, and a conflict with India's public policy as narrowed by the Explanation to Section 48(2)(b).
