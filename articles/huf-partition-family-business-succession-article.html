Partitioning a Hindu Undivided Family: Property, Tax and the Family Business It Often Sits On Top Of

A dispute that starts as 'who gets what' inside an HUF regularly turns out to be a fight over who actually controls the family business the HUF's assets are tied to

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A Hindu Undivided Family's assets rarely sit still as a neat basket of property waiting to be divided. More often, by the time a partition is actually sought, the HUF's land, shares and cash have become inseparable from a family business the karta has been running for decades, and a sibling or cousin who wants their share of the HUF is, in substance, also trying to prise loose a stake in that business. This piece sets out how HUF partition actually works in law, the real coparcenary rights a daughter and other family members now hold after Vineeta Sharma, the tax consequences under Section 171 of the Income-tax Act that a family rushing to partition often overlooks, and where a straightforward HUF partition turns into a genuine family business succession fight.

Key points

  • A Hindu Undivided Family and its Mitakshara coparcenary are not the same thing; only coparcenary property, ancestral property and property a coparcener has thrown into the common family fund, can be partitioned as of right, while property an individual member genuinely acquired separately remains theirs even if it happens to sit inside the same household.
  • The Supreme Court's 2026 decision in Darubai v. Kamalabai reinforced that the karta's special powers to bind the family, including to deal with property for legal necessity or the estate's benefit, depend on a coparcenary actually existing; property that passed to heirs as individual owners under Section 8 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 is held by them as tenants-in-common, not as a coparcenary a karta can manage.
  • The 2005 amendment to Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act made a daughter a coparcener by birth on the same footing as a son, and the Supreme Court's 2020 ruling in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma applied this retrospectively to every living daughter of a living coparcener as on 9 September 2005, regardless of when her father died, provided the coparcenary had not already been disrupted by an actual partition before that date.
  • Partition can be effected several ways, by a demand that severs status even before actual division, by a registered partition deed, by a decree in a partition suit, or by an arbitration award, and Vineeta Sharma also confirmed that, for working out shares after the 2005 amendment, the date a partition suit is instituted is treated as the date the coparcenary actually ceased to exist, not the date of the eventual decree.
  • A family settlement, which records and confirms existing claims among relatives rather than creating new rights through fresh transfers, does not need registration to be valid if it remains oral or is only evidenced in writing afterward, following the Supreme Court's long-standing position in Kale v. Deputy Director of Consolidation, but a document that itself creates or extinguishes rights in immovable property, a true partition deed, needs registration under the Registration Act regardless of what the parties choose to call it.
  • Section 171 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 governs how a partitioned HUF is taxed, and Section 171(9) has, since 1979, refused to recognise a partial partition, one that divides some but not all of the family's assets or some but not all of the coparceners, for an HUF that was already being assessed as an HUF, which means a family that partitions only part of its holdings to manage a dispute can find the Income Tax Department still treating the whole HUF as intact for tax purposes.
  • Where the HUF's assets are tied up in an operating family business, a partition dispute regularly becomes a fight over control of that business as well as over property, and the remedies available, a partition suit, an oppression and mismanagement petition before the NCLT if the business is incorporated, or arbitration under a family arrangement, each move at a different pace and reach different relief, which makes choosing the right forum at the outset as important as the underlying entitlement itself.

Two different things people call 'the HUF'

A Hindu Undivided Family, as a tax and legal concept, is broader than the coparcenary that sits inside it. The HUF as a whole can include the karta, his wife, unmarried daughters, and every other member of the joint family, but only the coparceners, those who take an interest in the ancestral or joint family property by birth, have the right to demand partition of that property. Not every family member, and not every asset the family happens to hold together, falls within this narrower coparcenary category, and that distinction decides who can actually force a division and of what.

Property that genuinely belongs to the coparcenary, ancestral property inherited through the male line before 2005, property acquired with the aid of joint family funds, and self-acquired property a coparcener has voluntarily thrown into the common family fund with a clear, unequivocal intention to abandon separate ownership, can be partitioned as of right by any coparcener. Property that remains an individual member's separate or self-acquired property, including property that passed to heirs as individual owners rather than as a continuing coparcenary, does not automatically become partitionable HUF property simply because the family happens to live and operate together, a distinction that recent Supreme Court authority has sharpened considerably, as the next section explains.

This distinction matters most at the point a family actually sits down to list out what belongs to the HUF and what does not, since families that have operated informally for decades often have no contemporaneous record of which asset was bought with whose money, or whether a particular contribution was ever meant to be treated as joint rather than personal. A coparcener asserting that a given asset is HUF property carries the burden of showing it was acquired from joint family funds, or ancestral in origin, or genuinely blended in by a clear declaration, and the absence of any such proof tends to leave the asset with whoever holds title to it, which is precisely the practical difficulty that often turns what began as a simple partition negotiation into years of contested litigation requiring witnesses and old financial records to sort out properly.

Darubai v. Kamalabai: inherited property is not automatically a coparcenary

The Supreme Court's 2026 ruling in Darubai v. Kamalabai (2026 INSC 613, decided 1 June 2026) addressed exactly this confusion. Where a Hindu male died intestate and his property passed to his heirs under Section 8 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, the Court held that those heirs take the property as individual owners, as tenants-in-common with each other, not as a Mitakshara coparcenary that continues automatically simply because several heirs now hold shares in the same property. The Court accordingly rejected an argument that a widow could deal with a portion of that property as its karta on the ground of legal necessity, holding that the concept of karta is inextricably linked to the actual existence of a coparcenary or joint family property, which was absent on the facts.

The practical consequence for a family dispute is significant. A family member who assumes that whoever manages the household's affairs automatically holds a karta's special powers, to sell or mortgage property for the family's benefit without every other member's individual consent, is assuming something the law does not grant unless a genuine coparcenary actually exists over that property. Where the property in question is simply inherited property held by several heirs as individual co-owners, any dealing with it needs each co-owner's own consent or a properly obtained court order, the same as any other jointly owned property between tenants-in-common, and a self-styled karta's unilateral dealing with it is vulnerable to challenge on exactly this ground.

A related point the Court's reasoning in Darubai v. Kamalabai brings into focus is that a Hindu widow cannot herself act as karta of an HUF, a position reiterated separately in other recent Supreme Court rulings, since the role of karta has traditionally been understood as tied to a coparcener's own standing within the coparcenary rather than to seniority or management responsibility within the household generally. A widow managing property on behalf of minor children, or looking after a family business after her husband's death, accordingly does so under whatever authority the facts actually support, as a natural guardian of minors, as a co-owner acting with the others' consent, or under a specific authorisation the family itself has given her, rather than under a karta's powers she does not, strictly speaking, hold.

Daughters as coparceners: Section 6 and Vineeta Sharma

The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 rewrote Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act to make a daughter a coparcener by birth in the same manner, and with the same rights and liabilities, as a son, reversing the earlier position under which only male members of a Mitakshara coparcenary took an interest in joint family property by birth. The amendment did not create a new category of family property; it simply extended an existing category of rights, coparcenary rights by birth, to daughters who had previously been excluded from it.

The Supreme Court's 2020 decision in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma settled a question that had divided High Courts and even earlier Supreme Court benches for years: whether the 2005 amendment benefited only daughters born after it came into force, or every living daughter regardless of her date of birth or even her father's date of death. The Court held that the amendment applies retrospectively in the sense that it confers coparcenary status on every daughter who was alive on 9 September 2005, the date the amendment came into force, whether her father was alive or had already died, and regardless of when she herself was born, so long as the coparcenary itself had not already been disrupted by an actual partition carried out before that date.

Vineeta Sharma also settled a related timing question that matters directly for families now working out partition shares. Where disruption of the coparcenary happens through a partition suit rather than a private deed or declared partition, the coparcenary is treated as having ceased to exist from the date the suit was instituted, not the date of the eventual decree, which fixes the shares, including a daughter's now-recognised coparcenary share, as of that earlier date rather than whatever the position happened to be by the time the litigation finally concludes years later.

The one real limit on a daughter's otherwise equal coparcenary standing is where the coparcenary had already been genuinely disrupted, through an actual partition, by private agreement or by decree, before 9 September 2005. A family that completed a full and final partition years before the amendment, and can show that the division was genuine rather than merely a paper exercise revived later to defeat a daughter's claim, is not required to reopen that settled partition to accommodate a coparcenary right that, on the facts, no longer existed by the relevant date. Families relying on this exception should expect it to be scrutinised closely, since courts are alert to a partition that was announced or recorded only superficially, perhaps in response to the prospect of the 2005 amendment itself, without the family's property or affairs actually having been divided in substance.

How a partition is actually brought about

Hindu law recognises more than one route to partition, and the route chosen affects both how quickly it takes effect and what formalities it needs. A partition can be effected simply by a clear and unequivocal expression of intention by a coparcener to separate, which severs joint status even before the property is physically divided, a position Hindu law has long recognised and that courts have applied to hold that a demand or declaration of this kind fixes the parties' shares from that point even while division of the actual assets is worked out afterward.

More commonly, families partition through a registered partition deed executed by agreement among the coparceners, through a decree in a partition suit where agreement cannot be reached, or through an arbitration award where the family has agreed to resolve the dispute outside court. Where the property is genuinely incapable of convenient physical division without loss to its value, a family house or a single business undertaking being the common example, Section 2 of the Partition Act, 1893 lets a court order a sale of the property and division of the proceeds instead, on the request of a shareholder, rather than forcing a division that would damage the property's value for everyone. A partition suit itself proceeds in two stages familiar from ordinary civil procedure, a preliminary decree declaring each party's share, and a final decree working out the actual division or sale, with execution of the final decree following the same Order 21 process that applies to any other decree once it is passed.

Choosing between these routes is rarely a purely legal question; it usually tracks how much the family members still trust each other to negotiate in good faith. A registered deed by agreement is fastest and least adversarial where the coparceners can actually sit down and agree on valuations and division, while a partition suit becomes necessary once one side refuses to engage, undervalues the property, or disputes which assets even belong to the coparcenary in the first place, questions a civil court is equipped to determine through evidence in a way an informal family negotiation is not. A family that moves to a partition suit too early, before genuinely attempting a negotiated division, sometimes finds the litigation itself hardens positions that might otherwise have been resolved amicably, which is a practical reason to attempt a documented negotiation first even where the family expects it may still fail in the end.

Family settlement or partition deed: a distinction that changes the paperwork

Families often use the phrase 'family settlement' loosely to cover whatever document they sign to resolve a dispute, but the law draws a real distinction between a family settlement and a partition deed, and the distinction changes whether registration is required. The Supreme Court's long-standing position in Kale v. Deputy Director of Consolidation treats a genuine family arrangement, one that records, confirms or acknowledges claims the parties already have, rather than creating fresh rights through a transfer, as valid even if made orally, and as not requiring registration under the Registration Act merely because its terms happen to be reduced to writing afterward as a record of what was agreed.

A document that itself creates, declares, assigns or extinguishes rights in immovable property, a true partition deed that actually divides the HUF's land or buildings among the coparceners, is a different matter and needs registration under Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908 regardless of what the family chooses to call it on the document's face. Courts examining a disputed document look at its substance, whether it merely records an arrangement already arrived at or whether it is itself the operative instrument creating the division, rather than accepting the label the parties gave it, which is why careful drafting, with legal advice on which category a particular document actually falls into, matters considerably more here than the choice of title at the top of the page.

Stamp duty on partition: a cost families underestimate

Most states charge stamp duty on a partition deed at rates considerably lower than the ad valorem rate charged on an ordinary sale or gift deed, reflecting the policy that a partition simply recognises each coparcener's pre-existing share rather than transferring property from one owner to another who had no prior interest in it. The specific rate, and whether it is a nominal fixed amount or a modest percentage tied to the property's value, varies from state to state and should be checked against the current stamp legislation of the state where the property is located rather than assumed to be uniform, since West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh and Delhi have each set their own schedules for this instrument.

A document that goes beyond recording a genuine pre-existing share, giving one coparcener property beyond what their actual entitlement would be, in substance effects a gift or a release of the excess from the other coparceners, and that excess portion can attract stamp duty at the higher rate applicable to a gift or release rather than the lower partition rate, a point the sub-registrar's office is generally alert to and that families negotiating an unequal division, for reasons of their own that have nothing to do with entitlement, should factor into the cost of the arrangement rather than discover only when the document is presented for registration.

A family that genuinely intends an unequal division, one adult child taking over the family home in exchange for a smaller share of other assets, for instance, is free to agree to that outcome, but should document it honestly as a partition combined with a release or a gift of the excess, rather than dressing the whole arrangement up as a simple partition to avoid the higher duty on the gifted portion. Mischaracterising the document does not usually succeed in avoiding the duty in any event, since the sub-registrar and, if the matter is later challenged, a court, will look at what the document actually does rather than what it is labelled, and an arrangement caught understating its own character can face a demand for the shortfall in duty along with a penalty, on top of whatever the correct duty would have been in the first place.

Section 171 of the Income-tax Act: how a partitioned HUF is actually taxed

An HUF is a distinct assessable unit for income tax purposes, and it continues to be assessed as an HUF until a partition, total or partial, is actually claimed and recognised. Section 171 governs the assessment once a partition is claimed, and requires the Assessing Officer to record a specific finding on whether the partition was total, dividing the entire joint family property among all the coparceners and ending the HUF altogether, or partial, dividing only some property or involving only some of the coparceners, and to fix the date on which that partition actually took place.

Income earned by the family up to the date of partition is assessed in the HUF's own hands, and income from the property after that date is assessed in the hands of the individual members who received it, or, where a recipient member has in turn formed a new HUF with that property, in the new HUF's hands instead. Every member is jointly and severally liable for the tax assessed on the undivided family's income, and the Assessing Officer retains the power to recover that tax from any former member even after the family has been found to have partitioned, if the partition comes to light only after the original assessment was already completed.

The Assessing Officer's own finding on whether a partition was total or partial, and on its date, is accordingly not a formality the family can simply assert on the strength of its own deed or decree; it is a specific determination the tax authority makes on the evidence before it, and a family that wants its civil-law partition recognised for tax purposes needs to put that evidence, the deed, the decree, the actual physical division of assets, before the Assessing Officer in the relevant assessment, rather than assuming the civil document will simply speak for itself once it has been filed away in a drawer somewhere. A gap between what the family's own lawyers have documented and what the tax record actually shows is one more reason the civil and tax sides of a partition should be handled together rather than treated as two unconnected exercises completed at different times by different advisors who are not speaking to each other about the same transaction.

The provision that catches families off guard most often is Section 171(9), in force since 31 December 1978, which simply refuses to recognise a partial partition for tax purposes where the HUF had previously been assessed as an HUF. A family that divides some property to settle a dispute while deliberately leaving the rest undivided, intending the partition to be only partial, finds that the Income Tax Department continues to treat the whole HUF, including the supposedly undivided portion, as if no partition of that portion had happened at all, which can produce a real mismatch between what the family believes it has agreed among itself and what the tax authorities are prepared to recognise. Planning an HUF partition with this provision in mind, generally by partitioning the entire family property rather than only the portion currently in dispute, avoids a result where the civil law and the tax law end up treating the same family's affairs differently.

Throwing separate property into the common hotchpot

A coparcener can voluntarily convert their own separate or self-acquired property into joint family property, a step traditionally described as throwing the property into the common hotchpot, through a clear and unambiguous declaration of that intention, without needing any formal deed of transfer, since the coparcener already owns an interest in the family and is simply changing the character of property they already hold rather than transferring it to someone else. Once genuinely blended in this way, the property takes on the character of coparcenary property and becomes partitionable among all the coparceners, including, after the 2005 amendment, daughters who are now coparceners in their own right.

This step is not reversible on a whim, and courts scrutinise a claimed blending carefully precisely because it has real consequences, diluting what would otherwise have been one person's exclusive property into something every coparcener now has a claim to. A family member who contributes funds or property to a joint family business without a sufficiently clear declaration of intention to abandon separate ownership may find, years later in a dispute, that the contribution is treated as a loan or an informal arrangement rather than as property that has actually become part of the coparcenary, a result that cuts both ways depending on which side of a later dispute that family member finds themselves on.

When the HUF sits on top of a running business

A family business run for decades out of what started as HUF property rarely stays a simple question of dividing land or a bank balance by the time a dispute actually surfaces. Shares in the family company, goodwill built up under a brand the family has traded under for years, and contracts and relationships the business depends on, all sit alongside the HUF's more conventional property, and a coparcener seeking partition is, in substance, often seeking a stake in or a payout from that business rather than simply a share of land or cash.

The karta's authority to run the business day to day, bind the family to contracts for legal necessity or the family's benefit, and manage the HUF's property generally, does not translate into an unchecked power to exclude other coparceners from the business's profits or governance once a genuine partition is sought, and a coparcener who believes they are being frozen out of decisions about a business the HUF's assets fund has remedies beyond a straightforward partition suit over the underlying property. Where the business itself is incorporated as a company, an aggrieved coparcener who is also a shareholder can pursue an oppression and mismanagement petition before the National Company Law Tribunal, our earlier piece on that remedy sets out the grounds and relief available, which reaches governance and share-value questions a partition suit over the HUF's property alone does not address.

Choosing between a partition suit, arbitration and a family settlement

Families facing a genuine HUF and business succession dispute usually have more than one procedural path available, and the right choice depends on how much of the dispute is really about dividing identifiable property versus restructuring ongoing control of a business. A straightforward partition suit works well where the dispute is mainly about dividing land, cash or passive investments cleanly among the coparceners, and it has the advantage of a settled procedure and the court's own power to order a sale under the Partition Act where physical division is not practical.

Where the family would rather keep the dispute private and move faster than ordinary civil litigation allows, an arbitration agreement among the coparceners, often entered into specifically for this purpose once a dispute surfaces, can resolve both the property partition and business succession questions together in one proceeding, provided the underlying claims are of a kind that can actually be arbitrated rather than reserved exclusively for a court, oppression and mismanagement claims before the NCLT being a notable example that generally cannot simply be folded into a private arbitration. A genuine family settlement, negotiated directly rather than through adversarial proceedings, remains the fastest and least expensive route where the family can still talk to each other constructively, though, as discussed earlier in this piece, the settlement should be prepared with a clear eye on whether its terms are confirming existing shares or creating new ones, since that distinction decides whether registration, and the stamp duty that comes with a true partition instrument, applies.

Maintenance rights of family members who are not coparceners

A partition dispute focused on coparceners can easily overlook that other family members, a widow, an unmarried daughter, a widowed daughter-in-law, or other dependants the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 recognises, hold maintenance rights that survive the partition even though they have no right to demand a share of the coparcenary property itself. Section 21 defines the class of dependants entitled to maintenance, and Section 22 obliges the heirs who inherit a deceased family member's estate to maintain those dependants out of what they have inherited, a duty tied to the property actually received rather than a general personal obligation unconnected to any inheritance.

Section 22(2) specifically addresses a dependant who has not received any share of the property on a partition or succession, allowing a maintenance claim to be pressed precisely because that person was left without an asset base of their own, and courts have applied this provision to recognise that a dependant's maintenance claim can survive, and in some circumstances be strengthened by, the fact that a partition left them without a share. A partition deed or decree that purports to divide the HUF's entire property among the coparceners without any provision for a dependent widow or daughter-in-law who is not herself a coparcener risks leaving that dependant's maintenance claim unresolved rather than extinguished, which is a loose end that tends to surface as a fresh dispute later if it is not addressed as part of the partition itself.

This maintenance obligation is tied to what each heir has actually inherited rather than being an open-ended personal liability, so the extent of a given coparcener's exposure to a dependant's maintenance claim depends on the value of the property that particular coparcener received, not on their income or assets generally. A coparcener who receives a larger share of the family's property on partition can accordingly expect to carry a correspondingly larger share of the maintenance burden for dependants the family as a whole is obliged to support, which is a point worth factoring into negotiations over an unequal division of the kind discussed earlier in this piece, since the dependant's claim follows the property rather than disappearing once it changes hands.

Families negotiating a partition or a family settlement accordingly do well to treat provision for non-coparcener dependants as part of the same negotiation, rather than as an afterthought to be dealt with separately once the coparceners have already divided the property among themselves. A maintenance provision built into the partition arrangement at the outset, whether a lump sum, a monthly amount, or a right of residence, tends to hold up better and generate less later litigation than a dependant's claim pursued independently, often with far less goodwill on either side, after the main partition among the coparceners is already complete.

A worked example: the family hardware business and an unequal partition

Consider a joint family running a hardware and building-materials business for three generations, with the business premises, inventory and goodwill all historically treated as HUF property, and three coparceners, two sons who have worked in the business for twenty years and a daughter who moved abroad after marriage and has had no involvement in running it. If the daughter now demands partition, the sons cannot simply offer her a token amount on the basis that she contributed nothing to building the business in recent years; her coparcenary share, confirmed on exactly the same footing as a son's by the 2005 amendment and Vineeta Sharma, is calculated on her entitlement to the coparcenary property as it stands, not adjusted downward for her lack of day-to-day involvement in running it.

Where the business cannot sensibly be split three ways without damaging its goodwill and operations, a hardware distribution business depends heavily on continuity of supplier relationships and credit lines that an abrupt division would disrupt, the family's practical options are a buyout of the daughter's share at a fairly valued amount, a sale of the whole business with the proceeds divided according to each coparcener's share, consistent with the court's power under the Partition Act to order sale rather than division where physical division would cause loss, or, if the business has been incorporated as a company along the way, a structured exit through a share buyback or transfer that a shareholders' agreement or an NCLT-sanctioned arrangement can formalise. Each of these options requires a fair valuation of the business as a genuine, separate exercise from valuing the family's other, more straightforward property, bank balances and land are valued at their market price on a given date, while an operating business's value depends on its earnings, its goodwill and its prospects, a valuation exercise the family is usually better served commissioning independently than arguing about informally among themselves.

Common pitfalls families run into

The error we see most often is a family assuming that everything sitting inside the household, including property one member clearly acquired with their own separate funds and never blended into the common fund, is automatically HUF property simply because the family lives and operates together, an assumption Darubai v. Kamalabai and the ordinary law of coparcenary property do not support. A second is overlooking a daughter's coparcenary status after Vineeta Sharma, either by excluding her from a partition deed altogether or by assuming her share is somehow smaller than a son's, when the amended Section 6 and the Supreme Court's ruling put her on exactly the same footing.

A third recurring error is executing a document the family calls a family settlement when its actual terms create or extinguish rights rather than merely confirming them, only to find a sub-registrar or a later court treating it as a partition deed that needed registration all along. A fourth is partitioning only part of the family's assets to resolve an immediate dispute, without accounting for Section 171(9)'s refusal to recognise that partial partition for tax purposes, leaving the family with a civil-law arrangement the Income Tax Department does not mirror. A fifth, specific to families with a business at stake, is treating the HUF partition and the business succession question as the same proceeding when they are not; a partition suit over the HUF's property generally cannot deliver the governance relief, a buyout, a restructuring of board control, an injunction against oppressive conduct, that an incorporated family business dispute often actually calls for, which is why identifying early whether the real fight is over property, over the business, or over both, shapes which forum the family should actually be in.

An HUF partition that looks, on its face, like a question of dividing land and bank balances among relatives is, in a surprising number of the disputes we see, actually a contest over control of a family business that has grown up around the HUF's original assets. Getting the underlying law right, what counts as coparcenary property at all, who holds coparcenary rights after the 2005 amendment and Vineeta Sharma, and how Section 171 treats a partition for tax purposes, is the foundation for resolving either question, but it is rarely the whole answer once a business is genuinely at stake.

Families that identify early whether their dispute is really about property, about the business, or about both together, and choose the forum, a partition suit, an NCLT petition, or arbitration, that actually reaches the relief they need, avoid the common and expensive mistake of winning a partition decree over the HUF's property while the underlying business dispute that actually mattered to them in the first place remains entirely unresolved.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is every family member entitled to a share when an HUF is partitioned?

No. Only coparceners, those who take an interest in coparcenary property by birth, sons, daughters after the 2005 amendment, and certain other descendants, can demand partition. Other family members, such as a karta's wife, do not hold a coparcenary share they can demand to be partitioned, though they may have other rights, such as maintenance, depending on the circumstances.

Can a daughter claim a coparcenary share even if her father died before 2005?

Yes, provided she was alive on 9 September 2005 and the coparcenary had not already been disrupted by an actual partition carried out before that date. The Supreme Court's 2020 decision in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma confirmed that the 2005 amendment to Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act applies on this basis regardless of when her father died.

Does a partition deed always need to be registered?

A document that itself divides immovable property among coparceners generally needs registration under the Registration Act, 1908, regardless of what the family calls it. A genuine family settlement that merely records an arrangement the parties had already reached, rather than creating new rights, does not require registration even if later put in writing, following the Supreme Court's position in Kale v. Deputy Director of Consolidation.

What happens for tax purposes if a family partitions only some of its HUF property?

Section 171(9) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 has, since 31 December 1978, refused to recognise a partial partition for tax purposes where the HUF was previously assessed as an HUF. The Income Tax Department continues to treat the undivided portion as still belonging to the HUF, regardless of what the family itself has agreed among its members.

Can a self-made karta sell HUF property without every coparcener's consent?

Only where a genuine coparcenary actually exists over that property and the sale is for a recognised purpose such as legal necessity or the estate's benefit. The Supreme Court's 2026 ruling in Darubai v. Kamalabai held that property inherited by heirs as individual owners under Section 8 of the Hindu Succession Act is not automatically a coparcenary a self-styled karta can deal with on the family's behalf.

From what date are shares fixed once a partition suit is filed?

The Supreme Court in Vineeta Sharma held that, where disruption happens through a partition suit, the coparcenary is treated as having ceased to exist from the date the suit was instituted, not the date of the eventual decree, which fixes each coparcener's share as of that earlier date.

If the HUF's assets fund a family company, does a partition suit resolve the business dispute too?

Not necessarily. A partition suit addresses the HUF's property but does not generally deliver governance relief over an incorporated business, a buyout, board restructuring, or an injunction against oppressive conduct. Where the business is a company, an aggrieved coparcener who is also a shareholder may need a separate oppression and mismanagement petition before the National Company Law Tribunal to reach that relief.
