Winding Up a Foreign-Owned Indian Subsidiary: Voluntary Liquidation, Strike-Off and Getting the Money Out

Closing an Indian branch or liaison office is a regulatory exit; closing the Indian company itself is a company-law process with its own FEMA and tax layer on top, and choosing the wrong route can leave a foreign parent's money stranded in India for years.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign parent deciding to exit India through a company structure, rather than a branch, liaison or project office, is closing a separate Indian legal person that holds its own assets, owes its own creditors, and received its own foreign investment under FEMA, each of which has to be unwound correctly before the parent sees its money again. The two company-law routes available, voluntary liquidation of a solvent company under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code and the lighter strike-off process under the Companies Act, serve genuinely different situations, and the FEMA reporting and tax clearance steps that sit on top of either route are where a foreign-owned subsidiary's closure most often loses months it did not need to lose.

Key points

  • A solvent company can be voluntarily liquidated under Section 59 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, which requires a declaration of solvency from a majority of directors supported by an affidavit, a special resolution of shareholders, and, where the company owes debts, approval from creditors holding two-thirds of the value of those debts within seven days of the resolution
  • Strike-off under Section 248 of the Companies Act, 2013 is a faster, lighter process built for a company that is not carrying on business or has not commenced business, and it is not available to a company that has been wound up under Chapter XX or the IBC in the preceding three months, has outstanding charges, or is under investigation, which rules it out for most operating subsidiaries with any real assets or liabilities still on the books
  • A false declaration of solvency in a voluntary liquidation is not a formality: Section 59(3) requires it to be verified by affidavit and based on a full inquiry into the company's affairs, which makes the solvency declaration a genuine certification the directors who sign it carry real personal exposure on if it later proves wrong
  • Distribution of a liquidated company's surplus assets to its foreign shareholder is a capital account transaction under FEMA, reportable through the standard foreign investment reporting framework, and a chartered accountant's certificate confirming the applicable tax position is generally required by the authorised dealer bank before the remittance is released
  • The foreign shareholder receiving a liquidation distribution is subject to capital gains tax on the difference between what is received and the shareholder's own cost of acquiring the shares, under the deemed-dividend and capital-gains provisions of the Income-tax Act, separate from any tax the company itself paid during its operating life
  • The company's obligation to file its Annual Return on Foreign Liabilities and Assets with the Reserve Bank of India does not end merely because liquidation has commenced; a company with foreign liabilities or assets continues to be expected to file for as long as it remains on the register
  • Winding up a foreign-owned subsidiary is a separate process from closing a foreign company's branch, liaison or project office in India, which this site's existing guide addresses; a foreign parent that operated through both a subsidiary and a separate branch or liaison office needs to close each through its own correct process rather than assuming one closure covers the other

Why the Exit Route Matters More Than It First Appears To

A foreign parent deciding to shut down its Indian operations has, broadly, two different kinds of Indian presence it might be closing, and the distinction matters considerably for how the closure actually proceeds. A branch office, liaison office or project office is a regulatory permission granted to the foreign company itself to operate in India; closing it is substantially a matter of surrendering that permission to the Reserve Bank of India and settling the office's own local obligations, a process this site's existing guide on closing a branch, liaison office or project office addresses separately and in detail.

The Indian subsidiary itself, wholly owned or majority owned, is a different animal entirely: a company incorporated under Indian law, with its own separate legal personality, its own board, its own creditors and employees, and its own foreign investment reported under FEMA at the time shares were issued to the foreign parent. Closing a subsidiary is not a matter of surrendering a permission; it is a matter of actually ending the company's legal existence, through either liquidation or strike-off, and only once that process is complete does the question of getting the foreign parent's residual money out of India even arise.

Choosing between the two available company-law exit routes, voluntary liquidation under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code and strike-off under the Companies Act, is not simply a matter of picking whichever sounds quicker. The two routes are built for genuinely different situations, and a foreign parent that picks the wrong one for its subsidiary's actual circumstances, typically by attempting a strike-off for a company that does not actually qualify for it, can lose considerably more time correcting course than it would have taken to choose correctly in the first place.

Voluntary Liquidation Under Section 59 of the IBC: The Route for a Real, Solvent Business

Section 59 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 allows a solvent corporate person, one not in default on any of its debts, to liquidate itself voluntarily, governed procedurally by the IBBI (Voluntary Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2017. The process begins with a declaration from a majority of the company's directors, supported by an affidavit, confirming that the company has no debts or will be able to pay its debts in full from the proceeds of the liquidation of its assets, and that the company is not being liquidated to defraud any person, accompanied by the company's latest audited financial statements and a report on the company's assets and liabilities valued at their current value.

Within four weeks of this declaration, the company must pass a special resolution of its shareholders approving the voluntary liquidation and appointing an insolvency professional as the liquidator. Where the company owes debts, the resolution also needs approval from creditors representing two-thirds in value of those debts, given within seven days of the resolution being passed, and the company must notify the Registrar of Companies and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India of the resolution within seven days of the resolution itself or, where creditor approval was needed, within seven days of that approval. Liquidation is treated as having commenced from the date the special resolution was passed, subject to the creditor approval where that approval was required.

The declaration of solvency is not a formality that directors can sign without genuine verification. Section 59(3) requires the declaration itself to be verified by affidavit, based on a full inquiry into the company's affairs, and to confirm the liquidation is not intended to defraud any person, which means the Indian directors of a foreign-owned subsidiary, who may be local nominee or professional directors rather than the foreign parent's own senior management, need to be genuinely satisfied of the company's solvency, based on properly prepared accounts and asset valuations, before signing. A foreign parent should not treat this step as a signature its local directors will simply provide on request; it is a certification those directors are personally exposed on if it later turns out to be wrong.

Once appointed, the liquidator takes over the conduct of the liquidation, realising the company's assets, settling its liabilities, and distributing any surplus to the shareholders at the end of the process, which for a wholly foreign-owned subsidiary means the foreign parent itself. The liquidator's report and the final distribution are made to the National Company Law Tribunal for the liquidation to be formally completed and the company's name struck off the register, a step that brings the voluntary liquidation process back into contact with the Tribunal even though the process otherwise runs largely outside it.

How Long the Process Actually Takes

Voluntary liquidation under the current IBBI regulations is designed to run on a defined timetable, generally completing within ninety days of commencement for a company with no creditors needing approval, and within two hundred and seventy days where creditor approval was required and the liquidator has correspondingly more claims to verify and settle before a final distribution can be made. These figures function as regulatory targets for the liquidator's own conduct of the process rather than as a guarantee that every voluntary liquidation will in fact be completed within that window, since a liquidation involving disputed claims, unresolved tax assessments, or assets that prove harder to realise than expected can run longer in practice.

A foreign parent planning the closure of an Indian subsidiary as part of a broader corporate restructuring, with a specific date in mind for when the Indian entity needs to be off the books, should build in a reasonable margin beyond the regulatory target rather than treating ninety or two hundred and seventy days as a firm deadline the liquidator is bound to meet. The steps outside the liquidator's own control, in particular obtaining the income-tax clearances addressed later in this article and completing the FEMA reporting for the final distribution, are frequently the actual pacesetters for how long the overall closure takes, more so than the liquidation process itself.

Strike-Off Under Section 248: Built for a Dormant Shell, Not an Operating Subsidiary

Section 248 of the Companies Act, 2013 allows the Registrar of Companies, on application by the company itself under the companion Section 248(2), to strike a company's name off the register where the company has not been carrying on any business or operation for two immediately preceding financial years and has not applied for dormant status, or has failed to commence business within one year of incorporation. The process, governed by the Companies (Removal of Names of Companies from Register of Companies) Rules, 2016, is considerably lighter than voluntary liquidation, built for a shell or dormant company with no real ongoing business rather than for an entity that has been genuinely operating and now needs to be wound down in an orderly way.

A companion provision, Section 249, bars a company from applying for strike-off where, among other conditions, it has been wound up under Chapter XX of the Companies Act or under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code within the preceding three months, and the Removal of Names Rules separately exclude a company with outstanding charges that have not been satisfied, a company that has accepted public deposits still outstanding, and a company under investigation or inspection by any authority. Listed companies cannot use the Section 248(2) route at all.

For a genuinely operating foreign-owned subsidiary, these restrictions matter considerably more than they might for a purely domestic dormant shell. A subsidiary that has actually traded, carries assets on its balance sheet, has outstanding liabilities to settle, or has foreign investment-related compliance still pending is, in most real cases, simply not a candidate for Section 248 strike-off at all, regardless of how administratively convenient the route might look on paper, because the underlying eligibility conditions are built around a company that has effectively stopped functioning as a business well before the application is even made.

Strike-off also does not, by itself, resolve the question of how the subsidiary's residual assets reach the foreign parent in the way a liquidator's formal distribution does. A company applying for strike-off typically needs to have already settled its liabilities and distributed or otherwise accounted for its assets before the application, which means the practical work of realising assets and repatriating proceeds to the foreign shareholder still has to happen through some other mechanism beforehand; strike-off is the final administrative step confirming the company has nothing left to wind up, not the mechanism that gets the money out in the first place. A foreign parent whose subsidiary genuinely has no remaining business, no material assets and no outstanding liabilities, true dormancy rather than a recently stopped operation, is the clearest case where strike-off is actually the faster and more appropriate route; anything short of that should be assessed carefully against the Section 249 and Removal of Names Rules restrictions before the application is attempted.

The FEMA Layer: Liquidation Proceeds Are a Capital Account Transaction

A foreign shareholder's original investment in the Indian subsidiary was reported to the Reserve Bank of India at the time the shares were issued, typically through a Form FC-GPR filing under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, and the distribution the same shareholder eventually receives on liquidation is treated, symmetrically, as a capital account transaction under Section 6 of FEMA rather than as an ordinary current account remittance. This means the distribution itself needs to be reported, generally through the Form FC-TRS mechanism on the Reserve Bank's FIRMS portal, within the time limit the applicable regulations prescribe for reporting such transactions, commonly cited as sixty days of the transaction.

The authorised dealer bank processing the actual outward remittance of the liquidation proceeds will, in practice, want to see considerably more than the FEMA reporting confirmation before releasing the funds. Banks typically require a chartered accountant's certificate confirming the amount being remitted, the tax that has been withheld or is otherwise due on it, and that the remittance is consistent with the liquidator's own computation of the distributable surplus, together with the standard Form 15CA and 15CB documentation, or their equivalents under the Income-tax Act, 2025's own renumbered provisions for remittances made after that Act's commencement, that apply to any outward remittance of this kind from India.

A separate, and in practice frequently the slower, step is confirming the subsidiary's own tax position is settled before the final distribution and closure. Authorised dealer banks and liquidators alike tend to look for confirmation from the jurisdictional tax authority that no outstanding tax demand or open assessment stands against the company before releasing a final distribution, and a foreign parent whose Indian subsidiary has any unresolved tax scrutiny, even one unrelated to the liquidation itself, should expect that open matter to delay the final remittance until it is resolved, rather than assuming the liquidation and the tax position can simply run on separate, unconnected tracks.

The Tax Position: What the Foreign Parent Actually Pays On

The subsidiary itself, as an Indian tax resident company, has been paying Indian corporate tax on its own profits throughout its operating life, and that tax history does not change on liquidation; the liquidator's final accounts need to reflect any tax the company itself still owes for its last operating period, settled before or as part of the liquidation distribution. What changes on liquidation is a separate, additional layer of tax at the shareholder's own level: the foreign parent receiving the liquidation distribution is generally subject to capital gains tax on the difference between what it actually receives and its own cost of acquiring the shares being extinguished, under the deemed-dividend and capital-gains provisions of the Income-tax Act that apply specifically to a distribution made on a company's liquidation.

This shareholder-level liability sits on top of, and is entirely separate from, whatever corporate tax the subsidiary itself paid on its operating profits over the years, and a foreign parent should not assume that having already paid Indian corporate tax on the underlying profits in prior years in any way reduces or offsets the separate capital gains exposure that arises on its own liquidation distribution. Relief against double taxation, where the parent's home jurisdiction would otherwise also tax the same distribution, depends on India's double taxation avoidance agreement, if any, with the parent's own country of residence, and the specific relief mechanism, exemption or credit, varies by treaty rather than following a single general rule.

The chartered accountant's certificate the authorised dealer bank requires before releasing the remittance is, in substance, confirming exactly this shareholder-level tax position has been correctly worked out and, where tax is due, withheld at source before the net amount is actually paid abroad. A foreign parent's own tax adviser in its home jurisdiction should be brought into this calculation early, alongside the Indian liquidator and tax adviser, since the Indian withholding figure and the parent's own home-country tax treatment of the same distribution need to be reconciled together rather than worked out independently and compared only after the money has already moved.

Reporting Obligations That Do Not Stop Merely Because Liquidation Has Started

A company with foreign liabilities or assets on its books is required to file an Annual Return on Foreign Liabilities and Assets with the Reserve Bank of India each year, and this obligation does not lapse simply because the company has entered voluntary liquidation or is otherwise winding down; a company undergoing winding up, or one that has become dormant pending closure, continues to be expected to file for as long as it remains on the register and has reportable foreign liabilities or assets to disclose. A liquidator taking charge of a foreign-owned subsidiary should confirm whether this annual filing is current and, if not, bring it up to date as part of the liquidation's own compliance housekeeping, since a gap in this filing history is the kind of loose end that can resurface as a query from the Reserve Bank well after the company itself has otherwise been closed.

The penalty exposure for a missed FLA filing is not trivial: contraventions can attract a penalty of up to three times the amount involved, or a flat amount where the contravention cannot be quantified in that way, with a further daily penalty for a continuing default, which makes catching up an overdue filing considerably cheaper to do proactively during the liquidation than to leave for the Reserve Bank to raise independently afterward. A liquidator or the foreign parent's own compliance team should treat the FLA return as a specific item on the pre-closure checklist rather than assuming it will simply be swept up as part of the company's general books being finalised for liquidation.

Employees, Statutory Dues and the Loose Ends a Liquidator Inherits

An Indian subsidiary that has actually employed staff carries a set of obligations that do not disappear simply because the company is being liquidated: provident fund and gratuity dues, accrued leave encashment, notice pay or retrenchment compensation where applicable under the relevant labour law, and the ordinary formalities of relieving letters and full and final settlement for each departing employee. A liquidator stepping into a genuinely operating subsidiary needs to treat these as liabilities to be settled in the ordinary course of the liquidation, generally ranking for payment before any surplus is available for distribution to the foreign shareholder, rather than as an afterthought to be dealt with informally outside the liquidation's own accounts.

Statutory registrations the subsidiary held in its own right, including its GST registration, its provident fund and employee state insurance establishment codes, and any sector-specific licence its business required, each need their own closure or surrender process, typically running in parallel with the liquidation rather than automatically terminating when the company's name is eventually struck off the register. A foreign parent that treats the liquidation as complete once the Tribunal confirms the company's dissolution, without having separately confirmed that these registration-level closures were also completed, risks statutory notices continuing to arrive against registrations nobody is any longer actively managing.

Contracts the subsidiary was party to, leases, supply agreements, licence agreements with the foreign parent itself for use of its brand or technology, also need to be formally terminated or assigned rather than simply left to lapse informally, since a counterparty to an unterminated contract can in principle still assert a claim against the company during the liquidation, which the liquidator then has to address as part of settling the company's liabilities before any distribution. Mapping out every contract, registration and employee obligation the subsidiary actually carries, ideally before the voluntary liquidation resolution is even passed, gives the liquidator a considerably cleaner starting point than discovering these items one at a time as the liquidation proceeds.

Where the Indian Process Intersects With the Foreign Parent's Own Group Restructuring

A foreign parent closing an Indian subsidiary is often doing so as one piece of a larger group restructuring, an exit from the Indian market altogether, a consolidation of several regional subsidiaries into one, or a decision to serve Indian customers through a different structure entirely, such as a distributor relationship or a different group entity. Where that is the case, the Indian liquidation needs to be sequenced correctly against the rest of the restructuring rather than treated as an isolated, purely local matter, particularly where contracts, employees or intellectual property are being transferred from the Indian subsidiary to another group entity as part of the same broader plan.

Transferring the subsidiary's business or assets to another Indian entity or to a different foreign group entity, carried out shortly before or as part of winding the subsidiary down, raises its own separate questions, under the Companies Act for any scheme of arrangement involved, under FEMA for any further foreign investment reporting the transfer itself triggers, and under the Income-tax Act for whether the transfer is itself a taxable event distinct from the liquidation that follows it. A foreign parent planning this kind of combined restructuring and exit should have the asset or business transfer and the liquidation reviewed together by the same advisers, rather than instructing the liquidation only after the transfer has already been completed on a separate, unconnected track.

Group-level tax planning around the exit, in particular where the foreign parent operates through an intermediate holding structure in a third jurisdiction with its own treaty position relative to India, is a further layer this article does not attempt to resolve in the abstract, since the correct answer depends entirely on the specific group structure and the treaties actually in play. What can be said generally is that the capital gains treatment on the Indian liquidation distribution, discussed earlier in this article, is assessed by reference to whichever entity actually holds the Indian subsidiary's shares at the time of the distribution, which makes the timing of any intra-group share transfer relative to the liquidation itself a point worth planning deliberately rather than leaving to coincide however the rest of the restructuring happens to fall.

A Practical Sequence for a Foreign Parent Planning a Subsidiary Exit

A foreign parent approaching the closure of an Indian subsidiary is generally better served working through the decision and the process in a deliberate sequence rather than starting with whichever route sounds administratively lighter. The first step is an honest assessment of the subsidiary's actual state: a company that has stopped operating, has settled its liabilities, and genuinely has nothing material left to wind up is a strike-off candidate, subject to the Section 249 and Removal of Names Rules restrictions; a company that still has assets to realise, creditors to pay, or employees to settle with is a voluntary liquidation candidate, and attempting strike-off for such a company is very likely to be refused or, worse, to generate a false declaration risk for whoever signs the eligibility certificate.

The second step, assuming voluntary liquidation is the correct route, is getting the solvency declaration and the underlying financial position genuinely right before the directors sign anything, given the affidavit-backed certification Section 59(3) requires, and the personal exposure directors can face for a declaration later shown to lack reasonable grounds. The third step is engaging the liquidator, the Indian tax adviser and the foreign parent's own home-country tax adviser together, early, on the shareholder-level tax and FEMA reporting questions, rather than leaving the FC-TRS filing, the chartered accountant's certificate and the tax clearance confirmation to be worked out only once the liquidator is ready to make the final distribution.

A fourth step is confirming the subsidiary's own compliance housekeeping, including its Annual Return on Foreign Liabilities and Assets filing history, is current before the liquidation closes, since a gap discovered afterward is considerably harder to cure once the company itself no longer exists to file the correction. Where the foreign parent also operates a separate branch, liaison or project office in India alongside the subsidiary being wound up, the fifth step is confirming that office's own closure is being handled through its own correct process, under the Reserve Bank's branch and liaison office framework rather than the company-law route this article describes, since the two closures do not substitute for each other even where both are happening around the same time.

Winding up a foreign-owned Indian subsidiary is, at its company-law core, either a voluntary liquidation under the IBC or a strike-off under the Companies Act, and the two routes are not interchangeable: one is built for a company with real assets and liabilities still to settle in an orderly way, the other for a shell that has genuinely stopped functioning. Choosing the route that actually matches the subsidiary's real condition, rather than the one that looks administratively lighter on paper, is the decision that most affects how smoothly the rest of the closure goes.

The company-law process itself is, in practice, rarely the longest part of the exit. The FEMA reporting on the final distribution, the chartered accountant's certification of the shareholder-level tax position, and confirmation that the subsidiary's own tax and FLA compliance history is clean before closure, tend to set the actual pace, which is why a foreign parent is best served bringing its liquidator, Indian tax adviser and home-country tax adviser together early, rather than treating the FEMA and tax steps as a final formality to be handled only once the liquidator is otherwise ready to distribute.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between voluntary liquidation and strike-off for an Indian subsidiary?

Voluntary liquidation under Section 59 of the IBC is for a solvent company that still has assets to realise and, possibly, debts to settle in an orderly process run by a liquidator. Strike-off under Section 248 of the Companies Act is a lighter route for a company that has genuinely stopped carrying on business, with no material assets or liabilities left to wind up.

Can a foreign-owned subsidiary with outstanding liabilities use the strike-off route to close?

Generally no. Strike-off under Section 248 is built for a dormant or non-operating company, and the Companies (Removal of Names of Companies from Register of Companies) Rules, 2016 exclude a company with outstanding charges or other unresolved liabilities. A subsidiary with real liabilities still to settle is better suited to voluntary liquidation.

How long does voluntary liquidation of an Indian company take?

The IBBI regulations target around ninety days for a company with no creditors needing approval, and up to two hundred and seventy days where creditor approval was required. These are regulatory targets rather than guarantees, and the actual timeline is often set by the FEMA reporting and tax clearance steps rather than by the liquidation process itself.

Does a foreign parent have to pay Indian tax on a liquidation distribution from its Indian subsidiary?

The foreign parent is generally subject to capital gains tax on the difference between what it receives on liquidation and its own cost of acquiring the shares, under the Income-tax Act's deemed-dividend and capital-gains provisions, separate from any corporate tax the subsidiary itself already paid on its operating profits.

What FEMA reporting is needed to repatriate liquidation proceeds to a foreign parent?

The distribution is a capital account transaction under FEMA and is generally reported through the Form FC-TRS mechanism on the RBI's FIRMS portal within the prescribed time limit. The authorised dealer bank will also typically require a chartered accountant's certificate on the applicable tax position before releasing the remittance.

What happens if a director signs a false solvency declaration for a voluntary liquidation?

Section 59(3) requires the declaration to be verified by affidavit, based on a full inquiry into the company's affairs and audited financials, and to confirm the liquidation is not intended to defraud any person. It should be based on properly prepared, current accounts and asset valuations rather than signed as a formality, since directors carry real exposure if a declaration later turns out to have been made without genuine verification.

Does the Annual Return on Foreign Liabilities and Assets still need to be filed once a subsidiary starts winding up?

Yes. A company with foreign liabilities or assets is expected to continue filing its FLA return for as long as it remains on the register, including during winding up, and non-filing can attract a penalty of up to three times the amount involved plus a daily penalty for continuing default.
