DDA and L&DO Leasehold Property in Delhi: Lease Terms, Freehold Conversion and What Happens When It Breaks Down

A working guide to perpetual leases, mortgage and sublet permission, misuse charges and the current standstill on converting leasehold flats and plots to freehold

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A large share of Delhi's housing stock and a good part of its commercial premises sit on land held not in freehold but under a perpetual lease from the Delhi Development Authority or the Land and Development Office. Most owners never read the lease deed until they need to mortgage the property, let it out, or sell it, and by then the clauses that control all three have usually already decided the outcome. This piece sets out what the lease actually permits, what conversion to freehold changes, and where things currently stand given the near total standstill in freehold conversion that the Delhi High Court has been hearing through much of 2026.

Key points

  • Delhi has two separate government lessors for leasehold land: the Delhi Development Authority for most DDA flats and plots, and the Land and Development Office under the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs for central government leasehold land including large parts of New Delhi.
  • Both authorities lease on long, usually perpetual or near-perpetual terms, but the lease deed, not general property law, decides whether the owner can mortgage, sublet, change the use, or transfer the property without the lessor's written consent.
  • Mortgaging or subletting without the lessor's prior written permission is a breach of the lease, and a bank's own loan sanction or a no-objection certificate to apply for a loan is not the same thing as the lessor's consent to create a mortgage.
  • Using leasehold premises for a purpose other than the one stated in the lease deed exposes the owner to misuse charges and, in a sustained case of breach, to cancellation of the lease itself.
  • Freehold conversion removes the lessor's control over transfer, mortgage and use, but it does not happen automatically on payment; it is a discretionary administrative process that both DDA and L&DO run, and as of late 2026 that process is substantially stalled for both authorities.
  • The Delhi High Court has been hearing a running set of petitions through 2026 over the DDA's conversion portal being non-functional since the start of the year, with over a thousand pending applications and crores already collected in conversion charges without the properties being converted.
  • Where an authority cancels a lease or refuses conversion without following its own notice requirements, the remedy usually starts with a representation to the authority and, if that fails, a writ petition before the Delhi High Court rather than a civil suit.

Two lessors, one city: telling DDA and L&DO apart

Anyone dealing with a leasehold flat, plot or bungalow in Delhi needs to first work out which government body is actually the lessor, because the two main ones run different schemes, different forms, and in practice different timelines. The Delhi Development Authority is the lessor for the great majority of DDA-built flats and for plots it allotted directly under its own housing and land disposal schemes. The Land and Development Office, a wing of the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs, is the lessor for an older and in several respects more valuable category of land: large parts of New Delhi, including much of the Lutyens' Bungalow Zone, government colonies, and plots that were leased out long before DDA came into existence in its present form.

The distinction traces back to how Delhi's land came to be held by the state in the first place. A body of land known as Nazul land was placed at the disposal of the Delhi Improvement Trust in 1937, and after the Delhi Development Act, 1957 created DDA, ownership and management of different Nazul estates was split across several agencies, among them DDA, L&DO, the Municipal Corporation of Delhi, the Central Public Works Department and the Railways. Properties that trace back to the Trust-era New Delhi layout generally remained with L&DO, while DDA administers the newer colonies and plots it itself developed and allotted from the 1960s onward. For an owner, the practical consequence is that the lease deed will name one authority or the other as lessor, and every permission, conversion application or dispute runs through that specific office. A title search or a lease deed review that does not first settle this point risks sending an application, or worse a legal notice, to the wrong desk.

Both DDA and L&DO lease on long terms, commonly described as perpetual leases for older Nazul-origin grants and as leases running for a fixed period, frequently cited at 99 years, for later allotments. Government audit material on DDA's own land management records two broad categories in use across its land holdings: a shorter duration lease and a longer one described as perpetual. The exact term in any individual case depends entirely on the lease deed executed for that specific plot or flat, and owners should not assume a figure without checking their own document, since sources describing DDA and L&DO leases generally are not consistent on whether a given category runs for 90, 99 years or in perpetuity.

What the lease deed actually controls

A DDA or L&DO lease is not a plain vanilla tenancy. It is closer to a conditional grant of land for a stated purpose, and the conditions are what give the lessor continuing control long after the premium has been paid and possession handed over. Four clauses recur across most such leases and account for almost all the disputes that reach the Delhi High Court: a use restriction confining the land and any building on it to the purpose stated in the lease, usually residential, commercial or institutional; a bar on transfer, mortgage or parting with possession without the lessor's prior written consent; a right for the lessor to charge an unearned increase, generally pitched at half the rise in value, when permission to transfer is sought; and a right of re-entry that lets the lessor resume the land if the lessee breaches any of these conditions.

Courts have repeatedly treated these as the operative terms rather than default property law, because many such leases are grants under the Government Grants Act, 1895, a short central statute that lets government leases and grants override the usual protections a tenant or lessee would otherwise get under the Transfer of Property Act. The practical effect is that an owner of a DDA or L&DO leasehold flat cannot assume the ordinary freedoms of ownership. Selling, mortgaging, changing the use, or even letting the property out can each independently breach the lease if done without the lessor's consent, regardless of how long the family has held and lived in the property.

This is also why a leasehold property's market value, and a bank's comfort in lending against it, is consistently lower than for an equivalent freehold property in the same locality. The uncertainty is not notional. Delhi High Court matters over the years have involved lessees who mortgaged without permission, sublet commercial space without a fresh consent, or converted residential premises to commercial use, and in each category the lessor's response has ranged from a demand for retrospective charges to outright cancellation of the lease.

Mortgaging a leasehold property: consent is not a formality

Raising a loan against a DDA or L&DO leasehold property requires more than a bank's willingness to lend. The lease deed typically requires the lessee to obtain the lessor's prior written consent before creating a mortgage, and Delhi High Court matters show authorities treating a loan no-objection certificate and an actual mortgage permission as two separate things. In one recorded instance, DDA issued a no-objection certificate allowing the lessee to apply for a bank loan but made the mortgage permission itself conditional on the lease deed first being executed and registered, and later took the position that its earlier no-objection had only cleared the loan application, not the mortgage.

Skipping this step carries real consequences. Where a lease requires the Lieutenant Governor's or the lessor's written consent before a mortgage, a mortgage created without that consent can be treated by the lessor as a breach carrying a right of re-entry, and in at least one matter that reached the courts, a mortgage created on this basis without the mandatory consent was found to vitiate the security itself, undermining a bank's later attempt to recover against the property. Banks that accept a DDA or L&DO lease deed as security without confirming that mortgage permission, as opposed to a mere loan no-objection, has actually been granted are taking on a risk that eventually surfaces at the recovery stage, not at disbursement.

For an owner, the safer sequence is to apply for mortgage permission separately from the loan application, to wait for a written order granting that permission rather than relying on a no-objection certificate addressed only to the bank, and to keep that permission with the title documents indefinitely, since its absence can resurface years later if the lender seeks to enforce the security or if the lessor later questions how the mortgage came to be created.

Subletting, institutional allotments and unauthorised letting

Subletting raises a parallel problem. Many DDA and L&DO leases, particularly those for commercial and institutional plots, require the lessor's prior permission before the lessee lets the premises to a third party, and the consequences of letting without that permission are set out in the lease terms rather than in the Rent Control Act or the Transfer of Property Act. DDA's terms for institutional land, for instance, treat unauthorised subletting as attracting a penalty pegged to a multiple of the rent or licence fee actually received, in addition to exposing the lease itself to determination.

The same logic extends to shops and commercial units built on leasehold land and later let out to a different business than the one named in the original allotment or lease. An authority that discovers an unpermitted sublet, whether through an inspection, a complaint from a competitor or neighbour, or a routine survey ahead of a conversion application, typically issues a show cause notice before taking action, but the lease terms rather than any general tenancy protection decide what happens next. A lessee who wants to let out leasehold commercial space, or an institution holding land on a concessional lease that wants to bring in an operating partner, should treat the lessor's prior written consent as a precondition and not as paperwork that can be regularised later.

Misuse of premises: the recurring ground for cancellation

Using leasehold land or a leasehold building for a purpose different from what the lease permits is the single most litigated ground for DDA action against lessees. A residential perpetual lease ordinarily confines the plot and the building on it to residential use, and running a business, a guest house, a clinic, or a commercial establishment on such premises without the lessor's conversion permission is treated as misuse. DDA has in reported matters cancelled leases on this basis, sometimes years after the use began, and has in parallel demanded restoration and misuse charges running into substantial sums as a condition for reviving a cancelled lease.

Not every use that looks commercial is automatically treated as misuse; trial courts have in individual cases accepted that a particular activity did not amount to the kind of commercial use the lease prohibited, and litigation over where the line falls continues to generate inconsistent first-instance outcomes even as the broader principle, that unpermitted change of use breaches the lease, remains settled. What is consistently accepted is that misuse charges, once levied under the authority's own circulars, are recurring rather than one-time unless the lessee either reverts to the permitted use or obtains a formal change of use or conversion permission, and that temporary postponement of re-entry on payment of misuse charges does not cure the underlying breach permanently.

A buyer looking at a leasehold property that is currently used for a purpose other than what the original allotment records show should treat this as a live risk rather than a historical curiosity, because the liability for misuse charges and the exposure to cancellation attaches to the property and its current lessee, and does not stop at whoever first changed the use.

Conversion to freehold: what the scheme is meant to do

Both DDA and L&DO run schemes that let a lessee convert leasehold rights into freehold ownership on payment of a conversion charge, after which the use restrictions, transfer consent requirements and unearned increase that attach to a lease fall away, because the land is no longer held on lease at all. For decades this was the standard route by which long-term DDA allottees and L&DO lessees in central Delhi colonies obtained a saleable, mortgageable, freely transferable title, and large numbers of conversions have gone through over the years; a 2021 government audit, covering L&DO's own administration, recorded that well over half of the roughly sixty thousand leases it then managed had already been converted to freehold.

The conversion fee itself has not stayed fixed. Reports on L&DO's current position describe conversion charges pegged to a percentage of land value, with one account placing the charge in the range of six to ten per cent of land value depending on plot size, though the precise, currently applicable rate schedule should be confirmed directly with the authority before an owner relies on any published figure, since both DDA and L&DO have revised their charge structures more than once and public reporting on the current rate is not fully consistent. DDA has separately allowed conversion charges to be paid in instalments over a period of up to five years with interest, rather than as a single lump sum, which matters for owners converting larger or more valuable plots.

The current standstill: why conversion applications are stuck

As matters stand in late 2026, converting a DDA or L&DO leasehold property to freehold is substantially harder than the scheme on paper suggests, because both authorities' conversion machinery has been effectively frozen for an extended period. The Delhi High Court, in a contempt proceeding titled Mala Sahni Seth and another versus Delhi Development Authority and the DDA's own appeal against the order in that proceeding, has recorded that the online portal through which DDA processes freehold conversion applications has remained non-functional since early January 2026, leaving applicants unable even to file, let alone have their applications decided. The court has separately noted a backlog of over thirteen hundred conversion applications filed between 2020 and 2026 that remain pending, alongside the fact that DDA has already collected conversion charges exceeding a hundred and fifty crore rupees from applicants whose properties have not actually been converted.

The court's frustration has not been confined to DDA. It has also directed the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs, DDA and L&DO jointly to place a final, clear conversion policy on record, after noting that the minutes placed before it showed no settled clarity even on basic questions of documentation and the charges payable. Reports through the second half of 2026 describe the court giving DDA a two-month window to process the pending backlog and, in individual writ petitions, directing DDA to take steps to convert specific flats on payment of the requisite charges, but as of the date of this piece there is no indication that the systemic portal and policy problems have been resolved, and owners filing today should expect delay rather than a routine administrative turnaround.

For an owner or a prospective buyer, the immediate implication is practical rather than legal: do not assume that a conversion application, once filed and fees paid, will be processed within any particular timeframe, and factor the current backlog into any transaction timeline, financing plan or family settlement that depends on the property first becoming freehold. Where an authority accepts conversion charges and then fails to act on the application for an extended period without any lawful basis for the delay, that delay itself becomes a ground for a writ petition seeking a direction to decide the application, as the individual mandamus petitions before the Delhi High Court through 2026 illustrate.

What conversion changes, and what it does not

Freehold conversion ends the lease relationship and with it the lessor's continuing rights over transfer, mortgage and use, but it is not a magic fix for every defect in a leasehold title. Conversion does not cure an underlying dispute over who the rightful allottee or legal heir is; it does not retrospectively regularise an unauthorised structure or a change of use that was never permitted, since the authority will ordinarily require the property to be free of such irregularities, or the irregularity to be separately regularised, before it processes the conversion at all; and it does not by itself complete the chain of title if there are intervening unregistered transfers, powers of attorney or family arrangements that were never reflected in the lessor's records.

Whether unearned increase is payable in a given case turns closely on the facts of how the property came to the current owner. In Ajai Chowdhry v. Delhi Development Authority, decided on 21 April 2026, the Delhi High Court quashed part of DDA's unearned increase demand because it related to an auction sale that followed a statutory acquisition by the Income Tax Department, relying on the Supreme Court's ruling in DDA v. Karamdeep Finance and Investment that no further unearned increase is leviable where a property is auctioned at market value after such an acquisition, while upholding a separate, older unearned increase demand relating to an earlier transaction in the same chain. This is a narrow, fact-specific outcome rather than a general rule that conversion alone is exempt from unearned increase, and an owner should get a specific opinion on their own chain of title before assuming either way.

Once conversion is granted and the conveyance deed executed and registered, the change needs to be reflected in the authority's own records and, where applicable, in municipal property tax records, a step generally described as mutation. DDA maintains its own internal procedure and documentation for this, and owners should treat mutation as a distinct, necessary follow-up step rather than something that happens automatically the moment the conversion order is issued.

Default, cancellation and resumption

Where a lessee defaults on ground rent, breaches the use restriction, mortgages or sublets without consent, or otherwise violates a material term of the lease, the lessor's remedy under most DDA and L&DO leases is re-entry or resumption, effectively taking the land back and extinguishing the lessee's rights. Because these are grants under the Government Grants Act, 1895, the authority is not bound by the full procedural protections that would apply to an ordinary private lease, but the Delhi High Court has consistently required at least a basic measure of fair procedure before cancellation: a show cause notice setting out the specific breach, and in several matters, a reasoned order rather than a bare cancellation, on the footing that an order adversely affecting a person's property rights must disclose the reasons for it.

The courts have not treated every procedural lapse the same way, however. Where the alleged default is genuinely disputed, courts have more readily intervened to require notice and a hearing first. Where the authority is instead exercising a discretionary power, such as whether to convert an expired lease into a fresh perpetual grant or whether to renew a lease at all, courts have been markedly more deferential, treating such decisions as matters of policy for the authority and the Lieutenant Governor rather than entitlements the lessee can compel through a writ. A lessee facing a cancellation or resumption notice should accordingly focus the response on the specific procedural and factual defects in the notice itself, rather than assuming that any show cause notice, once issued, automatically opens the door to a full merits review by the High Court.

Remedies: representation first, writ petition if that fails

Disputes over mortgage permission, misuse charges, cancellation or conversion delay against DDA or L&DO are almost never litigated as civil suits in the way a dispute between two private parties would be, because the lessor is a statutory or governmental authority and its decisions are generally tested under Article 226 of the Constitution rather than through an ordinary suit for declaration or injunction. In practice the sequence that Delhi High Court matters typically show is a representation to the authority first, whether seeking mortgage or sublet permission, challenging a misuse or cancellation notice, or seeking a decision on a pending conversion application, followed by a writ petition if the authority either refuses relief, delays unreasonably, or acts without following its own notice requirements.

A writ petition is the appropriate route for compelling an authority to decide a pending application within a reasonable time, for quashing a cancellation or demand that was issued without a show cause notice or without disclosing reasons, and for challenging a demand, such as an unearned increase claim, that the lease terms do not actually support. It is a less promising route where the complaint is really about how the authority has exercised a genuinely discretionary power, such as whether to grant a renewal or convert an expired lease into a fresh one, since courts tend to leave such calls to the authority unless the decision is shown to be arbitrary, mala fide or contrary to the authority's own stated policy. Owners and businesses dealing with DDA or L&DO should keep every notice, application, receipt and reply on file from the outset, since the strength of a later writ petition against delay or an unexplained demand depends heavily on being able to show, document by document, exactly what was sought, when, and what response, if any, the authority gave.

A practical checklist for businesses and investors

A business or investor looking at a DDA or L&DO leasehold property, whether for occupation, letting out or resale, should start by confirming which authority is the actual lessor and obtaining a copy of the original lease deed rather than relying on the sale deed or broker's description alone, since the lease deed and not the sale document is what fixes the use restriction, the transfer and mortgage consent requirements, and the unearned increase formula. The next step is to check the property's current use against what the lease actually permits, since a profitable-looking commercial tenancy running on a residential lease is a liability waiting to surface, whether at the time of sale, at the time of a bank's due diligence for a loan, or if a competitor or disgruntled neighbour brings it to the authority's attention.

Before financing a purchase or taking the property as security, a lender or buyer should separately confirm, in writing from the lessor, that mortgage permission, as distinct from a loan no-objection certificate, has actually been granted or will be granted before the mortgage is created. Where the plan depends on converting the property to freehold within a defined timeline, whether to support a sale, a family settlement or simply to improve the property's value, that timeline should build in the very real possibility of delay given the backlog and portal issues currently before the Delhi High Court, rather than assuming conversion will proceed on the statutory scheme's own terms.

DDA and L&DO leasehold ownership in Delhi carries real, continuing obligations that do not disappear simply because a family has lived in or run a business from the property for decades. The lease deed, not the sale deed and not general assumptions about ownership, decides whether a mortgage, a sublet, a change of use or a transfer needs the lessor's consent, and breach of these conditions remains a live ground for misuse charges or cancellation regardless of how long ago the breach began.

Freehold conversion remains the intended route out of these constraints, but the scheme currently sits under considerable strain, with DDA's own conversion portal non-functional for an extended period through 2026 and the Delhi High Court actively supervising both DDA and L&DO over the resulting backlog and the absence of a settled policy. Anyone transacting in, financing, or inheriting leasehold property in Delhi should treat the lease terms and the current state of the conversion machinery as live facts to be checked now, not assumptions to be revisited only when a dispute has already arisen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a DDA flat leasehold or freehold?

It depends on the specific allotment and whether conversion has already taken place. Most DDA flats were originally allotted on a long lease rather than in freehold, and a flat remains leasehold, with the use, transfer and mortgage restrictions that go with that status, until the owner has applied for and been granted freehold conversion and the conveyance deed has been registered.

Can I sell a DDA or L&DO leasehold property without the lessor's permission?

Most such leases require the lessor's prior written permission before the leasehold rights are transferred, and permission can come with conditions, including payment of an unearned increase. Transferring without that permission risks the lessor treating the transfer as a breach of the lease, which is a separate problem from whatever rights the buyer and seller agree between themselves.

Does converting a leasehold flat to freehold require paying unearned increase?

It depends on the specific chain of ownership. Delhi High Court rulings on unearned increase, including a 2026 decision involving a DDA auction sale that followed a statutory acquisition, turn on the facts of how the property was acquired and transferred rather than on a blanket rule that conversion alone is exempt. Always get a specific opinion on the property's own history before assuming either way.

Why are DDA freehold conversion applications stuck right now?

The Delhi High Court has recorded that DDA's online conversion portal has been non-functional since early January 2026, leaving well over a thousand applications filed since 2020 pending, even though DDA has already collected substantial conversion charges from many of these applicants. The court has directed DDA, L&DO and the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs to resolve both the portal issue and the absence of a clear, settled conversion policy.

Can DDA cancel my lease for using a residential flat commercially?

Yes. Using leasehold premises for a purpose other than what the lease permits is treated as misuse, and DDA has cancelled leases on this ground in reported cases, in addition to levying misuse charges. Reverting to the permitted use, or obtaining a formal change of use or conversion permission where available, is the way to cure the breach rather than simply continuing the unpermitted use.

What can I do if DDA cancels my lease without issuing a show cause notice?

The Delhi High Court has repeatedly held that natural justice requires a show cause notice and generally a reasoned order before a lease is cancelled, and a cancellation that skips this step can be challenged by way of a writ petition under Article 226. The usual first step is a written representation to the authority, followed by a writ petition if the authority does not respond or does not withdraw the cancellation.

Is mortgaging a DDA leasehold flat without permission just a technical lapse?

No. Courts have treated a mortgage created without the lessor's required written consent as a breach that can affect the validity of the security itself, which matters directly to the lending bank and can complicate recovery proceedings later. Mortgage permission should be obtained and kept on file separately from any loan no-objection certificate issued to the bank.

Does freehold conversion fix an unauthorised construction on the plot?

No. Authorities generally require unauthorised construction or an unpermitted change of use to be regularised or removed before processing a conversion application, rather than treating conversion as a way to clean up such irregularities. An owner with unresolved construction or use issues should expect the conversion application to be held up pending those issues.
