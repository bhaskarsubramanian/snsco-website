Succession Certificate or Probate in Kolkata: Which One a Family Actually Needs

Why Kolkata is one of the few places in India where a Hindu family cannot simply rely on a will without going to court first

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family in Kolkata that has just lost a parent, and now needs to operate a bank account, transfer shares, or sell a flat left behind, usually asks the wrong first question. The right first question is not "do we need a lawyer" but "which of two quite different court proceedings, a succession certificate or a probate, does this estate actually require." The two serve different purposes, go to different courts, and in one respect, a rule that applies specifically within the Calcutta High Court's ordinary original civil jurisdiction, Kolkata works differently from most of the rest of India. This piece sets out both routes, where each is filed, what each costs, and the Section 213 rule that makes probate compulsory for many Kolkata estates even where a Hindu family elsewhere in India could simply act on a will directly.

Key points

  • A succession certificate under Part X of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 is for debts and securities, bank deposits, shares, mutual fund units and similar movable assets, and it does not confer title to immovable property or settle who the rightful heirs actually are; it only identifies who may safely be paid or handed those assets.
  • Probate establishes a will's validity and confirms the executor's authority to administer the whole estate under that will, while letters of administration serve the equivalent function where there is no will, or where the will names no executor able or willing to act; both reach immovable property in a way a succession certificate does not.
  • Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act, read with Section 57, makes probate or letters of administration compulsory, not merely advisable, for a will made by a Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain within the Calcutta High Court's ordinary original civil jurisdiction, or relating to immovable property situated within it, a rule that does not apply in the same way to Hindu wills made in most other parts of India.
  • Non-contentious probate and letters of administration in Kolkata are granted by a District Delegate appointed under Section 265 of the Act, or, for estates with assets within the Calcutta High Court's own original jurisdiction, by the High Court's Original Side under its Testamentary and Intestate Jurisdiction, following the procedure in Chapter XXXV of the Original Side Rules.
  • The moment a caveat is lodged against a probate or letters of administration petition, or a genuine dispute over the will's validity surfaces, the proceeding is no longer non-contentious and converts into a regular suit, with citation, pleadings and trial, rather than the shorter process a family may have expected.
  • Court fee on probate and letters of administration in West Bengal is ad valorem under Schedule I to the West Bengal Court-Fees Act, 1970, rising in tiers with the estate's value and payable, per Calcutta High Court authority, at the rate prevailing on the date the grant is actually made rather than the date the petition was filed.
  • A succession certificate petition goes to the district judge within whose jurisdiction the deceased ordinarily resided at death, or, failing that, where the deceased's property is found, under Section 371, and the certificate, once granted, is valid throughout India under Section 380, not merely within the issuing court's own district.
  • Choosing the wrong route costs real time: a family that files for a succession certificate when the estate includes a flat or other immovable property discovers the certificate simply does not reach that property, while a family that assumes probate is optional for a Hindu will made in Kolkata, as it generally is elsewhere, can find a bank or the land registry refusing to act on the will at all.

Two different problems that look like one

Families dealing with a death in Kolkata commonly describe their problem the same way: we need a court paper to access the accounts and sort out the property. In law, that description actually covers two separate problems with two separate answers. A succession certificate, governed by Part X of the Indian Succession Act, 1925, Sections 370 to 390, exists to let a person collect debts and securities that belonged to the deceased, bank balances, fixed deposits, shares, mutual fund units, and the like, and to protect whoever pays out on the strength of it. Probate and letters of administration, governed mainly by Part IX of the same Act, Sections 217 to 269, exist instead to establish who has the legal authority to administer the deceased's entire estate, movable and immovable, under a will or, where there is none, under the ordinary rules of intestate succession.

The distinction is not academic. A succession certificate does not decide who is entitled to inherit anything; the Act is explicit that it decides only who may safely be paid, leaving the underlying question of entitlement to be fought out separately if it is genuinely disputed. Probate, by contrast, is the court's own certification that a particular will is genuine and validly executed, and it is that certification, not the will by itself, that a bank, a share registrar, or the sub-registrar dealing with a later sale of inherited property, is often entitled to insist on before dealing with the executor. Getting this choice wrong at the outset, applying for a certificate when probate was actually required, or the reverse, is one of the more common and more avoidable sources of delay we see in Kolkata succession matters.

Probate and letters of administration: what they actually establish

Probate is the certified copy of a will, issued under the seal of a court of competent jurisdiction, granted to the executor named in the will once the court is satisfied the will was validly executed and the person propounding it has proved it. Letters of administration serve the same administrative function where probate is unavailable, most often because the deceased left no will at all, in which case the grant is of letters of administration simpliciter to the person entitled under the rules of intestate succession, or because a will exists but names no executor, or names one who has died, renounced, or is otherwise unable to act, in which case the grant is of letters of administration with the will annexed.

Either grant, once made, is the court's own recognition of the grantee's authority to collect the deceased's assets, pay debts, and distribute the estate, and it covers the whole estate the grant describes, immovable property included, which is precisely what a succession certificate does not do. Section 227 makes probate relate back to the executor's authority from the moment of death, validating intermediate acts of administration carried out before the grant was formally made, and Section 273 gives probate and letters of administration conclusive effect, as against any other claimant, as to the right to the executor's or administrator's own role, throughout India, subject to a narrower rule for letters of administration concerning immovable property outside the granting court's own jurisdiction.

The Calcutta rule: why Section 213 bites harder in Kolkata

Across most of India, a Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain testator's will can usually be acted upon by the family, and relied on in court if the need arises, without first obtaining probate, since Section 213's general requirement of probate before an executor or legatee can establish a right in any court is subject to the exceptions Section 57 carves out for these communities. Kolkata is one of the places where that general freedom narrows considerably. Section 213(1), read with Section 57(a), makes probate or letters of administration compulsory, not merely a convenience, for a will made by a Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain within the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of the Calcutta, Madras and Bombay High Courts, and for any such will made outside those limits in so far as it relates to immovable property situated within them.

For a Kolkata family, this means a will executed by a Hindu testator who lived within the area the Calcutta High Court's ordinary original civil jurisdiction actually covers, broadly the town of Kolkata rather than the wider district or the state, cannot simply be handed to a bank or acted upon directly the way an equivalent will could be in, say, Lucknow or Jaipur. The executor has to obtain probate, or the legal heirs letters of administration, before that will can found any right in any court, and in practice before most banks and the land registry will act on it either. A will relating to a Kolkata flat, made by a testator who lived outside the city, can fall into the same compulsory-probate category purely because the immovable property itself sits within the Calcutta jurisdiction, a point families who inherited Kolkata property from a relative who lived elsewhere in India sometimes overlook entirely.

This rule has generated real litigation over exactly where the ordinary original civil jurisdiction's boundary runs, and over whether a will made partly outside and partly concerning property inside that area falls within the compulsory rule, questions that turn on the specific facts and are better tested with current counsel than assumed from a general description. What a Kolkata family can take from this section with confidence is the general shape of the rule, that the usual Hindu exemption from compulsory probate, relied on everywhere else, narrows sharply the moment the testator or the property is tied to Kolkata's own original jurisdiction, and that assuming the exemption applies without checking is a genuine risk, not a theoretical one.

Which court: District Delegate, District Judge, or the High Court's Original Side

Section 264 vests jurisdiction to grant probate and letters of administration in the District Judge, and Section 265 lets the High Court appoint judicial officers within the district, commonly called District Delegates, to exercise that jurisdiction in non-contentious cases on the District Judge's behalf. Most Kolkata probate and letters of administration petitions, where the estate is uncontested and the assets do not bring the matter within the Calcutta High Court's own original jurisdiction, are filed before the relevant District Delegate rather than before the District Judge directly, which keeps the process at the level the rules actually intend for routine, unopposed grants.

Where the estate includes immovable property within the Calcutta High Court's own ordinary original civil jurisdiction, the Original Side of the Calcutta High Court itself exercises concurrent testamentary and intestate jurisdiction, under its Letters Patent powers as applied to this subject, and the procedure for an Original Side probate or letters of administration petition follows Chapter XXXV of the Original Side Rules rather than the ordinary district court process. Families who hold a Kolkata flat as part of a larger estate, or whose relative lived within the city itself, typically end up filing on the Original Side for that reason, and the practical consequence is a different set of forms, a different registry, and often a somewhat different pace from a District Delegate matter.

The moment a probate or letters of administration proceeding, wherever filed, stops being non-contentious, a caveat is entered by someone with an interest in the estate, or a genuine dispute over the will's execution or validity surfaces, jurisdiction to decide it shifts to the District Judge, or, on the Original Side, the matter proceeds as a contested testamentary suit rather than a summary grant. A District Delegate has no authority to adjudicate a contested matter; once a caveat is lodged, the Delegate's role is limited to forwarding the proceeding onward rather than deciding the dispute.

Choosing between a District Delegate's court and the Original Side is not always left to the family's own preference. Where the estate's immovable property sits wholly outside the area the Calcutta High Court's ordinary original jurisdiction actually reaches, a family cannot elect to file on the Original Side simply because it finds that forum more convenient; jurisdiction follows the statute and the Letters Patent, not the petitioner's choice. Conversely, a family that files before a District Delegate when the estate genuinely includes property within the Original Side's own jurisdiction can find the petition returned for want of jurisdiction once the point is noticed, which is one more reason to settle the forum question carefully before filing rather than after.

Citation, caveat, and what turns a grant into a trial

Before granting probate or letters of administration, the court requires citation, notice to the persons entitled to the estate under the will or under intestacy, as the case may be, giving them the opportunity to object. Anyone with an interest in the estate who wishes to contest the grant can enter a caveat, a formal notice to the court that they object, and once a caveat is entered the petitioner can no longer obtain the grant simply on the strength of an uncontested petition; the matter proceeds as a contentious case, with the propounder of the will bearing the burden of proving its due execution, the testator's sound disposing mind, and the absence of circumstances that would cast suspicion on the will.

This conversion from a summary, paperwork-driven process to a full testamentary suit is where Kolkata probate matters most often lengthen from months into years, and it is worth a family anticipating at the outset rather than treating as an unlikely contingency. A will that disinherits a child who would otherwise have expected a share, a will executed shortly before death under circumstances that invite scrutiny, or a will that benefits someone who helped prepare it, are all patterns that commonly draw a caveat, and preparing the supporting evidence, attesting witnesses' availability, medical records bearing on capacity, and the circumstances of execution, before filing rather than after a caveat lands, materially improves the propounder's position once the matter does become contentious. The attesting witnesses in particular deserve attention early: a will requires attestation by two or more witnesses under Section 63 of the Indian Succession Act, and a propounder who cannot locate those witnesses, or who finds one unwilling years later to confirm the circumstances of signing, faces a materially harder task proving due execution once an objection is actually raised, which is why keeping a signed, dated record of the attestation circumstances alongside the will itself is a habit worth building into the drafting process from the start.

Court fee: ad valorem, and payable only on the grant

Probate and letters of administration in West Bengal attract an ad valorem court fee under Schedule I to the West Bengal Court-Fees Act, 1970, calculated as a percentage of the estate's value that rises in tiers as the value increases, rather than a flat fee regardless of size. The petitioner must file a valuation of the estate's movable and immovable property, in the form the Act prescribes, before the court will make an order granting probate or letters of administration, and the exact percentage applicable to a given estate's value should be checked against the Act's current schedule, since the tiered rate structure has itself been amended over the years and the bands are not something to assume from memory.

A point worth knowing before budgeting for a Kolkata probate matter is that the fee is payable, on Calcutta High Court authority, at the rate in force on the date the court actually grants probate, not the date the petition was filed, and only by a propounder who succeeds; someone who propounds a will unsuccessfully is not liable for the ad valorem fee on the estate's value in the same way. For a large estate, particularly one with Kolkata immovable property valued at current market rates, this fee can be a genuinely significant sum, which is one of the practical reasons families sometimes ask whether a succession certificate, which carries its own but generally smaller court fee geared to the movable assets alone, might suffice instead, a question the next section takes up directly.

The valuation itself is not a figure the petitioner can simply assert without support. Section 32 of the West Bengal Court-Fees Act requires the valuation to be filed in the prescribed form before any order granting probate or letters of administration can be made, and the court can call for evidence supporting the figures given, a bank statement showing an account balance as on the date of death, or a basis for the value attributed to a flat, rather than accepting a round number the family has simply estimated. Understating the estate's value to reduce the fee is not a shortcut worth taking; the court can revisit the valuation, and a deliberately understated figure given to avoid fee can itself attract consequences separate from the succession proceeding.

Succession certificate: purpose, petition, and who can be paid

Section 370 confines succession certificates to cases where the Act's probate and letters of administration provisions do not apply, and Section 372 sets out what the petition must contain: the time of the deceased's death, the deceased's ordinary residence at death and, failing a fixed residence, the location of property, the family or near relatives, the deceased's right in respect of which the certificate is sought, the absence of any impediment to the grant, and the debts and securities in respect of which the certificate is applied for. Section 371 fixes jurisdiction with the district judge within whose jurisdiction the deceased ordinarily resided at the time of death, or, if there was no fixed place of residence then, within whose jurisdiction any property of the deceased may be found.

A succession certificate is squarely aimed at movable, intangible assets, bank deposits, fixed deposits, shares, debentures, mutual fund folios, and similar debts and securities, and Section 381 gives the holder authority to receive interest or dividends on, and to negotiate or transfer, the securities specified, while also protecting a person who pays a debt to the certificate holder in good faith from being made to pay again even if the certificate is later found defective. Section 380 makes the certificate effective throughout India, so a certificate obtained from the relevant Kolkata district judge is not confined to assets or institutions within West Bengal and can be used to operate accounts or claim securities held with institutions anywhere in the country.

Section 375 requires the petitioner, before the certificate is issued, to furnish a bond with one or more sureties, or other security the court directs, conditioned on the holder duly collecting and accounting for the assets and debts actually received, a safeguard built into the certificate route precisely because it operates without the fuller scrutiny a probate or letters of administration petition involves. Courts have discretion over the amount and form of this security, and families sometimes find it the one step in an otherwise fairly quick process that takes longer than expected, since arranging acceptable sureties, or the court directing a different form of security such as a deposit, can itself require more than one hearing to settle before the certificate is actually released.

What a certificate cannot do, and the choice families actually face

A succession certificate carries no finding on who is entitled to inherit the deceased's estate, and it does not reach immovable property at all; a Kolkata family holding a flat as part of the estate cannot use a succession certificate to have that flat mutated in a municipal record or to execute a sale deed as the deceased's representative, since the certificate was never meant to confer that kind of title. Where the estate consists purely of bank accounts, demat holdings and similar movable assets, and there is no will, a succession certificate is usually the faster and cheaper route, since it avoids the probate process's citation and valuation requirements and, in an uncontested matter, generally moves through the district court more quickly.

Where the estate includes a flat, land, or other immovable property, or where a will exists and the Calcutta compulsory-probate rule discussed earlier applies to it, a succession certificate alone will not do, and the family needs letters of administration or probate regardless of how straightforward the movable assets might separately be. A succession certificate and a grant of probate or letters of administration are also not mutually exclusive in every case; a family might hold letters of administration covering the whole estate and still find a succession certificate the more convenient document for a specific debt or security a bank insists on seeing in that particular form, though in practice the broader grant usually suffices for most institutions once it is produced.

Letters of administration: with the will annexed, or without one at all

Letters of administration are not a single, uniform grant, and the distinction between its two main forms matters for a Kolkata family working out what to ask for. Letters of administration with the will annexed are granted where a valid will exists but there is no executor able or willing to take up the role, the named executor having died, renounced under Section 224, or being otherwise incapable, and the grant then goes, in order of priority the Act and its schedules fix, to a residuary legatee, a legal representative of the deceased, or another person the court considers fit, who administers the estate according to the terms of that will rather than according to the ordinary rules of intestacy.

Letters of administration simpliciter, without any will annexed, are granted where the deceased left no will at all, and the estate then passes according to the applicable law of intestate succession, the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 for a Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain deceased, or Part V of the Indian Succession Act itself for most other communities. The grant in this form goes to the person or persons who would take the estate, or the largest share of it, under those intestacy rules, and the practical effect for a Kolkata family with no will is that the letters of administration petition and the underlying question of who actually inherits under the Hindu Succession Act or the relevant personal law tend to be worked out together, rather than as two separate exercises, since the court granting letters needs to know who the rightful heirs are before deciding who should administer the estate on their behalf.

A family that assumes 'letters of administration' is a single, interchangeable phrase for whatever paperwork a bank happens to ask for sometimes files the wrong variant, naming the wrong applicant or omitting the will that should have been annexed, and has to amend or refile. Identifying at the outset whether a will exists, whether anyone named in it is willing and able to act as executor, and who the heirs would be if it turned out there were no valid will, settles which of the two forms is actually needed before the petition is drafted rather than after a Delegate or the Original Side registry raises a query.

A worked example: a flat in Kolkata and a bank account in Mumbai

Consider a Hindu parent who lived and died in Kolkata, within the area the Calcutta High Court's ordinary original civil jurisdiction covers, leaving a will that names one of three children as executor, a flat in south Kolkata, a fixed deposit and a demat account with a bank headquartered in Mumbai, and a smaller savings account with a Kolkata branch of a different bank. Because the testator lived within the Calcutta jurisdiction and left a will, Section 213 makes probate compulsory here; a succession certificate alone would not let the executor deal with the flat at all, and strictly speaking would not even be the correct document for the bank accounts once a will naming an executor exists, since probate, once granted, covers those movable assets as well.

The executor accordingly applies for probate, most likely before the Original Side of the Calcutta High Court given the Kolkata flat, files the valuation Section 32 of the West Bengal Court-Fees Act requires covering the flat at its current value and the accounts at their balances on the date of death, and, once probate is granted, uses the certified copy to approach the Mumbai bank, the Kolkata bank, and eventually the municipal authority to mutate the flat. The Mumbai bank's location is irrelevant to which Indian court grants probate, since jurisdiction under Section 264 and 265 follows the deceased's residence and, for the Original Side, the location of property within its own jurisdiction, not the location of each individual asset scattered across the country. Had the same parent left no will, letters of administration would follow the same forum logic, with the heirs determined under the Hindu Succession Act standing in the executor's place as administrators.

Revocation: a grant is not beyond challenge once made

A probate or letters of administration grant already made is not final against every later challenge. Section 263 lists grounds on which the court can revoke or annul a grant for just cause, including that the proceeding to obtain it was defective in substance, that the grant was obtained fraudulently by making a false suggestion or concealing a material fact, that the grant was obtained by concealing a later will revoking the one probated, or that the grant has become useless and inoperative through subsequent circumstances. An application for revocation goes before the same class of court that had jurisdiction to make the grant, which for a District Delegate's grant means a return to that same forum in the first instance rather than directly to the District Judge.

A succession certificate can similarly be revoked under Section 383 on comparable grounds, defective or fraudulent proceedings, a certificate obtained by concealing a material fact, or one that has become useless because of a later development such as the discovery of a will that was unknown when the certificate issued. Families relying on either document should treat a grant as durable but not immune, particularly where new facts, a later will surfacing, or proof that the original petition misstated a material fact, genuinely come to light after the grant was made.

Working out who the heirs are before the petition is drafted

Both a succession certificate petition without a will and a letters of administration petition where there is no will depend on an answer the Indian Succession Act itself does not supply: who actually inherits. For a Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain deceased, that question is answered by the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, which our earlier piece on intestate succession and coparcenary rights sets out in detail, including the 2005 amendment's effect on daughters' rights in joint family property and the order of Class I and Class II heirs where the deceased left no will. A family that has not worked through that question properly before filing, assuming, for instance, that only sons inherit, or overlooking a daughter's equal entitlement, risks a petition that has to be amended once the court or an objecting relative points out that a rightful heir was left out of the array of parties altogether.

This is a distinct exercise from choosing between a succession certificate and probate, but the two interact closely in practice. A succession certificate petition's own particulars under Section 372 require the petitioner to set out the family and near relatives of the deceased, and a petition that gets the heirs wrong on that count is vulnerable to objection on exactly the same ground a letters of administration petition would be. Getting independent advice on the actual line of succession under the applicable personal law, before finalising either petition, is accordingly not a separate, optional step but part of preparing the petition correctly in the first place.

Practical sequencing for NRI heirs and executors

An executor or heir who lives outside Kolkata, or outside India altogether, is not barred from either process, but has to act through the paperwork the Act and the court rules require of an absent party. A power of attorney, properly executed and, for one signed abroad, carrying the apostille or consular attestation the receiving country and India both recognise, lets a Kolkata advocate or a co-executor file and pursue the petition on the NRI's behalf, appear at hearings, and receive citations, without requiring the principal's physical presence at every stage. Where more than one person named as executor is willing to act, the others can proceed to probate while an executor who does not wish to be involved formally renounces under Section 224, a step that avoids the delay of waiting on someone who simply does not intend to participate.

What an NRI executor or heir should not assume is that distance shortens the timeline. Citation to co-heirs, the valuation process for probate, and any caveat that is lodged, all proceed on the same statutory timetable regardless of where the applicant happens to live, and the practical bottleneck for an overseas applicant is usually the time it takes to execute and legalise documents correctly in the first place, not any special leniency or special delay the court extends on account of the applicant's location. Where the deceased's will or the estate itself has a foreign dimension, a bank account or a property held abroad alongside the Kolkata assets, the Kolkata probate or letters of administration grant governs the Indian estate only; a separate process in the country where the foreign asset is held will usually still be required, and the two processes are best coordinated from the outset rather than treated as unconnected matters that can be dealt with one after the other at leisure.

What actually determines how long this takes

In an uncontested matter with properly prepared papers, a succession certificate petition for a straightforward set of bank and demat assets, and a non-contentious probate or letters of administration petition before a District Delegate, both move at a pace driven mainly by citation periods, the time it takes interested parties to respond or stay silent, and the particular court's own listing schedule, rather than by any inherent complexity in the law itself. What slows either proceeding down in Kolkata are the same few recurring causes: an incomplete or inconsistent description of the deceased's assets in the petition, a missing or improperly executed power of attorney for an NRI applicant, a valuation for probate that the court sends back for correction, and, most significantly, a caveat lodged by a family member who was not expecting to be excluded or was not properly brought into the process from the outset.

Families who want either process to move as quickly as the law allows generally benefit from identifying every heir and every asset at the outset, rather than filing an initial petition and adding details piecemeal as they are discovered, and from being candid early with every person who might have a claim on the estate, since a caveat lodged after months of proceedings have already run almost always costs more time than the same objection raised, and if possible resolved, before the petition was even filed.

A last practical point is that neither process runs on a fixed statutory deadline the way a civil suit's limitation period does, and there is no outer time limit within which a succession certificate or a probate petition has to be filed after death, though an unusually long gap between death and the application can itself invite questions from the court or from other heirs about why the family waited. Banks and other institutions, for their own part, sometimes allow a family to operate smaller accounts below a threshold the institution fixes through a simpler indemnity process, without insisting on a succession certificate at all, which is worth checking with each institution separately before assuming a court proceeding is unavoidable for every single asset the deceased held, however small.

A succession certificate and a probate or letters of administration grant answer different questions, and a Kolkata family's first job is identifying which question its own estate actually raises, movable debts and securities alone, or an estate that also includes immovable property or a will that falls within Section 213's compulsory rule. Getting that choice right at the outset, rather than discovering the mismatch after months of proceedings, is the single most effective way to keep either process on the shorter end of what the law allows.

Kolkata's version of this process carries one genuine local wrinkle that families moving here from elsewhere in India, or advising on an estate with ties to the city, should not assume away: the ordinary Hindu exemption from compulsory probate narrows within the Calcutta High Court's own original jurisdiction, and a will that could simply be acted upon in most of the country may need to be proved in court here before anyone can rely on it at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need probate for a will made in Kolkata by a Hindu testator?

It depends on where the testator lived and where the property is situated. Section 213 read with Section 57 of the Indian Succession Act makes probate or letters of administration compulsory for a Hindu will made within the Calcutta High Court's ordinary original civil jurisdiction, or concerning immovable property within that jurisdiction, even though a Hindu's will made in most other parts of India is generally exempt from that requirement.

Can a succession certificate be used to transfer a flat left by a deceased parent?

No. A succession certificate under Part X of the Indian Succession Act covers only debts and securities, such as bank deposits and shares, and does not confer any title to immovable property. Transferring a flat requires probate, letters of administration, or, where there is no will and no dispute, the ordinary succession and mutation process applicable to the property.

Which court handles a succession certificate application in Kolkata?

The district judge within whose jurisdiction the deceased ordinarily resided at the time of death, under Section 371 of the Indian Succession Act, or, if there was no fixed residence, the district judge within whose jurisdiction the deceased's property is found.

What happens if a family member objects to a probate petition?

Lodging a caveat converts the proceeding from a non-contentious grant into a contested testamentary matter. The propounder of the will then has to prove its due execution and the testator's sound disposing mind through formal evidence, and the case proceeds as a trial rather than a summary petition, usually taking considerably longer.

Is a succession certificate issued in Kolkata valid for a bank account outside West Bengal?

Yes. Section 380 of the Indian Succession Act makes a succession certificate valid throughout India, so a certificate obtained from the relevant Kolkata district judge can be used with banks, registrars and other institutions anywhere in the country, including outside West Bengal.

How much does probate cost in West Bengal?

Probate and letters of administration attract an ad valorem court fee under Schedule I to the West Bengal Court-Fees Act, 1970, which rises in tiers with the estate's value and is calculated on a valuation the petitioner must file. The fee is payable at the rate in force on the date the grant is actually made, so the current schedule should be checked at that stage rather than assumed from an earlier figure.

Can an NRI apply for probate or a succession certificate in Kolkata without travelling to India?

Yes, through a properly executed and legalised power of attorney authorising an advocate or a co-executor in Kolkata to file and pursue the petition. The underlying citation periods and, if a caveat is lodged, the contested process, proceed on the same timetable regardless of the applicant's location, so legalising the power of attorney correctly and early is the step that actually saves time.
