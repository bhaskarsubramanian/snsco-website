Lender Step-In Rights in Indian PPP Concessions: What a Substitution Agreement Actually Protects

A foreign lender financing an Indian road, port or power project rarely has a direct contract with the government authority that granted the concession. The substitution agreement is supposed to fix that gap. Two recent disputes show how much still depends on how carefully that agreement, and the escrow arrangement sitting next to it, were drafted.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 16 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A bank or infrastructure fund that lends several hundred crore rupees to an Indian road, port or power concessionaire is taking a credit risk on a company whose single most valuable asset, the concession itself, belongs to a government authority and can be taken away by that authority on terms the lender never signed. The concession agreement runs between the authority and the concessionaire. The loan agreement runs between the lender and the concessionaire. Nothing in either document, by itself, gives the lender a say in what happens to the concession if the concessionaire defaults, is terminated, or simply cannot finish the project. Indian PPP practice closes this gap with a separate contract, usually called a substitution agreement or an escrow and substitution agreement, signed by the authority, the concessionaire and the lenders together. What that document actually delivers, and what it does not, has been tested in arbitration and in court often enough over the last few years to give lenders, and the foreign investors who stand behind many of them, a reasonably clear picture of where the real protection lies and where it still depends on the specific wording a lender negotiated.

Key points

  • A concession agreement between a government authority and a concessionaire creates no privity between the authority and the concessionaire's lenders; a separate tripartite substitution agreement is what gives lenders notice, consultation and step-in rights against the authority.
  • Under a standard National Highways Authority of India substitution agreement, only the senior lenders, acting through a lenders' representative, may invoke substitution after a specified concessionaire default, and the authority's consent to the substitute is a precondition that the authority retains real leverage over.
  • The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways revised the hybrid annuity model concession agreement in 2020 to permit a 'harmonious substitution' exit for a stalled concessionaire, conditioned on lender approval and the absence of outstanding termination or damages notices.
  • Lenders financing toll and annuity road projects typically take a first charge over toll or annuity receivables, the escrow account and the debt service reserve account, and an assignment of the concessionaire's rights under project documents; the underlying right of way remains the authority's land and is not mortgaged to the lender.
  • In the Haryana State Industrial and Infrastructure Development Corporation dispute over the Kundli-Manesar-Palwal expressway, an arbitral tribunal held the authority liable for roughly Rs 1,737 crore in damages for breaching the lenders' substitution rights, an award the Delhi High Court and the Supreme Court each declined to disturb by 1 December 2023.
  • A Delhi High Court division bench in July 2026 rejected the National Highways Authority of India's attempt to avoid depositing roughly Rs 411 crore in termination payments into escrow for two Tamil Nadu road projects, holding that the concession agreements were incorporated into the escrow and substitution arrangements that bound the authority to the lender banks.
  • The Reserve Bank of India's Project Finance Directions, 2025, effective from 1 October 2025, replace the earlier default-based trigger with a 'credit event' standard and require every lender financing a project to be party to a common lender agreement, which strengthens the collective basis on which step-in decisions are taken.
  • A foreign lender negotiating into an Indian PPP financing should treat the substitution agreement's interplay with the escrow agreement, the state support agreement and the authority's consent timelines as the real battleground, not the headline step-in clause, because that is where recent disputes have actually turned.

The Privity Problem a Loan Agreement Cannot Solve on Its Own

Every public-private partnership in India begins with the same two-contract structure. The government authority, whether the National Highways Authority of India, a state road development corporation, a port trust or a state power utility, signs a concession agreement with a special purpose company set up to build and operate the project. That special purpose company then borrows the money to build the project from a syndicate of banks or, increasingly, from infrastructure debt funds and foreign lenders, under a separate facility agreement. The authority is not a party to the facility agreement. The lenders are not a party to the concession agreement. Each contract is enforceable only between the parties who signed it.

For an ordinary secured loan this separation causes no difficulty, because the lender's security sits over assets the borrower actually owns and can pledge. A PPP concessionaire's situation is different in a way that matters a great deal to anyone financing it. The right to build and operate the project, the concession itself, is not an asset the concessionaire owns outright. It is a bundle of rights and obligations running to the authority, terminable by the authority on grounds the concession agreement sets out, and the land on which the project sits typically continues to belong to the authority or the government rather than to the concessionaire. A lender who has advanced several hundred crore rupees against the future toll or annuity stream from that concession has, without something more, no way to stop the authority from terminating the concession, no way to be consulted before termination, and no direct claim against the authority for whatever the concession agreement says the concessionaire is owed on termination.

The fix Indian PPP practice settled on, borrowed loosely from project finance structures used elsewhere, is a separate agreement that brings the authority, the concessionaire and the lenders together as parties to a single document, generally called a substitution agreement where the National Highways Authority of India's model is followed, or sometimes folded into a combined escrow and substitution agreement. This is the document that actually gives lenders standing against the authority. Reading only the concession agreement and the facility agreement, as some foreign lenders financing their first Indian infrastructure deal are tempted to do, misses the one contract that determines whether the lender has any real recourse at all.

What a Substitution Agreement Is Built to Do

A substitution agreement is not, despite how often lawyers describe it in shorthand as a step-in right, a document that lets lenders take over and run a highway or a port themselves. What it typically does is narrower and more procedural. It gives a defined group of senior lenders, acting through a single lenders' representative so that the authority does not have to deal with a dozen banks individually, the right to notify the authority of an intention to substitute the concessionaire once a specified concessionaire default has occurred and has not been cured within an agreed period. The lenders' representative then identifies a substitute entity, which goes through a verification and approval process with the authority before any handover happens. In practice the substitute is usually found through a private sale process, an auction among interested developers, or occasionally a fresh tender, and the incoming entity is required to take on both the concession obligations and the outstanding project debt.

Two features of this structure matter more than they might first appear. First, the authority's consent to the substitute is not a formality. The authority typically retains the right to assess whether the proposed substitute meets the technical and financial capacity criteria the original concessionaire had to meet, and commentary on the National Highways Authority of India's standard documentation notes that the substitute is usually required to hold a majority equity stake itself rather than simply being installed as a nominee, which limits how quickly or informally a lender can effect a change of control. Second, the right belongs to the lenders collectively, exercised through the lenders' representative on behalf of the senior lender group, not to any individual bank acting alone, which is one reason syndicate documentation and intercreditor arrangements matter as much to the substitution mechanism as the substitution agreement's own text.

What the agreement is principally designed to prevent is the scenario where a concessionaire's financial distress forces the authority to terminate the concession outright, extinguishing the project's cash flows, before the lenders have had any chance to find a buyer or an operator who could keep the project running and keep servicing the debt. Termination is, from a lender's point of view, close to the worst outcome available, because whatever termination payment the concession agreement provides for is rarely enough to cover the outstanding debt in full, and the dispute over how much is actually owed can itself take years to resolve.

The Gap Law Firms Keep Flagging: Substitution Alongside the Escrow and State Support Agreements

A substitution agreement does not operate alone. Most road, port and power concessions also carry an escrow agreement, which routes toll or annuity revenue through a dedicated escrow account with a defined payment waterfall, and in several road projects a separate state support agreement under which a state government commits to specific obligations, land acquisition timelines among them, that affect the project's viability. Transactional lawyers who have worked on National Highways Authority of India financings have pointed out, correctly as far as the public record of disputes bears out, that the standard substitution agreement text does not spell out in detail how a change of concessionaire under the substitution mechanism is meant to interact with the escrow agreement or the state support agreement running alongside it.

In practice this gap gets closed, where it gets closed at all, by having the incoming substitute step into the escrow agreement through a separate novation executed at the same time as the substitution, and by treating the state support agreement as running with the concession rather than with the concessionaire as a legal person, so that a new concessionaire inherits the benefit of whatever state commitments remain outstanding. Neither of those solutions is automatic. A lender negotiating the financing documents for a new Indian PPP project, and this applies with particular force to a foreign lender unfamiliar with how loosely some of these interlocking agreements are actually drafted in practice, should ask specifically how the escrow agreement and any state support agreement are meant to survive a substitution, rather than assuming the substitution agreement's general language covers the point.

The practical lesson from how these disputes have actually played out is that the substitution agreement's value to a lender depends heavily on whether the escrow mechanism keeps functioning, and keeps paying the lender, while a substitution process that can take many months is worked through. A substitution right that exists on paper but leaves the lender with no claim on project revenue in the interim is a materially weaker protection than the headline clause suggests.

Harmonious Substitution Under the Hybrid Annuity Model

The hybrid annuity model, under which the authority pays the concessionaire a fixed annuity rather than letting the concessionaire collect tolls, became the dominant structure for new national highway awards through the second half of the last decade, and a cluster of hybrid annuity projects ran into financial stress once construction costs rose faster than the annuity payments originally bid. The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways responded in 2020 by revising the model concession agreement for hybrid annuity projects to introduce what has come to be called a harmonious substitution route, intended to let a stalled concessionaire exit and hand the project to a new developer without going through full termination first.

The revised mechanism conditions this exit on two safeguards that matter directly to lenders. First, the change of ownership or control needs the prior approval of the project lenders, which keeps the lender group's consent central to any handover rather than letting the original sponsor simply sell out from under the financing. Second, the exit is available only where there is no outstanding termination notice or claim for damages against the concessionaire at the time, which is the ministry's way of preventing a sponsor from using the harmonious substitution route to escape accountability for a default that has already crystallised into a termination dispute.

For a foreign developer or lender assessing whether to take on a hybrid annuity asset that already shows signs of financial stress, the harmonious substitution route is worth checking project by project, because the 2020 revision applies to the model agreement text used from that point forward and does not automatically rewrite the terms of concessions signed before it. An older hybrid annuity concession may still be governed by a substitution mechanism that predates this specific relief, which changes the practical options available if the existing concessionaire runs into trouble.

Security Over Project Assets: What Lenders Actually Hold

A second, related misunderstanding that comes up often with first-time foreign lenders into Indian infrastructure concerns what security they are actually taking when they finance a road or port concessionaire. The intuitive assumption, that the lender's security sits over the highway or the terminal itself in something like the way a mortgage sits over a building, is generally wrong for a standard PPP concession. The land underlying a national highway or a port terminal typically continues to belong to the authority or to the government, and the concessionaire has a right to use it for the concession period rather than ownership that it could mortgage outright.

What lenders actually take, and what reported disputes over these financings consistently describe, is a package built around the project's cash flows and contractual rights rather than its physical land: a first charge over toll or annuity receivables as they are credited to the escrow account, a first charge over the debt service reserve account maintained to cover a defined number of months of debt service, an assignment of the concessionaire's rights under the concession agreement and other project documents, and a pledge of the sponsor's shares in the special purpose concessionaire company. Where a concessionaire does create a mortgage or hypothecation over specific project assets it owns outright, such as plant and equipment, that security has in practice needed the authority's prior consent under the concession agreement, and later-ranking lenders creating security over the same assets have had to accept a position subordinate to the original project lenders' charge.

This matters for how a foreign lender should think about recovery if a project goes wrong. The substitution agreement and the escrow mechanism are not an alternative to conventional security; they are closer to being the primary form of security available on a PPP concession, because there is comparatively little hard collateral behind the facility in the way a conventional real estate or plant-and-machinery loan would have. A lender that treats the substitution agreement as a secondary protection, nice to have but not central to the credit decision, has usually misjudged what it is actually relying on to get repaid if the project runs into difficulty.

When the Authority Breaches the Substitution Right: The Kundli-Manesar-Palwal Expressway Dispute

The clearest illustration of what happens when an authority does not honour a substitution agreement comes from the Kundli-Manesar-Palwal expressway in Haryana. The concession for this expressway had been granted by the Haryana State Industrial and Infrastructure Development Corporation to KMP Expressways Limited, with a 2007 tripartite substitution agreement in place among the corporation, the concessionaire and IDBI Bank acting as the senior lenders' agent. When the concession ran into difficulty, the corporation replaced the original concessionaire with a new entity without requiring that new entity to take over the senior lenders' outstanding debt, which the lenders argued was precisely the obligation the substitution agreement was meant to secure.

The dispute went to a three-member arbitral tribunal that included a former Chief Justice of India among its members, and the tribunal's award, dated 24 March 2020, held the corporation liable to the senior lenders for breach of the substitution agreement, with damages reported at approximately Rs 1,737 crore. The corporation challenged the award, first before the Delhi High Court, which upheld it on 29 March 2022, and then on a further appeal under Section 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, which the High Court dismissed on 11 July 2023. The corporation's special leave petition to the Supreme Court was itself dismissed on 1 December 2023, leaving the award, and the roughly Rs 1,737 crore liability it fixed on the state agency, intact.

What makes this dispute useful to any lender assessing the real value of a substitution agreement is that the authority's breach here was not a termination of the concession as such. It was the narrower act of replacing the concessionaire while bypassing the debt-assumption obligation the lenders' substitution right was supposed to secure. Courts treating that bypass as a straightforward breach of contract, carrying full contractual damages rather than some reduced or discretionary remedy, is the strongest evidence available that Indian courts will enforce a properly drafted substitution agreement according to its terms rather than treating it as a lesser, aspirational commitment compared with the concession agreement itself.

Termination Payments Into Escrow: The South Indian Bank Litigation

A more recent dispute, this time directly between lender banks and the National Highways Authority of India rather than a state agency, tested whether the authority could avoid paying termination amounts into escrow by relying on technical arguments about the status of a provisional completion certificate. The litigation concerned two road concessions in Tamil Nadu where arbitral tribunals had separately directed the authority to deposit termination payments, reported at roughly Rs 229.50 crore and Rs 181.81 crore respectively, into the escrow accounts for the benefit of the lender banks, South Indian Bank among them.

A single judge of the Delhi High Court, in an order reported around 1 July 2025, upheld the tribunal's direction that the authority deposit the Rs 229.50 crore award into escrow, rejecting the authority's attempt to resist the deposit. The authority appealed, and a division bench of the Delhi High Court, in a ruling reported in mid-July 2026, dismissed the authority's appeals against both awards, holding, in substance, that the concession agreements had been incorporated into the escrow and substitution arrangements in a way that bound the authority to honour the termination payment obligation to the lender banks once the tribunals had made their findings, and that the authority could not avoid that obligation by later placing the provisional completion certificate in abeyance.

Read together with the Haryana dispute, this litigation reinforces the same point from a different angle. Where an escrow agreement and a substitution agreement expressly incorporate the concession agreement's payment obligations, a court reviewing an arbitral award on narrow grounds under Sections 34 and 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, has shown little appetite to let the authority reopen the tribunal's reading of that incorporation. For a lender, the practical takeaway is that the drafting choice to expressly incorporate the concession agreement's termination payment mechanism into the escrow and substitution documents, rather than leaving the connection to be inferred, is doing real work when a dispute eventually reaches a court.

The 2025 RBI Project Finance Directions and the Shift to a Credit Event Standard

Lenders financing Indian infrastructure through banks and non-banking financial companies now operate under the Reserve Bank of India's Project Finance Directions, 2025, issued on 19 June 2025 and effective from 1 October 2025, which replaced the earlier, more fragmented approach to provisioning and resolution for project finance exposures. One change with a direct bearing on how step-in and substitution decisions get made is the shift away from a strict default-based trigger toward a broader credit event standard, tied into the existing Prudential Framework for Resolution of Stressed Assets, which is meant to capture early signs of project distress rather than waiting for a formal payment default before lenders are required to act.

A second feature of the 2025 directions relevant to substitution practice is the requirement that an exposure counted as project finance must involve all lenders financing the project operating under a common agreement with the borrower, with at least 51 per cent of expected debt repayment sourced from the project's own cash flows for the exposure to be treated as project finance at all. A common lender agreement of this kind reinforces, at the regulatory level, the same collective decision-making structure that substitution agreements already build in contractually, that a step-in or substitution decision is a decision for the lender group acting together through its representative rather than for any single lender to take unilaterally.

A foreign lender joining an existing Indian lender syndicate, or taking an assignment of a loan already covered by one of these structures, should check where the project sits on the new credit event timeline and whether the common lender agreement required under the 2025 directions is already in place, because the interaction between this regulatory framework and the contractual substitution mechanism is still working itself out in practice barely a year after the directions took effect, and the professional commentary available on the point is itself still unsettled on some of the finer detail.

What a Foreign Lender Should Actually Negotiate

Put the case law and the regulatory framework together, and a reasonably specific negotiating checklist emerges for a foreign bank, infrastructure fund or institutional lender financing, or buying into financing for, an Indian PPP project. The headline substitution right matters less than the detail around it, and the detail is where most of the disputes discussed above actually originated.

A lender should confirm, first, that the substitution agreement expressly addresses what happens to the escrow agreement and any state support agreement on a substitution, rather than leaving the point to later negotiation with the authority at the worst possible moment, which is exactly when a concessionaire is already in distress. Second, a lender should look closely at the cure periods and notice timelines the substitution agreement gives the authority before it is entitled to terminate unilaterally, since a short cure period effectively shortens the window in which the lenders' representative can organise a substitute. Third, the authority's consent criteria for an incoming substitute, and any requirement that the substitute hold a majority equity stake rather than being installed as a passive nominee, should be understood up front, because these criteria determine how realistic a quick substitution actually is in a genuine default scenario rather than on paper.

Fourth, and this is the point the South Indian Bank litigation illustrates most directly, a lender should insist that the termination payment mechanism in the concession agreement is expressly incorporated into, or cross-referenced by, the escrow and substitution agreements, rather than assumed to apply by implication. Finally, a lender should treat the sponsor's loan and share pledge documentation, and the intercreditor agreement among syndicate lenders, as integral to how a substitution right actually gets exercised in practice, since the substitution agreement itself only ever operates through the lenders acting collectively, and a syndicate that cannot agree quickly among itself will struggle to exercise a right that depends on unified action within a limited cure period.

Direct Agreements With the EPC and O&M Contractors, and Insurance Proceeds

Lenders rarely stop at a substitution agreement with the authority. Most syndicated financings for Indian PPP projects also require direct agreements with the engineering, procurement and construction contractor building the project and with whichever entity operates and maintains it once construction finishes. These direct agreements give the lenders' representative notice of any contractor default or termination under the construction or operation contract, a separate cure period running alongside whatever cure period the contract itself allows the concessionaire, and in most cases a right for the lenders to step in and either cure the default themselves or novate the contract to a substitute contractor without the EPC or O&M contractor being entitled to treat the lenders' intervention as a repudiation.

Insurance works alongside this structure in a way that is easy to overlook until a loss actually occurs. Project insurance policies, covering construction risk, operational property damage and business interruption, are typically endorsed to name the lenders as loss payees or additional insureds, so that insurance proceeds following a major loss are payable directly into the escrow account rather than to the concessionaire alone, where the proceeds could otherwise be diverted to cover unrelated liabilities before the lenders see any of it. A lender reviewing an Indian PPP financing should check that this endorsement is actually in place and kept current each policy year, since a lapsed or incorrectly worded loss payee clause is a common and avoidable gap that only becomes visible after a loss has already happened.

None of these ancillary protections substitute for the substitution agreement with the authority itself, since only the authority can approve a change of concessionaire under the concession. They do, however, determine whether the underlying project keeps running, and keeps generating the cash flow the substitution right is meant to protect in the end, while any substitution process with the authority works its way through the notice and consent timelines described earlier in this piece.

Why the Dispute Resolution Clause in the Substitution Agreement Itself Matters

Both disputes discussed above, the Haryana expressway matter and the South Indian Bank litigation against the National Highways Authority of India, were fought through arbitration first and only reached the courts on narrow statutory grounds under Sections 34 and 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. That sequence is not a coincidence. Substitution and escrow agreements in Indian PPP practice generally carry their own arbitration clause, frequently mirroring the dispute resolution mechanism in the underlying concession agreement, and a lender should check that this clause actually names the lenders' representative as an entitled party to invoke it, alongside the authority and the concessionaire.

This matters because an arbitration clause drafted loosely enough to leave doubt about whether the lenders can invoke it in their own right, rather than only through the concessionaire, can leave a lender's substitution or termination payment claim stranded exactly when the concessionaire itself has gone into default or insolvency and has no further interest in pursuing a claim against the authority. Both reported disputes examined here involved lenders asserting claims in their own right against the authority, which the tribunals and courts accepted without difficulty, but that acceptance rested on documentation that named the lenders as parties entitled to invoke the clause, not on any general principle that lenders are always free to arbitrate a dispute arising under an agreement they happen to be named in.

A related point worth checking at financing stage is the seat of arbitration and the forum for any court proceedings in aid of the arbitration, since a foreign lender will usually prefer a seat it has direct experience litigating in support of, typically Delhi, Mumbai or an institutional seat such as the Mumbai Centre for International Arbitration, over a seat tied to the location of the project alone where the lender has no existing relationship with local counsel or the relevant High Court's commercial division.

A Practical Sequence When a Concessionaire Defaults

Reduced to the sequence that actually plays out, a concessionaire default under a typical National Highways Authority of India style structure runs roughly as follows. The concessionaire defaults, whether on a financial covenant, a construction milestone, or an operational obligation under the concession agreement, and the authority is required to notify the lenders' representative before issuing a termination notice, rather than terminating the concession outright without warning. The lenders then have a defined cure period in which either the concessionaire remedies the default or the lenders' representative notifies the authority of an intention to invoke substitution.

Where substitution is invoked, the lenders' representative identifies a proposed substitute, typically through a sale process run among qualified developers, and submits that substitute to the authority for approval against the technical and financial capacity criteria the authority applies. Only once the authority approves the substitute does the handover proceed, with the substitute assuming both the concession obligations and the project debt, and with the escrow and any state support arrangements novated or otherwise carried forward to the substitute as the documentation provides. If no acceptable substitute can be found within the time the substitution agreement allows, or if the authority does not approve any proposed substitute, the concession is liable to be terminated, and the dispute then shifts to what termination payment the concession agreement and the escrow mechanism actually require the authority to pay, which is the stage at which disputes like the South Indian Bank litigation and the Haryana dispute discussed above tend to arise.

The time this entire sequence takes, from first default to either a completed substitution or a termination payment actually reaching the lenders, routinely runs well beyond a single financial year once any part of it is contested, and a lender's internal credit assessment of an Indian PPP exposure should build in that timeline as a realistic recovery horizon rather than treating substitution as a quick fix available on short notice.

The substitution agreement is not a decorative add-on to an Indian PPP financing; it is, together with the escrow mechanism it sits beside, close to the entire security package available to a lender whose real collateral is a stream of toll or annuity payments rather than land the lender could ever hope to mortgage outright. Where that documentation has been drafted carefully, expressly tying the concession agreement's termination payment obligations into the escrow and substitution arrangements, and expressly addressing what happens to those arrangements if a substitution actually happens, Indian courts and arbitral tribunals have shown themselves willing to hold the authority to its bargain, as the Haryana and South Indian Bank disputes both show in their different ways.

Where that documentation leaves gaps, particularly around the interplay between substitution and the other project agreements running alongside it, a lender's recourse depends far more on how a dispute happens to be argued years later than on anything a lender actually bargained for at financial close. A foreign lender new to Indian infrastructure financing does better treating the substitution and escrow documents, not the headline step-in clause alone, as the centre of its due diligence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a lender take over and run an Indian highway or port project directly after a default?

Not directly. A substitution agreement gives the lenders' representative the right to propose a substitute concessionaire once a specified default is not cured in time, but the authority still has to approve that substitute against its technical and financial capacity criteria, and the substitute, not the lender itself, takes over the concession and the project debt.

Why do lenders need a separate substitution agreement when they already have a loan agreement with the concessionaire?

The loan agreement binds only the lender and the concessionaire. The concession, which is the asset that actually generates the revenue the loan is repaid from, belongs to the government authority under a separate concession agreement that the lender is not a party to. A tripartite substitution agreement is what gives the lender standing against the authority directly.

Does a lender financing a toll road hold a mortgage over the highway itself?

Generally no. The land under a national highway or similar project usually remains with the government authority. Lenders typically take a first charge over toll or annuity receivables, the escrow account and the debt service reserve account, and an assignment of the concessionaire's rights under the project documents, rather than a mortgage over the underlying land.

What happened in the Kundli-Manesar-Palwal expressway case that lenders should know about?

A Haryana state agency replaced the original concessionaire without requiring the new entity to take over the senior lenders' outstanding debt, which an arbitral tribunal held breached the 2007 substitution agreement. The tribunal awarded the lenders damages of roughly Rs 1,737 crore, and the Delhi High Court and the Supreme Court each declined to disturb that award by December 2023.

Is the harmonious substitution route available on every hybrid annuity road project?

No. It was introduced through a 2020 revision to the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways' model concession agreement for hybrid annuity projects and generally applies going forward from that revision. An older hybrid annuity concession signed before the revision may be governed by a different substitution mechanism, so the specific concession agreement needs to be checked.

What changed for project lenders under the RBI's 2025 Project Finance Directions?

The directions, effective from 1 October 2025, moved from a default-based trigger to a broader credit event standard for identifying project stress, and require all lenders financing a project to operate under a common lender agreement, reinforcing the collective, rather than individual, basis on which step-in and substitution decisions are meant to be taken.

Can the government authority avoid paying a termination payment into escrow by raising technical objections later?

Courts have generally been unwilling to let an authority avoid a termination payment this way where the escrow and substitution agreements expressly incorporate the concession agreement's payment obligations. In the South Indian Bank litigation, the Delhi High Court rejected the National Highways Authority of India's attempt to avoid depositing roughly Rs 411 crore into escrow by relying on the status of a provisional completion certificate.

What should a foreign lender check before financing an Indian PPP concession?

Beyond the headline substitution clause, check how the substitution agreement interacts with the escrow agreement and any state support agreement, the cure periods and consent criteria the authority applies to an incoming substitute, and whether the concession agreement's termination payment mechanism is expressly incorporated into the escrow and substitution documents rather than left to implication.
