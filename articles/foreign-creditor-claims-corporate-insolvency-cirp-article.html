Foreign Creditors in Indian Insolvency: Filing a Claim in a CIRP and Where Cross-Border Recognition Actually Stands

A foreign lender, supplier or award-holder can be a creditor in an Indian corporate insolvency; recognising a foreign insolvency proceeding in India is a separate and still largely unresolved question

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 14 November 2026 · Law stated as on 14 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign bank with a loan to an Indian company, a foreign supplier with unpaid invoices, or a foreign party holding an arbitral award against an Indian counterparty, all eventually ask the same question once that counterparty enters a corporate insolvency resolution process in India: can I actually get paid out of this. The answer splits into two quite different questions. The first, whether a foreign entity can be a creditor at all and file a claim in an Indian CIRP, is reasonably settled. The second, whether India will recognise and coordinate with an insolvency proceeding opened against the same debtor group in another country, is not, and the 2026 amendment to the Code has not fully closed that gap either.

Key points

  • Nothing in the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 limits who can be a creditor by residence. Section 3(10) defines a creditor to include a financial creditor, an operational creditor, a secured creditor, an unsecured creditor and a decree-holder, and a foreign bank, supplier or award-holder fits within whichever category its debt actually falls into.
  • Whether a foreign creditor's debt is treated as financial or operational drives real consequences inside the corporate insolvency resolution process: only financial creditors sit on the committee of creditors with voting rights on a resolution plan, while operational creditors are represented differently and have a narrower set of direct levers over the process.
  • A foreign-currency claim has to be converted into Indian rupees at the official exchange rate as on the insolvency commencement date to be filed and admitted in the process, and a foreign creditor should not assume the claim will be carried, or eventually paid, in the original foreign currency.
  • Whether an arbitral award made outside India, not yet enforced as a decree under Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, is good enough proof of a claim in a corporate insolvency resolution process has produced inconsistent outcomes at the tribunal level, and the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in Kalyani Transco v Bhushan Power and Steel Limited has made the unenforced route considerably harder to rely on going forward.
  • Recognising a foreign insolvency proceeding opened against the same debtor group in another country is a separate question from filing a claim, and India's statutory mechanism for it, Sections 234 and 235 of the Code, has never actually been used: no bilateral arrangement has been notified under Section 234, and no letter of request has gone out under Section 235.
  • The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026 inserted Section 240C, giving the Central Government a rule-making power for cross-border insolvency, but commentators disagree on whether that provision has actually been brought into force, and it does not, in itself, enact a complete cross-border framework or adopt the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency.

Two different questions that get run together

When an Indian company owing money to a foreign lender, a foreign supplier or a foreign award-holder is pushed into a corporate insolvency resolution process, the foreign party's first instinct is usually to ask whether it can get paid at all. That instinct actually hides two separate legal questions, and a good deal of the confusion foreign creditors run into comes from treating them as one.

The first question is whether the foreign party can be a creditor of the Indian company and file a claim in that company's own corporate insolvency resolution process, the CIRP, the same process a domestic lender or domestic supplier would use. This question is reasonably settled, and the short answer is yes, subject to the usual requirements of proving the debt. The second question is different: whether an insolvency proceeding opened against a related entity, perhaps the same corporate group's foreign parent or a foreign subsidiary, in a court outside India, will be recognised and coordinated with by an Indian tribunal. This is the cross-border insolvency question properly speaking, and it remains largely unresolved under Indian law as it stands today.

This article deals with both, in that order, because a foreign creditor who has only read commentary on the second, more publicised question, cross-border insolvency reform and the UNCITRAL Model Law, can come away with the mistaken impression that filing an ordinary claim as a creditor in an Indian company's own CIRP is similarly unsettled. It is not. The practical route for most foreign creditors, a bank owed money by an Indian borrower, a supplier owed money by an Indian buyer, is the first question, and that is where this article starts.

Can a foreign entity be a creditor under the Code at all?

Section 3(10) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 defines a creditor broadly, as any person to whom a debt is owed, and goes on to list financial creditors, operational creditors, secured creditors, unsecured creditors and decree-holders as falling within that definition. Nothing in this definition, or in the definitions of financial debt and operational debt that the Code builds on top of it, confines a creditor to a person resident in India, and a foreign bank, a foreign supplier or a foreign judgment or award holder is, as a starting proposition, as capable of being a creditor under the Code as an Indian one.

This is not simply a theoretical reading of the statute. Foreign entities have in practice participated as creditors and claimants in Indian corporate insolvency proceedings, including a Dutch administrator who was permitted to observe committee of creditors proceedings in the Jet Airways insolvency, discussed later in this article, and foreign parties who have filed claims founded on foreign arbitral awards during a CIRP, also discussed below. None of these required the foreign party to first establish some special standing distinct from the ordinary definition of creditor that any Indian creditor relies on.

What actually determines a foreign creditor's position is not its residence but which category of creditor its underlying debt falls into, and that classification carries real practical consequences for how much influence the creditor has over the resolution process, which the next section addresses.

Financial creditor or operational creditor: the classification that actually matters

A financial creditor is a person to whom a financial debt is owed, financial debt being, broadly, money disbursed against the consideration of the time value of money, which covers a straightforward loan, a bond or debenture, and a range of other lending and quasi-lending arrangements the Code lists. An operational creditor is a person to whom an operational debt is owed, operational debt covering claims arising from the supply of goods or services, including employment dues and certain statutory dues. A foreign bank that has lent money to an Indian company is a financial creditor on that debt; a foreign supplier owed money for goods shipped to an Indian buyer is an operational creditor on that debt.

The distinction is not a formality. Financial creditors sit on the committee of creditors, the body that effectively controls the corporate insolvency resolution process, approves or rejects a resolution plan, and decides on matters such as extending the resolution timeline or changing the resolution professional, each with voting rights proportionate to the financial debt owed to them. Operational creditors do not sit on the committee of creditors with voting rights in the same way; their interests are represented through other mechanisms the Code and the regulations provide, and their practical leverage over the outcome of the process is narrower as a result.

A foreign creditor should therefore work out, as early as possible once an Indian counterparty enters insolvency, which category its debt actually falls into, since this materially affects what the creditor can and cannot do inside the process, rather than assuming that being a creditor at all is the only threshold question that matters. A creditor that happens to hold both a financial debt and an operational debt against the same corporate debtor, which does happen, is treated as a financial creditor to the extent of the financial debt and an operational creditor to the extent of the operational debt, rather than being pushed into a single category for the whole of what it is owed.

A further distinction sits alongside the financial and operational classification: whether the creditor is secured or unsecured. A secured creditor is one in whose favour a security interest, a mortgage, charge or hypothecation over an asset of the corporate debtor, has been created, and a foreign lender that took such security when it extended credit retains that secured status inside the process, which affects how it is treated if the company instead goes into liquidation and the order in which the proceeds of the debtor's assets are distributed. A foreign creditor that lent on an unsecured basis, which is common for trade credit extended by an overseas supplier, ranks differently, and should not assume that simply being a large or long-standing creditor of the Indian debtor carries the same priority that a documented security interest would.

Where the corporate debtor has assets or operations outside India

A related complication for a foreign creditor arises where the Indian corporate debtor itself holds assets, bank accounts or operations outside India, since the moratorium that comes into effect once a corporate insolvency resolution process begins, and which generally bars creditors from pursuing recovery or enforcement actions against the corporate debtor during the process, is a feature of Indian law and does not automatically bind a court or enforcement authority in another country where those assets happen to be located.

A foreign creditor that is aware of such overseas assets, or that is itself pursuing a separate recovery action abroad against the same Indian corporate debtor, should take Indian insolvency counsel's advice on how that overseas action interacts with the Indian moratorium and with its own claim in the Indian process, since continuing to pursue overseas assets in parallel with an Indian claim can raise questions about double recovery, and can also affect how the Indian resolution professional or committee of creditors views the creditor's cooperation with the Indian process as a whole, even though nothing in Indian law by itself extends the Indian moratorium's reach to compel a foreign court to stay its own proceedings.

Filing the claim: the mechanics a foreign creditor has to work through

Once a corporate insolvency resolution process begins, the resolution professional makes a public announcement inviting claims from creditors, and creditors then have to submit their claim, with proof, in the manner the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India's regulations for the process specify. A claim is ordinarily to be filed within a defined window measured from the insolvency commencement date, and the regulations also address claims filed later, though late filing is best avoided given the practical difficulty of being folded into a process that has already progressed without the creditor's claim on record.

The regulations prescribe different forms depending on the creditor's category: broadly, one form for financial creditors other than through an authorised representative, a different form for operational creditors, a separate form for an authorised representative of workmen or employees, and a residual form for creditors who do not fall within the financial or operational creditor categories, which is where a pure decree-holder's claim, discussed further below, can end up being filed. A foreign creditor should establish which form actually applies to its debt before filing, since filing in the wrong form, while not fatal on its own, as discussed next, is an avoidable source of delay and scrutiny.

On the consequence of filing in the wrong form, the Supreme Court addressed this directly in Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority v Prabhjit Singh Soni & Anr, 2024 INSC 102, decided in February 2024, holding that the form in which a claim is submitted is directory rather than mandatory, and that a claim supported by proof cannot be overlooked merely because it was filed in a different form than the one that strictly applied to it. The resolution professional had asked the claimant to resubmit in the form meant for operational creditors instead of the one it had actually used, and when it did not do so, its claim was excluded from the resolution plan; the Supreme Court held that this was wrong and that what matters is that the claim carries supporting proof, not which specific form carries it. A foreign creditor whose claim is challenged purely on a technical, form-based ground has a reasonably strong basis to resist that challenge on the strength of this ruling, though it is not a substitute for filing in the correct form in the first place where that can reasonably be worked out.

A practical difficulty specific to a foreign creditor is assembling proof of the debt in a form an Indian resolution professional, and later the adjudicating authority if the claim is contested, will readily accept, which can mean certified or notarised copies of loan or supply agreements, invoices, and account statements, translated into English where the originals are not, and in some cases authenticated through the Indian embassy or consulate in the creditor's home jurisdiction or through an apostille where the home jurisdiction is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention. Starting this documentation process the moment the public announcement inviting claims is published, rather than waiting until closer to the filing deadline, matters more for a foreign creditor than a domestic one, given how much longer it typically takes to assemble and authenticate documents across jurisdictions.

What happens to a claim denominated in a foreign currency

A debt a foreign creditor is owed is very often denominated in a foreign currency, a loan in US dollars, an invoice in euros, an award quantified in pounds sterling, and that debt has to be converted into Indian rupees to be filed and admitted as a claim in the insolvency process, since the process itself, the resolution plan, and any eventual distribution to creditors, operate in rupees. The regulations governing the process specifically address debt denominated in foreign currency, and the closely related liquidation regulations, which operate on the same underlying principle, value a foreign-currency claim in Indian currency at the official exchange rate prevailing on the relevant commencement date, generally the rate published by, or derived from a rate published by, the Reserve Bank of India.

A foreign creditor should treat this conversion date, the insolvency commencement date rather than the date the claim happens to be filed or the date the debt originally fell due, as fixed and not something to negotiate around. Where a currency has moved significantly between the date the debt was originally incurred and the insolvency commencement date, the rupee value of the claim as filed can differ noticeably from what the creditor would calculate using the exchange rate on the date it originally extended credit or shipped goods, and this is simply how the valuation mechanism works rather than something a resolution professional has discretion to adjust creditor by creditor.

This conversion mechanism is also why a foreign creditor should not structure its expectations around eventual repayment in the original foreign currency. Whatever a resolution plan finally provides for creditors in the relevant class is typically expressed, and paid, in rupees, and a foreign creditor wanting to convert any eventual payout back into its own currency bears that further conversion and any associated cost and exchange risk itself, separately from the conversion already built into how the claim was valued and admitted.

Using a foreign arbitral award as proof of a claim

A foreign creditor that holds an arbitral award made outside India against the Indian corporate debtor, rather than a straightforward loan or invoice, faces an additional question: whether that award, on its own, is good enough proof to found a claim in the insolvency process, particularly where the award has not yet gone through the separate process of being recognised and enforced as a decree in India under Part II, Chapter I of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. Indian tribunals have not spoken with one voice on this question.

One line of authority, associated with a ruling of the National Company Law Tribunal at Mumbai, treated a foreign-seated arbitral award as adequate proof of a claim for the purpose of initiating or participating in an insolvency process even though the award had not been separately enforced under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act. The Kolkata bench of the Tribunal, in a December 2023 ruling on a Singapore-seated award a creditor held against Shristi Infrastructure Development Corporation Ltd and the corporate debtor, took a related but more qualified approach: it held that the debt arising from the unenforced foreign award did not meet the definition of financial debt under Section 5(8) of the Code, but admitted the claim as an other debt rather than rejecting it outright.

This is not the only pattern that has emerged, however. Other adjudicating authorities have declined to found an insolvency application on a foreign award that remained under challenge at its seat, and in at least one reported matter a decree-holder's claim, where the underlying award arose from an alleged breach of a shareholders' agreement, was not accepted as a financial debt for the purpose of the process. A foreign creditor relying on an unenforced award as its sole proof of claim should treat the outcome as genuinely uncertain rather than settled in its favour, and should not assume that one tribunal's approach in one matter will necessarily be followed in another.

Kalyani Transco and the harder question it has put over unenforced foreign awards

The Supreme Court's ruling in Kalyani Transco v Bhushan Power and Steel Limited, 2025 INSC 1165, delivered on 26 September 2025 in the long-running dispute over the resolution plan for Bhushan Power and Steel Limited, bears directly on this question, even though the case itself arose in the specific context of that resolution plan's approval rather than as a freestanding ruling on insolvency practice generally. The Court held that a foreign arbitral award is not automatically enforceable as a decree in India, and becomes a decree only once a court is satisfied, through the process the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 sets out, that it is in fact enforceable; until that happens, a claim founded purely on the award remains, in the Court's reasoning, contingent rather than crystallised.

Commentators reading the decision generally describe it as reinforcing the primacy of the insolvency process once it has begun, over a creditor's attempt to rely on an award that has not completed the separate enforcement route the Arbitration and Conciliation Act requires, though at least one commentator is careful to note that the decision does not purport to lay down an exhaustive rule governing every foreign-award claim in every insolvency matter. A foreign creditor holding an unenforced award against an Indian corporate debtor should, after this ruling, treat enforcement of the award under Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act as a step to pursue in parallel with, rather than as something it can defer until after, any insolvency claim it files founded on that award, since the ruling has made it considerably less safe to assume the award alone will be accepted as adequate proof if the point is seriously contested.

A foreign creditor in this position is, in effect, running two processes that used to be treated by some tribunals as more interchangeable than the Supreme Court is now willing to allow: the enforcement proceeding under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, which converts the award into something unambiguously equivalent to a decree, and the insolvency claim itself. Starting the enforcement proceeding promptly, rather than waiting to see whether the insolvency claim succeeds on the strength of the award alone, is the more defensible course going forward.

Decree-holders: included in the definition, uncertain in its practical effect

Section 3(10) of the Code expressly lists a decree-holder among the categories falling within the definition of creditor, which matters to a foreign creditor who has already obtained a money decree against the Indian debtor from a court, whether an Indian court or a foreign court whose judgment has separately been recognised in India, rather than holding an arbitral award or an ordinary contractual debt. Being within the definition of creditor, however, does not by itself answer whether a decree-holder's claim is a financial debt, an operational debt, or something that falls outside both categories.

Commentary on this point treats it as a live interpretive question rather than a settled one: the existence of a decree does not automatically transform the underlying claim into a financial debt or an operational debt, and the character of the underlying claim the decree merely confirms, whether it originated in a loan, a supply contract, or something else entirely, continues to matter for classification purposes even after judgment has been obtained on it. A foreign creditor holding a decree should be prepared to explain and support the character of the underlying claim the decree is based on, rather than assuming the decree itself answers the classification question.

Where a decree-holder's claim does not fit the financial or operational creditor categories at all, the regulations provide a residual form of proof of claim for creditors outside those two categories, which is the route such a claim is likely to be filed through, with the practical consequence that a creditor filing this way sits further from the levers the committee of creditors controls than either a financial or an operational creditor would.

The separate question: recognising a foreign insolvency proceeding in India

Everything discussed so far concerns a foreign party's position as a creditor inside an Indian company's own insolvency process. A different and, for a multinational group, often more consequential question arises where an insolvency or restructuring proceeding has already been opened against a related entity, a foreign parent, a foreign subsidiary, or the same corporate debtor's assets located abroad, in a court outside India, and the question is whether an Indian tribunal will recognise that foreign proceeding, cooperate with the foreign court or administrator, or stay Indian proceedings in deference to it.

The Code's drafters anticipated this question and included two provisions intended to address it. Section 234 empowers the Central Government to enter into bilateral agreements with other countries for enforcing the Code's provisions, and Section 235 allows an adjudicating authority, on an application by a resolution professional or liquidator, to issue a letter of request to a court in a country with which such an agreement exists, asking that court's assistance regarding the corporate debtor's assets located there. As on today, neither provision has actually been operationalised: no country has entered into a bilateral agreement with India under Section 234, and consequently no letter of request has ever been issued under Section 235, since a letter of request can only go to a country with which such an agreement already exists.

This is not a drafting oversight that has simply gone unnoticed; it reflects the practical difficulty of negotiating bilateral agreements country by country, which is slow and has, in the years since the Code was enacted, not produced a single concluded agreement, leaving Sections 234 and 235 as provisions that exist on the statute book without ever having been used in a single matter.

Jet Airways: the gap exposed, and an improvised fix

The practical consequences of Sections 234 and 235 never having been operationalised were worked out most visibly in the insolvency of Jet Airways (India) Limited, where the airline was also the subject of bankruptcy proceedings in the Netherlands and a Dutch administrator sought recognition of, and a role in, the Indian proceedings. The National Company Law Tribunal at Mumbai declined to recognise the Dutch proceedings or grant the administrator the standing sought, on the basis that Sections 234 and 235 had not been notified and there was accordingly no statutory mechanism through which recognition could be granted.

On appeal, the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal took a different, more pragmatic course. Rather than simply affirming or reversing the question of statutory recognition, it facilitated a bespoke Cross-Border Insolvency Protocol agreed between the Dutch administrator and the Indian resolution professional, under which the Dutch administrator was permitted to participate in meetings of the committee of creditors as an observer, without voting rights, and committed in turn not to deal with the airline's assets without consulting the Indian resolution professional. This protocol was a creditor-and-administrator-level arrangement crafted for that specific matter, not a precedent establishing a general statutory right to cross-border recognition, and it worked only because both sides were willing to cooperate; nothing in it compels a different foreign administrator in a different Indian insolvency to be given the same accommodation.

The Jet Airways episode is widely cited in commentary as the clearest illustration of the gap Sections 234 and 235 leave open, precisely because it shows what happens when a genuine cross-border insolvency arises with no working statutory mechanism to address it: the tribunals improvise a workable arrangement for that case, rather than applying a rule Parliament has already settled.

The IBC Amendment Act, 2026: an enabling power, not yet a framework

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026, Act No. 6 of 2026, received Presidential assent on 6 April 2026 and made a wide range of changes to the Code. Among them, it inserted Section 240C, which confers on the Central Government a power to make rules governing cross-border insolvency. This is, on its face, the first legislative movement on the cross-border insolvency question since Sections 234 and 235 were enacted and left unused.

What Section 240C does not do is itself establish a complete cross-border insolvency framework or adopt the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency, which various expert committees have recommended India adopt over the years, in whole or in substantial part. It is an enabling, rule-making provision: it tells the Central Government that it may make rules on the subject, without itself setting out recognition criteria, the treatment of a foreign representative, or any of the substantive content such rules would need to contain. Commentators have been explicit that the section, standing alone, leaves the actual framework to be written later through delegated rule-making, a point at least one critic has described pointedly as not providing a framework of its own.

Whether Section 240C has itself come into force is, as on today, a question commentary does not answer consistently. A Ministry of Corporate Affairs notification brought a substantial part of the 2026 Amendment Act into force with effect from 26 May 2026, and at least one source lists the cross-border insolvency provision among those commenced from that date, while other commentary from the same general period lists the section inserting the cross-border provisions as not yet in force. Given this conflict in the secondary sources available, a foreign creditor or its advisers should check the commencement notifications in the Official Gazette directly, rather than relying on this article, or any single piece of commentary, for whether Section 240C is actually operative on a given date, and in any event should not assume that rules have actually been framed under it merely because the enabling section itself may have commenced.

What a foreign creditor should actually do today

For the ordinary case, a foreign bank, supplier or other creditor owed money by an Indian company that has entered a corporate insolvency resolution process, the practical path is straightforward in outline even where individual steps are demanding in practice: establish promptly whether the debt is financial or operational in character, assemble and, where necessary, authenticate proof of the debt in a form the resolution professional will accept, file the claim within the window the public announcement specifies using the correct prescribed form, and plan for the claim to be valued in rupees at the exchange rate on the insolvency commencement date rather than any other date that might otherwise seem more natural.

Where the claim is founded on a foreign arbitral award rather than a straightforward loan or invoice, the creditor should treat enforcement of the award under Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 as a parallel track to pursue without delay, rather than as a fallback to consider only if the insolvency claim founded on the unenforced award runs into difficulty, given the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in Kalyani Transco. A decree-holder should be ready to explain the underlying character of the claim the decree confirms, since the decree alone will not settle how the claim is classified.

Where the real concern is not a claim in an Indian company's own insolvency but coordination with a foreign insolvency proceeding opened against a related entity, the creditor and its advisers should not assume Sections 234 and 235 offer a usable route, since neither has ever been operationalised, and should instead budget for the possibility that any cross-border coordination will have to be negotiated on a case-specific basis with the Indian resolution professional and tribunal, in the manner the Jet Airways protocol illustrates, rather than claimed as a statutory entitlement. Keeping a watch on whether rules are actually framed under Section 240C, and on the commencement status of that section itself, is worth building into the ongoing management of any matter where cross-border recognition genuinely matters to the outcome.

Mistakes that recur among foreign creditors in Indian insolvency matters

A recurring mistake is assuming that because a foreign creditor's debt is undisputed and well-documented in its own jurisdiction, admission of the claim in the Indian process will be a formality. Resolution professionals and, where a dispute arises, the adjudicating authority scrutinise proof of claim on its own terms, and documentation that would be accepted without question by a court or counterparty in the creditor's home jurisdiction can still need translation, certification or authentication before an Indian resolution professional will treat it as adequate proof.

A second recurring mistake, specific to award and decree holders, is treating an arbitral award or a foreign judgment as self-evidently equivalent to cash in hand once the Indian debtor enters insolvency, without accounting for the separate question of whether that award or judgment has been enforced in the manner Indian law requires. The Kalyani Transco ruling has made this mistake considerably more costly than it might once have been.

A third mistake is conflating the two questions this article opened with: a foreign creditor's own ability to file and have admitted a claim against an Indian debtor, which is reasonably well established, and the separate, genuinely unsettled question of whether a foreign insolvency proceeding against a related entity will be recognised or coordinated with by an Indian tribunal. A creditor advised, correctly, that it can file a claim in the Indian process should not take that as any assurance about how a related cross-border recognition question, if one arises, will be resolved, since the two rest on entirely different, and differently developed, parts of the Code.

A foreign creditor's standing to file and pursue a claim in an Indian company's own corporate insolvency resolution process does not turn on where the creditor is based; it turns on correctly classifying the debt, documenting it to a standard an Indian resolution professional will accept, and valuing it in rupees as the regulations require. Where the claim rests on a foreign arbitral award rather than an ordinary loan or invoice, the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in Kalyani Transco means enforcement of that award can no longer be treated as an optional afterthought. The genuinely unresolved question is a different one: whether India will recognise and coordinate with an insolvency proceeding opened abroad against a related entity. Sections 234 and 235 remain unused after years on the statute book, the 2026 Amendment Act's Section 240C is, at most, a promise of rules still to be written, and its own commencement is disputed among commentators as on today. A foreign creditor or its advisers should treat these as two separate files, and should verify the commencement and rule-making status of Section 240C against the Official Gazette before relying on it for any specific matter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a foreign bank or supplier file a claim in an Indian company's insolvency process?

Yes. The definition of creditor under Section 3(10) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 does not depend on residence, and a foreign bank, supplier or other creditor can file a claim as a financial or operational creditor depending on the nature of its debt, in the same way an Indian creditor would.

Is a claim owed in US dollars or another foreign currency paid in that currency?

No. A foreign-currency claim is converted into Indian rupees at the official exchange rate as on the insolvency commencement date for the purpose of filing, admission and valuation, and any eventual payout under a resolution plan is typically expressed and paid in rupees.

Can a foreign arbitral award be used as proof of a claim in a CIRP without first enforcing it in India?

Some tribunals have accepted an unenforced foreign-seated award as adequate proof in particular matters, but others have not, and the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling in Kalyani Transco v Bhushan Power and Steel Limited has made this route considerably less reliable. Pursuing enforcement of the award under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 alongside the insolvency claim is now the more defensible course.

Will an Indian tribunal recognise an insolvency proceeding opened against a related company abroad?

There is no reliable statutory route for this today. Sections 234 and 235 of the Code, which were meant to provide one through bilateral agreements and letters of request, have never been operationalised, and the 2026 Amendment Act's Section 240C is an enabling provision for future rules rather than a working framework.

What happened in the Jet Airways case and does it help other foreign creditors?

A Dutch administrator sought recognition of Dutch insolvency proceedings against Jet Airways in India; the Tribunal initially declined on the basis that Sections 234 and 235 had not been notified. On appeal, the Appellate Tribunal facilitated a case-specific Cross-Border Insolvency Protocol letting the Dutch administrator observe committee of creditors meetings. It is an example of what tribunals can improvise, not a statutory right another foreign party can simply invoke.

Does my claim get rejected if I file it in the wrong proof-of-claim form?

Not automatically. The Supreme Court held in Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority v Prabhjit Singh Soni & Anr, 2024 INSC 102, that the form is directory rather than mandatory, and a claim supported by proof should not be overlooked merely because it was filed in a different form. Filing in the correct form from the outset remains the safer course.

Is a decree-holder automatically treated as a financial or operational creditor?

No. A decree-holder is within the Code's definition of creditor, but whether the underlying claim is a financial debt, an operational debt, or neither depends on the character of the claim the decree confirms, which remains a live, unsettled question in several reported matters.

Has India adopted the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency?

No, not as on today. The 2026 Amendment Act's Section 240C gives the Central Government a power to frame cross-border insolvency rules, but it does not itself adopt the Model Law or set out a complete framework, and whether the section has even come into force is disputed among commentators.
