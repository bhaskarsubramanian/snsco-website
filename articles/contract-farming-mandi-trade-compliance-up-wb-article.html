Contract Farming, Mandi Law and Commodity Trade Compliance for Agribusiness in UP and West Bengal

What changed when the central farm laws were repealed, how Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal actually regulate produce trade today, and the compliance points that matter for an agribusiness, trader or lender financing against stock.

By Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 13 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

For about a year, India ran a single central scheme for farm produce trade outside the mandi system. That scheme was repealed in November 2021, and the law governing how an agribusiness buys, sells, stores and finances agricultural produce in Uttar Pradesh or West Bengal went back to being almost entirely a matter of state law, layered with the Essential Commodities Act's periodic stock-limit orders and a separate federal scheme for warehouse-receipt financing. Few agribusinesses operating across both states have actually mapped what survived the repeal and what did not. This piece sets out the mandi framework each state runs, what a contract farming arrangement can still achieve without it, and the points where stock limits, licensing and financing most often create compliance exposure.

Key points

  • The Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, 2020, which allowed trade outside physical mandi premises, was repealed by the Farm Laws Repeal Act, 2021; agricultural marketing in Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal has since reverted to being governed almost entirely by each state's own APMC-style statute.
  • Uttar Pradesh regulates mandi trade and a separate direct-marketing licence route under the Krishi Utpadan Mandi Adhiniyam, 1964, while West Bengal licenses storage, sale and purchase of notified produce under Section 13 of the Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation) Act, 1972, subject to an exemption for a producer selling their own produce.
  • A contract farming agreement between a company and a farmer remains enforceable as an ordinary contract after the repeal; what disappeared with the 2020 Act was the specific central registration and dispute-resolution mechanism that Act had created for such agreements, not the underlying ability to contract.
  • The Essential Commodities Act, 1955 continues to be used for time-bound stock-limit orders on specific commodities, sugar and raw jute among the commodities restricted at points through 2026, and an agri-trader's exposure under these orders changes with each notification rather than following one fixed, permanent limit.
  • Financing against stored produce increasingly runs through electronic negotiable warehouse receipts regulated by the Warehousing Development and Regulatory Authority, which lets a trader or processor raise working capital by pledging the eNWR rather than the physical stock itself, subject to the warehouse itself being WDRA-registered.
  • Cartelisation among input dealers and produce traders carries its own, separate exposure under the Competition Act, 2002, independent of and in addition to anything an agribusiness gets right under mandi or stock-limit law.

Back to a state-by-state patchwork, after a year of trying something else

Parliament enacted the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, 2020 to let a farmer or trader buy and sell notified produce outside the physical premises of an APMC mandi, in any state, without the mandi fee and licensing requirements that applied inside those premises. Sustained farmer protests followed, the Supreme Court stayed implementation in January 2021, and Parliament itself repealed the Act in late November 2021 through the Farm Laws Repeal Act, 2021, along with two companion statutes passed at the same time.

Agriculture and markets are state subjects, so the repeal did not leave a vacuum; it simply returned the field to whatever each state's own Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation) Act already said, for states that still run one. Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal both do, and both have amended their own statutes more than once since, in directions that do not mirror each other.

Bihar is the often-cited counter-example, having abolished its own APMC system in 2006, well before the 2020 central reform, and reports on its mandi infrastructure since have been mixed. Neither Uttar Pradesh nor West Bengal has taken that route, and Bihar's experience is mentioned here only because it is a frequent reference point in commentary on agricultural marketing reform generally, not because it has any direct bearing on the two states this piece actually covers.

For an agribusiness, trader or processor operating in both states, the practical consequence is that there is no single, central shortcut around mandi regulation to fall back on anymore. Each state's licensing, fee and direct-marketing rules have to be worked through separately, and an arrangement that is lawful in one state's mandi law is not automatically lawful in the other's.

This is not entirely new ground, even if the 2020 to 2021 episode was unusually visible. A central Model Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation) Act had been circulated to states since the early 2000s as a template for reform, encouraging direct marketing, private mandis and contract farming provisions within each state's own law, and a later Model Agricultural Produce and Livestock Marketing Act followed in 2017 with a similar aim. Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal each picked up some of that template and not other parts of it, on their own legislative timetables, which is itself part of why the two states' mandi laws read so differently from each other today.

Because adoption of these model templates was always voluntary, rather than mandated from the centre, an agribusiness cannot assume that a reform reported nationally, a new licensing category, a relaxed fee structure, a digital trading initiative, has actually been enacted in the specific state it operates in, let alone commenced or notified there. The gap between a reform being announced at the national policy level and it actually taking effect in a given mandi samiti's day-to-day practice is, on the reported record, one of the more persistent sources of avoidable compliance mistakes in this sector.

The mandi system in Uttar Pradesh: the Adhiniyam, licensing and direct marketing

Uttar Pradesh regulates the marketing of notified agricultural produce under the Krishi Utpadan Mandi Adhiniyam, 1964, administered through the mandi samitis and the state Mandi Parishad. The Adhiniyam's structure includes licensing provisions for trading within a notified mandi area, fee and cess recovery powers that have themselves been the subject of recurring litigation, including a reported Allahabad High Court ruling on the retrospective application of a licence fee, and a defined category for direct marketing outside the traditional mandi yard.

A 2020 amendment ordinance added defined terms for direct marketing and private mandi yards, and later amendments have introduced a licensing structure specifically for direct marketing, including provisions for grant, renewal, suspension and cancellation of that licence under the Act's later sections. This direct-marketing route is narrower than what the since-repealed central Act briefly allowed: it operates as a licensed alternative within the state's own regulatory structure rather than as an exemption from state regulation altogether, and an operator using it still answers to the Mandi Parishad for compliance.

We would not rely on any publicly available source for the current fee schedule or the precise current text of the direct-marketing provisions without confirming it against the Mandi Parishad's own notifications for the relevant mandi samiti, since fee orders and procedural amendments in this area have changed more than once in recent years and a figure that was current when written can be superseded quickly.

Reported litigation under the Adhiniyam has also tested the limits of compounding orders issued for specific trades, such as processing units for gur and khandsari, where fee and cess liability was fixed by reference to a specific proviso in the Act's fee-recovery provisions rather than the general mandi fee schedule. A processor operating in a specialised commodity line should check whether a sector-specific compounding or fee order applies to it before assuming the standard mandi fee structure is what governs its particular trade.

The mandi system in West Bengal: Section 13 licensing and the producer's exemption

West Bengal's Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation) Act, 1972 takes a differently structured approach. Section 13 requires a licence for the storage, sale or purchase of any notified agricultural produce, but carries a proviso exempting a producer who sells their own produce, along with retail sale and purchase by an individual for personal consumption. A 2014 amendment defined a producer, for this purpose, as a person who grows notified produce on their own account, which matters because that definition is what determines whether a given seller actually falls within the licensing exemption or needs a licence of their own.

A reported 2017 amendment is said to have moved the state toward a single licence valid for trading across markets within West Bengal, rather than requiring a separate licence for each market, though we would confirm the current licensing structure with the state Agricultural Marketing Department before relying on that description for a specific transaction. Litigation under Section 13 has included challenges by non-farm buyers, such as processors, to whether their activity falls inside or outside the licensing requirement, so a buyer that is not itself a farmer should not assume the producer exemption extends to it.

For an agribusiness sourcing from West Bengal, the practical question is almost always which side of the Section 13 licence requirement a given purchase falls on: buying directly and personally from the farmer who grew the produce is treated differently from buying through an intermediary trader, and the licensing exposure sits with whoever is actually doing the storing, buying or selling without the appropriate licence or exemption, a point that reaches well beyond the party sitting at the top of the supply chain.

Operating without the required licence under Section 13 exposes the trader to the Act's own penalty provisions rather than merely to a refusal to register, and a buyer who discovers mid-transaction that its counterparty's claimed producer exemption does not actually hold up, because the counterparty is itself an aggregator rather than the grower, should treat that as a reason to pause the purchase pending clarification rather than complete it on the assumption that the exemption question is the seller's problem alone.

Contract farming after the repeal: what survived and what did not

The repealed Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce Act's companion legislation, the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, 2020, had set up a specific statutory framework for contract farming agreements, including a prescribed registration and a dispute-resolution mechanism running through a Sub-Divisional Magistrate before a civil court could be approached. That Act was repealed along with the other two in November 2021.

What this repeal removed was the specific statutory scaffolding the 2020 Act had built around contract farming, not the underlying freedom of a farmer and a company to enter into a contract for future supply of produce on agreed terms, which continues to exist as an ordinary matter of contract law under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. A written agreement setting out price, quality specifications, delivery schedule and the consequences of a crop failure or a breach by either side remains fully enforceable; what a drafter no longer has available is the specific central registration process and the summary SDM-first dispute route the 2020 Act had created.

In practice, this means a contract farming arrangement today is only as good as its own drafting and the ordinary civil remedies available for breach of contract, including a suit for damages or, depending on the terms, specific performance, rather than any special statutory fast-track. Arbitration clauses, security for the farmer's advance where one is paid, and clear force majeure and quality-rejection terms matter more now than they did when the repealed Act's own dispute mechanism was available as a backstop. Where the farmer-side party is unlikely to be able to satisfy a damages award in practice, a company extending input or cash advances against future delivery should also think through what security it is actually taking against the advance, whether a lien over the standing crop, a guarantee from a cooperative or FPO counterparty, or simply pricing the credit risk into the contract rate, rather than assuming the supply contract itself is sufficient security.

A second, earlier generation of state templates, the Model Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation) Act of 2003, had taken a different approach, providing for contract farming agreements to be registered with the local APMC itself rather than with a separate central authority. Depending on whether Uttar Pradesh or West Bengal carried any version of that 2003-style registration provision into their own current Act, a contract farming arrangement in one state may still have a state-level registration step available or required even though the 2020 central scheme is gone, which is a further reason the position needs checking state by state rather than assumed to be identical.

Yet another draft model law, published in 2018 and separately titled to place contract farming entirely outside the ambit of the state APMC structure under a dedicated state-level authority, never achieved the same level of central attention as the 2020 Act and its repeal, and whether either state has since enacted anything resembling it is a question worth checking against each state's current legislative record before relying on any particular version of a contract farming registration regime being available.

Essential Commodities Act stock limits: how they actually work

Section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 lets the central government, and state governments under delegated power, control the production, supply and distribution of a notified essential commodity, including by fixing the maximum quantity a dealer may hold at any time. These orders are deliberately time-bound and commodity-specific rather than a single standing rule, which is precisely why an agri-trader's actual exposure changes through the year rather than following one fixed figure.

2026 has seen this power used more than once. A sugar stock-limit order took effect from 1 August 2026, capping dealer holdings at 4,000 quintals with a maximum 30-day holding period, and a further notification reduced the general dealer cap to 2,000 quintals with effect from 15 September 2026, while carving out a higher limit for Kolkata and its extended metropolitan area. Separately, in April 2026, the Jute Commissioner cut the stock limit for raw jute held by traders and balers to zero and directed them to sell their entire holdings to jute mills by a fixed date, a measure reported as aimed at curbing hoarding amid price volatility in that commodity.

A trader or processor dealing in a commodity that has previously been the subject of a stock-limit order should check the current notification before assuming either that an old limit still applies or that no limit applies at all, since these orders are amended and superseded on short notice and the consequence of exceeding a limit that is in force, even unknowingly, is prosecution under the Act rather than a civil penalty that can simply be negotiated afterward.

Stock-limit orders frequently distinguish between categories of holder, and the sugar order noted above is a case in point: the general dealer cap sat alongside a separate order for bulk consumers, covering confectioners, soft-drink manufacturers, food-processing units and sweetmeat sellers, whose permitted holding is calculated differently from a pure trading dealer's. An agribusiness that processes a restricted commodity rather than simply trading in it should confirm which category its own operation falls into under the specific notification, since the bulk-consumer and dealer categories have not consistently carried the same limit.

e-NAM and the limits of a digital trading layer

The electronic National Agriculture Market, e-NAM, is a government-promoted online trading platform that links mandis across states to let buyers bid on produce listed at a mandi without being physically present there. It operates as a trading layer on top of the mandi system rather than as a replacement for it, so listing and settlement still happen through the mandi infrastructure and licensing it is layered onto, and a trader using e-NAM in Uttar Pradesh or West Bengal still needs whatever licence the underlying state Act requires for that trade.

Our review of currently available material did not turn up a single authoritative, current figure for e-NAM's coverage of mandis in either state that we are confident enough to state here, and participation and functionality have both been reported to vary by mandi and by commodity. An agribusiness considering e-NAM as its primary sourcing channel in either state should confirm current participation and settlement practice with the specific mandi samiti it intends to trade through, rather than assume uniform coverage across the state.

Quality assaying remains, in practice, a physical process even where bidding happens online: a buyer on e-NAM is still bidding against a sample or a graded lot assessed at the mandi itself, so disputes about quality mismatch between the sample and the delivered produce are resolved through the same mandi-level grievance mechanism that would apply to an ordinary physical transaction, not through any separate online dispute process specific to the platform.

Payment settlement under e-NAM is also tied to the mandi's own banking and licensing arrangements rather than to a platform-level escrow independent of state regulation, so a seller's actual ability to recover payment promptly still depends on the financial standing and compliance record of the licensed trader on the other side of the transaction, a point that an e-NAM listing by itself does nothing to guarantee.

Financing stock: electronic negotiable warehouse receipts

The Warehousing Development and Regulatory Authority regulates electronic negotiable warehouse receipts, eNWRs, issued against produce stored in a WDRA-registered warehouse, under the Electronic Negotiable Warehouse Receipts Regulations, 2017. An eNWR, once issued, can be transferred or pledged electronically through the repository system, which lets a trader, processor or farmer raise working capital against stored produce without physically moving the stock or relying on a traditional godown pledge arrangement that a lender has to separately verify and monitor.

This financing route has grown quickly in reported scale: the WDRA's own annual report for 2022-23 recorded pledge financing against eNWRs running into several thousand crores, and National e-Repository Limited, a WDRA-regulated repository, reported crossing ten thousand crore rupees in cumulative pledge finance against eNWRs by early 2025. A Credit Guarantee Scheme for eNWR-based pledge financing, launched in December 2024, is intended to make lenders more willing to extend this kind of finance by covering part of the default risk.

The practical limitation for an agribusiness in Uttar Pradesh or West Bengal is that this entire financing route depends on the stock being held in a warehouse that is itself registered with the WDRA; produce stored in an unregistered godown, however reputable, cannot generate a negotiable eNWR, and a lender relying on this route as security should confirm the warehouse's registration status directly with the WDRA rather than taking the warehouse operator's own representation at face value.

A negotiable eNWR also needs to be distinguished from a non-negotiable receipt, which merely evidences that goods are held in store without conferring the same transferability or pledge mechanics; disputes have arisen, including reported cases of double pledging of the same stock against more than one lender, where a warehouse or an intermediary issued or dealt with receipts outside the WDRA's electronic repository framework. A lender or financier should insist on dealing only through the registered repository system rather than accepting a paper or informally issued receipt as equivalent security.

Collective structures: a brief word on FPOs, producer companies and cooperatives

Many agribusiness arrangements in both states now run through a Farmer Producer Organisation or a producer company rather than through individual farmer contracts, aggregating supply and giving the counterparty a single entity to deal with instead of many smallholders. A producer company is registered and governed under Part IXA of the Companies Act, 1956, which the Ministry of Corporate Affairs has confirmed continues to apply to producer companies notwithstanding the repeal of the 1956 Act by the Companies Act, 2013, with membership restricted broadly to producers and defined institutions and with every producer company generally required to deal primarily with the produce of its active members.

The choice between contracting with an FPO, a producer company or a traditional cooperative society changes the counterparty's governance, liability and dispute-resolution profile in ways that matter to how a supply agreement should be drafted, including who actually has authority to bind the entity to a multi-year contract. We have covered the structural comparison between these vehicles in detail elsewhere and do not repeat it here; the point for this piece is narrower, that the collective-structure question and the mandi licensing question discussed above are independent of each other, and resolving one does not resolve the other.

A traditional agricultural cooperative society remains a live alternative in both states, particularly in West Bengal's dairy and allied sectors, where disputes have more often concerned governance, such as supersession of a cooperative's board by the Registrar, than the marketing arrangement itself. An agribusiness contracting with a cooperative counterparty should confirm that the office-bearers signing the contract actually have current authority to do so, since a supersession or an expired board term occurring mid-contract has, in reported cases, been used to question whether the cooperative was validly bound at all.

Export and import restrictions: a recurring compliance risk

Beyond domestic stock limits, agricultural commodities are regularly subject to export restrictions or outright export bans imposed under the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992 and the trade policy notified under it, sugar, wheat and non-basmati rice having each been restricted at different points in recent years in response to domestic price and supply conditions. These restrictions are notified with immediate or near-immediate effect and do not generally grandfather contracts entered into before the notification, which has produced reported litigation where an exporter had already taken payment or committed shipment before a ban was announced.

An agribusiness with export exposure should treat the possibility of a sudden export restriction as a contractual risk to be allocated in the sale contract itself, through force majeure or change-in-law clauses addressing a trade restriction specifically, rather than as a risk it discovers only when a notification actually lands and an already-agreed shipment can no longer go out.

The import side carries its own, separate compliance layer for an agribusiness bringing in seed, planting material, pesticide formulations or agricultural machinery, running through sanitary and phytosanitary clearance, the Insecticides Act, 1968 registration regime for pesticide products, and ordinary customs classification, none of which is displaced or simplified by anything discussed elsewhere in this piece. An importer should treat the import clearance timeline as a separate planning item from the domestic mandi and stock-limit compliance covered above, since the two run through entirely different authorities and documentation, and a delay on the customs or quarantine side can sit entirely outside the agribusiness's own control even where every mandi and stock-limit obligation has been met on time.

Competition law exposure: cartelisation among dealers and traders

Agricultural input and produce trading is not exempt from the Competition Act, 2002, and the Competition Commission of India has in fact penalised associations of agro-input dealers for coordinated conduct amounting to bid-rigging or cartelisation. This exposure sits entirely separately from mandi licensing or stock-limit compliance: an agri-trader or input dealer can hold every licence correctly and still face proceedings before the CCI if it coordinates pricing, allocates territories or restricts supply in concert with competitors, whether through a formal trade association or an informal understanding.

Dealer and distributor associations common in agricultural input and produce trade, of the kind that exist in most mandi towns, should be particularly careful about what gets discussed or agreed at association meetings, since the CCI has treated association-level coordination on pricing or terms as cartelisation even where no single dominant player orchestrated it. Minutes of association meetings, circulars suggesting a common price or a common response to a buyer, and informal messaging among dealers ahead of a mandi auction have all, in reported enforcement practice across sectors, served as the kind of evidence the CCI relies on to establish coordination, so agribusinesses sitting on these associations should treat routine record-keeping around pricing discussions as a genuine compliance exposure, not a formality.

The Competition Act's leniency provisions give a party to a cartel arrangement a route to reduced penalties by approaching the CCI first with full disclosure, and an agribusiness that discovers it has been drawn into association-level price coordination, even informally, should treat that disclosure option as a live choice to weigh against continuing exposure, rather than something to consider only after an investigation has already begun.

Dispute and enforcement forums across the two states

A licensing dispute under either state's APMC-style Act, a suspension or cancellation of a mandi or direct-marketing licence, generally starts with a representation to the Mandi Parishad or the Agricultural Marketing Department and, where the Act provides one, an appeal to a designated appellate authority before a court is approached. A straightforward civil suit to recover price for produce sold, by contrast, proceeds in the ordinary civil courts of the district where the transaction took place or where the contract specifies, since that kind of claim is not itself a licensing dispute.

Exceeding a stock-limit order under the Essential Commodities Act is a criminal matter, prosecuted under the Act rather than contested through a civil appeal process, which is a materially different risk profile from a licensing dispute and should be treated accordingly by compliance teams; the appropriate response to a stock-limit notification is to adjust holdings promptly, not to contest the notification's validity while continuing to hold stock above the new limit.

Writ jurisdiction, before the Allahabad High Court for Uttar Pradesh matters and the Calcutta High Court for West Bengal matters, becomes relevant where a licensing or stock-limit order is challenged on the ground that the authority acted beyond its power, without proper notice, or in a manner that the Act itself did not contemplate, rather than as a routine first recourse for an ordinary licensing refusal that the Act's own appellate structure is designed to handle. Both High Courts have also shown themselves willing, on a proper case, to direct a mandi or licensing authority to decide a pending application within a fixed timeframe, which can matter more to a seasonal agribusiness than the underlying merits of the dispute if the authority's delay itself is the real commercial problem.

Claims to recover an outstanding price for produce already delivered, or to recover mandi fee or cess the authority claims is owed, are both subject to the ordinary law of limitation, generally three years from when the amount fell due under the Limitation Act, 1963 as applied to contract and money claims, and a party sitting on either kind of claim for too long risks losing it regardless of how strong the underlying facts are. Agribusinesses that extend credit terms to buyers or sellers as a matter of trade practice should track the limitation clock from delivery or invoice date, not from the date a dispute eventually surfaces.

GST and the e-way bill: the tax layer that sits alongside mandi licensing

Mandi licensing and GST compliance run on separate tracks, and getting one right says nothing about the other. Fresh agricultural produce sold by the cultivator in its primary, unprocessed form, allowing for basic operations such as cleaning, drying or grading, is generally exempt from GST, and an individual farmer selling raw, unprocessed produce is generally not required to register for GST at all regardless of turnover. That exemption is specifically for the unprocessed state of the produce, and a step as ordinary as converting raw cashew into kernels or pepper into powder is commonly treated as commercial processing that removes the product from the exemption and brings it into the ordinary GST rate structure.

Transport of agricultural produce carries its own, separate relief: freight for goods transport is generally taxed on a reverse-charge basis, but a long-standing exemption under the central GST rate notification excludes transportation of agricultural produce from that reverse-charge liability, an exemption that, consistent with the point above, stops applying once the produce has been processed beyond what the exemption contemplates. Rule 138(14) of the GST Rules separately lists categories of goods, heavily weighted toward unprocessed agricultural and essential items, that do not require an e-way bill regardless of consignment value, though the exemption is often conditional on the goods remaining in their basic, unbranded or unpackaged form, loose rice being treated differently from branded, packaged rice for this purpose.

An agribusiness that moves produce through several stages, from farm-gate purchase through cleaning or grading to branded packaging and onward sale, should map at which stage in that chain the GST and e-way bill exemptions actually stop applying, since the same lorry-load of produce can be exempt at one point in the supply chain and fully taxable, with a mandatory e-way bill, only a short distance and a processing step later.

A compliance checklist for an agribusiness working across both states

Six checks are worth building into a standing compliance calendar rather than confirming once and filing away. First, whether the specific produce and transaction need a mandi licence, a direct-marketing licence, or fall within a producer's exemption, confirmed separately for Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal rather than assumed to be the same. Second, whether any Essential Commodities Act stock-limit order currently applies to the commodity being traded, checked against the live notification rather than a figure from an earlier year, and against the correct holder category, dealer or bulk consumer, for that commodity. Third, whether contract farming or supply agreements are drafted with their own dispute-resolution and security terms, given that the repealed 2020 Act's statutory backstop no longer exists. Fourth, where produce is being financed through a warehouse receipt, that the specific warehouse is currently WDRA-registered and that the receipt being relied on is a genuine electronic negotiable instrument rather than a paper or informally issued document. Fifth, that any trade association or dealer-to-dealer communication on pricing or supply terms is reviewed for competition law exposure before it happens, not after a complaint is filed. Sixth, that outstanding price or fee claims are tracked against the limitation clock from the date they fell due, not from the date a dispute happens to surface.

None of these six checks is especially burdensome on its own, and the practical risk we see most often is not any single one being missed but the assumption that getting the mandi licence right in one state means the same compliance position carries over to the other, or that a contract farming template that worked under the repealed central scheme can simply be reused today without redrafting the dispute and security provisions it used to rely on. A short annual review against this list, timed to pick up any new stock-limit notification or amendment to either state's Adhiniyam or Marketing Act, costs far less than discovering a gap during an inspection, a financing dispute or a CCI inquiry.

The repeal of the 2020 farm laws did not create a gap in agricultural trade regulation; it returned the field to Uttar Pradesh's and West Bengal's own, independently amended mandi statutes, each with its own licensing structure, its own direct-marketing or producer exemptions, and its own appellate route. An agribusiness operating across both states has to treat them as two separate compliance regimes rather than one regional framework, and the points most likely to be missed are the ones that sit outside the mandi Act altogether: a stock-limit notification that changed since the last time anyone checked, a warehouse receipt resting on a warehouse whose WDRA registration was never reconfirmed, or a contract farming template still drafted for a dispute mechanism that no longer exists.

None of this is a reason to avoid contract farming, direct sourcing or warehouse-receipt financing, all of which remain legitimate and increasingly common ways to structure agribusiness in both states. It is a reason to treat each of these structures as resting on its own, currently applicable legal basis, confirmed state by state and notification by notification, rather than on a general sense of how agricultural trade law used to work before November 2021.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it still legal to buy farm produce directly from a farmer outside the mandi in Uttar Pradesh or West Bengal?

It depends on the state and the route used, not on the repealed central Act, which no longer provides a general exemption from mandi regulation. Uttar Pradesh has its own licensed direct-marketing route under the Krishi Utpadan Mandi Adhiniyam, 1964. West Bengal's Act exempts a producer selling their own produce from the Section 13 licence requirement, but a buyer who is not the producer, such as a trader or processor, generally still needs the appropriate licence.

Can a company still enter into a contract farming agreement with farmers after the 2020 farm laws were repealed?

Yes. The repeal removed the specific statutory registration and SDM-first dispute mechanism the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement Act, 2020 had created, not the general freedom to contract. A written contract farming agreement remains enforceable under the ordinary law of contract, so the drafting now has to carry its own dispute-resolution, security and quality-rejection provisions rather than relying on a statutory backstop.

Is there a fixed limit on how much sugar or grain a trader can stock in India?

No single fixed limit. The Essential Commodities Act, 1955 allows the government to impose stock limits by notification, commodity by commodity and for a defined period, rather than as one permanent rule. Sugar, for example, was subject to a 4,000-quintal, 30-day limit from August 2026 and a reduced 2,000-quintal general limit from September 2026, with different figures for different regions, so the applicable limit has to be checked against the current notification.

What is an electronic negotiable warehouse receipt and why does it matter for financing?

An eNWR is a negotiable receipt issued electronically against produce stored in a warehouse registered with the Warehousing Development and Regulatory Authority. It can be transferred or pledged through the repository system, letting a trader or processor raise working capital against stored stock without a traditional physical pledge. It only works if the specific warehouse is WDRA-registered; stock in an unregistered godown cannot generate a valid eNWR.

Does e-NAM let a buyer in one state bypass the mandi licensing requirements of another state?

No. e-NAM is a trading layer that sits on top of the existing mandi infrastructure and licensing in each state; it does not replace the underlying state law. A buyer trading through e-NAM in Uttar Pradesh or West Bengal still needs whatever licence or exemption the relevant state Act requires for that transaction.

Can agricultural input dealers coordinate on pricing if they are a registered trade association?

No. The Competition Commission of India has penalised associations of agro-input dealers for coordinated conduct amounting to cartelisation, and registration as a trade association does not exempt pricing or supply coordination from the Competition Act, 2002. This exposure is separate from, and in addition to, any mandi licensing or stock-limit compliance the dealers may otherwise have in order.

What happens if an export ban is announced after an agribusiness has already agreed to ship a commodity abroad?

Export restrictions notified under the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992 generally take effect immediately and do not automatically grandfather contracts already agreed, which has produced disputes where an exporter had already received payment or committed a shipment. Because of this, export sale contracts should allocate the risk of a sudden trade restriction explicitly, through a force majeure or change-in-law clause addressing that specific risk, rather than leaving it to be argued after a ban is announced.
