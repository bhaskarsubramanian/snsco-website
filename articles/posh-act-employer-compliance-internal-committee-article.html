The POSH Act for Employers: Internal Committee, Inquiry Timelines and the Rules That Actually Carry a Penalty

A compliance framework most employers set up once and then stop thinking about, even though the obligations under it do not stop.

By Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 is now well into its second decade, and most employers of any size know, at least in outline, that they need an Internal Committee. What a surprising number of employers do not have a clear grip on is the detail underneath that single fact: exactly who has to sit on the committee, how long an inquiry can run before it is overdue, what the annual report actually requires even when there have been no complaints at all, and what a court can actually do to an employer who gets this wrong. The Supreme Court has spent the last few years pushing precisely on this gap between formal awareness and actual compliance, and the direction of that pressure is toward closer scrutiny rather than less. This piece sets out the compliance framework as it stands, section by section, for an employer trying to get it right rather than merely to have a policy document on file.

Key points

  • An employer with ten or more employees must constitute an Internal Committee under Section 4 of the Act; a workplace with fewer than ten employees, and any complaint against the employer personally regardless of the establishment's size, falls instead to the Local Committee constituted by the District Officer under Section 6, not to an Internal Committee at all.
  • Section 4 requires a Presiding Officer who is a woman employed at a senior level, not less than two members from among employees with experience in social work or legal knowledge or committed to the cause of women, and one external member from an NGO or association working on women's issues or familiar with sexual harassment matters, with not less than half the total membership being women.
  • An inquiry must ordinarily be completed within 90 days of the complaint under Section 11, the Internal Committee's report must go to the employer within 10 days of completing the inquiry under Section 13, and the employer must act on the recommendations within 60 days of receiving the report under Section 13, with a further appeal available under Section 18 within 90 days of the recommendations or the employer's implementation of them.
  • A complaint must ordinarily be filed within three months of the incident, or the last incident in a series, under Section 9; the Internal or Local Committee has a limited, non-automatic discretion under the same section to extend this by a further three months, for reasons recorded in writing, where the complainant can show circumstances that prevented a timely filing, making six months the practical outer limit in the ordinary case.
  • Section 21 requires the Internal Committee to prepare an annual report, including in years with no complaints at all, for submission to the employer and onward to the District Officer; Section 22 requires the employer to include relevant particulars in its own annual report where applicable.
  • Section 26 penalises an employer's failure to constitute an Internal Committee, failure to act on the Committee's recommendations, or contravention of any other provision of the Act or its rules, with a fine that commentary consistently reports at up to fifty thousand rupees for a first contravention, with repeat contravention carrying a higher penalty and, potentially, cancellation or non-renewal of the business licence or registration required to operate.
  • Supreme Court directions issued across 2023 to 2025 in the Aureliano Fernandes litigation have pushed state governments toward a nationwide compliance survey and activation of the She-Box portal for complaints, signalling continuing judicial attention to whether employers, particularly smaller and private-sector ones, have actually constituted and trained a functioning Internal Committee rather than merely adopted a policy on paper.

Two committees, and which one actually has jurisdiction

The Act creates two separate bodies to receive and inquire into complaints, and a surprising amount of confusion in practice comes from employers assuming their own Internal Committee has jurisdiction over every complaint connected to their workplace, when it does not. Section 4 requires every employer of a workplace with ten or more employees to constitute an Internal Committee at each office or branch with that headcount. Section 6 requires the District Officer, separately, to constitute a Local Committee for each district, specifically to receive complaints from establishments with fewer than ten employees, from domestic workers, and, significantly, from complaints made against the employer personally, regardless of how large that employer's organisation otherwise is.

This last point matters more than its brief mention in the Act suggests. An organisation with hundreds of employees and a properly constituted Internal Committee cannot have that Committee inquire into a complaint made against the managing director or a proprietor who personally falls within the Act's definition of employer, because the Act specifically routes a complaint against the employer to the Local Committee instead. Employers should know, before a complaint of that kind actually arises, which District Officer and Local Committee has jurisdiction over their workplace location, rather than discovering the correct forum only after a complaint naming a senior figure in the organisation has already been filed with the wrong body.

Constituting the Internal Committee: who actually has to be on it

Section 4(2) sets out four categories of required membership. A Presiding Officer, who must be a woman employed at a senior level in the workplace; not less than two members from amongst employees, who should preferably have experience in social work or legal knowledge or who are committed to the cause of women; one member from a non-governmental organisation or association committed to the cause of women, or who is otherwise familiar with issues relating to sexual harassment; and, running across all of these categories, a requirement that not less than half of the total members nominated be women. Where the workplace does not have a sufficiently senior woman employee available to act as Presiding Officer, or genuinely lacks ten employees in a particular office to assemble a full Committee, the employer should look to a nominee from another office of the same organisation or, where the headcount itself falls below ten, recognise that the Local Committee route applies instead rather than attempting to constitute an under-strength Internal Committee.

Each member, including the Presiding Officer, holds office for a period not exceeding three years from nomination under Section 4(3), which means a Committee constituted once at the organisation's founding and never refreshed is, almost certainly, operating with at least some members whose term has technically lapsed by the time a complaint eventually arises years later. Keeping a simple internal record of each member's appointment date and three-year expiry, and re-nominating or replacing members proactively as those dates approach, avoids the entirely avoidable problem of discovering mid-inquiry that half the Committee's current composition is procedurally out of date.

The external member deserves particular attention because this is the single Committee position employers most often fill inadequately, either by leaving it vacant, by nominating someone with only a nominal connection to women's issues, or by treating the position as a formality rather than a substantive safeguard. The external member's role exists specifically to reduce the risk that an Internal Committee drawn entirely from the employer's own staff, however well-intentioned, ends up deciding a sensitive complaint without any perspective independent of the organisation's own internal hierarchy and incentives, and a Committee that proceeds with an inquiry while the external member position is vacant, or filled by someone who does not meet the Act's description, exposes its eventual findings to a real risk of challenge on that basis alone.

The complaint window: three months, extendable once, by a further three

Section 9 requires a complaint to be made within three months of the date of the incident, and where the harassment is a continuing pattern of conduct rather than a single incident, within three months of the date of the last incident in that pattern. This is not, however, a hard and absolute cutoff in every case. The same section gives the Internal or Local Committee discretion to extend the period for filing by a further period not exceeding three months, if it is satisfied that the circumstances were such that the complainant was prevented from filing the complaint within the original period, and the Committee is expected to record its reasons for granting or refusing that extension rather than simply accepting or rejecting a late complaint without explanation.

In practice, this gives most complaints a six-month outer limit from the relevant incident, but that six months is not an entitlement the complainant can simply claim; the further three months is available only where genuine circumstances, commonly illness, trauma, fear of retaliation, or other documented reasons, actually prevented earlier filing, and a Committee asked to extend the period should look for some basis for that explanation rather than extend automatically on request. Courts reviewing this area have not applied the limitation question with complete uniformity, with some rulings taking a stricter view that a complaint filed beyond even the extended period cannot be entertained at all, and at least one High Court taking the view that certain serious allegations should be assessed on their substance rather than dismissed purely on a timing technicality. Employers and Committees navigating a borderline case should treat the current position as genuinely contested rather than settled, and should document the Committee's own reasoning on the timing question carefully whichever way the decision goes, since that reasoning is exactly what an appellate body or a court is likely to examine most closely if the decision is challenged.

The inquiry itself: 90 days, and what happens at each handoff

Once a complaint is accepted, Section 11 directs the Internal or Local Committee to complete the inquiry within a period of 90 days. This is the figure most commonly cited in POSH compliance material, and it is also the figure most often missed in practice, generally not through any single dramatic failure but through a series of smaller delays, scheduling difficulties among Committee members who are themselves full-time employees with their own workloads, difficulty securing witness availability, and a natural reluctance to move quickly through evidence in a sensitive matter, that together push the inquiry past its notional deadline without any one point in the process being obviously at fault.

Once the inquiry concludes, the Committee's report must be submitted to the employer, and where relevant to the District Officer, within 10 days of completing the inquiry under Section 13. The report must record findings and, if the complaint is upheld, a recommendation on the action to be taken, which can range from disciplinary measures under the employer's own service rules to a recommendation on a specific sum to be deducted from the respondent's salary and paid to the aggrieved woman as compensation, calculated with reference to factors the Act itself lists, including the mental trauma suffered, the loss of career opportunity, and the respondent's income and financial status.

The employer then has 60 days from receipt of the report to act on its recommendations under Section 13(4), and an employer who receives a Committee's findings and simply does nothing with them for months is not merely administratively slow but is in direct breach of a specific statutory deadline that Section 26 separately penalises. A party aggrieved by the Committee's recommendations, or by the employer's manner of implementing them, has a further right of appeal under Section 18, to be filed within 90 days of the recommendations or of the employer's action on them, which gives this entire sequence, complaint, inquiry, report, implementation, appeal, a reasonably well-defined set of clocks that an employer should be actively tracking rather than treating the 90-day inquiry figure as the only deadline that matters.

Section 14: the narrow power over a false complaint, and why it is used sparingly

Section 14 gives the Internal or Local Committee a power to recommend action against a complainant where the Committee concludes, after inquiry, that the complaint was false or malicious, or that forged or misleading documents were produced to support it, but this power is drawn narrowly and is not meant to be triggered merely because a complaint was not upheld on the merits at the end of the inquiry. A complaint that fails for lack of sufficient evidence is a different outcome entirely from a complaint the Committee affirmatively finds to have been false or malicious, and the Act's own structure, requiring a specific finding to that effect rather than treating an unproven complaint as automatically malicious, is meant to avoid discouraging genuine complainants from coming forward for fear that an unsuccessful complaint will itself be punished.

Employers and Committee members should treat Section 14 as the exception rather than a routine closing step in every inquiry that does not result in a finding against the respondent, and should be able to point to specific evidence of bad faith or fabrication, rather than simply the absence of corroborating evidence for the original complaint, before recommending any action against the complainant under this provision.

The annual report: a nil return is still a return

Section 21 requires the Internal Committee, and separately the Local Committee, to prepare an annual report in the form the rules prescribe, and to submit it to the employer and, in the case of the Internal Committee, onward to the District Officer. A genuinely common misconception is that an organisation with no complaints in a given year has nothing to report; the obligation to file is not conditional on having received a complaint, and a nil report, confirming that the Committee existed, was properly constituted through the year, and received no complaints, is itself the compliant filing for a year with no incidents. Treating silence as compliance, rather than actually filing the nil report, is one of the more common and most easily avoidable compliance gaps employers create for themselves.

Section 22 separately requires the employer to include in its own annual report the number of cases filed and disposed of during the year, a requirement distinct from, though closely related to, the Internal Committee's own Section 21 filing, and relevant particularly to larger organisations that already prepare statutory annual reports or disclosures for other regulatory purposes and should ensure the POSH particulars are not simply overlooked in that broader reporting exercise.

Section 26: what actually carries a penalty, and what does not

Section 26 is the enforcement backbone of the entire Act, and it penalises a defined set of failures rather than every imaginable compliance shortfall. The failures it names include an employer's failure to constitute an Internal Committee as required under Section 4, failure to act on the recommendations made under Section 13, and contravention of or non-compliance with any other provision of the Act or rules made under it, which in practice has been read to extend to the Section 21 annual reporting obligation and, more broadly, to a range of other procedural requirements the Act and rules impose on the employer. Commentary consistently reports the penalty for a first contravention as a fine of up to fifty thousand rupees, with a second or subsequent contravention attracting a correspondingly higher penalty, and, under Section 26(2), the possibility that the relevant licensing or registering authority could cancel or refuse to renew the registration or licence required to carry on the business or activity, a considerably more serious consequence for a regulated business than the monetary fine itself.

What this provision does not do is create a general, free-standing right for an aggrieved employee to sue the employer for damages purely for a compliance failure disconnected from an actual complaint; the fine is a penalty enforced through the mechanism the Act and rules set out, not an independent civil cause of action available to any employee who notices the Committee has not been properly constituted. In practice, the more consequential exposure for most employers has turned out to be reputational and operational rather than the comparatively modest statutory fine itself: a finding, in litigation or in a regulatory inspection, that an organisation never properly constituted its Internal Committee tends to surface at the worst possible moment, typically in the middle of defending an actual harassment complaint, where the absence of a properly constituted Committee can itself undermine the employer's position on how that specific complaint was, or was not, handled.

The Supreme Court's continuing push: Aureliano Fernandes and what followed

The Supreme Court's judgment in Aureliano Fernandes v. State of Goa, delivered on 12 May 2023, began as a due-process challenge to a university's own internal inquiry, with the Court finding that the inquiry in question had been conducted in unseemly haste and without a fair hearing for the person accused. The case did not end with that finding. In subsequent orders in the same matter, the Court moved from the specific facts before it to a broader concern about how consistently the Act is actually being implemented across the country, directing a nationwide, district-wise survey intended to establish, concretely, how many public and private establishments have actually constituted a functioning Internal Committee, as opposed to simply having a policy document that references the Act.

The Court separately pressed state governments to activate their She-Box portals, the government's online mechanism for registering sexual harassment complaints, with later orders in 2025 continuing to press for compliance data to be collected, consolidated and placed before the Court's amicus. The specific procedural timeline of these later orders has been reported with some variation across different commentary, and employers should treat the overall direction, continuing and increasing judicial pressure toward verified, documented compliance rather than self-certified policy statements, as the more durable takeaway than any single reported deadline.

For an individual employer, the practical significance of this litigation is less about any specific obligation it creates beyond what the Act already required, and more about the signal it sends: regulators, courts and, increasingly, business counterparties conducting diligence before a transaction are paying closer attention to whether an organisation's POSH compliance is real and current rather than a policy drafted once and left unchanged for years. An employer whose Internal Committee has lapsed, whose external member position has sat vacant, or whose annual reports have not actually been filed, is now more likely than before to have that gap surface in a context considerably more awkward than a routine internal audit.

Smaller workplaces and the Local Committee in practice

An employer with fewer than ten employees often assumes, incorrectly, that falling below the Internal Committee threshold means the Act simply does not apply. It does apply; what changes is only the forum, with complaints from such workplaces going to the District Officer's Local Committee rather than to an internally constituted body the small employer would otherwise have to set up. A small employer's practical obligations are correspondingly lighter, generally limited to displaying the required notice about the Act and the relevant Local Committee's contact details, and cooperating with a Local Committee inquiry if a complaint is actually filed, rather than the fuller set of constitution, training and annual reporting obligations that fall on an employer large enough to require its own Internal Committee.

Employers that operate multiple small branches, each individually below the ten-employee threshold but collectively a much larger organisation, should not assume every branch automatically falls outside Internal Committee requirements simply because each site, taken alone, has fewer than ten employees; where the branches function as part of a single establishment for other regulatory purposes, the correct treatment of headcount across the organisation is worth confirming specifically rather than assumed from the smallest individual site's own numbers.

Training, display and the obligations that come before any complaint

Section 19 places a set of preventive obligations on the employer that operate independently of whether any complaint has ever been made, and these tend to be the obligations most casually treated, precisely because there is no inquiry clock or report deadline forcing attention to them. An employer must provide a safe working environment, display the penal consequences of sexual harassment and the Internal or Local Committee's composition and contact details conspicuously at the workplace, organise workshops and awareness programmes at regular intervals, and provide the Committee with whatever assistance it needs to secure the attendance of the respondent and witnesses during an inquiry.

A Committee that has never been trained on the inquiry procedure the Act and rules actually set out, on how to record evidence, maintain confidentiality, or structure a reasoned finding, is at real risk of conducting an inquiry that looks procedurally compliant on its face but falls apart on challenge for exactly the kind of fairness defect the Supreme Court identified in Aureliano Fernandes itself. Training is not a one-time induction exercise either; new members joining as earlier ones complete their three-year term need the same grounding the original Committee received, and an employer that trained its first Committee carefully in year one but has not repeated that exercise for subsequent members is, in substance, back to an untrained Committee without having noticed the drift.

Confidentiality during the inquiry is a distinct obligation under Section 16, which bars disclosure of the identity and details of the complainant, respondent, witnesses, and the inquiry's proceedings and recommendations, to the public, media or in any other manner, subject to limited exceptions the Act itself carves out. A breach of this confidentiality requirement is treated separately from the substantive inquiry and can itself expose the person responsible, whether a Committee member or someone else within the organisation who was not authorised to know the details, to consequences under the Act's own rules, independent of whatever the underlying complaint's outcome turns out to be.

Multiple locations, one organisation: keeping the structure consistent

An organisation with several offices across different cities faces a structural choice the Act does not spell out in granular detail: whether each qualifying office location needs its own standalone Internal Committee, or whether a central structure with location-specific points of contact can serve the same purpose. The more cautious and more commonly adopted approach, particularly once an organisation has more than a handful of offices, is to constitute a properly composed Committee at each location that independently meets the ten-employee threshold, sometimes supplemented by a central apex body that coordinates training, policy consistency and escalation across locations without itself displacing each location's own Committee as the body that actually receives and inquires into complaints arising there.

Whatever structure an organisation chooses, the test that eventually matters is not how the internal chart is drawn but whether a complainant at any given location has clear, accurate, displayed information about which Committee, with which members and which contact details, actually has jurisdiction over a complaint from that specific office, and whether that Committee is, in fact, properly constituted and current rather than a committee that exists mainly on an organisation chart maintained at head office. A rotating workforce, common in retail, logistics and hospitality establishments with high staff turnover, adds a further practical wrinkle: the headcount that determines whether a given location crosses the ten-employee threshold should be checked periodically rather than assumed fixed from whenever the business first opened that location, since a site that has grown or shrunk past that threshold changes which committee structure actually applies to it.

The POSH Act's compliance architecture is not, on its own terms, especially complicated: constitute a properly composed Committee, keep its membership current, meet the filing and inquiry deadlines the Act sets out, and submit the annual report whether or not there was a complaint to report. What makes it a recurring source of exposure is less the complexity of any individual obligation and more the tendency of each of these small, procedural requirements to drift out of date quietly, a three-year member term that lapses unnoticed, an external member position left vacant after someone resigns, an annual nil report that nobody remembers to actually file, until a complaint arrives and surfaces every one of those gaps at once.

Given the Supreme Court's continuing attention to actual implementation rather than paper compliance, an employer is better served treating this as a standing obligation to review periodically, membership terms, the external member position, and the annual filing, rather than a box ticked once when the Committee was first set up and then left untouched for years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do we need an Internal Committee if we have only eight employees?

No, not an Internal Committee specifically, since Section 4 applies to establishments with ten or more employees. A complaint from your workplace instead goes to the Local Committee constituted by the District Officer for your district under Section 6. You should still display the required statutory notice with the Local Committee's contact details, since the Act applies to your workplace even though the committee structure differs.

A complaint has been made against our managing director. Can our own Internal Committee inquire into it?

No. Section 6 specifically routes a complaint made against the employer, which generally includes a person in overall control or management such as a proprietor or managing director, to the Local Committee rather than to the organisation's own Internal Committee, regardless of how large the organisation otherwise is.

We had no complaints at all this year. Do we still need to file anything?

Yes. Section 21 requires the Internal Committee to prepare and submit an annual report every year, and a year with no complaints still requires a nil report confirming the Committee existed and received nothing. Not filing anything at all, on the assumption that silence is sufficient, is itself a compliance gap.

Our Internal Committee was set up four years ago and we haven't changed anyone since. Is that a problem?

Likely yes. Under Section 4(3), each member's term, including the Presiding Officer's, runs for a maximum of three years from nomination. A Committee not refreshed since it was first constituted four years ago is very likely operating with at least some members whose term has already lapsed, which can expose any inquiry conducted by that Committee to challenge on procedural grounds.

How long does a complainant have to file a complaint after an incident?

Ordinarily three months from the incident, or from the last incident where the harassment is a continuing pattern, under Section 9. The Committee has a limited discretion to extend this by a further three months, for reasons it must record in writing, where genuine circumstances prevented earlier filing, making six months the practical outer limit in most cases, though courts have not applied this uniformly in every reported decision.

What happens if our Internal Committee takes longer than 90 days to complete an inquiry?

The Act does not specify an automatic consequence for exceeding the 90-day period under Section 11, but a significant, unexplained delay weakens the employer's position if the inquiry or its outcome is later challenged, and repeated or systemic delay can itself be treated as non-compliance with the Act's procedural requirements, which Section 26 penalises in a general sense alongside the more specific failures it names.

Can the Committee punish an employee for filing a complaint that turned out not to be upheld?

Only in narrow circumstances. Section 14 allows the Committee to recommend action against a complainant specifically where it finds the complaint was false or malicious, or supported by forged or misleading documents, not simply because the complaint was not proven on the evidence by the end of the inquiry. An unproven complaint is not, by itself, a false or malicious one.

What is the actual penalty if we never got around to constituting an Internal Committee?

Section 26 provides a fine commonly reported at up to fifty thousand rupees for a first contravention, a higher penalty for repeat contravention, and the possibility that your business licence or registration could be cancelled or not renewed under Section 26(2). In practice, the bigger exposure tends to surface later, when the absence of a properly constituted Committee undermines your position while defending an actual harassment complaint.
