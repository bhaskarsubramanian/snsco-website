Non-Compete, Confidentiality, Notice and Training Bonds: What an Indian Employment Contract Can Actually Enforce

Section 27 of the Contract Act draws one clear line between employment and its end, and almost every real dispute sits on one side or the other of it.

By Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Most Indian employment contracts carry some version of the same four clauses: a restriction on working for a competitor, an obligation to keep the employer's information confidential, a notice period with pay in lieu, and, for some employees, a training bond that promises to recover costs if the person leaves too soon. Employers draft these as a single package of protection. Courts do not read them as one. Each clause is tested against a different rule, and the outcome for one tells you almost nothing about the outcome for another. A non-compete that binds cleanly during employment becomes void the day employment ends. A training bond stands or falls on whether the number in it reflects a real cost. A notice clause that looks identical to a non-compete clause in form is treated completely differently in substance. This piece sets out where each of these clauses actually lands, and why conflating them is the single most common reason both employers and employees misjudge their own position.

Key points

  • Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 voids any agreement restraining a person from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business, and Indian courts apply it with no built-in reasonableness test of the kind English and American courts use; a restraint is either inside Section 27's bar or outside it, not partly enforceable because it is narrowly drawn.
  • A restriction operating during the subsistence of the employment contract, including an exclusivity clause, a restriction on moonlighting, and the enforceability of garden leave within the notice period, falls outside Section 27 under the settled authority of Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning, because the restraint of trade doctrine does not apply while the contract of employment continues.
  • A restriction operating after employment ends, however reasonable in duration, geography or scope, is void and unenforceable under Section 27, a position the Supreme Court restated in Percept D'Mark (India) Pvt Ltd v. Zaheer Khan and that the Delhi High Court applied again in 2025 in the Varun Tyagi matter; the reasonableness of a post-termination non-compete is not a question Indian courts need to reach, because the clause is void regardless of how reasonable it is.
  • Confidentiality and non-solicitation obligations are conceptually distinct from a non-compete and generally survive termination, because protecting trade secrets or client relationships does not, on its own, stop a former employee from carrying on their profession or trade; a confidentiality clause drafted so broadly that it effectively bars the employee from using their own skill and experience risks being treated as a disguised non-compete and struck down on that basis instead.
  • Garden leave operated within a notice period, with full pay continuing, is generally treated as enforceable because the employment relationship has not yet ended; a garden leave clause drafted to extend beyond the notice period, or applied without continuing to pay the employee in full, moves into the same Section 27 difficulty that an ordinary post-termination non-compete faces.
  • A training bond recovering liquidated costs for early departure is assessed under Section 74 of the Contract Act, which caps recovery at a reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount named in the bond; a bond tied to a genuine, provable training expenditure and a proportionate, reducing figure over the bond period stands a materially better chance of enforcement than a flat, unexplained penalty figure.
  • Notice-pay disputes are generally a straightforward contractual question once the notice period and the pay-in-lieu formula are clear, but a workman covered by the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, now substantially replaced for most purposes by the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 once it is brought fully into force, can carry additional statutory protection around retrenchment notice that a contractual notice clause alone does not address.

One section, four clauses, and why the outcomes diverge

Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 is a short provision stating that every agreement by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind is, to that extent, void. It is the single most important sentence in this area of Indian employment law, and almost every dispute about non-compete clauses, garden leave, confidentiality obligations and even, indirectly, training bonds eventually comes back to whether the clause in question is or is not a restraint caught by that sentence.

What makes the law genuinely counter-intuitive to many employers, and to HR teams who have worked with contracts drafted on a foreign template, is that Section 27 does not ask whether a restraint is reasonable. English law, and American law in most states, test a non-compete for reasonableness of duration, geography and scope, striking down only restraints that go further than necessary to protect a legitimate interest. Indian courts, applying Section 27 as written, generally do not run that same reasonableness exercise for a post-employment restraint at all; the question is simply whether the clause restrains the person from carrying on their trade after the contract has ended, and if it does, it is void regardless of how narrow or generous its terms otherwise are. This single difference explains why non-compete clauses drafted by multinational legal teams, often reasonable by the standards of the jurisdiction they came from, routinely fail when tested against Indian law.

During employment: where restraints generally hold

The foundational authority on this point remains the Supreme Court's 1967 decision in Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning and Manufacturing Company, which drew the line that the rest of this area of law still follows: a restraint operating during the subsistence of a contract of employment, requiring the employee to serve the employer exclusively and not to work for a competitor while still employed, does not fall within the restraint of trade doctrine at all, because the person has not yet been restrained from exercising their trade generally, only from doing so for a competing employer while still bound by the current contract. An exclusivity obligation of this kind, including a bar on moonlighting for a competitor, a requirement to devote full working time to the employer, and a prohibition on holding a second job in the same industry while still employed, is accordingly treated as an ordinary, enforceable term of the contract rather than as a restraint of trade.

This distinction is also why injunctive relief is realistically available to an employer seeking to stop an employee moonlighting or working for a competitor while still under contract, in a way that is simply not available once the same employee has resigned and the employment has ended. Courts have been willing to grant interim injunctions restraining a current employee from breaching an exclusivity obligation, because doing so enforces the contract as it currently stands rather than extending its reach beyond the relationship's own natural end point.

The practical lesson for drafting is straightforward but frequently missed: an employer genuinely concerned about an employee competing while employed should rely on, and be prepared to enforce, the exclusivity and full-time-service obligations that already apply during employment, rather than assuming a post-termination non-compete clause will pick up where those obligations leave off. It generally will not.

After employment: why reasonableness does not save a non-compete

Once employment ends, the position reverses completely. The Supreme Court's decision in Percept D'Mark (India) Pvt Ltd v. Zaheer Khan reaffirmed that a restrictive covenant operating after the term of a contract has expired is a restraint of trade within Section 27 and, subject only to the limited statutory exceptions the section itself names, void. The employer in that case argued that the restraint should be enforced because it was reasonable in scope; the court's answer was, in substance, that reasonableness is not the test Indian law applies to a post-termination restraint, because Section 27 does not carve out an exception for reasonable restraints the way the common law in some other jurisdictions does.

The Delhi High Court's 2025 ruling in the Varun Tyagi matter applied the same settled position to a modern, fairly typical employment dispute, reaffirming that a post-termination non-compete and an accompanying non-solicitation restriction drawn broadly enough to function as a disguised non-compete do not survive Section 27 scrutiny merely because the employer can point to a legitimate business reason for wanting the restriction. Decisions of this kind are not a departure from Niranjan Shankar Golikari or Percept D'Mark; they are a continuing application of the same during-employment-versus-after-employment line that has held for several decades now, and employers who continue to insert broad post-termination non-compete clauses into Indian contracts, often because the clause is standard in the employer's other jurisdictions, should treat such a clause as largely unenforceable in India rather than as a genuine deterrent with real legal teeth.

None of this means a post-termination non-compete clause is pointless to include in every single case. A clause of this kind can still function as a practical deterrent for an employee who does not know, or has not checked, that it is unenforceable, and in some negotiated exits, particularly at senior levels where a separation agreement is being bargained with other consideration changing hands, a former employee may agree contractually to honour a restriction regardless of its underlying enforceability as a matter of ordinary contract law. What a well-advised employer should not do is rely on such a clause as the primary legal protection against a departing employee joining a competitor, since litigation to enforce it against a determined employee is very likely to fail on Section 27 grounds alone.

Confidentiality obligations: a genuinely different animal

A confidentiality obligation, properly drafted, protects the employer's trade secrets, client lists, pricing information, source code or other proprietary information from disclosure or use by a former employee, without restraining that employee from working in the same industry, for a competitor, or in any particular role. Because the obligation does not stop the person exercising their trade, only from using specific confidential information wrongfully taken from the previous employer, Indian courts have generally treated confidentiality clauses as falling outside Section 27 altogether and as enforceable after employment ends, in a way a non-compete simply is not.

The distinction breaks down, in practice, where a confidentiality clause is drafted so broadly that it captures not just genuinely confidential proprietary information but the employee's own accumulated skill, know-how and general professional experience gained during the employment, information the employee would reasonably be expected to carry into their next role regardless of which employer they work for. A clause that, in substance, prevents a former employee from using their own professional competence anywhere else functions as a disguised non-compete and risks being read, and struck down, as exactly that, whatever label the contract gives it. Drafting a confidentiality clause to focus specifically on defined categories of proprietary business information, rather than broadly on everything the employee learned or did during employment, is the practical way to keep the clause on the enforceable side of this line.

Non-solicitation obligations, restricting a former employee from soliciting the former employer's clients or poaching former colleagues, sit in a similar but not identical position to confidentiality clauses. Courts have shown somewhat more willingness to scrutinise non-solicitation clauses closely, particularly where they are drafted broadly enough to amount to a practical bar on operating in the same market, and the Varun Tyagi decision itself is notable for having examined a non-solicitation clause's limitations alongside the non-compete clause in the same contract, rather than treating non-solicitation as automatically enforceable in every case merely because it is not, in name, a non-compete.

Garden leave: enforceable inside the notice period, risky beyond it

Garden leave, under which an employer requires a resigning or terminated employee to stay away from work, and often from any competing engagement, during the notice period while continuing to pay full salary and benefits, has no dedicated statute in India and is governed by what the specific employment contract provides. Because the employment relationship has not actually ended during the notice period, the relevant authority treats a garden leave clause operating strictly within that period as closer to the during-employment exclusivity obligations discussed earlier than to a post-termination restraint, and such a clause has generally been upheld as enforceable where the employee continues to be paid in full throughout.

The difficulty arises where a garden leave clause is drafted, or applied in practice, to extend beyond the actual notice period, effectively functioning as an unpaid or underpaid non-compete dressed up in garden leave language. A clause of that kind moves back into Section 27 territory and faces the same enforceability problem any other post-termination restraint faces, regardless of the garden leave label attached to it. Employers using garden leave should keep the mechanism tightly tied to the notice period itself, continue full pay without interruption for its entire duration, and avoid any drafting that reads as extending the restriction past the date the notice period actually ends, since that is the detail most likely to be challenged if the employee later disputes the clause.

Notice pay: a contractual question, with a statutory layer underneath

For most employees, notice-period disputes are resolved by reading the contract: the number of days or months of notice required, and whether either side may instead pay a sum in lieu of serving that notice, usually calculated as the gross or basic salary that would have been earned during the unserved period. Where the contract is clear and the employer has either allowed the employee to serve the notice or paid the agreed sum in lieu, there is generally little room for a genuine dispute, and most notice-pay disagreements that do arise turn on calculation errors, disputed deductions, or disagreement about whether certain allowances count toward the pay-in-lieu figure, rather than on any deeper legal question.

A separate layer of protection exists for employees who qualify as 'workmen' under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, where a retrenchment, as opposed to an ordinary resignation, triggers specific statutory notice and compensation requirements under that Act independent of whatever the individual contract says, and an employer cannot contract out of those statutory minimums merely by including a shorter contractual notice period. The Industrial Disputes Act is being substantially replaced by the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 as part of the broader consolidation of India's labour codes, and employers and employees alike should check the current state of implementation and transition provisions applicable to the specific establishment and state before assuming either the old Act or the new Code governs a particular retrenchment notice question, since the transition has not been uniform across every state and sector.

Where an employee resigns voluntarily rather than being retrenched, this additional statutory layer generally has far less to say, and the dispute, if any, returns to the ordinary contractual notice-pay question described above. The distinction between a genuine voluntary resignation and a resignation effectively forced by the employer's conduct, sometimes litigated as a constructive termination, is where this area occasionally gets contested in practice, though that is a separate and more fact-specific inquiry than the notice-pay calculation itself.

A probationer is often treated differently under the contract itself, with a shorter notice period, commonly between one week and one month, applying during probation and the longer, confirmed-employee notice period taking effect only once probation ends or confirmation is recorded. Disputes in this area tend to arise where an employer treats an employee as still on probation, and therefore subject to the shorter notice, well beyond any date the contract itself fixed for automatic confirmation, or where the contract is silent on what happens if probation is neither extended nor confirmed within its stated period; absent a clear contractual mechanism addressing that gap, the safer reading generally favours treating the employee as confirmed once the stated probation period has run its course without an express extension.

Training bonds: Section 74, not Section 27

A training bond, under which an employee who leaves before completing a specified minimum service period after receiving employer-funded training must pay a defined sum, raises a different question from a non-compete clause and is generally not analysed under Section 27 at all, since the bond does not restrain the employee from working anywhere; it simply requires payment of a sum on early departure. The relevant provision is instead Section 74 of the Contract Act, which governs liquidated damages and compensation for breach generally, and which caps what the employer can actually recover at a reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount named in the contract, whether or not that named amount is itself described as a penalty.

In practice, this means the figure named in the bond operates as a ceiling, not as an automatic entitlement. Courts examining training bond disputes have looked at whether the amount reflects something close to a genuine pre-estimate of the employer's actual training expenditure, proportionate to the remaining unserved period of the bond, rather than an arbitrary round figure untethered to any real cost the employer can point to. A bond that ties the recoverable amount to documented training costs, reduces proportionately for each month of the bond period actually served, and is applied consistently rather than selectively against departing employees, stands a materially better chance of being enforced at or close to the stated figure than a flat penalty figure the employer cannot substantiate with any record of actual expenditure.

Employers relying on training bonds should accordingly keep a clear record of what was actually spent on the training in question, invoices, course fees, salary paid during a training-only period, and any other documented cost, rather than treating the bond figure as something that can simply be asserted and recovered on the strength of the signed document alone. An employee disputing a training bond demand has a correspondingly real argument where the employer cannot show the figure bears any relationship to an actual cost, and that argument has succeeded in reported decisions where the employer's claimed figure looked more like a deterrent penalty than a considered pre-estimate of loss.

A training bond that also includes a post-termination non-compete style restriction, rather than a straightforward payment obligation, reintroduces the Section 27 problem discussed earlier for that separate restriction, even if the payment obligation itself survives scrutiny under Section 74. The two should be analysed separately rather than assuming that because a payment-based training bond is generally enforceable, any additional restraint bundled into the same clause is equally safe.

Injunctions: available during employment, rarely available after it

An employer seeking an injunction to actually stop a departing or departed employee from doing something, rather than simply seeking damages after the fact, faces a sharply different prospect depending on timing. During employment, an injunction restraining breach of an exclusivity obligation or a garden leave clause within the notice period is a realistic remedy, consistent with the enforceability of such clauses discussed above, and interim relief of this kind has been granted in reported decisions where the employer could show a clear, ongoing breach.

An injunction to stop a former employee from working for a competitor after employment has ended runs directly into the Section 27 difficulty, since restraining the person from working for the competitor is, in substance, enforcing the very restraint the law treats as void; an employer cannot generally obtain through an injunction what it could not obtain through a straightforward enforcement action on the underlying clause. An injunction restraining a former employee from using or disclosing specific confidential information, by contrast, remains realistically available, since that relief does not restrain the person's trade generally, only the wrongful use of particular proprietary information, which brings the analysis back to the same during-employment-versus-confidentiality distinction that runs through this entire area.

Where these disputes actually get litigated

A growing number of Indian employment contracts, particularly at senior and mid-management levels in technology, finance and professional services, carry an arbitration clause covering disputes arising from the contract, including disputes over a non-compete, confidentiality breach or training bond. Whether an arbitration clause of this kind can actually be used to resolve a restrictive-covenant dispute, as opposed to an ordinary commercial dispute, has generated its own body of commentary, and the safer working assumption for both employers and employees is that an arbitration clause in an employment contract does not automatically oust a court's jurisdiction to grant urgent interim relief, particularly an injunction, even where the underlying dispute is otherwise arbitrable.

In practice, an employer seeking to stop an ongoing breach, for instance a current employee working for a competitor in violation of an exclusivity clause, generally moves first for interim relief under Order XXXIX of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, or under Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 where arbitration has been invoked, rather than waiting for a final arbitral award on the merits, since the practical value of stopping the breach lies almost entirely in acting quickly rather than in the eventual final outcome months or years later. A confidentiality breach involving misuse of electronic data or trade secrets can separately attract liability under Section 72 of the Information Technology Act, 2000, which penalises unauthorised disclosure of information obtained while providing services under a contract, giving an employer a statutory remedy that sits alongside, rather than instead of, the contractual confidentiality clause itself.

None of this changes the underlying Section 27 analysis; choosing arbitration as the forum, or seeking relief under the IT Act rather than purely under contract, does not make an otherwise void post-termination non-compete enforceable. What forum and procedural choices actually affect is how quickly a genuine, enforceable claim, on confidentiality, on a during-employment exclusivity breach, or on a training bond payment, can be brought to a head, which is frequently the more important practical question once a dispute has actually arisen.

Drafting with the actual line in mind

Employers drafting or reviewing Indian employment contracts get more practical protection from investing care in the clauses that actually survive scrutiny than from a long, aggressively worded non-compete clause that reads impressively but is unlikely to be enforced if ever tested. A tightly defined confidentiality clause covering specific categories of proprietary information, a properly scoped non-solicitation clause aimed at genuine client relationships rather than at the industry generally, a garden leave mechanism kept strictly within the notice period with uninterrupted full pay, and a training bond tied to documented, provable costs, together do considerably more real work than a sweeping post-termination non-compete that a court is very likely to strike down the first time it is actually litigated.

Employees reviewing a contract before signing, or facing a demand after resignation, benefit from the same clarity in reverse. A post-termination non-compete clause in an Indian employment contract is, on the weight of current authority, very unlikely to be enforced regardless of how it is worded, which is useful to know but is not, on its own, a reason to disregard a training bond demand that is genuinely tied to real training costs, or a confidentiality obligation that is narrowly and properly drafted around specific proprietary information rather than the employee's general professional experience. Each clause deserves its own assessment against its own governing rule, not a single verdict borrowed from whichever clause happens to be the most visible in the contract.

The pattern running through all four clauses discussed here is that Indian law protects the employment relationship as it stands, and the employer's legitimate interests within that relationship, far more readily than it protects an employer's interest in restricting what a person does once that relationship has ended. Exclusivity during employment, garden leave within notice, confidentiality narrowly drawn, and a training bond tied to real cost all tend to hold up. A broad post-termination non-compete, by contrast, almost never does, whatever its wording and however reasonable it might look on paper.

Where this piece has been more cautious, on the exact current transition between the Industrial Disputes Act and the Industrial Relations Code for retrenchment notice, and on how far non-solicitation clauses can be drawn before they are treated as disguised non-competes, that caution reflects genuine, continuing uncertainty in how courts are applying settled principles to newer fact patterns, not an attempt to avoid a clear answer, and either question is worth specific advice on the facts of a particular contract rather than a general rule applied mechanically.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my employer stop me from joining a competitor after I resign, using the non-compete clause in my contract?

In most cases, no. A restraint operating after employment has ended is void under Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, following the settled position in Percept D'Mark v. Zaheer Khan and reaffirmed by the Delhi High Court in 2025 in the Varun Tyagi matter. Indian courts generally do not ask whether the restriction is reasonable; a post-termination non-compete is void regardless of how narrowly it is drafted.

Is a non-compete clause enforceable while I am still employed?

Yes, generally. A restraint operating during the subsistence of employment, such as a bar on working for a competitor or taking a second job in the same industry while still employed, falls outside Section 27 under Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning, because the restraint of trade doctrine applies only once the contract has ended, not while it continues.

My employment contract has a confidentiality clause with no time limit. Does that expire when I leave?

Not necessarily, and that is the point: a properly drawn confidentiality clause protecting genuine trade secrets and proprietary information generally survives termination and is not treated as a restraint of trade, because it does not stop you from working in your field, only from misusing specific confidential information. It becomes vulnerable only if it is drafted so broadly that it effectively bars you from using your own general skill and experience.

Can my employer put me on garden leave and stop paying me, or extend it past my notice period?

Garden leave is generally enforceable only within the notice period and only while full pay continues. A garden leave arrangement that extends beyond the notice period, or that is not fully paid, loses the protection that comes from the employment relationship still being active, and risks being challenged on the same Section 27 grounds as an ordinary post-termination non-compete.

I signed a training bond for a fixed amount and I want to resign early. Do I actually owe the full amount?

The amount named in the bond is a ceiling, not an automatic entitlement, under Section 74 of the Contract Act. What the employer can actually recover depends on whether the figure reflects a genuine, documented training cost rather than an arbitrary penalty, and courts have reduced or disallowed bond amounts the employer could not substantiate with real expenditure records.

Can my employer get an injunction to stop me working for a competitor after I have already left?

This is difficult under current Indian law, because restraining you from working for a competitor after employment has ended is, in substance, enforcing a Section 27 restraint that is void. An injunction is more realistically available to stop you using or disclosing specific confidential information you took from the employer, which is a different and narrower kind of relief.

Does a non-solicitation clause get treated the same way as a non-compete clause after I leave?

Not automatically, but courts have scrutinised broadly drawn non-solicitation clauses closely, and a clause that functions, in substance, as a bar on operating in the same market rather than a narrow restriction on actively poaching specific former clients risks being treated the same way a post-termination non-compete is treated. The 2025 Varun Tyagi decision examined exactly this kind of overlap.

I am being asked to serve a longer notice period than my contract states, or pay more in lieu. Is that allowed?

The contract's notice terms generally govern, so check what you actually signed; an employer cannot unilaterally lengthen the notice period or increase the pay-in-lieu figure beyond what the contract provides. If you qualify as a 'workman' under the applicable industrial law and the situation is a retrenchment rather than a resignation, a separate statutory notice and compensation framework may apply independently of the contract.
