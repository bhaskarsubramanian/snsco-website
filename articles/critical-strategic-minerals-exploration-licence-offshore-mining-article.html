Critical and Strategic Minerals in India: Exploration Licences, Offshore Mining Auctions and the 2023 Reform

What the new exploration licence, the Section 11D auction power and the offshore mining regime actually allow, and where an investor or an existing leaseholder can still run into a dispute

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 25 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

India rewrote its mining law twice in August 2023 to chase lithium, cobalt, rare earths and two dozen other critical minerals. Two years on, the exploration licence, the central auction power and the offshore mining regime have each produced their first auctions, their first disputes and their first open questions for anyone looking to invest.

Key points

  • The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2023 inserted Section 10BA, creating a new exploration licence for the roughly 29 minerals listed in a new Seventh Schedule, granted by State Governments through e-auction for five years, extendable by up to two more.
  • Section 11D lets the Central Government itself select the preferred bidder for a mining lease or composite licence over the 24 critical and strategic minerals listed in Part D of the First Schedule; the State Government then issues the grant to that bidder.
  • A separate 2023 amendment to the Offshore Areas Mineral (Development and Regulation) Act moved offshore allocation to auction, scrapped lease renewal in favour of a flat 50-year production lease, and created a trust funded by up to one-third of royalty for exploration and environmental work.
  • The first offshore tranche of 13 blocks, seven of them off Great Nicobar Island, went up for bidding in November 2024; reporting into 2025 describes resistance from Kerala's fishing community and delay, and the final allocation is not yet confirmed in reliable reporting.
  • FDI into mining and exploration of metal and non-metal ores sits at 100% on the automatic route, with titanium-bearing minerals carved out to the government route and atomic minerals barred outright; Press Note 3's land-border screening still applies on the side.
  • The National Critical Mineral Mission, approved by the Cabinet in January 2025 with an outlay of roughly Rs 34,300 crore over seven years, funds exploration, processing and the acquisition of mineral assets abroad rather than changing the grant process itself.
  • A Section 30 revision to the Central Government remains the usual first step for a leaseholder or bidder aggrieved by a State order, and at least two lines of litigation, over auction-rule vires and over lease-lapse declarations, have already tested that route.
  • Businesses evaluating entry should treat the exploration licence, the mining lease and the composite licence as three distinct instruments with different durations, different auction routes and different post-grant obligations, not as interchangeable labels for the same thing.

Why the law changed twice in one August

For decades, a company that wanted to look for minerals in India without yet knowing whether they existed in commercial quantity had an awkward choice: apply for a reconnaissance permit, which gave almost no certainty of a later lease, or wait for a State Government to notify a block for the regular mining lease auction under rules framed after the 2015 overhaul. Neither route was built for lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite or the rare earth elements that electric vehicles, batteries and defence manufacturing now depend on, because very little prospecting work had been done on them and no State had a ready block to auction in the first place.

Parliament answered this in the same week in August 2023 with two separate statutes. The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2023 reworked the domestic law that has governed onshore mining since 1957, inserting a new category of grant and a new route for the Centre to auction critical minerals directly. The Offshore Areas Mineral (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2023 did the parallel job for the seabed in India's territorial waters, continental shelf and exclusive economic zone, where no mineral had ever been commercially mined before.

Read together, the two Acts and the National Critical Mineral Mission that followed in January 2025 form the current architecture, and a business coming to any part of it needs to know which piece it is actually dealing with. A mistake made at this stage, treating an exploration licence as though it were a mining lease, or assuming the State Government controls an auction that the Centre in fact runs, tends to surface later as a dispute over who had authority to decide what, which is avoidable if the structure is understood from the outset.

The exploration licence: Section 10BA's new instrument

Section 10BA, inserted after the existing Section 10B, creates the exploration licence as a grant distinct from both the old reconnaissance permit and the mining lease. It covers only the minerals listed in a newly inserted Seventh Schedule, a list of roughly 29 minerals running from diamond and gold to copper, lead, zinc and the rare earth group. A State Government grants the licence, but not by discretion or first-come application; the statute requires it to be granted through auction, by competitive bidding including e-auction, once the Centre has notified the area and the mineral.

The term is five years from the date of execution, with a possible extension of up to two further years, a structure meant to give an explorer long enough to establish whether a deposit is worth developing before anyone has to commit to the far larger investment of a mining lease. The licence carries conditions on the pace and reporting of exploration work, and a holder who sits on the area without genuine prospecting activity risks losing the benefit of the extension, though the statute leaves the State Government with discretion on whether to grant it.

What the exploration licence does not do is convert automatically into a lease. The holder who proves a deposit still has to compete, or in the case of the 24 critical and strategic minerals, be selected through the separate Section 11D process, for the right to actually extract it. A business evaluating an exploration licence opportunity should treat the prospecting phase and the extraction phase as two distinct competitive processes, because the statute keeps them that way even where the same company ends up winning both, and even a successful explorer with promising results carries no guarantee of being the eventual lessee.

Section 11D: the Centre's own auction power over the 24 critical minerals

Where the exploration licence sits with the State, Section 11D hands the Central Government a direct role it did not previously have in granting a mining lease or composite licence. For the 24 minerals listed in Part D of the First Schedule, a list that includes lithium, graphite, potash, beryl and beryllium-bearing minerals, tungsten-bearing minerals and zirconium-bearing minerals among others, the Centre itself conducts the auction and selects the preferred bidder. The State Government's role is narrowed to executing the grant in favour of whoever the Centre has selected, rather than running its own competitive process.

This matters for anyone structuring a bid, because the usual division of labour between Centre and State on mining questions does not hold for these 24 minerals. A dispute over the auction's conduct, the eligibility criteria, or the selection of the preferred bidder is a dispute with the Central Government's process even though the paperwork that follows is issued by a State mining department. Any representation or clarification sought during the bidding stage should therefore go to the Ministry of Mines, and any legal challenge to the auction itself needs to be framed against the Centre, not the State that later signs the lease deed.

The practical effect is a two-step relationship for a successful bidder: a Central decision on who wins, followed by a State-executed grant that then governs the day-to-day relationship, royalty payment, compliance reporting, mine plan approval, for the life of the lease. A bidder should read the Central auction conditions and the State's standard lease terms as two linked but separately enforceable documents, because a grievance that arises later during operation may have to be raised with the State even though the original selection decision sat with the Centre.

Offshore mining: the same logic, a different seabed

The Offshore Areas Mineral (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2023 took the auction principle and applied it to a space the 1957 Act had never reached: the territorial waters, the continental shelf, the exclusive economic zone and other maritime zones around India. Before this amendment, the governing 2002 Act allowed production leases to be renewed; the 2023 amendment removed that renewal provision altogether and replaced it with a single, non-renewable 50-year production lease term, aligned to the term available under the onshore MMDR Act.

The amendment also created a trust, funded by a levy of up to one-third of the royalty collected on offshore production, earmarked for exploration, research, environmental mitigation and disaster relief connected to offshore mining. This is a meaningful departure from the onshore regime, where no equivalent ring-fenced fund exists at the central level, and it signals that the drafters expected offshore mining to carry environmental and research costs that an ordinary royalty stream would not otherwise cover.

Implementing rules, the Offshore Areas Mineral (Auction) Rules, 2024, followed a 2023 consultation draft and were finalised in 2024, broadly mirroring the onshore Mineral (Auction) Rules, 2015, including a provision that lets an auction proceed to its next stage even if only one bidder remains technically qualified after the first round. Composite licences covering both exploration and production are the typical form of offshore grant, rather than a separate exploration-then-lease sequence, which shortens the path to extraction compared with the onshore route but also means an offshore bidder commits capital earlier, before the exploration phase has told it as much about the deposit as an onshore explorer would know by the time it bids for a lease.

The first offshore tranche and what it has shown so far

The Ministry of Mines launched the first tranche of offshore blocks for auction in late November 2024: 13 blocks spanning the Arabian Sea and the Andaman Sea, split across three lime mud blocks, three construction sand blocks, and seven polymetallic nodule and crust blocks, seven of the thirteen located off Great Nicobar Island. It was, on the government's own description, the first time operating rights in Indian offshore waters had ever been put up for competitive auction, and the mix of blocks, construction material close to shore alongside polymetallic nodules in deeper water, shows the government testing both a near-term commercial use and a longer-term strategic one in the same tranche.

Reporting into 2025 suggests the process has not been straightforward. A Mongabay India report from March 2025 describes resistance from fishing communities in Kerala, who argued the blocks would affect their livelihoods, and points to delays in the process. Objections of this kind are not unique to offshore mining, the Coastal Regulation Zone framework generates similar friction for ports and power projects, but they take on added weight offshore because fishing rights and marine ecology sit directly on top of the same water column the mining blocks occupy.

We have not found reliable reporting confirming the final allocation of these 13 blocks, the identity of successful bidders, or the bid values, and a business tracking this space should treat the tranche as a live, unresolved process rather than a completed one until the Ministry publishes confirmed results. A second or third tranche, if and when notified, is likely to draw on whatever lessons the Ministry takes from the objections and delays reported around the first round, including possibly tighter consultation requirements before blocks near sensitive coastlines are offered.

The National Critical Mineral Mission: money, not a new grant route

The Union Cabinet approved the National Critical Mineral Mission on 29 January 2025, with a total outlay reported at roughly Rs 34,300 crore over seven years, split between about Rs 16,300 crore in direct government expenditure and an expected further Rs 18,000 crore of investment by public sector undertakings. The Mission had first been flagged in the July 2024 Union Budget, and the 2025-26 Budget added a basic customs duty exemption on 15 further critical minerals on top of the 25 identified the previous year.

The Mission funds exploration by the Geological Survey of India, which reportedly took up around 195 exploration projects for critical and strategic minerals in 2024-25 alone, supports downstream processing and recycling capacity that India currently lacks for several of these minerals, and backs Indian companies acquiring mineral assets abroad, with publicly stated targets around 1,200 domestic exploration projects by 2030-31 and roughly 50 overseas mining assets for Indian firms.

None of this changes how a licence or lease is actually granted; the Mission is a funding and coordination vehicle layered on top of the Section 10BA and Section 11D grant mechanisms described above, and a business should not expect Mission funding to substitute for winning an auction. Where the Mission is relevant to a private investor is indirectly, through the processing and recycling incentives it may make available once a company has already secured the raw mineral, and through the exploration data the Geological Survey generates, some of which becomes public and can narrow the guesswork before a company commits to bidding for an exploration licence of its own.

Getting into the sector as a foreign investor

India's foreign investment policy, in force since October 2020, permits 100% foreign direct investment under the automatic route for mining and exploration of metal and non-metal ores, meaning no prior government approval is needed purely on account of the activity being mining. Two carve-outs sit on top of that general permission. Titanium-bearing minerals and ores are taken out of the automatic route and placed under a 100% government-approval route instead, and mining of substances prescribed by the Department of Atomic Energy remains barred to foreign investment altogether.

A separate, cross-cutting restriction then applies regardless of sector. Press Note 3 of 2020 requires prior government approval for any investment, direct or through a beneficial owner, from an entity based in a country that shares a land border with India, a category that in practice has meant China, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Bhutan, Nepal, Myanmar and Afghanistan. This has already been tested in commercial coal mining, where the Coal Ministry clarified that the sector's 100% automatic-route status does not exempt an investor from a bordering country, who still needs approval under Press Note 3 on top of the coal-specific rules.

Some 2026 reporting describes a Cabinet move to allow non-controlling beneficial ownership of up to 10% from land-border countries through the automatic route, but other reporting says the position has not changed, and we have not been able to confirm from reliable sources whether any such relaxation extends to mining. A foreign investor from a land-border jurisdiction should assume Press Note 3 approval is still required until the position is confirmed against a current DPIIT notification, and should build the time such approval can take into any bid timetable rather than assume the mining sector's automatic-route status is enough on its own.

Where this still ends up in court

A party unhappy with a State Government's order under the MMDR Act, whether that is a refusal to grant, a declaration that a lease has lapsed, or a decision taken in the course of an auction, has a statutory revision to the Central Government under Section 30 as its ordinary first recourse. Delhi High Court records show this route being used in both directions: lessees have filed Section 30 revisions against orders declaring a lease lapsed under Section 4A(4), and parties have challenged a common Central Government order rejecting multiple Section 30 revision petitions together.

The auction framework itself has already been challenged on constitutional grounds. In litigation before the Rajasthan High Court, Hindustan Zinc questioned the vires of the amended Section 11 and the Mineral (Auction) Rules, 2015, after having first pursued a Section 30 revision against the order it was aggrieved by. A CAG audit report separately confirms the underlying shift the litigation was reacting to: since the 2015 amendment, a mining lease can only be granted through auction, removing the discretionary allotment route that had existed earlier and that had itself been the subject of long-running disputes.

That pattern, exhaust the Section 30 revision, then move to a writ petition if the statutory remedy does not resolve the grievance, is likely to repeat itself as the newer Section 10BA and Section 11D processes generate their own first disputes, simply because no reported decision yet exists testing either provision directly. A company planning to litigate an adverse decision under the new provisions should expect to start with the Section 30 revision rather than go straight to a High Court, since a writ petition filed without first exhausting that remedy risks being thrown out on maintainability grounds alone, independent of how strong the underlying grievance is.

A practical way to read the three instruments

Confusion between an exploration licence, a mining lease and a composite licence is an easy mistake to make, because all three now involve an auction and all three can apply to the same deposit at different stages of its life. The exploration licence under Section 10BA is a time-limited right to prospect, for five years with up to a two-year extension, over Seventh Schedule minerals, granted by the State. It does not carry extraction rights. A mining lease or composite licence, by contrast, carries extraction rights from the start and, for the 24 Part D minerals, is auctioned by the Centre under Section 11D rather than the State.

Offshore, the composite licence combining exploration and production is the norm, running for up to 50 years without a renewal option, which collapses the onshore sequence of a separate exploration stage followed by a separate lease auction into a single grant. This is not simply a drafting shortcut; it reflects the practical reality that offshore prospecting is expensive enough that splitting exploration and production into two competitive processes would likely deter bidders altogether.

A business planning to bid should map its target mineral against both Schedules before assuming which process applies. A mineral on the Seventh Schedule but not on Part D of the First Schedule will typically follow the State-run exploration licence route and the ordinary mining lease auction that follows it. A mineral on Part D, the smaller and more strategically sensitive list, pulls the grant of the lease itself into the Centre's hands under Section 11D, changing who to approach for clarifications, who the auction conditions bind, and who a legal challenge should actually name if the process goes wrong.

A further wrinkle worth tracking is that a single deposit can sometimes contain more than one listed mineral, a lithium-bearing pegmatite that also carries beryl, for instance, raising the question of which Schedule and which authority governs the grant when the minerals present do not sort neatly into one category. The statute does not spell out a tie-breaking rule for this situation, and until a reported decision or a departmental clarification addresses it, a bidder facing a mixed deposit should raise the question with the Ministry of Mines in writing before the auction closes rather than assume the more favourable of the two routes will automatically apply.

The environmental clearance layer runs alongside, not instead of, the mining process

Winning an auction, onshore or offshore, is only the mining-law half of what a project needs. Environmental clearance under the EIA Notification, 2006 applies to mining projects above specified capacity thresholds, and for offshore blocks the project is also likely to intersect with Coastal Regulation Zone restrictions, since much offshore and near-shore mineral activity sits within or close to the zones the CRZ Notification, 2019 regulates. These are separate statutory processes from the mining auction itself, run by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change and the State Coastal Zone Management Authorities rather than the Ministry of Mines.

A bidder who wins an auction without having mapped these parallel clearances against the auction's own timelines risks a gap between winning the right to mine and actually being allowed to start, since a mining lease deed does not substitute for an environmental clearance and the two processes can run on different, uncoordinated schedules. The friction reported around the first offshore tranche, resistance from fishing communities and delay, is as much an environmental and consultation problem as a mining-law one, and a prudent bidder treats the two tracks as needing separate legal and technical teams rather than assuming the mining auction process will carry the environmental approval along with it.

What a prospective investor or existing leaseholder should check now

Before committing to a bid, confirm which Schedule the target mineral sits on, since that single fact determines whether the State or the Centre controls the lease-grant process. Check the specific auction notice for eligibility conditions, since technical and financial qualification thresholds have varied across tranches and are not fixed by the statute itself. For an offshore block, build in the environmental and coastal clearance timeline separately from the mining clearance, because the Coastal Regulation Zone framework and the Ministry of Environment's clearance process run alongside, not instead of, the mining auction.

An existing leaseholder facing a show-cause notice, a lapse declaration or an adverse auction-related order should calendar the Section 30 revision period from the date of the order, not from when the order is actually received or understood, since delay in filing has defeated revisions in reported cases. A foreign investor should separately confirm, before signing any bid document, whether its ultimate beneficial ownership touches a land-border country within the meaning of Press Note 3, since that status can require government approval even where the mining activity itself sits on the automatic route.

Finally, a company already holding an exploration licence should track the extension window closely. Because the extension beyond the initial five years is discretionary rather than automatic, waiting until close to expiry to apply, or assuming continued exploration activity on the ground will be read favourably without a formal application on record, leaves the decision entirely to the State Government's view of the file rather than to any entitlement the statute itself creates.

Two years after the August 2023 amendments, the exploration licence and the Section 11D auction power have moved from statute book to functioning, if still thin, practice: licences have been notified, an offshore tranche has been launched, and the Mission has put real budget behind the policy. What has not yet happened, on the evidence available as on 25 November 2026, is a settled body of reported decisions testing Section 10BA or Section 11D the way Section 30 revisions and auction-vires challenges tested the 2015 reform.

That gap is exactly where an investor or an existing leaseholder needs care rather than confidence. Relying on how the earlier, better-litigated mining lease regime worked will get a party most of the way, but the newer instruments carry their own schedules, their own timelines and their own allocation of authority between Centre and State, and treating them as identical to what came before is where avoidable disputes tend to start.

The sector is still young enough that a single reported decision interpreting Section 10BA or Section 11D would likely reshape how cautious the rest of this guidance needs to be, and anyone with a live bid, lease or dispute in this space should keep an eye on that first wave of litigation as it emerges rather than treat the position described here as fixed for good.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an exploration licence under Section 10BA, and how is it different from a mining lease?

An exploration licence is a time-limited right, five years with a possible two-year extension, to carry out reconnaissance and prospecting for a mineral listed in the Seventh Schedule to the MMDR Act. It is granted by the State Government through auction and does not itself carry the right to extract and sell the mineral; a separate mining lease or composite licence is still needed for that.

Which minerals fall under the Centre's Section 11D auction power?

Section 11D applies to the 24 minerals listed as critical and strategic minerals in Part D of the First Schedule to the MMDR Act, a list that includes lithium, graphite, potash, beryl and beryllium-bearing minerals, tungsten-bearing minerals and several others. For these minerals, the Central Government, not the State, selects the preferred bidder for the mining lease or composite licence.

Can a foreign company get 100% FDI into mineral exploration in India?

Yes, in general. The FDI policy in force since October 2020 allows 100% foreign investment under the automatic route for mining and exploration of metal and non-metal ores. Titanium-bearing minerals require government-route approval instead, and mining of substances prescribed by the Department of Atomic Energy is not open to foreign investment at all.

Does Press Note 3 apply to a mining investment even though mining is on the automatic route?

It can. Press Note 3 of 2020 requires prior government approval for investment, direct or through a beneficial owner, from an entity in a country sharing a land border with India, regardless of which sector the investment is otherwise permitted in. The Coal Ministry has specifically clarified that this applies to commercial coal mining despite its automatic-route status, and the same logic is likely to extend to other mining activity until a specific exemption is shown.

Has India actually auctioned any offshore mineral blocks yet?

The Ministry of Mines launched the first tranche of 13 offshore blocks for auction in late November 2024, covering the Arabian Sea and Andaman Sea, including several blocks off Great Nicobar Island. Reporting into 2025 describes resistance from coastal fishing communities and delay in the process, and we have not found reliable reporting confirming the final allocation or the identity of successful bidders.

What is the National Critical Mineral Mission, and does it change how leases are granted?

The Mission, approved by the Union Cabinet in January 2025 with an outlay reported at roughly Rs 34,300 crore over seven years, funds exploration by the Geological Survey of India, processing and recycling capacity, and the acquisition of mineral assets abroad by Indian companies. It does not change the grant process itself; licences and leases are still granted through the Section 10BA and Section 11D auction mechanisms.

What can a company do if a State Government rejects its mining lease application or declares its lease lapsed?

The ordinary first remedy is a revision petition to the Central Government under Section 30 of the MMDR Act, filed within the limitation period that runs from the date of the order. If the revision does not resolve the grievance, or if the challenge goes to the validity of the auction rules themselves rather than to a particular order, a writ petition before the jurisdictional High Court is the next step, as has happened in reported litigation over the 2015 auction rules.

How long does an offshore production lease run, and can it be renewed?

Under the 2023 amendment to the Offshore Areas Mineral (Development and Regulation) Act, an offshore production lease runs for 50 years, aligned with the term under the onshore MMDR Act, and the earlier provision allowing renewal has been removed. A lessee needs to plan the full extraction and investment timeline within that fixed, non-renewable period.
