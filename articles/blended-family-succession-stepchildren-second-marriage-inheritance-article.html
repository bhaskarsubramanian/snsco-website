Blended Families and Succession in India: What a Step-Child or a Second Marriage's Children Can Inherit

Why a step-child has no statutory share at all, why a child of a void or bigamous marriage is treated differently after the Supreme Court's 2023 ruling, and what a will actually needs to say to avoid a dispute between half-siblings later

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 24 November 2026 · Law stated as on 23 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family with children from more than one marriage, a step-parent, or a relationship the family itself is not entirely sure was ever legally valid, usually discovers the gaps in Hindu succession law only after someone has died intestate. The statute draws sharper lines than most families expect between a child born of a marriage later found void, a child legally adopted into a new household, and a step-child who was simply raised as one but never formally brought within the family in law. Each sits in a different place, and the 2023 Supreme Court ruling on children of void and voidable marriages moved one of those lines without touching the others.

Key points

  • A step-child, meaning a spouse's child from an earlier relationship with whom the new spouse shares no blood tie and who has not been legally adopted, has no statutory right to inherit from the step-parent under the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, and can take a share only if named as a beneficiary in a will or formally adopted under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956.
  • A marriage entered into while an earlier marriage of either party subsists is void under Section 11 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, read with Section 5(i), which means the second spouse is not a legal widow or widower for inheritance purposes, but this does not by itself affect the inheritance rights of children born of that second marriage.
  • Section 16 of the Hindu Marriage Act, as it stands after the 1976 amendment, treats a child born of a void or voidable marriage as legitimate for all purposes, but Section 16(3) limits that child's property rights to the property of the child's own parents and does not, on its own wording, extend to the property of any other relative.
  • In Revanasiddappa and another versus Mallikarjun and others, decided on 1 September 2023 by a three-judge bench led by Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, the Supreme Court held that a child of a void or voidable marriage can claim a share equal to what the parent would have received on a notional partition of Hindu joint family property, correcting the narrower position taken in Jinia Keotin and later cases that had confined such a child to the parent's separate property alone.
  • The 2023 ruling did not make a child of a void or voidable marriage a coparcener by birth; the child's entitlement is still derived through the parent's own notional share, and the child cannot claim against the share of any other coparcener in the family.
  • Adoption under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 gives an adopted child the same inheritance rights as a biological child in the adoptive family, as a Class I heir, but it also severs the child's inheritance rights in the birth family going forward, a trade-off families considering a step-parent adoption should weigh deliberately rather than treat as a formality.
  • Section 18 of the Hindu Succession Act prefers an heir related by full blood over an heir related by half blood when the two stand in the same degree and the nature of the relationship is otherwise identical, a rule that affects how half-siblings from different marriages compete for a share, though reported decisions are not fully consistent on how far the preference extends to every category of sibling.

Why a blended family finds out about these gaps only after a death

Most families with children from more than one marriage, or with a step-parent who has raised a spouse's child as their own for twenty years, assume that love and lived relationship translate into legal entitlement. Hindu succession law does not work that way. It recognises blood relationship, legally valid marriage, and formal adoption as the three routes into a deceased person's estate when there is no will, and it is largely indifferent to how a family actually lived, who called whom a parent, or how long a step-child was treated exactly like a biological one. The gap between social fact and legal entitlement surfaces almost always at the worst possible time, when a parent has died without a will and the family is left to work out, often through litigation, which of its members the statute actually recognises. By the time that litigation begins, the family's own account of who was treated as a child, who contributed to the household, and who the deceased intended to benefit carries far less legal weight than the question of which category, blood relation, valid marriage, or formal adoption, the claimant actually falls into, and a family that has not addressed the gap in advance is rarely well served by trying to argue the statute's categories out of existence after the fact.

The practical cost of discovering these gaps late goes beyond money. A dispute over whether a step-child or a child of a once-void marriage has any claim at all tends to be fought in terms that revisit the family's most sensitive history, the fact of a parent's bigamy, the circumstances of a child's birth, or the degree to which a step-parent actually treated a step-child as their own, in open court, between surviving family members who otherwise might have continued to get along. Addressing the underlying legal position while every family member is alive and able to participate in the conversation, rather than leaving a court to reconstruct it from documentary and testimonial evidence after a death, is the more humane as well as the more legally reliable course.

This article maps three situations that look similar from inside a family but are treated quite differently by the statute: a child born of a marriage that turns out to have been void because an earlier marriage of one parent was still subsisting, a child who has no blood tie to a step-parent at all, and siblings who share only one parent in common. Each has its own statutory answer, and a 2023 Supreme Court ruling changed the answer for the first category in a way that is frequently summarised too broadly in family conversation.

It is worth being specific about why this piece focuses on Hindu succession law rather than attempting a single account covering every community. The Hindu Succession Act, 1956, the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 and the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 apply to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, and govern the large majority of the blended-family succession questions that come up in practice for families of those communities. Christians, Parsis and persons married under the Special Marriage Act, 1954 are governed by different, and in places materially different, provisions of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 and other personal laws, particularly on questions like legitimacy and the inheritance position of a step-child, and this article does not attempt to state those separate positions; a family governed by a different personal law should treat the statutory analysis here as inapplicable to its own succession and seek advice under the law that actually governs it.

A second framing point is that this article addresses intestate succession, meaning what happens when a person dies without a valid will, and the specific question of what a will can and should say to avoid the gaps intestate succession leaves open. It does not address the separate question of maintenance, which is governed by different provisions and gives some family members, including in certain circumstances a step-child or a second wife whose marriage is void, a right to maintenance during the paying relative's lifetime that is distinct from, and does not depend on, any inheritance right discussed in this article.

When the second marriage itself is void: Section 11 and the status of the second spouse

Section 5 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 sets the conditions a Hindu marriage must satisfy to be valid, and Section 5(i) requires that neither party have a spouse living at the time of the marriage. Section 11 declares a marriage solemnised in breach of that condition, among others, void, which in law means the marriage never had legal existence at all, rather than existing until someone successfully challenges it. A person who marries while an earlier marriage subsists therefore does not become a lawfully wedded second spouse in any sense the law recognises, regardless of how the ceremony was conducted or how long the relationship continued.

The direct consequence for succession is that the second spouse in such a marriage is not a widow or widower of the deceased for inheritance purposes and has no claim as a Class I heir under the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 on that basis. This is a point families sometimes get backward, assuming that because the second marriage was conducted openly, recognised socially, and perhaps even registered, the second spouse must have some residual inheritance claim. The marriage's void status under Section 11 forecloses that claim regardless of how it was otherwise treated by the family or the community. What Section 11 does not foreclose, and this is the more frequently litigated question, is the inheritance position of any children born of that void marriage, which is governed by an entirely separate provision discussed next.

A related point that families often raise is whether the first spouse's own position changes because the husband or wife went on to contract a second, void marriage. It generally does not. The first marriage, being the only legally valid one, continues to govern the first spouse's status as a lawfully wedded spouse and heir, and the existence of a void second marriage does not by itself dilute the first spouse's own share on intestacy. Where a family is dealing with both a first spouse and the children of a void second marriage competing for a share of the same estate, the first spouse's own entitlement as the legal widow or widower is unaffected by the second marriage's invalidity, while the children of that second marriage separately take the share discussed in the following two sections, so the two claims sit alongside rather than in competition with each other in most cases.

Section 16: how children of a void or voidable marriage became legitimate, and what it still withholds

Section 16 of the Hindu Marriage Act, as amended by the Marriage Laws (Amendment) Act, 1976, provides that a child born of a marriage that is void under Section 11, or of a voidable marriage annulled under Section 12, is deemed legitimate for all purposes as if born of a valid marriage, whether or not a formal decree of nullity is obtained. The 1976 amendment made clear that a void marriage's children do not need to wait for a court to declare the marriage void before their own legitimacy is settled; the legitimacy follows from the fact of the marriage's invalidity, not from a subsequent decree.

Section 16(3), however, places a specific limit on what that legitimacy actually confers for property purposes. It confines the child's property rights to the property of the child's own parents, and courts have read this to mean the child acquires no claim against the property of any other relative purely by virtue of Section 16. For several decades, the leading judicial reading of this limit, traced through the Supreme Court's decision in Jinia Keotin versus Kumar Sitaram Manjhi in 2002, held that a child covered by Section 16 could inherit a share of the parent's separate, self-acquired property, but had no claim at all to a share of the parent's interest in Hindu joint family coparcenary property, on the reasoning that Section 16(3) spoke only of the parent's own property and coparcenary property belonged, in a meaningful sense, to the joint family rather than to the parent individually. That reading stood as the settled position for more than two decades before the Supreme Court revisited it in 2023.

A voidable marriage, the second category Section 16 addresses, works slightly differently in principle even though the succession consequence for children ends up largely aligned. Section 12 of the Hindu Marriage Act lists grounds, such as the husband's impotence, a party's incapacity to give valid consent, pregnancy by a person other than the husband at the time of marriage, or fraud going to the marriage's validity, on which a marriage is voidable rather than void, meaning the marriage is treated as valid unless and until a court annuls it on a petition brought by a party entitled to bring one. A voidable marriage that is never actually annulled continues as a valid marriage for all purposes, including succession, and Section 16 only comes into play once an annulment decree has actually been obtained. Families sometimes assume any marriage they consider procedurally irregular is automatically voidable in this sense; the grounds are specific and limited, and a marriage simply regretted later, without one of the specific statutory grounds, remains fully valid and is not affected by Section 16 at all.

Revanasiddappa versus Mallikarjun: the coparcenary question reopened and resolved

In Revanasiddappa and another versus Mallikarjun and others, decided on 1 September 2023 and reported as 2023 INSC 783, a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court, led by Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud and including Justices J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra, revisited the correctness of Jinia Keotin and the related decisions in Neelamma and Bharatha Matha, whose reasoning an earlier two-judge bench had already doubted when referring the matter for a fuller hearing. The appeal itself had originated as a 2011 reference, Civil Appeal No. 2844 of 2011, and took over a decade to reach final resolution before the larger bench.

The Court held that a child of a void or voidable marriage is entitled to a share in the property that would have been allotted to the deceased parent on a notional partition of the Hindu joint family's coparcenary property, applying the statutory fiction in Section 6(3) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 under which a coparcener's interest is deemed to be the share that would have resulted from a partition immediately before death. This corrected the position under Jinia Keotin, which had confined such children to the parent's separate property alone, and brought coparcenary property within the scope of what a Section 16 child can inherit, provided the inheritance runs through the deceased parent's own notional share rather than any wider claim against the joint family as a whole.

It matters to be precise about what the Court did not hold, because commentary on this ruling has sometimes overstated its reach. The Court did not hold that a child of a void or voidable marriage becomes a coparcener by birth in the Hindu undivided family, the status an ordinary, legitimately born son or daughter acquires automatically. The child's claim is derivative, confined to the share the parent would have taken on a notional partition, and does not extend to a direct claim against the shares of other coparceners in the family who are not the child's own parent. The distinction matters practically in a family with several coparceners: a Section 16 child inherits through, and only to the extent of, their own parent's notional share, not an independent stake in the joint family property at large.

An illustration makes the distinction concrete. Suppose a man is a coparcener in a Hindu joint family alongside his two brothers, each holding, say, a one-third notional share of the ancestral property, and he has one son from his lawful first marriage and one son from a second marriage later found void. On his death, Revanasiddappa entitles the second son to a share of his father's own one-third notional share, divided between the two sons, rather than to any claim against the one-third shares held by the father's brothers. The second son's entitlement is real and enforceable, but it is carved entirely out of the father's own portion of the joint family property; the uncles' shares remain untouched by the nephew's claim, because the nephew from the void marriage was never himself a coparcener and the uncles were never, at any point, his father's property in the sense Section 16(3) and the 2023 ruling address.

Step-children: no blood tie, no automatic share, and the two ways around it

A step-child, in the sense used here, is a spouse's child from an earlier marriage or relationship with whom the current spouse shares no blood relationship and has not formally adopted. Hindu succession law treats this relationship as outside the statutory scheme entirely. The Hindu Succession Act, 1956 defines the heirs entitled to a share by reference to son, daughter, widow, mother and the other categories listed in the Schedule, and a stepdaughter or stepson simply does not fall within any of those categories. A step-child raised from infancy, treated identically to a biological child in every respect, and genuinely regarded by the family as a son or daughter, still has no statutory claim to the step-parent's estate if the step-parent dies without a will.

Two routes exist to change this position, and families considering either should understand that they work quite differently. The first is a will: a step-parent is free to leave property to a step-child by will in exactly the same way as to any other person, since testamentary freedom under the general law does not depend on blood relationship, and a will remains, in practice, the most direct and commonly used way to provide for a step-child. The second route is formal adoption under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, which brings its own, more significant consequence discussed in the next section.

It is also worth addressing directly a related but distinct relationship families sometimes confuse with the step-child situation: a step-parent's own position toward a step-child's property, rather than the step-child's position toward the step-parent's property. A step-mother or step-father likewise has no statutory inheritance claim against a step-child's own estate, for the same reason the relationship runs in neither direction under the Act absent adoption or a will. This becomes practically relevant where a step-child predeceases the step-parent holding independent property, perhaps acquired through the step-child's own career or an earlier inheritance from the biological parent's side; the step-parent who raised that child cannot claim a share of that property purely as a step-parent, and would need to have been named in the child's own will to inherit anything.

Adoption's double edge: full inheritance rights in, severance of the old family's rights out

Where a step-parent formally adopts a spouse's child under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, the adopted child acquires the same inheritance rights in the adoptive family as a biological child, ranking as a Class I heir under the Hindu Succession Act with an equal claim alongside the adoptive parent's other children. This is the only route, short of a will, that gives a step-child an intestate share at all, and it resolves the inheritance question fully rather than piecemeal.

The trade-off is that a valid adoption severs the child's ties to the birth family for inheritance purposes going forward. A child adopted by a step-parent typically loses the right to claim against the estate of their biological parent or that parent's relatives, except in respect of property that had already vested in the child before the adoption took place. For a family where the biological parent (commonly the parent who is not the new spouse, in a case where only one parent has remarried) is still alive and the child's relationship with that parent and that side of the family remains intact and important, adoption by the step-parent can create more difficulty than it resolves, because it formally cuts the child out of the biological parent's side of the family's eventual succession unless that parent separately provides for the child by will. Families weighing a step-parent adoption purely to fix an inheritance gap should consider whether a will achieves the same practical result without this consequence.

There is also a formal requirement families frequently overlook: a valid adoption under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act requires, among other conditions, that the person being adopted actually be a Hindu, that the adoptive parent have the capacity and the right to adopt under the Act, and that the adoption be carried out in accordance with the Act's specific requirements, including the giving and taking of the child in adoption. A social or informal arrangement in which a step-parent has simply raised a spouse's child as their own for years, without ever completing a legally valid adoption, does not satisfy these requirements and leaves the step-child in exactly the unprotected position described above regardless of how long the arrangement has lasted or how completely the family has treated the child as its own. Families who believe an adoption took place informally, through a ceremony or family understanding rather than a document meeting the Act's requirements, should have that belief verified by a lawyer well before relying on it, since an adoption asserted after the adoptive parent's death, when the other heirs dispute it, is a considerably harder case to prove than one documented at the time.

A child born outside any marriage: a narrower, separate route

A distinct category, worth separating clearly from the void or voidable marriage situation discussed above, is a child born where there was no marriage at all between the parents, however informal or long-standing the relationship. Section 3(1)(j) of the Hindu Succession Act defines the term 'related' for purposes of the Act as related by legitimate kinship, and on that basis such a child is generally treated as related to the mother, and to the mother's other children, by illegitimate kinship recognised for inheritance between them, while the position with respect to the father's side and the father's other relatives has historically been read far more narrowly, in several decisions confined to the mother's line rather than extending to any claim through the father against the father's other relatives.

This is a genuinely more unsettled corner of the law than the void-marriage situation addressed by Section 16 and the 2023 Revanasiddappa ruling, and the reported decisions do not speak with one voice on exactly how far a child in this category can reach into the father's estate or the father's family. A family dealing with this situation should treat it as requiring specific legal advice on the facts, including when and how any relationship between the parents was conducted, rather than assuming either that the child has no rights at all or that the child's position is identical to that of a child covered by Section 16, which it is not.

The practical difference between the two categories is worth restating plainly, because the terms 'illegitimate' and 'child of a void marriage' are often used loosely and interchangeably in ordinary conversation even though the statute treats them on different footing. A child of a void or voidable marriage has, by definition, parents who went through a marriage ceremony that the law treats as ineffective for a specific, identifiable reason, usually bigamy or a defect going to consent or capacity, and that child's position is squarely addressed by Section 16 and, after 2023, by Revanasiddappa's extension to coparcenary property. A child whose parents never married at all, in any ceremony the law could even potentially have recognised, falls instead into the narrower, less settled category addressed by Section 3(1)(j) and the older case law on illegitimate kinship, where the claim through the father's side is genuinely contested rather than merely unexplored. A family advisor asked to assess a child's position needs to establish, as a first step, which of these two factual situations actually applies, since the legal route and the strength of the claim differ substantially between them.

Full blood versus half blood: Section 18 and where the preference actually applies

A separate complication in a blended family arises once there are siblings who share only one parent, typically where a parent has children from two different marriages. Section 18 of the Hindu Succession Act provides that heirs related to an intestate by full blood are preferred to heirs related by half blood, where the nature of the relationship is the same in every other respect. The provision is generally understood as codifying an older Hindu law principle that whole-blood relations take precedence over half-blood relations standing in the same degree of relationship to the deceased.

Where the provision becomes genuinely contested is in mixed sibling situations, for instance where a deceased person is survived by a full-blood sister and a half-blood brother, both claiming as siblings in the same class of heirs. Reported decisions have not spoken with one voice on whether Section 18's preference operates across that kind of mixed comparison or only where the siblings being compared are identically placed in every respect, including the same sex of sibling relationship, and at least one High Court has revisited and departed from an earlier precedent on this specific point. A family with half-siblings from different marriages who may eventually compete for a share in an intestate estate should treat this as a live area of dispute risk rather than a settled rule, and should weigh that risk specifically when deciding whether to leave matters to intestate succession or to set out shares explicitly in a will.

Section 18 is also easy to misapply by extending it beyond where it operates. The preference only arises as between heirs who would otherwise rank equally, standing in the same degree and class of heirs under the Act; it is a tie-breaking rule for heirs who compete at the same level, not a general principle that a full-blood relative of any kind outranks a half-blood relative of any kind. A full-blood sibling does not, for instance, outrank the deceased's own widow or children, who rank in a different and prior class of heirs altogether under the Schedule to the Act. Families sometimes reach for Section 18 to argue a broader point about blood relationship mattering more generally in Hindu succession than the provision actually supports, and a claim built on that broader reading is likely to fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the specific, narrower, and genuinely unsettled half-blood sibling question this section has focused on.

What a will should actually say in a blended family, beyond simply naming the beneficiaries

A will remains the most reliable way to avoid every uncertainty catalogued in this article, but a will drafted without the blended-family context in mind can create its own ambiguity. A will that leaves property to 'my children' without naming them, intending to include a step-child the testator has always treated as a child but never adopted, risks being read strictly against that step-child, since the step-child is not a child in the legal sense the Hindu Succession Act and most testamentary drafting conventions use, and a court construing the will is likely to confine the gift to the testator's legal children unless the testator's intention to include the step-child is stated with real specificity.

The more defensible drafting approach names every intended beneficiary individually, states expressly whether a named step-child is included within any general reference to 'children' elsewhere in the will, and addresses, where relevant, how the testator's share of coparcenary property, if any, interacts with a Section 16 child's own notional-partition entitlement under the Revanasiddappa line of authority, since a will cannot simply override a coparcener's statutory entitlement to their own share but can address how the testator's separate property and personal share of joint family property are to be distributed among the family's various children. Where the family anticipates a dispute, particularly over the application of Section 18's full-blood preference or the scope of a void marriage's children's claim, a carefully drafted will, read together with a contemporaneous family settlement where appropriate, does more to prevent litigation than any later attempt to explain the testator's intentions after death.

A testator in a blended family should also think specifically about the choice of executor and witnesses, not only the distribution clauses, because a will benefiting a step-child or a child of a once-void marriage over the objection of other family members is exactly the kind of will likely to be challenged for want of testamentary capacity or undue influence after the testator's death. Choosing an executor who is not one of the beneficiaries, recording the testator's reasons for the chosen distribution in the will itself or in a separate, contemporaneous memorandum, and having the will properly witnessed by two or more attesting witnesses in accordance with Section 63 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925, which governs the formal execution of a Hindu testator's will, each reduce the practical likelihood that a disappointed heir can successfully challenge the will's validity rather than merely contesting the fairness of its contents, which the law does not generally allow a disappointed heir to do.

A practical checklist for a family business or HNI family with a blended structure

A family business or high-net-worth family carrying this kind of structure, a second marriage, step-children, or children from more than one relationship, benefits from treating succession planning as a specific, named workstream rather than an incidental part of estate planning generally. That workstream should include a clear-eyed legal assessment of whether any marriage in the family's history is void or voidable under Sections 11 and 12 of the Hindu Marriage Act, since the answer determines both the spouse's own status and the Section 16 position of any children of that marriage; a decision, made deliberately rather than by default, on whether a step-child's inheritance gap is better closed through adoption or through a will, given the severance consequence adoption carries; and an explicit allocation in the will of the testator's own property, separate and coparcenary, among the family's children, drafted with enough specificity to survive the kind of full-blood-versus-half-blood or Section 16 coparcenary dispute this article has described.

None of this requires resolving every open legal question discussed above in the family's favour before acting. It requires the family's advisers to identify which of these open questions actually apply to the family's own facts, and to draft around the risk those open questions create rather than wait for a court to resolve them after a death, at which point the family's control over the outcome, and often the family's relationships with each other, has already been lost.

Where the family's assets include a shareholding in a family-run company, this planning should extend to the company's own articles of association and any shareholders' agreement, not only the personal will. A will can direct that shares pass to a particular child or step-child, but if the company's articles contain a restriction on transfer, a right of first refusal in favour of the other shareholders, or a requirement that a transferee be approved by the board, those provisions can constrain what the will's beneficiary actually receives in practice, regardless of what the will itself says. A family business succession plan that addresses only the will, without checking whether the company's constitutional documents will actually give effect to it, risks leaving a step-child or a child of a once-void marriage with an inheritance on paper that the company's own governance documents prevent from being realised as actual control or even a freely transferable shareholding.

Hindu succession law treats a blended family's internal relationships with far more precision, and far less sentiment, than the family itself usually does. A child born of a marriage that turns out to have been void is legitimate and, after the Supreme Court's 2023 ruling in Revanasiddappa, entitled to a real share of coparcenary property through the parent's own notional partition, but that child is still not a coparcener in their own right. A step-child raised with every appearance of being a son or daughter has, absent adoption or a will, no claim at all. Half-siblings from different marriages may or may not be treated identically depending on a point of law the High Courts have not uniformly settled.

None of these gaps close themselves with time or goodwill, and each tends to surface only once a family member has died and the rest of the family is left to work out, often adversarially, what the statute actually provides. A will drafted with the family's actual structure in mind, naming beneficiaries specifically rather than relying on general terms like 'children', and a deliberate, informed choice between adoption and testamentary provision for a step-child, do more to protect a blended family's relationships than any attempt to settle these questions after the fact.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a step-child automatically inherit from a step-parent in India if the step-parent dies without a will?

No. A step-child, meaning a spouse's child from an earlier relationship with no blood tie to the step-parent, does not fall within any category of heir listed in the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 and has no statutory claim to the step-parent's estate on intestacy. The only ways to give a step-child an inheritance right are a will naming the step-child specifically, or formal adoption by the step-parent under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, which carries its own consequence of severing the child's rights in the birth family.

Can children born of a bigamous second marriage inherit from their father?

Yes, in respect of their father's own property. Section 16 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 treats a child born of a marriage void under Section 11, including a bigamous marriage entered into while an earlier marriage subsisted, as legitimate for all purposes. Following the Supreme Court's 2023 ruling in Revanasiddappa versus Mallikarjun, such a child can also claim a share equal to what the father would have received on a notional partition of Hindu joint family coparcenary property, though the child is not a coparcener in their own right and cannot claim against other coparceners' shares.

What did the Supreme Court decide in Revanasiddappa versus Mallikarjun in 2023?

A three-judge bench led by Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud held, on 1 September 2023, that a child of a void or voidable marriage is entitled to a share of Hindu joint family coparcenary property equal to what the deceased parent would have received on a notional partition under Section 6(3) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956. This corrected the narrower position in the Court's earlier Jinia Keotin line of decisions, which had confined such a child's inheritance to the parent's separate, self-acquired property alone and excluded coparcenary property entirely.

Is a second marriage automatically void just because it is the husband's or wife's second marriage?

No. A second marriage is void under Section 11 of the Hindu Marriage Act only if an earlier marriage of either party was still subsisting, in breach of the condition in Section 5(i) that neither party have a living spouse at the time of marriage. A second marriage entered into after a valid divorce or after the death of the first spouse is not void on this ground. The void status matters because it determines whether the second spouse counts as a legal widow or widower for inheritance, though it does not, by itself, affect the separate Section 16 position of any children born of that marriage.

Does adopting a step-child cut off the child's right to inherit from their biological parent?

Generally, yes. Once a child is validly adopted under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, the child typically loses the right to claim against the estate of the biological parent and that parent's relatives going forward, except for property that had already vested in the child before the adoption. A family where the child's relationship with the non-remarried biological parent remains important should weigh this consequence carefully before adopting through a step-parent, since a will achieves a similar inheritance outcome without severing that side of the family relationship in law.

Do half-siblings inherit an equal share with full-blood siblings under Hindu law?

Not always. Section 18 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 prefers an heir related by full blood over an heir related by half blood where both stand in the same degree of relationship and the nature of that relationship is otherwise identical. Reported High Court decisions are not fully consistent on how far this preference extends in mixed cases, such as a full-blood sister competing with a half-blood brother, so a family with half-siblings from different marriages should treat the point as a genuine dispute risk rather than assume either equal division or automatic preference.

What should a will for a blended family specifically include to avoid disputes later?

The will should name every intended beneficiary individually rather than relying on general terms like 'my children', state expressly whether a step-child is meant to be included within any such general reference, and set out how the testator's separate property and personal share of any joint family property are to be divided among children from different marriages. Where the family anticipates a dispute over a void marriage's children's coparcenary claim or over full-blood and half-blood shares, drafting the allocation explicitly, rather than leaving it to intestate succession rules, is the more reliable way to prevent litigation after death.
