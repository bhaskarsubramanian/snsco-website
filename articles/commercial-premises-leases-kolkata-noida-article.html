Commercial Premises Leases in Kolkata and Noida: Registration, Rent Law and Recovery Compared

Why the same shop, office or warehouse lease is governed by two different rent laws, two different registration regimes and two different eviction routes depending on whether it sits in Kolkata or Noida.

By Adv. Rahul Jangra, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A retailer taking a shop on Park Street and a logistics company taking a warehouse in Sector 63, Noida, are both signing a 'commercial lease,' but from the moment either document is drafted, the two transactions are governed by different rent statutes, different stamp and registration thresholds, and different forums if the relationship breaks down. A business operating in both cities, or a landlord dealing with tenants in both, cannot safely assume that what works for one lease will work for the other.

Key points

  • West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh both require a lease exceeding one year, running from year to year, or reserving a yearly rent to be made by a registered instrument under Section 107 of the Transfer of Property Act read with Section 17(1)(d) of the Registration Act, 1908; the real difference between the two states lies in rent-law coverage and stamp duty, not in this basic registration rule.
  • Registering a commercial lease in Kolkata can take it outside the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997 altogether, since the Act exempts tenancies created by a registered lease; many commercial landlords in Kolkata use this deliberately to avoid the Act's eviction restrictions.
  • Non-residential premises let at a monthly rent above the threshold fixed in Section 3(f)(i) of the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, reported at ten thousand rupees, fall outside the Act regardless of registration, so most serious commercial leases in Kolkata are already governed by ordinary contract and property law rather than rent control.
  • Uttar Pradesh has recently capped stamp duty and registration charges on lease agreements of up to ten years, a change aimed specifically at easing the cost of formal lease registration for the retail and commercial sector.
  • An unregistered lease for a term that legally required registration cannot create that lease, following the Supreme Court's settled position in Anthony v. K.C. Ittoop & Sons, but the tenancy still exists as a month-to-month arrangement under Section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, and a Calcutta-origin dispute, Paul Rubber Industries v. Amit Chand Mitra, reached the Supreme Court in 2023 on exactly this point.
  • Lock-in clauses are enforceable in both cities as ordinary contractual terms, but a landlord claiming the balance of lock-in rent after an early exit still has to plead and prove the loss actually suffered rather than simply invoking the clause as a penalty.
  • Sub-letting defaults differently in the two states: West Bengal's rent statute requires the landlord's written consent before a tenant can sublet or assign a tenancy it covers, while the ordinary Transfer of Property Act position that applies more broadly in Uttar Pradesh allows sub-letting unless the lease deed itself prohibits it.
  • Eviction for a lease inside Uttar Pradesh's rent-control statute goes through a Prescribed Authority and an Appellate Authority rather than the ordinary civil court, while a lease outside that Act, or outside West Bengal's rent statute, is enforced through an ordinary civil suit for ejectment under the Transfer of Property Act.

Two cities, two rent laws, one shared starting point

A commercial lease agreed in Kolkata and one agreed in Noida both start from the same central statute: the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, and its Section 107, which decides when a lease must be made by a registered instrument rather than by a simple written or oral agreement. From that common starting point, the two states diverge quickly. West Bengal has its own rent-control statute, the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997, which carves out a defined category of tenancies, generally the lower-value ones, for special protection against eviction, while leaving higher-value and registered tenancies to be governed by ordinary contract and property law. Uttar Pradesh has a comparable statute in the Uttar Pradesh Urban Buildings (Regulation of Letting, Rent and Eviction) Act, 1972, which applies across the state but exempts, among other categories, buildings let at a rent above a specified figure and, for a defined period, newly constructed buildings.

For a business taking space in either city, the practical question is not simply 'what does the lease say' but 'which law actually governs this tenancy once the lease is signed.' Getting that wrong matters more in a commercial lease than it might first appear, because it decides whether a dispute over possession goes to an ordinary civil court or to a specialised rent authority, what grounds justify eviction, and, in West Bengal particularly, whether registering the lease at all works for or against the landlord's interest.

This piece works through registration and stamp duty, rent-law coverage, lock-in and escalation terms, security deposits, the consequences of leaving a lease unregistered, sub-letting, and the actual route to recovering possession or arrears in each city, flagging wherever the figures or the precise statutory position could not be confirmed from more than one source so that a reader knows exactly where to verify before relying on this piece for a live transaction.

Registration and stamp duty: the same trigger, two different cost regimes

Section 107 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 provides that a lease of immovable property from year to year, or for a term exceeding one year, or reserving a yearly rent, can only be made by a registered instrument; any other lease may be made either by a registered instrument or by an oral agreement accompanied by delivery of possession. Section 17(1)(d) of the Registration Act, 1908 makes registration compulsory for the same category of leases, and once a lease is registered, stamp duty and registration fees follow as a matter of course. Uttar Pradesh has retained this structure through its own amendment to Section 107, which keeps the same basic trigger, a term exceeding one year, a year-to-year arrangement, or a yearly rent, while West Bengal applies the central provision without a comparable state amendment.

Where the two states differ sharply is in the cost of actually registering a commercial lease and in how that cost interacts with other legal consequences. Uttar Pradesh has recently capped stamp duty and registration charges on lease agreements running up to ten years, a change reported as aimed specifically at the retail and commercial sector, where high and unpredictable stamp duty had been cited by industry bodies as a deterrent to formal registration in the first place. A business taking commercial space in Noida under a lease of this kind should check the current capped rate against the actual term and value of its lease before assuming an older, higher figure applies, since the change is recent and the precise slab structure was not confirmed in the sources gathered for this piece.

West Bengal has not been reported as introducing a comparable cap, and stamp duty on a Kolkata commercial lease continues to be assessed under the ordinary stamp duty schedule applicable to leases in the state, calculated with reference to the term, the rent and any premium or deposit payable. A business and its landlord negotiating a Kolkata commercial lease should get the stamp duty calculated on the actual structure of the deal, including any security deposit the stamp authorities might treat as additional consideration, before finalising the lease term and the deposit amount, since restructuring the deal after the fact to reduce stamp duty is far harder than getting the structure right from the outset.

Paying too little stamp duty, whether by mistake or by deliberately undervaluing the lease, carries its own separate risk in both states under Section 35 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899: an instrument that is not duly stamped cannot be admitted in evidence or acted upon for any purpose by a court or any person having authority to receive evidence, until the deficient duty and the prescribed penalty are paid. A landlord or tenant in either Kolkata or Noida who finds itself relying on an under-stamped lease in a dispute, whether to prove the rent, the term or any other clause, should expect the other side to raise the stamping objection early, and should be prepared to pay the deficiency and penalty to the court or the stamp authority before the document can be used for anything beyond the limited purposes, such as showing the nature of possession rather than the lease's specific terms, that the case law on unregistered and under-stamped instruments, discussed further below, actually allows.

Registering a Kolkata lease can take it outside rent control altogether

The West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997 contains an exemption that gives a Kolkata landlord a real strategic choice that does not exist in quite the same form in Uttar Pradesh. The Act's exemption provision excludes from its coverage any tenancy where the lease has been registered under the Registration Act, 1908, whether the registration happened before or after the 1997 Act itself commenced, which the Act's text records as 10 July 2001. A reported Calcutta-area decision applied exactly this exemption to a thirty-year registered lease, holding that the tenancy was governed by the ordinary law under the Transfer of Property Act rather than by the Premises Tenancy Act's special eviction protections, because the lease had been registered.

A second, independent route out of the 1997 Act applies regardless of registration: non-residential premises let at a monthly rent above a threshold fixed in Section 3(f)(i) of the Act, reported in a Calcutta High Court decision at ten thousand rupees, fall outside the Act's coverage entirely. Given how far commercial rents in Kolkata's established business districts have moved since that figure was fixed, the great majority of serious commercial leases in the city are likely to clear this threshold comfortably and sit outside the Act on rent value alone, independent of whether the lease happens to be registered.

The combined effect is that a Kolkata landlord letting commercial space at a realistic market rent, and registering the lease as Section 107 of the Transfer of Property Act requires for any term exceeding a year, is very likely renting outside the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act's special protections twice over, through the rent threshold and through registration. This is a meaningful point for a tenant to understand before signing: a Kolkata commercial tenant should not assume the Act's eviction safeguards, discussed further below, will be available to it simply because the premises are commercial and sit within Kolkata, and should negotiate the lease's own termination and renewal terms carefully rather than relying on statutory protection that is unlikely to apply.

Lock-in periods and rent escalation: enforceable, but not unconditionally

Lock-in clauses, under which a tenant commits not to terminate the lease before a fixed period and agrees to pay a defined sum if it exits early, are routine in commercial leases in both cities and are enforceable under ordinary contract principles as a term the parties freely negotiated, generally reported as running somewhere between three and five years though the actual period is whatever the parties agree. A landlord relying on a lock-in clause to recover rent for the unexpired period, however, cannot simply point to the clause and claim the full remaining rent as a matter of course. The Delhi High Court's Division Bench, in Tower Vision India Private Limited v. Procall Private Limited (2012), held that rent for the lock-in period cannot be claimed without pleading the actual loss suffered, treating the clause as subject to the ordinary requirement under Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 that a sum fixed as damages for breach is a ceiling on recoverable compensation rather than an automatic entitlement.

More recent commercial court decisions illustrate how this plays out in practice: in one 2025 Delhi commercial court matter, a tenant under a twenty-four-month lease with a lock-in clause faced a claim for a fixed number of months' rent on early exit, while a separate 2024 dispute concerned a tenant that terminated only after its thirty-six-month lock-in had already expired, avoiding the clause altogether. A tenant negotiating a lock-in clause in either Kolkata or Noida should press for the clause to state a specific, calculable sum or formula for early exit rather than leaving the figure to later argument over actual loss, since a specific and reasonable figure is both easier to enforce for the landlord and easier for the tenant to budget around than an open-ended damages claim.

Escalation clauses, fixing a periodic increase in rent, usually expressed as a percentage after a defined number of years, are equally a matter of contract in both states and are not separately regulated by either the West Bengal or the Uttar Pradesh rent statute for leases that fall within those statutes' coverage in the first place; for leases outside those statutes, which, as discussed above, is the position for most serious commercial leases in Kolkata and for many in Noida, there is no statutory escalation cap at all, and the clause as drafted is what governs. Businesses entering a multi-year commercial lease in either city should model the escalation clause's actual effect over the full term before signing, since a seemingly modest percentage compounded every few years over a long lease term can move the effective rent considerably by the end of the period.

Security deposits: largely a matter of the contract, not the statute

Commercial leases in both cities commonly require a security deposit, often expressed as a multiple of the monthly rent, to be held by the landlord against unpaid rent, damage to the premises, or other breaches, and refundable on the tenant vacating in accordance with the lease. Neither the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act nor the Uttar Pradesh Urban Buildings Act was found, in the sources gathered for this piece, to impose a specific statutory cap or interest obligation on a commercial security deposit, which leaves the deposit's treatment almost entirely to what the lease itself provides, in sharp contrast to some other states that have introduced caps for residential tenancies specifically.

A dispute over withholding or short-refund of a security deposit at the end of a commercial lease, where the lease itself does not sit within either state's rent-control statute, is ordinarily pursued as a straightforward civil claim for recovery of money, before the forum that would otherwise have jurisdiction over the lease, discussed in the sections on eviction and recovery below, rather than through any specialised rent-authority process. Businesses negotiating a commercial lease in either city do well to set out, in the lease itself, the specific heads against which the deposit can be adjusted, and a timeline for refund after vacating, since the absence of any statutory backstop on either of these points in a commercial lease means the contract's own wording is effectively the only protection either side has.

A landlord's position on the deposit can also be complicated if the tenant itself later goes into liquidation or corporate insolvency resolution, since the deposit, once paid over, is an asset of the tenant that a liquidator or resolution professional may claim back as part of the tenant's estate, while the landlord simultaneously wants to set it off against unpaid rent or damage. Landlords taking a sizeable deposit from a corporate tenant in either city should draft the set-off right in the lease in clear, self-executing terms, specifying that the deposit is adjusted automatically against defined heads of claim on vacating or on termination, rather than leaving the adjustment to a later determination that a liquidator or resolution professional might be able to contest as an unapproved set-off against the insolvent tenant's estate.

Leaving a lease unregistered: what survives and what does not

A lease that should have been registered under Section 107 of the Transfer of Property Act, because its term exceeds a year, runs from year to year, or reserves a yearly rent, but was never actually registered, does not simply become void for all purposes. The Supreme Court's settled position, from Anthony v. K.C. Ittoop & Sons (2000), reading Section 107 of the Transfer of Property Act together with Sections 17 and 49 of the Registration Act, 1908, is that such an unregistered instrument cannot itself create the lease it purports to grant, but the Court can still look at the parties' conduct and the fact of possession to decide whether a lessor-lessee relationship exists independently of the defective document. Where no valid lease has been created in this way, Section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act supplies a default: absent a different written contract, local law, or usage, a lease for a purpose other than agriculture or manufacture is deemed to run from month to month, terminable by fifteen days' notice, a position the Supreme Court applied as early as Khaja Midhat Noor's case, reported at AIR 1988 SC 1470.

This exact question reached the Supreme Court again from Kolkata itself in Paul Rubber Industries Private Limited v. Amit Chand Mitra & Anr., 2023 INSC 854, decided on 25 September 2023, where the Court considered whether an unregistered lease agreement could be looked at to establish the nature and character of a tenant's possession once that question became the primary dispute between the parties. The Court held that the earlier authority of Park Street Properties Private Limited v. Dipak Kumar Singh and Another, (2016) 9 SCC 268, itself arising from a Kolkata tenancy, is not authority for treating the nature and character of possession as always available as a 'collateral purpose' for which an unregistered document can be used under the Registration Act's Section 49 proviso; where that question is itself the central dispute in the case, the Court held it is excluded by law from relying on the unregistered deed to decide it, and the tenant's appeal was dismissed, with the tenancy treated as having run month to month.

The practical lesson for a business in either Kolkata or Noida is the same regardless of which city's statute otherwise governs the relationship: an unregistered lease for a term that legally required registration is not a safe way to secure a long-term tenancy, since the tenant cannot rely on the document itself to establish the term, the rent escalation, or any other specific clause it contains, and in a genuine dispute over the character of possession, the unregistered document may not even be available as evidence on that specific question. A business taking commercial space for a term of more than a year should treat registration as a cost of doing the deal properly rather than an optional formality to be skipped to save stamp duty.

Sub-letting: consent required by statute in Kolkata, by the lease itself in Noida

Where a Kolkata tenancy actually falls within the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997, because it is both unregistered and below the Act's rent threshold, the Act itself requires the landlord's written consent before the tenant can sublet the premises or assign its tenancy, in either case, to any other party; a tenant that sublets or assigns without that consent risks the landlord treating the act itself as a ground for eviction under the Act. Because, as discussed earlier in this piece, most serious commercial leases in Kolkata sit outside the Act through registration or the rent threshold, this statutory consent requirement in practice applies more often to smaller or longer-standing commercial tenancies than to a fresh, well-drafted commercial lease at a market rent.

For a commercial lease that sits outside the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, and for the general run of commercial leases in Noida that are not covered by the Uttar Pradesh Urban Buildings Act, the default position instead comes from the ordinary law of leases under the Transfer of Property Act, which permits a lessee to transfer or sublet its interest unless the lease itself restricts or prohibits this. In practice, nearly every commercial lease drafted for either city expressly addresses sub-letting one way or the other, so the statutory default rarely ends up deciding the point, but a business taking a lease that is silent on sub-letting should understand that, outside the specific statutory consent requirement described above, its ability to sublet will usually turn on the general law's permissive default rather than on any need to seek the landlord's consent, unless the lease itself says otherwise.

Getting the tenant out: two different eviction machineries

Where a commercial tenancy in Kolkata genuinely falls within the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997, eviction is tightly controlled: Section 6 of the Act sets out the specific grounds on which a decree for recovery of possession can be passed, and a landlord cannot obtain an eviction decree on any ground outside this list however clearly the lease itself might otherwise appear to entitle the landlord to possession. A tenant defending such a suit can protect itself by complying with the Act's provisions on depositing admitted rent into court, generally understood as a precondition the tenant must satisfy to keep its defence alive, and the Act separately provides, in Section 12, a specific route for restoring a tenancy where a decree for recovery of possession has been passed on particular grounds, giving a tenant evicted on those grounds a defined opportunity to seek the tenancy back.

Where a Kolkata commercial lease sits outside the Act, through registration or the rent threshold discussed earlier, eviction instead follows the ordinary route under the Transfer of Property Act: the landlord terminates the lease by the notice the lease itself requires, or, absent such a term, the fifteen-day notice period under Section 106 for a non-agricultural, non-manufacturing tenancy, and then files an ordinary civil suit for ejectment and recovery of possession before whichever of the forums described in this site's companion article on the Kolkata forum map actually has jurisdiction over the dispute, generally the City Civil Court or the High Court's Original Side depending on the property's location and the suit's valuation.

Uttar Pradesh takes a structurally different approach for any tenancy that does fall within the Uttar Pradesh Urban Buildings (Regulation of Letting, Rent and Eviction) Act, 1972: eviction proceeds not before an ordinary civil court but before a Prescribed Authority constituted under the Act, on specified statutory grounds, with an appeal to a District Judge or an officer designated as the Appellate Authority rather than to the High Court in the first instance. For a Noida commercial tenancy that falls outside this Act, whether because the rent exceeds the exemption threshold or because the building is new construction still within whatever exemption period the Act allows for newly built premises, the position reverts to the ordinary Transfer of Property Act route described above for Kolkata, with the civil suit filed before the appropriate court in Gautam Buddh Nagar, as this site's companion article on commercial litigation in Noida and Greater Noida addresses in more detail. A landlord or tenant in Noida should establish, before any dispute actually arises, which of these two very different procedures applies to its specific tenancy, since filing in the wrong forum, an eviction application before the Prescribed Authority for a tenancy that is actually exempt from the Act, or a civil suit for one that is not, wastes time that is often already tight once a relationship has broken down.

Whichever route eventually applies, the lease's own termination clause is usually what actually gets tested first, since most commercial disputes begin with one side purporting to terminate for the other's breach rather than with an immediate court filing. A lease that sets out a clear notice period, commonly thirty, sixty or ninety days depending on the term and the parties' bargaining position, and a specific cure period for a breach before termination takes effect, gives both sides a cleaner record to work from if the matter does end up before a court or a Prescribed Authority, since the first question in nearly every one of the reported disputes examined for this piece was whether the notice actually given matched what the lease, or the applicable statute in its absence, required. A business negotiating either a Kolkata or a Noida commercial lease should treat the termination clause as being as important as the rent clause itself, rather than as boilerplate copied from an earlier agreement.

Recovering arrears: the civil suit, the commercial courts, and the cheque route

Where rent itself, rather than possession, is the issue, a landlord in either city who is owed arrears can pursue an ordinary civil suit for the amount due, generally alongside or as part of an ejectment suit where both possession and arrears are in dispute at once. Where the arrears claim is of a sufficient value and the underlying lease genuinely counts as a transaction relating to immovable property used exclusively in trade or commerce, within the categories recognised under Section 2(1)(c) of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, the claim can fall within the commercial courts structure in either state, discussed in greater detail in this site's companion pieces on the Kolkata forum map and on commercial litigation in Noida and Greater Noida, rather than before an ordinary civil court; whether a given lease dispute actually clears that bar depends on the specified value and the categorisation of the dispute, which should be checked case by case rather than assumed.

A landlord holding post-dated or security cheques from a commercial tenant for rent, a common feature of Indian commercial leases in both cities, has the additional option of proceeding under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 if a cheque is dishonoured on presentation, a route this site's companion article on cheque dishonour practice addresses separately; this criminal-law remedy runs alongside, rather than instead of, a civil claim for the underlying rent, and a landlord pursuing both should keep the two proceedings coordinated through the same counsel to avoid inconsistent positions being taken in each.

A practical sequence for negotiating a commercial lease in either city

A business taking commercial space in Kolkata or Noida does better working through the registration and rent-law questions before negotiating the commercial terms, rather than after. The first step is establishing the proposed term and rent, since both decide whether Section 107 of the Transfer of Property Act requires registration at all and, for a Kolkata lease, whether the rent clears the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act's exemption threshold. The second is deciding, for a Kolkata lease, whether registering the lease, which most landlords of serious commercial space will want to do in any case to take the tenancy outside the Act's eviction restrictions, changes the balance of negotiating leverage in a way either side should account for in the commercial terms, such as the length of notice required to terminate or the grounds on which either party can end the lease early.

The third step is pricing the stamp duty and registration cost into the deal honestly, checking the current Uttar Pradesh capped rate for a Noida lease of up to ten years, or the applicable West Bengal stamp schedule for a Kolkata lease, before the parties finalise who bears that cost, since this is frequently negotiated as part of the overall deal and gets harder to revisit once heads of terms are agreed. The fourth is drafting the lock-in, escalation, security deposit and sub-letting clauses with specific, calculable figures and clear consent mechanisms rather than general language, given that the statutory backstops on all four points are thin to non-existent for most commercial leases in both cities once they fall outside the respective rent statutes.

The fifth and final step is confirming, in writing within the lease itself if possible, which eviction and recovery route will actually apply if the relationship breaks down, the ordinary civil suit route, the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act route, or, for a Noida lease that might fall within it, the Uttar Pradesh Urban Buildings Act's Prescribed Authority route, so that neither side is left working this out for the first time only once a dispute has already started and time is already short.

A sixth step, often left until too late, is keeping the paperwork trail current throughout the life of the lease rather than only at signing: copies of every rent receipt and payment confirmation, any written consent given for sub-letting or alteration, correspondence about repairs or defects, and any amendment or side letter varying the original terms. Several of the disputes referred to in this piece turned less on what the original lease said than on what the parties actually did afterward, whether dues were paid and accepted without protest, whether a sub-letting was tacitly allowed, or whether a notice was genuinely received, and a business that can produce this record cleanly is in a far stronger position than one relying on memory or a single signed document from years earlier.

Where the position remains unsettled

Several points in this comparison deserve a flag rather than a firm answer. The precise current rent threshold under Section 3(f)(i) of the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, reported at ten thousand rupees in a Calcutta High Court decision, may have been revisited since that decision, and a reader relying on this threshold for a specific Kolkata tenancy should confirm the current figure against the Act's text rather than this piece. The exact slab structure of Uttar Pradesh's recently capped stamp duty on lease agreements up to ten years was not confirmed from the sources gathered for this piece beyond the fact that a cap now exists, and the actual rate payable on a specific Noida lease should be confirmed with the Stamp and Registration Department or a local registration practitioner before a transaction is finalised.

Whether, and to what extent, the Uttar Pradesh Urban Buildings Act's general exemption for newly constructed buildings, and its rent-based exemption, apply in practice to commercial premises specifically within Gautam Buddh Nagar, given the district's relatively recent and ongoing development under the separate Uttar Pradesh Industrial Area Development Act framework addressed in this site's companion article on industrial and commercial plots in Noida and Greater Noida, was not confirmed by a dedicated source examined for this piece, and a business taking commercial space in Noida should get this specifically checked rather than assume either that the Act does or does not apply to its particular building.

Finally, the precise reasoning behind the 2023 Delhi High Court decision reported as invalidating a liquidated damages clause in a lease carrying a lock-in period was not available from the sources gathered for this piece beyond a single commentary's headline, and is accordingly left out of the body of this article rather than described in terms this piece cannot fully stand behind; a reader with a live dispute turning on a liquidated damages clause in a lock-in period should have that decision's full text checked directly rather than relying on any general statement about its effect.

A commercial lease in Kolkata and one in Noida can look identical on the page and still be governed by entirely different rules the moment a dispute arises, because the two states have built different exemption structures around the same central registration requirement, and because West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh route eviction through different kinds of forums once their respective rent statutes are actually engaged. Working out which regime applies, registered or unregistered, within the rent statute or outside it, before signing rather than after, is the single most useful thing either a landlord or a tenant can do in either city.

The deeper common thread across both cities is that once a commercial lease clears the relevant exemption thresholds, which most serious commercial leases do, the lease document itself, not any statutory rent-control scheme, becomes the real law of the relationship. That makes careful, specific drafting of the lock-in, escalation, deposit, sub-letting and termination clauses considerably more important for a commercial tenant or landlord in either Kolkata or Noida than it would be for a residential tenancy still squarely within the protective reach of either state's rent statute.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a commercial lease in Kolkata or Noida always need to be registered?

Only if its term exceeds one year, it runs from year to year, or it reserves a yearly rent, under Section 107 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, read with Section 17(1)(d) of the Registration Act, 1908. A shorter lease can be made by a written agreement that is not registered, or even orally if accompanied by delivery of possession, though a written, dated record is always the safer practice.

Why would a Kolkata landlord want to register a commercial lease if it is not strictly required?

Because the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997 exempts tenancies created by a registered lease from its coverage, taking the tenancy outside the Act's restrictive eviction grounds and placing it under the more flexible ordinary law of leases instead. Many Kolkata commercial landlords register deliberately for this reason, independent of whether Section 107 would otherwise have required it.

Does the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act apply to high-rent commercial premises in Kolkata?

Generally not. Section 3(f)(i) of the Act exempts non-residential premises let above a specified monthly rent, reported at ten thousand rupees in a Calcutta High Court decision, and most commercial leases in Kolkata's established business districts are let well above that figure, so they typically fall outside the Act's protection regardless of registration.

What happens if a commercial lease that should have been registered was left unregistered?

The document cannot create the lease it purports to grant, following the Supreme Court's position in Anthony v. K.C. Ittoop & Sons, but the tenant's possession does not become unlawful; the tenancy is instead treated as running month to month under Section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act. A 2023 Supreme Court decision in a Kolkata dispute, Paul Rubber Industries v. Amit Chand Mitra, confirmed that the unregistered document may not even be usable as evidence where the nature of the tenant's possession is itself the central dispute.

Can a landlord claim the full remaining rent under a lock-in clause if a tenant exits early?

Not automatically. Following the Delhi High Court's ruling in Tower Vision India v. Procall, a landlord claiming lock-in rent still has to plead and prove the actual loss suffered, since a figure fixed in the lease operates as a ceiling on damages under Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act rather than an amount payable regardless of loss.

Can a commercial tenant sublet the premises without the landlord's consent?

It depends on which law governs the tenancy. Where the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act applies to a Kolkata tenancy, the landlord's written consent is required by the Act itself. Outside that Act, and generally in Noida, the ordinary Transfer of Property Act position allows sub-letting unless the lease itself prohibits it, so most commercial leases address the point expressly in the lease rather than relying on either statutory default.

How does a landlord evict a commercial tenant in Noida?

It depends on whether the tenancy falls within the Uttar Pradesh Urban Buildings (Regulation of Letting, Rent and Eviction) Act, 1972. If it does, eviction proceeds before a Prescribed Authority on specified statutory grounds, with an appeal to an Appellate Authority. If the tenancy is exempt, commonly because of the rent charged or because the building is still within a new-construction exemption period, the landlord instead files an ordinary civil suit for ejectment under the Transfer of Property Act.

Is there a cap on security deposits for commercial leases in Kolkata or Noida?

No statutory cap was confirmed for commercial leases in either state. Security deposit terms, including the amount, the heads against which it can be adjusted, and the refund timeline, are governed almost entirely by what the lease itself provides, making careful drafting of this clause more important than it would be for a residential tenancy with statutory protection.
