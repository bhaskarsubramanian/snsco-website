Enforcing an Indian Decree Abroad: Taking a Money Judgment to the UK, the USA and Canada

An Indian decree does not travel on its own. Each destination country has its own rulebook, and a judgment creditor who does not learn it in advance usually loses a year to the wrong first step.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A decree from an Indian court is a piece of paper that means something in India and nothing, by itself, anywhere else. Getting a foreign court to treat it as a debt that can be collected against a bank account, a house or a business abroad takes a separate legal step in that country, and the step is different in London, in a US state court and in a Canadian province. This piece sets out what each route actually requires, where the law is settled and where it still depends on the facts, so that a decree holder chasing a debtor's assets abroad knows which door to knock on first.

Key points

  • The United Kingdom and India recognise each other's superior court judgments under a reciprocal statutory scheme going back to 1958, so an Indian High Court or Supreme Court money decree can usually be registered directly in England and Wales rather than re-litigated.
  • Registration under the Foreign Judgments (Reciprocal Enforcement) Act 1933 must happen within six years of the Indian judgment, and that clock is not restarted by the act of registration itself.
  • The United States has no federal statute or treaty on this subject at all. Recognition is governed by the law of the state where enforcement is sought, under either a 2005 or 1962 version of the Uniform Foreign-Country Money Judgments Recognition Act, or, in states that have adopted neither, by common law comity.
  • A US court asked to recognise an Indian decree will not re-open the merits, but it can refuse recognition on specific statutory grounds: lack of personal or subject-matter jurisdiction in the Indian court, inadequate notice, fraud, or conflict with the recognising state's own public policy.
  • Canada has no treaty or reciprocal statute with India at all. A decree holder must bring a fresh civil action on the judgment debt in the relevant province, though the Supreme Court of Canada's 2015 ruling in Chevron Corp. v. Yaiguaje made that action considerably easier to bring than it once was.
  • Limitation periods differ sharply by country and even by Canadian province: six years in England under the 1933 Act, a basic two years from discovery for a common law enforcement action in Ontario, and a patchwork of state-specific periods in the US that can run as long as fifteen years.
  • Before instructing counsel abroad, a decree holder should have a certified copy of the decree, proof that the time for appeal has expired or that any appeal has been finally decided, an apostille from India's Ministry of External Affairs where the destination recognises the Hague Apostille Convention, and a worked-out calculation of principal and accrued interest.
  • None of these routes are quick. Even a straightforward registration in England takes months, and a common law suit in Canada or the US can take over a year before a creditor has anything to execute against.

Two different problems that get confused with each other

There is a well-worn question in Indian litigation about what happens when a foreign court's judgment arrives here and a debtor's assets sit in India. That question has its own statutory answer in Section 13 and Section 44A of the Code of Civil Procedure, and it has been covered elsewhere on this site in the context of a foreign money decree being enforced against an NRI's assets in India. This piece is about the opposite direction: an Indian court has already decided the case, the decree holder has a piece of paper from a Delhi High Court or a Calcutta High Court suit, and the debtor, or the debtor's money, is in London, New York or Toronto.

The two problems look similar on paper because both involve a judgment crossing a border, but the law that governs each direction is entirely separate. India's reciprocity with a handful of notified countries under Section 44A works for decrees coming into India from those countries. It says nothing about what the United Kingdom, the United States or Canada will do with a decree going out from India, and each of those three destinations answers that question on its own terms, under its own domestic law.

A decree holder who assumes that UK or US courts will treat an Indian money decree as self-executing, or who assumes the opposite, that nothing short of a brand new Indian-style suit will do, will usually waste time. The truth sits between those two assumptions and differs by country.

This piece deals only with civil money decrees from Indian courts against an individual or company debtor. It does not cover enforcing a decree against the Government of India or a state government, which raises separate questions of sovereign immunity in all three destination countries, nor does it cover family court orders for maintenance or custody, which are generally enforced, where they are enforced at all, through different statutory schemes specific to family law rather than the civil judgment regimes discussed below.

The United Kingdom: a reciprocal scheme that actually works both ways

England and Wales are the one destination among the three covered here where India benefits from a genuine, long-standing reciprocal arrangement. The Foreign Judgments (Reciprocal Enforcement) Act 1933 lets the UK extend a registration procedure to judgments from courts of a country the Lord Chancellor has designated, and India was designated by the Reciprocal Enforcement of Judgments (India) Order 1958 (S.I. 1958/425), which replaced an earlier 1953 order and names the Indian courts whose judgments qualify as coming from a 'superior court' for this purpose. In practical terms, a money decree from the Supreme Court of India or from a High Court exercising its ordinary civil jurisdiction falls within the scheme; the 1958 Order's schedule also extends to District Courts and any other Indian court whose civil jurisdiction carries no pecuniary limit, so the exclusion is narrower than it first appears; only a decree from a court whose jurisdiction is monetarily capped falls outside the scheme and would have to be enforced in England by a fresh common law action instead.

Where the 1933 Act applies, the decree holder does not sue afresh. The holder applies to the English High Court to have the Indian judgment registered, and once registered, it has the same force and effect for enforcement purposes as a judgment originally given by that English court. The judgment debtor can apply to set the registration aside on a defined set of grounds, among them that the original court lacked jurisdiction over the debtor by the standards English private international law applies, that the debtor did not receive notice of the Indian proceedings in time to defend them, that the judgment was obtained by fraud, or that enforcing it would be contrary to English public policy. None of these grounds allow the English court to reconsider whether the Indian court reached the right result on the facts or the law; they go only to the integrity and fairness of how the result was reached.

The registration application carries a hard time limit. Section 2(1) of the 1933 Act allows an application within six years of the date of the Indian judgment, calculated, where the judgment has been the subject of an appeal, from the date of the final appellate decision rather than the original one. That window is unforgiving. English case law on judgments from other reciprocating countries has treated it as a limitation on the court's jurisdiction to register at all, not a procedural technicality a court can waive out of sympathy for a late applicant, and the English courts have made clear that registering the judgment does not restart a fresh limitation clock for later execution steps against the debtor's assets.

United Kingdom: when the statutory route is not available

A decree from an Indian court that does not qualify as a superior court judgment under the 1958 Order, or one more than six years old, is not beyond reach in England. The older common law route survives alongside the statutory one: the decree holder treats the foreign judgment as establishing a debt and sues on that debt in an ordinary English civil claim. The English court conducts a far narrower inquiry than it would in a dispute about the underlying facts. It asks whether the Indian court had jurisdiction over the defendant in the internationally recognised sense (broadly, that the defendant was present in India, agreed to India's jurisdiction, or submitted to the proceedings by defending them on the merits), and whether any of the standard defences, fraud, a breach of natural justice, or conflict with English public policy, are made out. If none are, the English court gives summary judgment on the debt without reopening the merits.

This route takes longer and costs more than registration, because it is a lawsuit rather than an application, complete with its own procedural timetable and the debtor's right to contest jurisdiction and defences at a hearing. It also carries its own limitation period, generally treated as a six-year period under section 24 of the Limitation Act 1980, which bars a fresh action brought upon a judgment, including a foreign judgment sued on as a debt, more than six years after it became enforceable, though the exact characterisation can turn on how the claim is pleaded and is worth confirming with English counsel on the specific facts rather than assumed.

The United States: fifty states, no single answer

The United States has never joined any multilateral treaty on recognising foreign money judgments, and it has no bilateral treaty with India on the subject. Recognition and enforcement is a matter of state law, which means the first practical question for a decree holder is not what American law says but which state's law applies, usually the state where the debtor's assets or the debtor itself can be found.

Most states have adopted some version of the Uniform Foreign-Country Money Judgments Recognition Act, either the 1962 original or the 2005 revision, and the two differ in important ways. The 2005 Act added two grounds of non-recognition the 1962 Act did not have: a systemic lack of due process in the foreign country's courts generally (not just unfairness in the particular case), and substantial doubt about the integrity of the rendering court in the specific matter. The 2005 Act also places the burden of proving any ground for non-recognition squarely on the party resisting enforcement, which works in a decree holder's favour once the judgment is shown to be final and for a fixed sum of money. A handful of states still apply the older 1962 Act, and a smaller number apply neither statute, relying instead on common law comity principles that trace back to the US Supreme Court's 1895 decision in Hilton v. Guyot. The practical effect across all three regimes is broadly similar, but the fine print, including which grounds are mandatory and which are left to the court's discretion, is not identical, and a decree holder should expect counsel to confirm the specific statute, or absence of one, in the state where the application will be filed.

Neither version of the Uniform Act, nor the common law approach, lets an American court reopen the merits of the Indian decree. What they do allow is a structured challenge on specific grounds: that the Indian court lacked personal jurisdiction over the debtor or subject-matter jurisdiction over the dispute by standards the American court recognises, that the debtor did not receive adequate notice of the Indian proceedings in time to defend, that the judgment was obtained by fraud, that it conflicts with the public policy of the recognising state, or, under the 2005 Act specifically, that it comes from a judicial system that does not provide impartial tribunals or procedures compatible with due process generally. A useful illustration of how seriously American courts take the public policy ground, even though it concerns an English rather than an Indian judgment, is Bachchan v. India Abroad Publications, a 1992 decision of the New York Supreme Court refusing to enforce an English libel judgment against an American publisher on the ground that the English law of defamation, without the First Amendment-style protections American law requires, offended New York public policy. The case did not involve India as the rendering court, but it shows the kind of scrutiny a foreign money judgment, Indian or otherwise, will receive if the recognising state's public policy is genuinely engaged, as distinct from a mere disagreement with the foreign court's reasoning.

United States: procedure, proof and the limitation problem

Bringing an Indian decree to an American court for recognition is itself a civil proceeding, filed in the state court (or, in some circumstances, a federal court sitting in diversity) where the debtor or the debtor's assets are located. The decree holder has to plead and prove that the Indian judgment is final, conclusive and for a fixed or readily calculable sum of money; judgments for taxes, fines, penalties, or other non-compensatory relief generally fall outside the Uniform Act's scope and have to be pursued, if at all, by other means. Authenticated or certified copies of the decree and the underlying record, properly apostilled for use in the United States, are standard requirements, and an affidavit or expert evidence on Indian procedural law establishing finality is often needed because American courts cannot be assumed to know, for instance, when an Indian decree becomes immune from further appeal.

Limitation periods vary by state and are set by each state's own statute rather than by any uniform rule; some states tie the period to the Uniform Act's own text, others apply a general judgment-enforcement period, and a few extend it to as long as fifteen years provided the judgment remains enforceable in its country of origin. This variation matters because a decree that has sat unenforced for several years in India, perhaps while the creditor hunted for assets, can find itself time-barred in one state's courts and still within time in another's. Checking the specific state's limitation rule before filing, rather than assuming a single national period, is not optional.

Canada: no treaty, no reciprocal statute, a fresh action required

Canada sits in a position closer to the United States than to the United Kingdom. There is no treaty between Canada and India on the recognition of civil judgments, and India does not appear on the lists of reciprocating jurisdictions that most provinces maintain under their own Reciprocal Enforcement of Judgments Acts, statutes of that kind generally apply only to judgments from a jurisdiction the provincial government has specifically designated by regulation, and that designation has historically been reserved for other Canadian provinces, the United Kingdom and a short list of other Commonwealth jurisdictions. An Indian decree holder should proceed on the footing that the provincial reciprocal enforcement statute does not apply and that a fresh common law action on the judgment debt is the route available.

That common law action became considerably more workable after the Supreme Court of Canada's 2015 decision in Chevron Corp. v. Yaiguaje. Chevron had argued that a Canadian court needed a 'real and substantial connection' between the dispute or the defendant and Canada before it could even entertain an action to enforce a foreign judgment, mirroring the test Canadian courts apply when deciding whether they have jurisdiction over a fresh lawsuit on the underlying facts. The Supreme Court rejected that argument. It held that an action to recognise and enforce a foreign judgment is not the same kind of proceeding as an action on the underlying claim; the Canadian court is not being asked to decide the dispute, only to recognise that it has already been decided elsewhere, and jurisdiction to do that exists once the foreign court itself had a real and substantial connection to the original dispute, or the recognised traditional bases of jurisdiction, such as the defendant's presence or service, were satisfied in that foreign proceeding. The practical result is that an Indian decree holder bringing a Canadian enforcement action does not have to show that the debtor or the dispute had any particular connection to the Canadian province chosen; it is generally enough that the debtor can be served there or has assets there.

Once jurisdiction is established, the enforcing Canadian court applies a similarly narrow set of defences to those seen in England and the United States: a genuine jurisdictional defect in the original Indian proceeding, fraud, a breach of natural justice, or conflict with Canadian public policy. As with the other two jurisdictions, an error of Indian law or an unfavourable assessment of the facts by the Indian court is not itself a defence.

Canada: the limitation trap that catches more creditors than the merits ever do

Limitation periods in Canada are set province by province, and Ontario's experience is a useful warning for any decree holder tempted to assume that enforcing a judgment is not really a fresh legal claim with its own clock. The Ontario Court of Appeal has held that a common law action to enforce a foreign judgment is subject to the ordinary two-year basic limitation period under Ontario's Limitations Act, 2002, not the longer period, or the absence of any period, that applies to enforcing an order already made by an Ontario court itself. The reasoning is straightforward once stated: a foreign judgment is not yet an order of an Ontario court, and it only becomes enforceable there once the Ontario proceeding to recognise it succeeds, so the shorter limitation period for ordinary civil claims applies to that proceeding rather than the longer period reserved for enforcing existing Ontario orders.

The two-year clock does not necessarily start on the date of the Indian decree itself. Ontario applies a discoverability principle, so the period can be read as beginning once the creditor knew, or ought reasonably to have known, that it had a claim worth pursuing, which in practice often means once any Indian appeal has run its course and the debtor's assets or presence in Ontario have become apparent. That flexibility helps a creditor who could not realistically have acted sooner, but it is not a substitute for moving promptly once those facts are known. Other Canadian provinces set their own limitation periods under their own legislation, and a province-by-province check is necessary rather than assumed from the Ontario position.

What a decree holder needs in hand before instructing counsel abroad

Every one of the three routes above starts from the same basic documentary foundation, and gathering it in India before engaging counsel abroad saves real time. A certified copy of the decree, together with the plaint, the operative order and, where relevant, any appellate order affirming it, is the starting point. Proof that the decree is final, meaning either that the period for filing an appeal in India has expired without an appeal being filed, or that any appeal filed has been finally disposed of, needs to be capable of being stated clearly and supported by a certificate from the Indian court registry or counsel's own affidavit, because foreign courts will not independently investigate Indian appellate timelines.

Documents prepared in India for use in any of these three countries will generally need to be authenticated for use abroad. India has been a member of the Hague Apostille Convention since 2005, and the Ministry of External Affairs, acting through its designated agencies, issues an apostille that is accepted without further legalisation in every other Convention member state, which includes the United Kingdom and most provinces' recognised document chains in Canada; the United States is also a Convention member, so an apostilled Indian document should be accepted there too, though local counsel should confirm the specific court's documentary requirements in each case since court practice on this point is not always uniform.

A precise calculation of the sum due, principal plus interest at whatever rate the Indian decree specifies, carried forward to the date of the foreign application, is worth preparing in advance rather than left to the foreign court to work out from the decree text alone. Where the decree is silent on a post-decree interest rate, or where the rate specified creates ambiguity about whether it continues after the decree date, that ambiguity should be resolved, if possible, by a clarificatory application in the Indian court itself before the foreign proceeding begins, since asking an English, American or Canadian court to interpret an unclear Indian decree is slower and less certain than asking the court that wrote it.

Practical limits: what none of these routes will do quickly or cheaply

None of the three jurisdictions covered here offer anything resembling an instant or low-cost process. Registration under the UK's 1933 Act is the fastest of the three because it is an application rather than a lawsuit, but it still typically runs several months once service on the debtor, any contest to the registration, and the court's own listing time are accounted for. A common law action in the United States or Canada is a full civil proceeding, and even an uncontested one rarely concludes in under a year once filing, service (which itself can be complicated where the debtor is outside the forum state or province), and the court's own calendar are factored in.

A debtor with no identifiable assets in the country chosen defeats the exercise regardless of how clean the legal route is. Before starting any of these proceedings, locating the debtor's bank accounts, real property or business interests in the specific jurisdiction is worth doing properly, including through local asset-tracing counsel where the debtor is known to be evasive, because a judgment registered or recognised against a debtor with nothing to seize achieves very little beyond the legal milestone itself. Where there is a real risk of assets being moved or dissipated once proceedings start, interim relief such as a freezing or Mareva-type injunction, available in all three jurisdictions in some form, is worth raising with local counsel at the outset rather than after the debtor has had notice.

Serving the debtor once a foreign proceeding is filed

Once filed, a registration application in England, or a recognition suit in an American state or a Canadian province, still has to be served on the judgment debtor before it can proceed to a result the decree holder can rely on, and service across a border is rarely as simple as posting a letter. Where the debtor is physically present in the country where enforcement is sought, ordinary local rules of service apply and the exercise is no different from serving any other defendant there. Where the debtor has moved on again, perhaps back to India or to a third country, service has to follow whatever rules the enforcing court applies to serving parties outside its own territory, which in Convention countries often means the Hague Service Convention's own channels rather than an informal method the decree holder might otherwise prefer.

Courts in all three countries have shown some willingness to permit alternative service, such as service by email or through a debtor's known local counsel, where the usual channels have failed or would cause unreasonable delay, but this is granted case by case rather than assumed, and a decree holder should expect to justify the request with evidence of earlier failed attempts rather than ask for it as a first resort.

Converting the decree into a figure the foreign court can act on

An Indian decree expressed in rupees does not automatically become a figure in pounds, dollars or Canadian dollars the moment it crosses a border; the foreign court has to convert it, and the date chosen for that conversion can meaningfully change the amount recovered given how currencies move over the months or years a dispute takes to resolve. English, American and Canadian courts have each developed their own rules on which date governs a currency conversion for a foreign money judgment, and the general tendency across all three is to convert as close as practicable to the date of actual payment or enforcement rather than the date of the original Indian decree, on the reasoning that a creditor should not bear the risk of currency movement between judgment and payment where the debtor has simply delayed paying.

Interest raises a related but separate problem. An Indian decree typically specifies a rate of post-decree interest under Indian procedural rules, and that rate does not necessarily match what the enforcing country's own law would award on a domestic judgment of the same age. Most of the regimes discussed here will enforce the interest rate the Indian decree itself specifies, as part of the sum being recognised, rather than substitute their own domestic rate, but this should be confirmed with local counsel rather than assumed, particularly where the Indian decree's interest provision is unusually drafted or silent on the period after the decree date.

A decree is not the same thing as an arbitral award, and the two routes should not be mixed up

Everything above concerns a civil decree from an Indian court. A foreign arbitral award arising from an arbitration seated in India, or an Indian arbitral award a party wants to enforce abroad, follows an entirely different and generally more favourable regime under the New York Convention of 1958, to which the United Kingdom, the United States and Canada are all parties, as is India. This site's separate article on enforcing foreign arbitral awards in India covers that regime from the India-facing side. The point worth making here is a narrow one: a decree holder who also happens to hold an arbitral award covering the same or a related dispute should check which instrument gives the faster, cleaner route into a particular country's courts before assuming the court decree is the only tool available, because in several cases the Convention route will be quicker precisely because it was designed for cross-border recognition from the outset, unlike an ordinary civil decree.

Conversely, a decree that merely confirms or gives effect to an arbitral award under Section 36 or Section 49 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 is still, for the purposes of the countries discussed in this piece, a court decree once it has been passed, and the analysis above applies to it in the same way as to any other Indian money decree. The distinction matters only at the stage of deciding which original instrument, the award or the decree confirming it, to present abroad.

Choosing a country first when the debtor has assets in more than one

A debtor who has moved abroad rarely leaves assets in only one country, and a decree holder facing a choice between the United Kingdom, the United States and Canada should weigh more than which route looks procedurally simplest. Registration under the UK's 1933 Act is usually the quickest and least expensive of the three once the six-year window and the superior-court requirement are satisfied, which makes England the obvious first stop where the debtor has any meaningful assets there, even if the larger pool of assets sits elsewhere.

Where the debtor's assets are concentrated in the United States or Canada and nothing meaningful sits in the UK, the choice between an American state and a Canadian province often turns on practical factors unrelated to the underlying law: where the debtor can be reliably served, where local counsel has direct experience with Indian judgments specifically, and how quickly a court in that state or province typically lists an uncontested or lightly contested recognition matter. None of the three legal regimes described here is dramatically more favourable to a decree holder than the others once a jurisdiction and its specific statute or common law rule are correctly identified; the real variable is usually the debtor's conduct, not the forum's law.

A decree holder with assets spread across more than one of these three countries does not have to choose only one. Nothing in the law of any of the three prevents parallel enforcement proceedings being filed in more than one jurisdiction at the same time, provided the decree holder is careful to credit any amount actually recovered in one proceeding against the balance still being pursued in another, so that the same underlying debt is not collected twice over. Running proceedings in parallel costs more in fees across the board, so it generally makes sense only where the amount at stake is large enough to justify it and the debtor's assets genuinely cannot be concentrated in a single country.

What this costs, and who bears it

None of these three routes are available without paying for them, and a decree holder should budget for local counsel's fees, court filing fees, and the cost of authenticating and translating documents, before deciding whether the amount at stake justifies the exercise. Registration under the UK's 1933 Act is comparatively cheap because it is an application rather than a full suit, but court fees and solicitors' fees still apply, and a debtor who contests the registration turns it into something closer to a mini-trial on jurisdiction and the statutory defences, with costs rising accordingly.

A common law action in the United States or Canada carries the full cost structure of ordinary civil litigation in that country, including the possibility of the debtor filing a detailed defence, seeking discovery on the underlying Indian proceeding, or appealing an adverse decision on recognition. English, American and Canadian courts generally have the power to award costs to a successful decree holder against the judgment debtor, which offsets some of this exposure if the recognition proceeding succeeds, but a decree holder should not assume full cost recovery, particularly in the American states where each side commonly bears its own legal fees regardless of outcome unless a specific statute or contract term says otherwise.

Whether any of this is worth pursuing for a modest sum should be treated as a genuine commercial question rather than a point of principle. Spending a meaningful sum on foreign counsel to chase a debt that barely covers the fees produces a poor outcome even for a decree holder who succeeds on every single legal point along the way, and a short, candid cost estimate from local counsel before filing anything is usually the most useful single step in this entire process. That estimate should also factor in the realistic prospect of the debtor filing a contested defence, since a case that looked straightforward on paper can turn considerably more expensive once a debtor with something to protect decides to fight the recognition proceeding rather than let it pass unopposed.

An Indian decree is a strong starting point in all three jurisdictions discussed here, stronger in the United Kingdom than in the United States or Canada, because none of the three will reopen the underlying dispute on its merits once satisfied that the Indian proceeding was fair and the Indian court had jurisdiction. The work that remains is procedural rather than substantive: choosing the right statutory or common law route, respecting a limitation period that can be shorter than instinct suggests, and arriving with the documentary proof a foreign court will expect rather than assume.

Decree holders who plan this stage before the Indian litigation even concludes, by keeping limitation dates, appeal timelines and interest calculations in order from the outset, generally reach a foreign court with a cleaner case than those who start preparing only after the debtor has already moved money out of reach.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I enforce any Indian court order in England under the 1933 Act, or only certain kinds?

Only money judgments from the courts the 1958 Order designates as superior courts of India, which include the Supreme Court, the High Courts, all District Courts, and any other Indian court whose civil jurisdiction is subject to no pecuniary limit, qualify for registration under the 1933 Act. A decree from a court whose jurisdiction is monetarily capped, or an order that is not a judgment for a fixed sum of money, falls outside the scheme and would need to be enforced, if at all, by a fresh common law action instead.

Does the six-year limit under the UK's 1933 Act run from the original Indian judgment or from when an appeal was decided?

Where the Indian judgment was appealed, the six years generally runs from the date of the final appellate decision rather than the original trial court judgment. Where there was no appeal, it runs from the date of the judgment itself. Either way, the date needs to be confirmed precisely before filing, since the application fails outright once the period has run.

Is there a treaty between India and the United States on enforcing court judgments?

No. There is no federal statute or bilateral treaty between India and the United States covering recognition of civil money judgments. Enforcement depends entirely on the law of the individual American state where the debtor or the debtor's assets are found, under that state's own version of the Uniform Foreign-Country Money Judgments Recognition Act or, failing that, common law comity principles.

Will a US or Canadian court reconsider whether the Indian court decided the case correctly?

No, in neither country does the enforcing court re-examine the facts or the legal reasoning of the Indian judgment. The inquiry is limited to specific, defined grounds: whether the Indian court had jurisdiction by the recognising court's own standards, whether the debtor had proper notice and an opportunity to defend, whether the judgment was obtained by fraud, and whether enforcing it would conflict with the recognising jurisdiction's public policy.

Is Canada a reciprocating jurisdiction for Indian judgments the way the UK is?

No. Canada has no treaty with India and India does not generally appear among the countries most Canadian provinces have designated as reciprocating jurisdictions under their own provincial statutes. An Indian decree holder in Canada should expect to bring a fresh common law action on the judgment debt rather than a simplified registration application.

How long do I have to bring an enforcement action in Ontario?

Ontario courts have held that the ordinary two-year basic limitation period under the Limitations Act, 2002 applies to a common law action to enforce a foreign judgment, measured from when the creditor knew or ought to have known it had an enforceable claim, which is not necessarily the same date as the Indian decree itself. Other provinces have their own limitation rules, which should be checked separately.

Do I need to get my Indian decree apostilled before using it abroad?

In most cases, yes. India has been a member of the Hague Apostille Convention since 2005, and an apostille from the Ministry of External Affairs' designated agencies is generally accepted without further legalisation in the United Kingdom, the United States and Canada, all of which are also Convention members. Local counsel should still be asked to confirm the specific court's documentary requirements, since practice is not perfectly uniform across every court in every one of these countries.

What happens if the debtor has already moved their money out of the country where I want to enforce?

A judgment recognised or registered against a debtor with no traceable assets in that jurisdiction has limited practical value until assets are located, possibly in another country entirely. Where dissipation is a real risk, interim relief such as a freezing injunction is available in all three jurisdictions in some form and is worth raising with local counsel before the debtor is put on notice of the enforcement proceeding.
