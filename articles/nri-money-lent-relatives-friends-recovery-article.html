Money You Lent to Family or Friends in India: What It Actually Takes to Get It Back

An NRI who wires money to a brother, a childhood friend or a cousin's new business rarely treats it as a transaction that needs documentation. Years later, when the relationship has soured and the money has not come back, that same transfer often turns out to be the only evidence anyone has. This is what the law actually requires to recover it, and why so many of these loans fail in court for reasons that had nothing to do with whether the debt was genuine.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 5 November 2026 · Law stated as on 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A loan between relatives or friends is, in the eyes of the law, exactly the same kind of debt as a loan between strangers. What differs is the evidence. A bank or an NBFC lending to a stranger signs a loan agreement, takes security, and keeps a file. A person lending to a brother-in-law or a college friend usually does none of this, because asking for paperwork can feel like an accusation of distrust directed at someone they care about. That gap in documentation, not any defect in the underlying claim, is what sinks most of these recoveries. This article sets out the recovery routes actually available to an NRI lender, what each one needs to succeed, and how to fix the documentation problem before it becomes fatal rather than after.

Key points

  • A bank transfer by itself proves money moved from one account to another; it does not, on its own, prove the money moved as a loan rather than as a gift, an investment, or repayment of something else. Written evidence that both sides treated it as a loan, however informal, a message, an email, a signed acknowledgment, matters as much as the transfer record itself.
  • An unstamped or insufficiently stamped promissory note or loan agreement can be barred from being admitted as evidence under Section 35 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899. Courts are divided on whether the underlying debt can still be proved by other evidence once the note itself is excluded, so the safer course is to stamp the document correctly when it is signed, not to rely on being able to fix it later.
  • A civil recovery suit under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, the summary suit procedure, is open only where the claim is based on a written contract, a bill of exchange, a hundi or a promissory note; it is considerably faster than an ordinary suit because the defendant must apply for leave to defend and can be denied it if they raise no triable issue. A loan with nothing in writing cannot use this route and has to proceed as an ordinary suit instead.
  • A Section 138 complaint under the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 is available only where the loan was repaid, in whole or part, by a cheque that was then dishonoured for insufficiency of funds or because it exceeded an arrangement with the bank; it is not a general remedy for an unpaid loan that was never represented by a cheque at all.
  • A money-lent suit generally has to be filed within three years, under the Limitation Act, 1963, though which article applies, and therefore exactly when that three years starts running, depends on whether the loan was repayable on demand, repayable on a fixed date, or had no repayment term at all; a written acknowledgment of the debt signed by the borrower before the period expires restarts the clock under Section 18 of the Act and is one of the most useful things an NRI lender can obtain even years into a stalled repayment.
  • FEMA's specific rupee-loan dispensation for an NRI and a resident is built around a defined close relative, with conditions including an interest-free loan, a minimum one-year maturity, and routing through a crossed cheque or bank transfer into the resident's or NRI's NRO account as applicable; lending to a friend who is not a close relative falls outside that specific route entirely and should be handled as an ordinary, properly documented remittance rather than assumed to enjoy the same dispensation.

The loan nobody wrote down

The fact pattern is familiar to anyone who has handled NRI recovery disputes. Money moves from an account in the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom to an account in India, sent to help a sibling through a medical emergency, to support a friend starting a business, or simply because a relative asked and it felt awkward to refuse. No promissory note is signed. No interest rate is discussed. The sender does not think of themselves as a creditor, and the recipient, at the time, probably does not think of themselves as a debtor in any formal sense either. Years pass. The business does not succeed, or the relationship changes, or the money is simply never mentioned again, and what started as an act of family or friendship becomes a debt the NRI wants back and the other side is unwilling or unable to pay.

What this article sets out is not a single remedy but a map of the routes that exist, legal notice, civil suit, summary suit, a cheque-bounce complaint where one applies, and what each one needs to work. It also addresses the two questions that come up in almost every one of these matters: what actually counts as proof that the money was a loan at all, and how the money should have moved, and should move if lent again, to avoid creating a second problem on top of the first.

What actually counts as proof of a loan

A bank transfer record shows that money moved from the lender's account to the borrower's. It does not, by itself, show why. A court asked to decide whether a given transfer was a loan, a gift, an investment in a venture, or repayment of some earlier debt the lender owed the recipient, will look beyond the transfer to whatever else exists: a message confirming the amount is to be repaid, an email discussing a repayment date, a signed acknowledgment, witnesses who were told at the time that the payment was a loan, or the recipient's own later conduct, such as a part payment back, that is consistent with treating it as a debt rather than a gift.

This is why the single most useful document an NRI lender can obtain, even for a loan already advanced without paperwork, is a written acknowledgment signed by the borrower confirming the amount, that it was a loan, and ideally when it is to be repaid. This does not need to be elaborate. A short letter or even a clear WhatsApp or email exchange in which the borrower accepts the sum as a loan carries real evidentiary weight, and, as covered later in this article, can also serve the separate and valuable function of resetting the limitation clock under Section 18 of the Limitation Act, 1963, if it is signed before the original limitation period runs out.

Where no such acknowledgment exists and the borrower now disputes that the money was ever a loan, the lender's case rests on surrounding evidence: the timing and pattern of transfers, any contemporaneous messages, the absence of any plausible alternative explanation for the payment, and witness testimony. This is a materially weaker position than a signed document, and it is the position a large share of family loans are actually fought from, which is itself the strongest argument for documenting a loan properly at the time it is made, not after it has gone bad.

An NRI lender has one further, practical housekeeping task that a resident lender does not: keeping copies of this evidence organised and accessible from abroad, rather than leaving the only copy on a phone or in an email account that could later be lost, changed, or become harder to access. Bank statements showing the transfer, any written acknowledgment, and the correspondence surrounding the loan should be saved in at least two places, ideally including a copy with counsel in India, and, where the original communication was in a regional language, a certified translation prepared early rather than scrambled together once litigation is already underway. None of this is legally required, but a lender who can hand their advocate a complete, organised file on the first consultation is in a far better position than one reconstructing events from memory several years after the money was sent.

The stamping trap

Where a promissory note or loan agreement was signed, a separate problem can defeat it before the merits are even reached. Section 35 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 bars an instrument that is not duly stamped, or stamped with an insufficient duty, from being admitted in evidence for any purpose, subject to limited exceptions including payment of the deficient duty and penalty at the time it is sought to be used. Courts have divided on what this means for the underlying debt once the note itself is kept out: one line of authority holds that if the note contains the entire contract between the parties, the creditor cannot fall back on oral evidence of the same debt, since that would defeat the purpose of the stamp law; another, tracing back to an Allahabad High Court decision in the 1940s, allows oral evidence of any terms the note itself does not fully cover; and some courts have allowed the document to be used for a collateral purpose even where it cannot be used to directly prove the loan.

The safer course for an NRI lender, by a considerable margin, is not to rely on any of these exceptions being available later, but to have a promissory note or loan agreement properly stamped under the relevant State's Stamp Act at the time it is executed, and to keep the receipt for that stamp duty with the document itself. Where an existing, informally signed note turns out to be unstamped or under-stamped, it can generally still be validated by paying the deficient duty and any penalty before it is relied on in court, which is a problem worth fixing as soon as it is discovered rather than leaving for the day the note actually needs to be produced.

Routing the loan correctly: FEMA and a close relative's special dispensation

FEMA's rules on rupee borrowing and lending between a resident and a non-resident set out a specific, conditional route for a loan between an NRI or PIO and a close relative, defined by reference to the relative definition in the Companies Act, 2013. Where that relationship exists, the loan must be interest-free, carry a minimum maturity of one year, be made by a crossed cheque or electronic transfer rather than cash, and stay within the lender's applicable remittance limits where the lender is the resident side of the transaction. Repayment, where the borrower is the NRI relative, is generally required to be credited to that person's NRO account and is not freely repatriable abroad; a separate, older RBI allowance permits a close relative in India to repay such a loan on the NRI's behalf, but only where the loan was itself taken for a housing purpose.

This dispensation is built around the close relative relationship specifically, and it does not extend, on the clear wording the regulations use, to a loan between an NRI and a friend who is not a relative within that statutory definition. For that category of loan, which covers a meaningful share of the disputes this article is about, there is no equivalent named RBI route to point to, and the safer practical approach is to treat the transfer as an ordinary remittance into the borrower's bank account, properly documented by a loan agreement or acknowledgment as described above, rather than assuming any special FEMA exemption applies. Where significant sums are involved, confirming the specific position with a FEMA specialist before the transfer is made, rather than after a dispute arises, is time well spent.

A further point worth flagging for the close relative route itself: the regulations, as currently reported, speak more clearly to a resident borrowing from an NRI relative than to the reverse flow of an NRI lending directly to a resident relative, and secondary commentary on this point is itself uncertain about exactly how the reverse direction is treated. An NRI lending to a resident relative should not assume the interest-free, one-year-maturity conditions automatically apply in their favour without confirming the current position, since getting this wrong can create a FEMA compliance problem layered on top of the original recovery dispute.

Before you sue: the legal notice

A legal notice demanding repayment is not, for an ordinary private loan between individuals, a mandatory precondition to filing a civil suit, unlike a claim against the government or a public officer, where Section 80 of the Code of Civil Procedure does require advance notice. For a family or friend loan, sending one anyway is still worthwhile for three practical reasons: it creates a dated, written demand that can later help fix exactly when the cause of action to sue accrued, it sometimes resolves the matter without litigation once the other side understands the lender is serious, and, where the loan was represented by a cheque that has since bounced, it becomes the mandatory statutory notice that Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act requires before any criminal complaint can be filed.

There is no single prescribed statutory format for an ordinary demand notice, but it should set out the amount lent, when, the basis for treating it as a loan rather than anything else, and a clear deadline, commonly fifteen to thirty days, within which repayment is expected before further legal steps are taken. The notice, and proof that it was actually delivered, by registered post or courier with tracking, becomes part of the lender's evidence regardless of which route is pursued afterward, which is reason enough to have it drafted and sent by counsel rather than informally by the lender themselves.

A notice sent by an NRI from abroad carries one additional practical wrinkle worth planning for: service on a borrower in India can be delayed or complicated by holidays, an incorrect or outdated address, or simple non-collection of registered post, and the proof of delivery that later matters in court depends on getting this right the first time. Sending the notice through local counsel, to a verified current address, and by more than one method, registered post and email where an address for the latter is known, reduces the risk of a dispute later over whether notice was actually received, which is exactly the kind of procedural argument a reluctant borrower is likely to raise if the substance of the claim is otherwise hard to contest.

The civil recovery suit, and why Order XXXVII can be faster

An ordinary civil suit for recovery of money is always available, whether or not there is anything in writing, and proceeds through the full trial process: pleadings, evidence, and a final decree, which in a contested matter can take a considerable amount of time. Where the loan is evidenced by a written contract, a promissory note, a bill of exchange or a hundi, Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure offers a materially faster alternative, the summary suit procedure, precisely because it is designed for claims that should not need a full trial to establish.

Under this procedure, the defendant served with summons cannot simply contest the suit by filing an ordinary defence; they must apply for leave to defend, and the court will grant it only if the defence discloses a triable issue or a substantial question of fact or law, not a vague or general denial. Recent High Court decisions continue to apply this standard strictly, while also recognising that a genuine dispute, for instance a defendant's claim that the promissory note itself was fabricated, cannot simply be rejected without being examined; leave is sometimes granted on conditions, such as the defendant furnishing security for the claimed amount, rather than being refused outright. Once leave is granted, the matter proceeds as an ordinary suit from that point.

For an NRI lender, the practical advantage of Order XXXVII, where the facts allow it, is substantial: a borrower with no real defence, which describes a large share of these family and friend loans, often cannot clear the leave-to-defend threshold at all, letting the lender obtain a decree without the delay of a full trial. This is also the clearest practical reason, beyond the stamping and evidentiary points already covered, to insist on a written promissory note or loan agreement at the time money is lent; without one, this faster route is simply not available, whatever the merits of the underlying claim.

When Section 138 is available, and when it is not

Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 makes it an offence to issue a cheque for the discharge, in whole or in part, of a debt or other liability, where that cheque is returned unpaid because the account has insufficient funds or the amount exceeds an arrangement with the bank. This is a genuinely useful route where a loan, even an originally informal one, was later repaid or partly repaid by cheque and that cheque bounced; it is not available at all for a loan that was never represented by any cheque, since there is no dishonoured instrument for the provision to attach to.

The procedure is time-sensitive at every stage. A notice demanding payment of the cheque amount must be sent to the drawer within thirty days of receiving information from the bank that the cheque was dishonoured. If the drawer does not pay within fifteen days of receiving that notice, a complaint can be filed, and the complaint itself must be filed within one month of the date the cause of action arose, which is generally understood to be the point the fifteen-day payment window expired, though courts have sometimes extended this on a showing of sufficient cause for the delay. Missing these windows, particularly the thirty-day notice period, has been treated as fatal to the complaint, which makes prompt action on receiving a dishonour memo considerably more important than most first-time complainants appreciate.

A lender who holds only an informal loan, with nothing reduced to writing and no cheque ever exchanged, sometimes considers asking the borrower, at the point repayment becomes due, to issue a post-dated cheque for the outstanding amount. Where the borrower agrees, this is a legitimate and commonly used way of converting an otherwise undocumented debt into one that carries Section 138 exposure if it is not honoured; it does not retroactively create a criminal remedy for the original failure to repay in cash, but it does give the lender a materially stronger position going forward, provided the cheque is genuinely issued and not merely promised.

Filing and attending a Section 138 complaint from abroad

An NRI complainant does not need to personally file the complaint if someone else is properly authorised to do so. The Supreme Court settled a long-running conflict in A.C. Narayan v. State of Maharashtra, holding that a power of attorney holder can file a Section 138 complaint on the payee's behalf, subject to certain essential conditions, and more recent decisions, including the Court's ruling in M.S. Naresh Potteries v. M.S. Aarti Industries, have continued to uphold such complaints where the attorney holder is duly authorised and has personal knowledge of the underlying transaction. Courts have been clear that the attorney holder's personal knowledge of the transaction is what makes the complaint maintainable; an authorisation on paper without any real knowledge of the facts is not enough, and at least one High Court has held that a power of attorney holder can file the complaint but cannot themselves give evidence as the complainant, only as a witness.

For the accused side of a complaint, which an NRI family member sometimes finds themselves on, the general criminal procedure allows a Magistrate to dispense with personal attendance and permit appearance through a lawyer once summons has issued, now under Section 228 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which replaced the materially similar Section 205 of the former Code of Criminal Procedure from 1 July 2024 without changing the substance of the provision. This exemption is not automatic or permanent; it has to be applied for and can be withdrawn if the Magistrate later requires personal appearance, so an NRI accused who cannot travel should apply for it promptly rather than assuming distance alone will be accepted as a sufficient reason for non-appearance.

The clock that is already running

A suit for recovery of money lent is governed by the Limitation Act, 1963, and generally has to be filed within three years, though which specific article of the Act's schedule applies, and therefore when exactly that three years begins, depends on the nature of the loan. Where money was lent without any fixed date for repayment, Article 19 is generally treated as the applicable provision, with the period running from the date the loan was made. Where the loan was expressly made repayable on demand, Article 21 applies on broadly the same basis. At least one High Court has held that a simple suit for recovery of money does not always fall within either of these specific articles and can instead be governed by the residual three-year period under Article 113, running from when the right to sue actually accrues, which can matter where the loan's terms are unclear or disputed.

This is one of the more consequential gaps in how these disputes are actually handled: a family lender often waits years, out of reluctance to pressure a relative, before treating the non-payment as a legal problem, by which point the limitation period calculated from the date of the loan may already have run out, regardless of how clearly the debt is still owed as a moral and factual matter. Section 18 of the Limitation Act offers the one real safeguard against this. A written acknowledgment of the debt, signed by the borrower before the original limitation period expires, starts a fresh period running from the date of that acknowledgment, and no payment needs to accompany it for Section 18 to apply. A part payment made and recorded in writing before the original period expires has a similar resetting effect under Section 19.

The practical lesson for an NRI lender who has not yet sued is to obtain a signed acknowledgment of the outstanding amount from the borrower now, while it is still possible to do so, rather than waiting for the relationship to deteriorate further or for legal advice to be sought only once a dispute has already crystallised. A borrower who is willing to put their name to such an acknowledgment, even informally, both strengthens the lender's evidentiary position and buys additional time to pursue formal recovery if repayment still does not follow.

Security and interest, if the loan is made again

A lender who has been through one difficult recovery rarely wants to repeat the experience on the same informal basis next time, and a few structural choices make a real difference. Taking security, a mortgage over property registered in the lender's favour, a pledge of shares or other assets, or a personal guarantee from a third party with attachable assets in India, converts an unsecured claim into one that can be enforced against a specific asset rather than depending entirely on the borrower's cooperation or solvency at the time of recovery. A loan to a close relative that falls within FEMA's specific dispensation must be interest-free under the regulations discussed earlier; a loan to a friend outside that category is not subject to the same FEMA interest restriction, but interest charged between individuals is still subject to ordinary contract principles and, in some States, to Money Lenders Act provisions that can treat an unreasonably high or exploitative rate as unenforceable, and that can require a person who regularly lends money as a business, rather than as an occasional accommodation to family or friends, to hold a moneylender's licence under the relevant State law.

Whatever the structure, the document itself, a properly stamped promissory note or loan agreement, a registered mortgage where security is involved, or at minimum a dated and signed acknowledgment, remains the foundation everything else in this article depends on. An NRI lending for the second time to the same relative or friend, having already been through a contested recovery once, is usually far better served by a modest amount of paperwork at the outset than by a larger amount of litigation later.

Where the security is a mortgage over immovable property, the mortgage deed itself falls within the same compulsory registration requirement under the Registration Act, 1908 that applies to a sale deed; an unregistered mortgage document, like an unregistered sale, generally cannot be relied on to establish the charge it purports to create. A lender taking a mortgage as security for a loan to a relative or friend should accordingly treat its registration, not merely its signing, as the step that actually gives the security its legal effect, and should confirm this has been completed at the relevant Sub-Registrar's office rather than assuming a signed and notarised document is sufficient on its own.

When non-payment looks like fraud, and why it usually is not treated that way

A lender whose money has not come back, particularly where the borrower has been evasive or has given a series of excuses, often wants to treat the matter as a criminal fraud rather than a civil debt, and asks whether a complaint of cheating can be filed. The Supreme Court has been consistent and emphatic on this point: a mere failure to repay a debt, or to keep a promise, is not cheating under Section 318 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (which replaced Section 420 of the Indian Penal Code from 1 July 2024 without materially changing the ingredients of the offence), unless the lender can show that the borrower had a dishonest intention to deceive from the very inception of the transaction, not merely that the intention to repay evaporated later once the money had been spent or the venture failed.

Courts have repeatedly quashed criminal complaints filed in substance to pressure a debtor into paying what is, on the facts, an ordinary civil debt, holding that the dishonour of a post-dated cheque or a general inability to repay does not by itself establish the fraudulent intent the offence requires. This is not a technicality designed to protect defaulters; it reflects a considered line drawn between a business or personal relationship that simply did not work out, which the civil recovery routes in this article exist to address, and a transaction entered into dishonestly from the start, which is what the criminal law is aimed at. An NRI lender should go into any recovery attempt expecting to use the civil and, where a cheque is involved, the Section 138 routes, and should treat a straightforward cheating complaint as realistic only where there is genuine evidence that the borrower never intended to repay at the time the money was taken, not simply because repayment has not happened.

When the borrower has no assets in India, or has also left

A decree, whether obtained through an ordinary suit, an Order XXXVII summary suit, or the fine imposed following a Section 138 conviction, is only as useful as the assets available to enforce it against. Where the borrower holds property, a bank account, or other attachable assets in India, the decree holder can proceed to execution, including attachment and sale of property, garnishee proceedings against a bank account, or, in the case of a Section 138 matter, enforcement of the compensation the court orders under the Act alongside or instead of imprisonment. A lender who already knows, from the relationship, roughly what the borrower owns in India is in a materially better position at this stage than one who has to search for assets from scratch after years of silence.

The harder case is a borrower who has since moved abroad, or who never held significant assets in India to begin with. An Indian decree does not automatically carry force in another country, and whether it can be enforced there depends on that country's own law and on whether India has a reciprocal arrangement with it; the position differs between a reciprocating territory, where an Indian decree can sometimes be executed more directly, and a country with no such arrangement, where a fresh suit on the Indian judgment, or on the original debt, would generally have to be brought in the local courts. This is a separate and often more expensive undertaking than the Indian recovery process itself, and it is worth having a realistic conversation with counsel about whether it is proportionate to the amount involved before committing to it.

For this reason, the documentation and security steps discussed earlier in this article matter even more where a borrower's long-term location is uncertain. A lender who secures a loan against property in India, or who obtains a personal guarantee from a relative with assets in India, is protecting themselves against precisely this scenario, rather than discovering only after a dispute has already begun that the only assets worth pursuing have left the jurisdiction along with the borrower.

A working sequence for a loan that has already gone bad

Where repayment is already overdue and nothing has been formalised, the sequence that protects an NRI lender's position best starts with getting whatever documentation can still be obtained in writing: a signed acknowledgment of the amount owed, ideally including an agreed repayment date, sent and responded to by email or messaging if a physical signature is not practical. This single step both strengthens the evidentiary position and, where limitation is a live concern, can reset the clock under Section 18 of the Limitation Act if it is obtained before the original period expires.

Next, send a formal legal notice through counsel, setting out the amount, the basis of the claim, and a clear deadline for repayment, and keep proof of its delivery. If the loan was represented by a cheque that has since bounced, treat the thirty-day window to send the Section 138 notice as the controlling deadline above all else, since missing it forecloses that route entirely regardless of how strong the underlying claim is. If repayment still does not follow, assess whether a written promissory note, loan agreement or similar instrument exists that would allow an Order XXXVII summary suit, which should generally be preferred over an ordinary suit wherever the facts permit it, and confirm the applicable limitation period and its start date before filing, correcting for any acknowledgment obtained along the way.

Throughout this sequence, keep the civil, cheque-bounce and any FEMA documentation questions analytically separate from the question of whether the borrower's conduct was criminal in nature; treating every stalled family loan as a potential fraud complaint is, on the weight of current Supreme Court authority, more likely to see the complaint quashed than to accelerate recovery, and the civil and Order XXXVII routes remain the more reliable tools for getting the money back.

None of the individual steps in recovering a loan made to family or a friend are especially difficult on their own; a legal notice, a civil suit, a summary suit where the facts allow it, a Section 138 complaint where a cheque was involved. What actually defeats most of these claims is a gap that opened years earlier, at the moment the money was sent without a signed acknowledgment, without a properly stamped note, and without anyone giving a thought to how the limitation clock would eventually run.

The remedy for a loan already in that position is mostly about closing those gaps as far as they can still be closed: obtaining a written acknowledgment now rather than later, choosing the recovery route the available documents actually support, and treating the FEMA and limitation questions with the same care as the recovery itself. For a loan not yet made, the lesson is simpler still. A short, properly stamped document signed at the time the money changes hands costs very little against a relationship built on trust, and it is almost always what decides, years later, whether that trust is enough to get the money back.

Frequently Asked Questions

I have only bank transfer records showing I sent money to my brother. Is that enough to prove it was a loan?

It proves money moved; it does not by itself prove the purpose. Courts look at surrounding evidence, messages or emails discussing repayment, a signed acknowledgment, witness accounts, or the recipient's own later conduct, to decide whether a transfer was a loan rather than a gift or something else. If no such evidence exists, consider asking the borrower now for a signed, dated acknowledgment of the amount as a loan; this strengthens your position considerably and can also extend the time you have to sue.

My friend signed a promissory note for the loan, but we never got it stamped. Can I still use it in court?

An insufficiently stamped or unstamped promissory note can be barred from being admitted as evidence under Section 35 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899, though this is usually curable by paying the deficient stamp duty and any penalty before the document is relied on in court. Courts are divided on whether you can prove the debt through other evidence if the note itself is excluded, so the safer step is to have the deficiency cured promptly rather than discovering the problem for the first time during litigation.

Can I file a summary suit under Order XXXVII if there's no written loan agreement at all?

No. Order XXXVII's summary procedure is available only for claims based on a written contract, a bill of exchange, a hundi or a promissory note. A loan with nothing in writing has to proceed as an ordinary civil suit, which takes considerably longer because it goes through a full trial rather than the faster leave-to-defend process available under Order XXXVII.

The person who owes me money gave me a cheque that bounced. What should I do first?

Send a legal notice demanding payment within thirty days of learning the cheque was dishonoured; this deadline is strict and missing it can be fatal to a later Section 138 complaint. If the borrower does not pay within fifteen days of receiving that notice, you can file a complaint under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, which itself must be filed within one month of the fifteen-day window expiring.

How long do I have to sue for an old family loan that was never repaid?

Generally three years under the Limitation Act, 1963, but when that three years starts depends on the loan's terms, whether it was payable on demand, on a fixed date, or had no term at all. If the original period has already run or is close to running, a written acknowledgment of the debt signed by the borrower before it expires restarts the clock under Section 18 of the Act, which is worth obtaining even many years into a stalled repayment.

Can I charge interest on money I lend from abroad to a relative in India?

If the relative is a close relative within FEMA's specific rupee-loan rules, the loan generally has to be interest-free under those rules. For a loan to a friend who does not fall within that definition, FEMA's interest restriction does not apply in the same way, but the rate is still subject to ordinary contract principles and, in some States, to Money Lenders Act provisions that can treat an exploitative rate as unenforceable or require a licence if lending is done regularly as a business rather than as an occasional accommodation.

Can I file a cheating complaint against someone who borrowed money and simply refuses to pay it back?

Generally no, and courts have repeatedly quashed complaints that try this. The Supreme Court has held consistently that mere failure to repay a debt is not cheating under Section 318 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (the successor to Section 420 of the Indian Penal Code) unless there was a dishonest intention to deceive from the very start of the transaction, not merely a later failure to keep a promise. A straightforward unpaid loan is a civil matter, to be pursued through the recovery routes in this guide, not a criminal one.

I live abroad. Do I have to personally appear in India to file or pursue the recovery?

For a civil suit, you can generally be represented by counsel and, where needed, a properly authorised power of attorney holder for procedural steps. For a Section 138 complaint, a power of attorney holder with genuine personal knowledge of the transaction can file the complaint on your behalf, per the Supreme Court's decisions on this point, though you may still need to testify yourself at some stage depending on how the case develops. If you are on the receiving end of a complaint rather than bringing one, you can apply for exemption from personal attendance under Section 228 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, though this has to be sought and is not automatic.
