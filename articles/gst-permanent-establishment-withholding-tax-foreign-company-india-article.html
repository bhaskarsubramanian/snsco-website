GST, Permanent Establishment and Withholding Tax for a Foreign Company Selling Into India

How GST on cross-border digital supplies, the business connection and permanent establishment rules, and withholding tax under Section 195 and its 2025 Act successor apply to a foreign company that has no Indian office at all

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 14 November 2026 · Law stated as on 14 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company that sells software, digital content or services to Indian customers without ever opening an Indian office still has to answer three separate Indian tax questions on every rupee it earns here: whether GST applies and who pays it, whether its own profits have become taxable in India through a permanent establishment or business connection, and whether the Indian payer has to withhold tax before the money leaves the country. This article sets out each question, the equalisation levy's withdrawal, and the position under the Income-tax Act, 2025 from 1 April 2026, as they stand today.

Key points

  • A foreign company supplying OIDAR digital services to Indian consumers has to register for GST itself under the simplified scheme and account for IGST under forward charge; where the Indian recipient is GST-registered and buying for business, the reverse charge mechanism applies instead and the foreign supplier need not register for that supply.
  • The equalisation levy regime is withdrawn: the 2% e-commerce levy stopped applying to transactions from 1 August 2024 under the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024, and the 6% online advertisement levy was withdrawn with effect from 1 April 2025 under the Finance Act, 2025, though liabilities that accrued before those dates remain live.
  • India taxes a foreign company's own India-connected profits, separately from GST and from withholding on a specific payment, where the company has a business connection under the Income-tax Act or a permanent establishment under an applicable tax treaty, including through the Significant Economic Presence test aimed at foreign digital businesses with no physical footprint in India.
  • The Significant Economic Presence test is based on a prescribed aggregate transaction value or a prescribed number of Indian users, currently three lakh users for the systematic and continuous interaction limb, and was amended by the Finance Act, 2025 to exclude purchases made in India purely for export.
  • Indian payers generally must withhold tax before paying a non-resident, under Section 195 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, carried forward from 1 April 2026 into Section 393(2) of the Income-tax Act, 2025, and the applicable rate depends on whether the foreign recipient has furnished a Tax Residency Certificate and other documentation to claim a lower treaty rate.
  • The three questions, GST, permanent establishment or business connection, and withholding tax, are legally separate and have to be analysed together against the foreign company's actual India-facing activity, not assumed to track each other automatically.

The three questions a foreign company selling into India actually faces

A foreign company that sells goods, software, digital services or professional services to Indian customers, without setting up any office or subsidiary in India, still has to answer three separate Indian tax questions before it can treat a deal as closed. First, does Indian GST apply to what is being supplied, and if so, who actually has to pay it, the foreign supplier or the Indian buyer. Second, does the company's activity in India create a permanent establishment or business connection that exposes its profits to Indian income tax, over and above any withholding already suffered on the payment itself. Third, does the Indian payer have to withhold tax at source before paying the foreign company at all, and if so, at what rate.

These three questions are related but legally distinct, and a foreign company can easily get one right while getting another wrong, for instance correctly registering for GST on digital services while overlooking that its growing India-facing sales team has, without anyone planning it that way, created a business connection that exposes head office profits to Indian tax. This article addresses each question as it stands today, including the equalisation levy's withdrawal and the Income-tax Act, 2025's effect on withholding and permanent establishment provisions from 1 April 2026.

None of these three questions is unique to large multinational groups. A small or mid-sized foreign company signing its first substantial India contract, a software licence, a consulting engagement, a supply agreement with an Indian distributor, faces exactly the same analysis as a large group, and often has fewer internal resources to catch a problem before it becomes expensive. The practical guidance in this article is written with that smaller company in mind as much as the larger one, since the cost of getting these three questions wrong does not scale down simply because the company itself is smaller.

GST on cross-border digital supplies: the OIDAR framework

Where a foreign company supplies what the GST law calls Online Information Database Access and Retrieval services, broadly, automated digital content and services delivered over the internet with minimal human intervention, to recipients in India, the GST treatment depends on who the recipient is. Where the Indian recipient is a non-taxable online recipient, in practice an individual consumer or an unregistered entity buying for personal or non-business use, the foreign supplier itself has to register for GST in India under a simplified registration scheme and charge and pay Integrated GST under forward charge, since there is no registered Indian recipient who could otherwise be made to self-assess the tax.

Registration under this scheme is made on Form GST REG-10, and a non-resident OIDAR supplier with no physical presence in India must appoint a person in India as its authorised representative for compliance purposes under Rule 14 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017. Returns are filed monthly on Form GSTR-5A, due by the twentieth of the month following the tax period, and because the return requires the supplier to separate its business-to-consumer and business-to-business revenue, accurate classification of each customer at the point of sale matters considerably more than it would under an ordinary domestic GST registration.

Where the Indian recipient is instead GST-registered and buying for business use, the reverse charge mechanism applies: the Indian recipient self-assesses and pays the IGST directly, the foreign supplier issues its invoice without charging IGST, and that particular supply does not have to be reported on the supplier's own GSTR-5A. A foreign company selling the same digital product to both individual subscribers and registered Indian businesses therefore has two different compliance tracks running side by side for what looks, from the product side, like a single offering, and getting the classification wrong, treating a business customer's purchase as business-to-consumer, or the reverse, is one of the more common and costly errors foreign digital suppliers make.

The equalisation levy is gone; do not plan around it any more

Foreign companies that structured pricing or contracts around India's equalisation levy regime should treat that regime as closed. The 2% levy on e-commerce supply or services made, provided or facilitated by an e-commerce operator, introduced by the Finance Act, 2020, stopped applying to transactions from 1 August 2024, following its withdrawal by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024. The older, narrower 6% levy on online advertisement services was removed later still, withdrawn with effect from 1 April 2025 by the Finance Act, 2025. As matters stand today, neither levy applies to any transaction carried out after its respective withdrawal date.

This does not close out every liability that arose while the levies were in force. Any equalisation levy that should have been collected and deposited on a transaction carried out before the relevant withdrawal date remains a live compliance matter, including the filing of the annual equalisation levy statement for that period and the possibility of a notice or penalty proceeding relating to an earlier year, and the withdrawal of the levy going forward does not extinguish liabilities that had already accrued under it.

Foreign companies that paid the equalisation levy in earlier years, or that structured contract pricing on the assumption that a customer would bear the levy as a separate line item, should also revisit those contracts now that the levy no longer applies going forward, since a pricing clause drafted around a cost that has since disappeared can leave money on the table, or create an unnecessary dispute with an Indian customer, if nobody updates it to reflect the current position.

Permanent establishment and business connection: when India taxes the foreign company's own profits

Separately from GST and separately from withholding tax on a specific payment, a foreign company's profits attributable to India can themselves be taxed in India if the company has a business connection in India under the Income-tax Act, which is the domestic law concept, or a permanent establishment under the applicable double taxation avoidance agreement, which is the treaty concept that, where a treaty applies, generally operates as the narrower and more favourable test for the taxpayer. A fixed place of business, an agent in India habitually concluding contracts on the foreign company's behalf, or a dependent agent carrying on the company's business in India, can each create a permanent establishment even where the foreign company has never incorporated an Indian entity or opened a registered office.

India's domestic law also recognises a Significant Economic Presence test, introduced to reach foreign digital businesses with no physical footprint in India at all. Under the Explanation to the relevant provision, a foreign company's transactions in goods, services or property with Indian persons, including the provision of download of data or software, can create a significant economic presence, and therefore a business connection, once the aggregate value of such transactions in a year crosses a prescribed threshold, or once the foreign company engages with a prescribed number of users in India through systematic and continuous soliciting of business or interaction, specified by rule as three lakh users in the current regulatory framework.

The Significant Economic Presence rule has been amended more than once since it was introduced, most recently by the Finance Act, 2025, which added a carve-out so that purchases made in India purely for export are not counted toward the threshold, aligning the significant economic presence test with an export exclusion that already existed for the ordinary business connection rule. A foreign company should treat its own exposure under this test as something to monitor each year against its actual India-facing revenue and user numbers, rather than as a one-time assessment made when it first started selling into India, since crossing the threshold in a later year, as the business grows, is exactly the point at which this provision is designed to bite.

GST on ordinary cross-border services, beyond the OIDAR category

Not every cross-border service a foreign company supplies to India falls within the narrower OIDAR definition, and a great deal of ordinary business-to-business services, consulting, engineering, management services, licensing of non-digital intellectual property, are instead treated under the general import of services rules rather than the OIDAR scheme. Where an Indian GST-registered recipient imports such a service from a foreign supplier for use in its business, the Indian recipient is generally liable to pay IGST under the ordinary reverse charge mechanism applicable to import of services, regardless of whether the specific service happens to be delivered digitally, and the foreign supplier in that situation typically has no GST registration obligation of its own in India at all.

The position becomes less straightforward where the foreign company's Indian customer is not GST-registered, or where the supply is structured through an Indian intermediary or distributor rather than a direct sale, since the place of supply rules and the question of who is actually making the supply to whom can change which party ends up liable to account for the tax. Contracts with Indian counterparties should specify clearly who bears GST, and foreign companies should not assume that a reverse charge mechanism that applies to one category of Indian customer, a registered business, for instance, extends automatically to every category of Indian customer the same product or service might reach.

Customs duty: the separate track that applies to physical goods

A foreign company exporting physical goods into India, rather than supplying services or digital content, faces an entirely separate regime under the Customs Act, 1962 and the customs tariff, with basic customs duty, any applicable social welfare surcharge, and integrated GST on imports all collected at the point of import, generally from the Indian importer rather than from the foreign seller, unless the foreign company has structured the transaction so that it is itself the importer of record. Classification of the goods under the correct customs tariff heading drives the duty rate that applies, and a foreign exporter unfamiliar with Indian classification practice can find its Indian buyer facing a materially different landed cost than was quoted, if the goods are classified differently on arrival than the parties assumed when pricing the deal.

Where a foreign company sells into India through a distribution or consignment arrangement rather than a straightforward sale to an independent Indian buyer, the valuation of the goods for customs purposes, and whether the relationship between the foreign company and its Indian counterparty is treated as one between related parties for customs valuation purposes, can itself become contested, since customs valuation rules apply their own tests for related-party transactions that are not identical to the transfer pricing tests applied for income tax purposes, even though both sets of rules are, in substance, trying to answer a similar question about whether the declared price reflects an arm's length value.

Transfer pricing: the question that follows once there is a related Indian party

When a foreign company's Indian activity runs through a related party, a subsidiary, a branch treated as a separate taxable presence, or simply a group company that the foreign company deals with on cross-border transactions, India's transfer pricing rules under Chapter X of the Income-tax Act, 1961 require that the pricing of those transactions reflect what unrelated parties would have agreed, and the Indian related party has to maintain contemporaneous documentation supporting that position and file an accountant's report, commonly referred to by its old form number, 3CEB, confirming the international transactions and their pricing. A transfer pricing adjustment, where the tax authorities conclude the related-party pricing understated Indian taxable income, falls on the Indian entity's own assessment, but it frequently drives the commercial negotiation between the foreign parent and its Indian counterparty over how intercompany pricing is set in the first place.

A foreign company that also has a permanent establishment in India, rather than only a separately incorporated related party, faces a related but distinct question that arises only once the anterior permanent establishment question has already been answered against it: how much of the foreign company's own global profit should then be attributed to that permanent establishment for Indian tax purposes. This attribution exercise uses transfer pricing style reasoning, treating the permanent establishment as if it were a distinct and separate enterprise dealing with the rest of the foreign company at arm's length, but it operates within the single foreign company's own assessment rather than between two separately incorporated entities, and disputes over profit attribution to a permanent establishment are a recurring source of litigation precisely because there is no actual arm's length transaction to point to, only an attribution exercise the tax authorities and the company can reasonably disagree about.

Advance Pricing Agreements are available to a taxpayer, including the Indian related party of a foreign company, who wants certainty on a transfer pricing method before a dispute arises, running for an agreed future period and, where a rollback is agreed, covering a limited number of past years as well. An Advance Pricing Agreement does not, on its own, resolve a separate permanent establishment or business connection dispute, since it is addressed to pricing between related parties or, in a unilateral or bilateral form, to specific cross-border transactions, rather than to the earlier question of whether a taxable presence exists in India at all, and a foreign company should not treat an Advance Pricing Agreement covering its related-party pricing as having also settled, by implication, whether a permanent establishment exists, since the two questions are addressed through entirely separate processes and a favourable outcome on one does not carry over to the other.

Errors that recur across foreign companies new to the Indian market

A recurring error is treating the GST, permanent establishment and withholding tax questions as a single compliance task handled once at the point a company starts selling into India, rather than as three separate, ongoing questions that each need to be revisited as the business grows, adds local staff, or changes its distribution model. A company that correctly concluded it had no permanent establishment when it sold purely through independent distributors can create one without realising it once it places its own employees in India to support those distributors, or once a distributor's own conduct crosses the line from an independent agent acting on its own account to one habitually concluding contracts on the foreign company's behalf, a line that is often crossed gradually and without any single decision that flagged the change.

A second recurring error is assuming that a tax treaty's lower withholding rate applies automatically, without the foreign company actually holding and being ready to produce a current Tax Residency Certificate at the point an Indian payer needs it. Indian payers, aware that an incorrectly applied low withholding rate exposes them personally to a shortfall assessment, tend to default to the higher domestic rate whenever the supporting paperwork is not immediately available, which then leaves the foreign company to recover the difference through a refund claim on an Indian tax return, a materially slower and more expensive route than providing the certificate at the time of payment.

A third error, more specific to digital businesses, is monitoring Significant Economic Presence exposure only in the year a company first starts selling into India and never again, when the test is explicitly built around a threshold that current, ongoing activity can cross in a later year even though it was not crossed when the company began. Building an annual review of India-facing transaction value and user numbers into the company's regular compliance calendar, rather than treating the original market-entry assessment as permanent, is the more defensible practice.

A fourth pattern worth naming separately affects companies that grow through acquisition of, or a joint venture with, an existing Indian business, since the acquired or joint venture entity's own historical compliance position, past GST classification, withholding practice, and any pending assessment or dispute, becomes the foreign company's problem the moment the deal closes. Due diligence on an Indian target should specifically cover these three questions rather than treat Indian tax compliance as a single line item, since a clean-looking balance sheet can still sit on top of an unresolved permanent establishment or GST classification dispute that only surfaces once the new owner's own advisers look closely at it.

Where a tax treaty changes the analysis

A foreign company resident in a country that has a double taxation avoidance agreement with India can generally rely on the treaty's permanent establishment definition in preference to the wider domestic business connection concept, where the treaty definition is narrower, which it usually is. Most of India's treaties follow some version of the OECD or UN model's permanent establishment article, requiring a fixed place of business through which the company's business is wholly or partly carried on, or a dependent agent who habitually exercises authority to conclude contracts in the company's name, with specific exclusions for activities of a preparatory or auxiliary character, a warehouse used only for storage, for instance, or a liaison function that does no more than gather information.

Relying on a treaty is not automatic. The foreign company has to be a tax resident of the treaty partner country, hold and be able to produce a Tax Residency Certificate when the Indian payer or the tax authorities ask for one, and in many cases satisfy a further test under the treaty or domestic general anti-avoidance provisions confirming that the structure was not entered into principally to obtain the treaty benefit. A foreign company that assumes treaty protection without having the Tax Residency Certificate and supporting documentation ready can find an Indian payer withholding tax at the higher domestic rate by default, precisely because the payer cannot safely apply the treaty rate without that paperwork in hand.

Treaty access also does not answer the underlying question of whether a permanent establishment exists in the first place. A company can hold a perfectly valid Tax Residency Certificate and still be found to have a permanent establishment in India, in which case the treaty's business profits article, rather than its royalty or fees for technical services article, governs how those attributable profits are taxed, generally at ordinary rates on a net income basis rather than at the lower gross withholding rate that would apply to a payment that is not connected to a permanent establishment at all. Confusing treaty access with the absence of a permanent establishment is a mistake that surfaces most often once a tax authority raises a permanent establishment assessment years after the relevant payments were already made and withheld on the assumption that no such establishment existed.

Withholding tax on payments to a foreign company: Section 195 and its successor

Any person in India making a payment to a non-resident that is chargeable to tax in India, royalties, fee for technical services, interest, or payments for other services, generally has to deduct tax at source before making the payment, under Section 195 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, carried forward from 1 April 2026 into Section 393(2) of the Income-tax Act, 2025. The rate depends on the nature of the payment and on whether a tax treaty applies, the Indian deductor typically applying the domestic rate under the Act unless the foreign recipient has furnished the documentation, including the Tax Residency Certificate and, where the Act requires it, Form 10F, that lets a lower treaty rate be applied instead.

A foreign company receiving payments from India should expect its Indian counterparty to ask for exactly this documentation before releasing payment, and delays in providing it are a common and avoidable cause of a payment either being held up or having tax withheld at a higher rate than the treaty would actually allow, recoverable later only through an Indian tax return and refund claim that takes considerably longer, and ties up more working capital, than getting the paperwork right at the time of payment in the first place. Quarterly withholding statements, which moved from the earlier Form 27Q to a renumbered equivalent under the 2025 Act's forms regime, are filed by the Indian deductor, not the foreign recipient, but the foreign recipient still needs the certificate the deductor issues to support any tax credit or refund claim it later makes in its home country or in India.

That same foreign company can also apply to the Indian tax authorities for a certificate authorising a lower, or nil, rate of withholding on a specific payment or category of payments, where it can demonstrate that its actual Indian tax liability on that income will be lower than the standard withholding rate would otherwise suggest, for instance because the income is covered by a favourable treaty provision or because the company's own expenses against that income reduce its true net tax liability well below the gross withholding rate. Obtaining such a certificate in advance avoids the slower route of paying tax at the higher rate and then claiming a refund once the company's own Indian tax return is filed and processed, though the certificate itself has to be renewed and is not a one-time, indefinite authorisation.

How the three questions interact in a single transaction

A foreign SaaS company licensing software to Indian business customers illustrates how the three questions sit together. The GST question is generally answered through the OIDAR reverse charge mechanism if the customers are GST-registered businesses, so the company itself need not register for GST on those particular sales, though a reasonable volume of individual, non-business Indian subscribers on the same platform would separately require the simplified OIDAR registration for that slice of revenue. The permanent establishment question depends on whether the company has gone beyond simply selling a product remotely, for instance by placing an India-based sales team with authority to negotiate and close deals, which can create a dependent agent permanent establishment even where no Indian office exists on paper. The withholding question arises on the licence fee itself, which Indian tax law generally characterises as royalty income subject to withholding under Section 195 or its 2025 Act successor, at a rate that depends on whether the company can establish treaty protection with the required documentation.

Getting all three analyses to a consistent, defensible position, rather than treating them as separate boxes to tick, is where foreign companies most often need advice tailored to their specific business model, since a structure that works cleanly for a pure software licence can produce a different answer once local sales, support staff or data processing activity is added in India, even where the underlying product offering has not changed. The same discipline applies to a foreign manufacturer selling physical goods through an Indian distributor, where the customs and GST treatment of the goods sits alongside a separate question of whether the distributor's conduct, if it goes beyond buying and reselling on its own account, has itself created an agency permanent establishment for the foreign manufacturer.

Practical steps for a foreign company selling into India without a local entity

Before signing its first substantial Indian contract, a foreign company should work out whether its customer base includes non-business Indian recipients that would trigger OIDAR registration, obtain its own Tax Residency Certificate from its home tax authority so it can support a treaty-based withholding rate from the outset rather than after the first payment is held up, and review its planned India-facing activity, sales staff, support staff, any India-based decision-making, against the permanent establishment and significant economic presence tests before that activity grows to a scale where unwinding an inadvertent permanent establishment becomes disruptive.

Ongoing monitoring matters as much as the initial analysis. A company that starts with a modest, clearly passive sale of digital content can, within a few years of organic growth, cross the Significant Economic Presence thresholds on transaction value or user numbers without any change in how the business is actually run, simply because the numbers grew, and a company that does not track this against the current thresholds each year can find itself with an unplanned business connection assessment covering several past years at once, which is a materially worse position than addressing the exposure as it develops.

Contract drafting with Indian counterparties is a further area where small decisions made early have outsized consequences later. A gross-up clause that makes the Indian payer responsible for any shortfall in withholding, a clear allocation of responsibility for GST between the parties, and an obligation on the foreign company to provide its Tax Residency Certificate and any other documentation the Indian payer reasonably needs each year, are all details that cost little to negotiate at the outset of a relationship and save considerable argument later, once a payment is due and the parties discover they never actually agreed who bears a given tax cost.

Where these disputes are actually fought

A foreign company that disagrees with an Indian tax officer's view that it has a permanent establishment, or with an adjustment to the profit attributed to one, is usually first assessed through the ordinary scrutiny assessment process, and, because many such companies qualify as foreign companies for this purpose, the dispute often proceeds through the Dispute Resolution Panel mechanism, a specialised route intended to give certain categories of taxpayer, including foreign companies, a faster first-level review of a draft assessment order before it becomes final, with a further appeal available to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal and, on questions of law, to the High Court and, at the final stage, the Supreme Court.

A company that instead wants certainty before a transaction, rather than a dispute after one, has historically been able to approach the Authority for Advance Rulings, or its successor body under the current statutory framework, for a binding ruling on the tax consequences of a proposed transaction, including whether it would create a permanent establishment, though the time such a ruling takes and the scope of questions the authority will actually entertain should be checked against current practice before a company builds its transaction timeline around obtaining one. Litigation over permanent establishment and profit attribution in India has, over several years, produced a substantial body of tribunal and High Court decisions turning closely on each company's own facts, agency arrangements, employee roles, decision-making location, which is why generic guidance of the kind given in this article can identify the tests that apply but cannot predict how a specific company's own structure will be assessed against them.

What remains unsettled or worth confirming before relying on this

The Significant Economic Presence thresholds are set by rule rather than by the Act itself and have been revised before, so a foreign company should confirm the current monetary and user-count thresholds against the rule in force at the time it is assessing its own position, rather than assume the figures described here remain unchanged indefinitely. The renumbering of Section 195 into Section 393(2) of the Income-tax Act, 2025, and of the business connection and significant economic presence provisions into their new section numbers, should likewise be checked against the official text of the Act as it stood on the date any specific transaction is being assessed, since this article describes the position as understood from secondary commentary on the 2025 Act's commencement rather than from an independent reading of the enacted text's every renumbered provision.

The equalisation levy's withdrawal is confirmed from multiple sources and is treated here as settled, but a foreign company with transactions that straddle the withdrawal date, part of a longer engagement that started before 1 August 2024 or before 1 April 2025 and continued afterward, should still have those transactions reviewed individually rather than assume the whole engagement falls neatly on one side of the cut-off. Similarly, the GST treatment of a specific product or service that does not fit cleanly into either the OIDAR category or the ordinary import of services category, a hybrid digital-and-physical offering, for instance, is not addressed by the general rules set out in this article and needs its own classification analysis.

A foreign company that never incorporates in India can still end up paying Indian tax in three different ways on a single stream of revenue, through GST it either charges or its Indian customer self-assesses, through tax on profits India treats as connected to a business connection or permanent establishment here, and through withholding on the payment itself. None of these three outcomes is avoided by simply not having an Indian office. They are avoided, or correctly managed, only by analysing the company's actual activity in India each year against the current rules and keeping the documentation, Tax Residency Certificates above all, that lets the lower, treaty-based outcome actually be claimed rather than assumed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a foreign company have to register for GST in India if it sells digital services to Indian customers?

Only in some cases. If the Indian customers are non-taxable online recipients, broadly individual consumers or unregistered buyers, the foreign supplier must register under the simplified OIDAR scheme and charge GST itself. If the Indian customers are GST-registered businesses, the reverse charge mechanism applies instead, the Indian business self-assesses the tax, and the foreign supplier does not need to register for that particular supply.

Is India's equalisation levy on digital services still in force?

No. The 2% equalisation levy on e-commerce supply stopped applying to transactions from 1 August 2024, withdrawn by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024, and the separate 6% levy on online advertisement services was withdrawn with effect from 1 April 2025 under the Finance Act, 2025. Transactions carried out before those dates can still attract liability and compliance obligations that arose while the levies were in force.

Can a foreign company create a taxable presence in India without opening an office there?

Yes. India's domestic law recognises a business connection, and applicable tax treaties recognise a permanent establishment, either of which can arise from a dependent agent concluding contracts in India, a fixed place of business, or, for digital businesses, a Significant Economic Presence based on the value of transactions with Indian customers or the number of Indian users the company systematically engages, even where the company has never incorporated an Indian entity.

What is the Significant Economic Presence test and who does it affect?

It is a domestic law test aimed at foreign businesses, typically digital ones, that have no physical presence in India but still do significant business with Indian customers. A foreign company's transactions with Indian persons can create a Significant Economic Presence once their aggregate value crosses a prescribed threshold, or once the company engages a prescribed number of Indian users, currently three lakh for that limb, through systematic and continuous soliciting of business or interaction.

Does an Indian company have to withhold tax before paying a foreign company?

Generally yes, where the payment is chargeable to tax in India, under Section 195 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, carried forward from 1 April 2026 into Section 393(2) of the Income-tax Act, 2025. The rate depends on the nature of the payment and on whether the foreign recipient has provided a Tax Residency Certificate and other documentation supporting a lower rate under an applicable tax treaty; without that documentation, the Indian payer will typically withhold at the higher domestic rate.

How does a foreign company claim a lower withholding tax rate under a tax treaty with India?

The foreign company generally needs to be a tax resident of the treaty partner country and provide the Indian payer with a Tax Residency Certificate from its home tax authority, together with any further form the Indian tax law requires, such as Form 10F, before the Indian payer can safely apply the treaty rate rather than the higher domestic withholding rate.

Can a foreign company be taxed in India on digital sales even if its servers and staff are entirely outside India?

Yes, in principle, through the Significant Economic Presence test, which looks at transactions with and engagement of users in India rather than at where the company's servers or staff are physically located. Whether it actually applies to a given company depends on whether its Indian-facing transaction value or user numbers cross the prescribed thresholds in a given year.
