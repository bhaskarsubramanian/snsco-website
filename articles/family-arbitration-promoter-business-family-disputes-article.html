Family Arbitration for Promoter and Business-Family Disputes in India

An arbitration clause tucked into a family settlement or a shareholders' agreement looks like a clean way to keep a business dispute out of court, until the dispute turns out to be about a trust deed or an oppression petition, which an arbitrator has no power to decide at all.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 19 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Promoter families running a shared business tend to reach for arbitration the moment a dispute surfaces between branches of the family, on the reasonable instinct that a private forum keeps the disagreement, and the business, out of the public record a civil suit creates. That instinct is right for a large share of these disputes, a partition of inherited property, a disagreement over a family settlement, a breach of a shareholders' agreement between cousins who jointly hold a company. It is flatly wrong for others, an oppression petition under the Companies Act, a dispute over how a family trust should be administered, which no arbitration clause, however carefully drafted, can actually send to arbitration. Knowing which side of that line a given family dispute falls on, before signing the clause rather than after the dispute has already started, is the entire point of this article.

Key points

  • Indian law treats arbitrability as a question about the nature of the right in dispute, not simply whether the parties agreed to arbitrate it: rights in personam between specific parties are generally arbitrable, rights in rem that bind the world, or a specific category the Supreme Court has carved out, are not
  • The Supreme Court's 2020 decision in Vidya Drolia v. Durga Trading Corporation reorganised the law into a four-fold test and confirmed that the categories first listed in Booz Allen & Hamilton v. SBI Home Finance, including matrimonial, insolvency, testamentary and criminal matters, are not an exhaustive or rigid checklist
  • Partition of family property and disputes over a family settlement are generally arbitrable, and a Delhi High Court order in January 2026, Pranav Gupta v. Adeesh Gupta, referred a sprawling combination of family partition and business disputes to a sole arbitrator
  • Disputes arising from a trust deed are a specific exception the Supreme Court carved out in Vimal Kishor Shah v. Jayesh Dinesh Shah in 2016, holding the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 to be a complete code that displaces arbitration even where the trust deed itself contains an arbitration clause, a point many family trusts used for business succession overlook
  • Allegations of oppression and mismanagement under Sections 241 and 242 of the Companies Act, 2013 generally cannot be sent to arbitration even where the underlying shareholders' agreement has an arbitration clause, because the NCLT's jurisdiction over that relief is treated as exclusive, though a petition that is merely a dressed-up contractual dispute can still be referred
  • An allegation of fraud no longer automatically defeats an arbitration clause; the Supreme Court in A. Ayyasamy v. A. Paramasivam confined that exception to serious fraud going to the root of the contract and requiring extensive evidence, though the law here has shifted more than once and is worth checking against the latest authority before relying on it in a specific case
  • A family business structure built on a Family Settlement Agreement and a Shareholders' Agreement, each with its own carefully scoped arbitration clause, generally survives a jurisdictional challenge better than a single agreement that tries to cover partition, trust administration and company governance all at once
  • Where a dispute mixes arbitrable and non-arbitrable elements, for instance a partition claim bundled with an oppression allegation, Indian courts and tribunals increasingly decline to split the matter artificially, which makes careful drafting at the outset more valuable than arguing about severability after the dispute has already begun

Why Family Businesses Reach for Arbitration, and Why It Is Not Automatic

A dispute between branches of a business family carries a reputational cost a stranger-to-stranger commercial dispute does not. Suppliers, lenders, employees and competitors all watch a public court filing between cousins who jointly run a listed or soon-to-be-listed company rather differently than they watch an ordinary contract claim, which is why families overwhelmingly prefer to resolve these disputes privately, through arbitration, mediation or a negotiated family settlement, rather than in open court. Arbitration in particular offers confidentiality, a chosen decision-maker who can be selected for commercial and family sensitivity rather than assigned by a court's roster, and a timeline that does not depend on a congested civil docket.

None of that changes the basic rule that arbitration is a creature of consent operating within limits the law itself sets, not a forum the parties can extend to any dispute simply by writing a clause that says so. An arbitrator derives authority from the arbitration agreement and from the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, and where the dispute's subject matter falls into a category the law reserves for a court or a specific statutory tribunal, an arbitration clause covering that dispute is simply unenforceable for that purpose, however broadly it is worded and however willingly both sides signed it.

This matters more for family businesses than for ordinary commercial counterparties because a single family dispute so often bundles several different legal characters into one quarrel: a disagreement that starts as a partition of inherited property can shade into a dispute over how a family trust holding shares should be run, which can shade further into an allegation that one branch is oppressing the others as minority shareholders in the operating company. Each of those three layers is tested for arbitrability separately, and a family that assumes the whole dispute travels together, because it feels like one argument, is often surprised to find a court splitting it, or refusing to split it, in ways nobody anticipated when the governing documents were drafted.

The confusion is compounded by the fact that most family business documents are drafted during a calm period, often at the point of a succession event or a new investment, when every branch of the family is still speaking to the others and nobody is thinking seriously about what happens if that stops being true. A broadly worded arbitration clause, promising that "any dispute arising out of or in connection with this agreement, or the parties' family relationship generally," will be resolved by arbitration reads as thorough and protective at the signing table, but it does nothing to change which disputes the law actually allows to be arbitrated once the relationship sours. Families are better served drafting for the dispute they hope never happens than for the harmony they currently enjoy, which means confronting the arbitrability limits described in this article while everyone is still willing to discuss them calmly, rather than leaving the question to be litigated for the first time once trust between the branches has already broken down.

The Booz Allen Categories and the Vidya Drolia Four-Fold Test

The modern starting point for arbitrability in India is the Supreme Court's 2011 decision in Booz Allen & Hamilton Inc. v. SBI Home Finance Ltd., reported at (2011) 5 SCC 532, which drew the basic distinction between rights in rem, enforceable against the world and generally reserved for courts and public tribunals, and rights in personam, enforceable against specific persons and generally arbitrable. The Court illustrated the distinction with a list of disputes commentary generally summarises as covering criminal matters, matrimonial disputes such as divorce, guardianship, insolvency and winding-up, testamentary matters concerning the validity of a will, and tenancy or eviction disputes governed by special rent-control legislation.

The Supreme Court revisited and reorganised this framework in Vidya Drolia v. Durga Trading Corporation, decided on 14 December 2020, setting out a four-fold test for when a dispute is non-arbitrable: where the cause of action and subject matter relate to actions in rem that do not concern subordinate rights in personam arising from those rights in rem; where the dispute affects third-party rights with an erga omnes effect requiring centralised adjudication; where it concerns the State's inalienable sovereign or public-interest functions; or where a mandatory statute expressly or by necessary implication reserves the dispute for a specific forum. The Court was explicit that the Booz Allen list is illustrative rather than a closed, mechanical checklist, which means a family dispute that does not fit neatly into one of the named categories still has to be tested against this broader four-fold framework rather than assumed to be arbitrable by default.

For a business family, the practical takeaway from this framework is that the label a family puts on its own dispute, calling it a "business disagreement" or a "family matter", carries no legal weight; what matters is the actual legal character of the relief being sought. A claim for damages for breach of a shareholders' agreement is a right in personam and squarely arbitrable. A claim that seeks to invalidate a company's board resolutions on the ground of oppression, or to have a court wind up the company, or to probate a will, reaches toward relief the law has reserved for a specific public forum, and dressing that claim up in the language of a private family dispute does not change which side of the line it falls on.

Vidya Drolia also addressed who decides the arbitrability question in the first instance, holding that an arbitral tribunal is generally the preferred first authority to rule on its own jurisdiction, including contested questions of arbitrability, subject to a narrower role for the referral court at the Section 8 or Section 11 stage, which only intervenes to weed out claims that are manifestly and ex facie non-arbitrable without needing a detailed examination. For a family dispute, this means a court asked to refer the matter to arbitration at the outset will often decline to conduct a full inquiry into which parts of a tangled family quarrel are and are not arbitrable, leaving much of that sorting to the arbitrator appointed to hear the case, with the tribunal's own ruling on its jurisdiction then open to challenge later under Section 34 if a party believes it got the question wrong.

Partition and Family Settlement Disputes: Generally Arbitrable

Disputes over the partition of family property, and disputes over whether a family settlement was validly reached or should be enforced, are generally treated as arbitrable, because at their core they involve rights in personam between specific family members over specific property, rather than a right in rem of the kind the Booz Allen categories are concerned with. Indian courts have referred partition disputes, including ones bundled together with related business disagreements between the same family members, to arbitration where the parties' arbitration agreement, or their clear and informed consent at the time of the dispute, covers the claim.

A useful recent illustration is the Delhi High Court's order in Mr. Pranav Gupta v. Adeesh Gupta & Ors., decided on 23 January 2026 in CS(OS) 154/2018, where the Court referred a combination of family partition and business disputes to arbitration and appointed a sole arbitrator to adjudicate the full dispute, expressly reserving each side's right to raise claims and counterclaims and to argue arbitrability issues within the arbitration itself. The order is a reminder that a court faced with a genuinely tangled family dispute will often prefer to let a single arbitrator sort out which parts of the dispute it can decide, rather than pre-emptively carving the matter up itself, provided the parties are broadly agreed that arbitration is the right route.

This general arbitrability does not survive contact with a dispute over whether the underlying family arrangement, or the HUF itself, actually exists in the first place, since a party who denies the existence of the HUF or the family settlement is, in substance, denying that there is any agreement to arbitrate at all, a threshold question the arbitrator or the referring court still has to resolve before the merits of the partition claim can proceed. Formalities also matter more than families often expect: an unregistered or insufficiently stamped family settlement can face an admissibility challenge even where nobody disputes the family relationship itself, which is a separate, document-specific risk from the broader arbitrability question this article addresses.

A separate line of authority deals with family settlements reached through mediation rather than negotiated directly between the branches, and treats the outcome more generously than a purely private settlement might expect. A reported 2024 Delhi High Court decision held that a mediated family settlement was enforceable under Section 73 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, and that even an unregistered family settlement reached that way could operate as an estoppel against the family members who signed it, preventing them from later denying its terms even though the settlement itself had not gone through registration. This gives a family a genuine incentive to route a partition disagreement through mediation, where a reasonably experienced mediator can often narrow the dispute considerably even where full agreement is not reached, before falling back on arbitration or litigation for whatever remains unresolved.

Families should also expect that adding further disputed properties or claims to an existing partition arbitration, once it is underway, does not automatically take the expanded claim outside the arbitrator's reach. Courts have held, in the context of a family arrangement's arbitration clause, that a party cannot defeat an otherwise valid reference to arbitration simply by enlarging the scope of what is in dispute after the fact, provided the newly added claim still falls within the same underlying family relationship and the same category of arbitrable right the original reference covered. The practical lesson is that a family member looking to escape an arbitration clause already covering a partition dispute cannot do so simply by adding claims or properties to the mix; escaping the clause requires the added claim to genuinely be of a different, non-arbitrable character, such as a real oppression claim or a trust administration dispute, not merely a larger or more dramatically framed version of the same partition dispute.

Trust Deeds Are a Hard No: The Vimal Kishor Shah Exception

Many business families hold their shared operating company, or their shared pool of investments, through a private family trust, often precisely because a trust offers a cleaner succession structure than direct shareholding across multiple generations. It is exactly this structure that runs into the sharpest, least forgiving exception to general arbitrability in Indian law. In Vimal Kishor Shah v. Jayesh Dinesh Shah, decided on 17 August 2016, the Supreme Court held that disputes arising out of a trust deed and the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 are not arbitrable at all, even where the trust deed itself contains an arbitration clause, adding what commentary describes as a seventh category to the Booz Allen list.

The Court's reasoning rested on two points: that the Indian Trusts Act is a complete code providing its own mechanism for resolving disputes between trustees and beneficiaries, and that beneficiaries of a trust are not, in the Court's view, parties to any arbitration agreement the trust deed might purport to contain, since a beneficiary's rights arise from the trust instrument and the statute rather than from a bilateral contract in the ordinary sense. Commentators have criticised the reasoning, pointing out that a dispute between trustees and beneficiaries over how trust property should be managed looks, on its face, like a right in personam between specific people rather than a right in rem, which is the usual marker for non-arbitrability elsewhere in this body of law. The criticism has not translated into the ruling being overturned, and no later decision reconsidering or overruling it was found in the research for this article, so it should be treated as good law.

The practical consequence for a family that has structured its business succession around a private trust is stark: an arbitration clause in the trust deed, however carefully negotiated, does not bind a dispute between trustees and beneficiaries over how the trust is administered, how trustees are appointed or removed, or how trust property should be distributed. A family relying on arbitration to keep such disputes private and out of court needs to understand that this particular category of dispute will end up before a civil court regardless of what the trust deed says, and should plan the rest of its dispute resolution strategy, and its expectations about confidentiality, accordingly.

The exception has a narrower reach than families sometimes assume, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not cover. Vimal Kishor Shah concerns disputes that genuinely arise out of the trust deed itself, between trustees and beneficiaries acting in those capacities, over how the trust is being run. It does not, on its own terms, extend to a separate contractual dispute between the same individuals that happens to also be beneficiaries of the same trust, for instance a dispute under a shareholders' agreement the beneficiaries separately signed in their personal capacity as shareholders of the operating company the trust happens to hold shares in. The distinction between a dispute that is truly about the trust and a dispute that is merely between people who happen to also be beneficiaries matters a great deal in practice, and families should not assume every disagreement touching a trust-held asset is automatically swept into the non-arbitrable category simply because a trust sits somewhere in the ownership chain.

Shareholder Oppression and Mismanagement: The NCLT's Jealously Guarded Turf

A second major exception that catches family businesses specifically concerns disputes framed as oppression or mismanagement under Sections 241 and 242 of the Companies Act, 2013, the relief a minority shareholder, often a sidelined family branch, seeks when it alleges the majority has run the company unfairly or against its interests. The Bombay High Court, in Rakesh Malhotra v. Rajinder Kumar Malhotra, held that a genuine oppression petition cannot be referred to arbitration merely because the shareholders among the family hold an arbitration clause in a shareholders' agreement, since the NCLT's jurisdiction to grant the specific statutory relief Sections 241 and 242 provide is treated as exclusive and not something the parties can displace by private agreement.

The same ruling carved out an important exception of its own: a petition that is merely "dressed up" as an oppression claim, when in substance it seeks to relitigate an ordinary contractual dispute that properly belongs in arbitration, or a petition that is itself vexatious or brought in bad faith to escape an inconvenient arbitration clause, can still be referred to arbitration, or have the arbitration clause enforced against it, because the label a petitioner chooses does not control the forum any more than the family's own description of the dispute does. The NCLT, in a more recent matter reported as USAR Commerce Technologies v. Utsav Soi, is reported to have declined to split a dispute between its arbitrable and non-arbitrable elements, retaining the whole matter before itself rather than risk two tribunals reaching conflicting findings on overlapping facts.

For a family business, this means the choice of forum for a dispute between shareholding branches often turns less on what the shareholders' agreement says and more on how the dissatisfied branch frames its complaint. A family member unhappy with how dividends are distributed, who wants damages for breach of a specific clause in the shareholders' agreement, is squarely within arbitration. The same family member, framing the identical set of facts as oppressive conduct designed to force them out of the company, is reaching for a remedy only the NCLT can give, and an arbitration clause will not stop that petition from being filed, whatever it predicts about the forum for "all disputes arising under this agreement".

Fraud Allegations No Longer Automatically Defeat Arbitration, Usually

Family disputes frequently come wrapped in allegations that one branch forged a signature, backdated a document, or diverted company funds for personal benefit, allegations that once reliably killed an arbitration clause outright. The Supreme Court's 2010 decision in N. Radhakrishnan v. Maestro Engineers held that where serious fraud and malpractice are alleged, the matter can only be resolved by a court rather than an arbitrator, on the reasoning that such allegations require a detailed evidentiary inquiry a civil court, not an arbitral tribunal, is better placed to conduct.

The Supreme Court narrowed that position considerably in A. Ayyasamy v. A. Paramasivam & Ors., holding that an allegation of fraud does not, by itself, oust an arbitration clause, and confining the exception to cases of serious fraud that goes to the very root of the contract and genuinely requires extensive evidence to resolve, as opposed to the ordinary fraud allegations that accompany a great many commercial and family disputes without actually being complex enough to need a civil trial rather than an arbitration. Commentary on this line of cases notes that the Court did not expressly overrule N. Radhakrishnan so much as confine it to its own, more extreme facts, which leaves a degree of uncertainty about exactly how serious an allegation has to be before it tips a dispute out of arbitration altogether.

A family facing a dispute where one side has raised allegations of forgery, misappropriation or diversion of funds against the other should not assume this alone defeats an otherwise valid arbitration clause, but should also not assume the opposite, that any arbitration clause survives any fraud allegation regardless of how serious it is. Where the allegation genuinely strikes at whether the underlying agreement, the shareholders' agreement, the family settlement, the trust deed, was ever validly formed at all, rather than merely alleging a breach of its terms, the Ayyasamy exception is more likely to apply, and the current state of this specific area of law is worth checking against the latest reported authority before assuming either outcome in a live dispute.

A family tempted to raise a fraud allegation purely as a tactic to avoid an arbitration clause it would otherwise prefer to escape should weigh the cost of that approach carefully. Courts that have seen through dressed-up fraud allegations in the oppression context, as Rakesh Malhotra illustrates for that specific category, have shown no particular reluctance to apply the same scepticism to a fraud allegation raised mainly to avoid arbitration rather than because the facts genuinely support it, and a party found to have exaggerated or fabricated the seriousness of a fraud claim for that purpose risks losing credibility on the substantive dispute as well, not merely on the forum question.

Drafting an Arbitration Clause That Actually Survives Challenge

The structure most likely to hold up when a family business dispute eventually tests it is not a single, all-encompassing agreement but a deliberately separated set of documents, each with an arbitration clause scoped to what that document actually governs. A Family Settlement Agreement dealing with the division of ancestral property and family holdings is one document, with its own arbitration clause covering disputes about that division. A Shareholders' Agreement governing the operating company's management, transfer restrictions and exit mechanics is a separate document, with its own arbitration clause covering disputes about those specific contractual rights. Where a family trust sits in the structure, the trust deed's own arbitration clause, if the family insists on including one despite Vimal Kishor Shah, should not be relied upon as actually enforceable for disputes between trustees and beneficiaries, and the family's planning should proceed on the assumption that such disputes will end up in civil court regardless.

Each clause should also say plainly what it does not cover, carving out expressly any relief that only the NCLT can grant under Sections 241 and 242, so that a dissatisfied family member cannot later argue the broad wording of a shareholders' agreement's arbitration clause was meant to, or accidentally did, oust the NCLT's jurisdiction over oppression and mismanagement claims, an argument that is unlikely to succeed given Rakesh Malhotra but that costs time and legal fees to resolve even when it fails.

Naming the seat and the institution matters as much here as in any other arbitration, but a family dispute benefits particularly from an arbitrator selection mechanism that anticipates the parties may not agree on a sole arbitrator once a real dispute has broken out between branches that no longer trust each other's judgment. A standing panel named in advance, or an institutional rule that appoints an arbitrator by default if the family cannot agree within a set period, avoids the arbitration itself stalling at the appointment stage, which is precisely the moment family disputes are most likely to deadlock.

Families should also think through, at the drafting stage rather than after a dispute has started, who within the family is actually authorised to invoke the arbitration clause and on whose behalf, particularly where shares are held through a karta of an HUF, a holding company, or a trustee acting for multiple beneficiaries rather than by each family member directly in their own name. A clause that assumes every dispute will be a simple two-sided disagreement between named individual signatories can run into real difficulty where the actual dispute is between branches of the family represented through several different corporate and fiduciary layers, each of which may need to be joined to the arbitration, or may dispute that it is bound by the clause at all, before the substantive dispute can even be reached.

Interim Relief While a Family Arbitration Is Pending

A family business dispute often needs urgent protection before an arbitral tribunal is even constituted, freezing a bank account, restraining the transfer or pledge of shares, or preserving company records that one branch controls and the other fears will be altered or destroyed. Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 lets a party apply to a court for interim measures either before arbitral proceedings begin, during them, or at any time before enforcement of the award, and once the tribunal itself is constituted, Section 17 gives it broadly equivalent powers to grant interim relief directly, without the parties needing to return to court for every subsequent protective order.

For a family dispute specifically, the most commonly sought interim relief tends to concern control, an order restraining one branch from exercising voting rights over disputed shares, from convening a board or shareholder meeting pending the arbitration, or from dealing with specific family assets the partition or settlement dispute concerns, rather than the more conventional commercial interim relief of freezing a bank account or an asset pending a money claim. Courts granting this kind of relief in a family context tend to look closely at whether the status quo the applicant wants preserved was genuine and longstanding, or whether it is itself the product of a recent, disputed change one branch pushed through shortly before the dispute broke out, since protecting a recently and unilaterally altered status quo serves the interests of the branch that changed it rather than the interests of fairness between the parties.

When a Dispute Mixes Arbitrable and Non-Arbitrable Elements

The hardest cases in this area are not the ones where a dispute is cleanly arbitrable or cleanly reserved for a court or tribunal, but the ones where a single family quarrel genuinely has both kinds of claims tangled together, a partition claim that is arbitrable sitting alongside an oppression allegation that is not, or a shareholders' agreement dispute sitting alongside a challenge to how a family trust holding the shares is being run. Indian tribunals and courts have shown a growing reluctance to split such disputes mechanically between forums, as the NCLT's approach in USAR Commerce Technologies illustrates, preferring instead to let one forum, often whichever one the bulk of the dispute's substance belongs to, retain the whole matter rather than risk two decision-makers reaching inconsistent findings on overlapping facts.

This preference for keeping a tangled dispute together cuts in different directions depending on which forum ends up asserting that preference. Where the NCLT decides to retain a dispute in full because part of it involves oppression relief, a family that structured its shareholders' agreement expecting arbitration to resolve the bulk of its disagreements can find the entire matter, including the purely contractual parts that would otherwise plainly be arbitrable, swept into the NCLT proceeding instead. A family drafting its governing documents with this risk in mind is better served keeping its truly non-arbitrable exposure, anything that could plausibly be framed as oppression or as a trust administration dispute, as narrow and well-contained as possible, precisely because a broader non-arbitrable claim has a greater gravitational pull on the rest of the dispute than a narrow one does.

Practical Guidance for Drafting Family Business Succession Documents

Families setting up or restructuring a shared business are better served treating the arbitrability question as a drafting exercise to be resolved document by document, rather than a single clause to be copied from a template and dropped into every agreement the family signs. Each document, the family settlement, the shareholders' agreement, any trust deed the family uses, should be drafted with a clear-eyed view of what kind of dispute it is actually likely to produce, and the arbitration clause in each should be written to match that specific document's own likely disputes rather than aspiring to cover the whole family relationship in one sweep.

Families who have already structured their business around a trust holding company shares should treat Vimal Kishor Shah as a planning constraint rather than a drafting problem to be solved with cleverer wording, since no clause currently available under Indian law reliably routes a trustee-beneficiary dispute into arbitration. Where confidentiality about disputes concerning the trust specifically matters to the family, mediation, which does not face the same statutory bar, or a privately negotiated settlement before any dispute reaches a court filing, are more realistic tools than an arbitration clause the courts will not enforce for that purpose.

Finally, families should revisit these documents whenever the business itself changes materially, a new generation taking an operational role, a listing or external investment bringing in shareholders outside the family, or a branch of the family becoming a passive rather than active participant, since each of these changes can shift which category a future dispute is likely to fall into, and a clause drafted for a wholly family-owned private company does not automatically continue to make sense once outside shareholders, and the heightened regulatory scrutiny that comes with them, enter the picture.

What Remains Unsettled

Three points in this area deserve a caveat. The precise boundary between an ordinary fraud allegation and the "serious fraud" that still defeats an arbitration clause under the Ayyasamy line of cases has not been pinned down with the kind of bright-line test families would find useful in planning their documents, and depends heavily on the specific facts a court is asked to assess at the time a Section 8 or Section 11 application is made. Whether the NCLT's practice of retaining an entire mixed dispute, rather than referring the arbitrable parts out, will harden into a settled rule or continue to be decided case by case was not fully resolved in the material reviewed for this article. And while no decision reconsidering Vimal Kishor Shah's treatment of trust disputes was found in this research, the criticism that ruling has attracted from commentators means a family relying on it, in either direction, should check for any later Supreme Court development before treating the seventh category as permanently fixed in its current form.

An arbitration clause in a family business's governing documents is a genuinely useful tool, and for the largest share of disputes these families actually have, a disagreement over a partition, a breach of a shareholders' agreement, a dispute over the terms of a family settlement, it works exactly as intended, keeping the fight private and resolving it on a timeline a court docket cannot match. It simply does not reach every kind of dispute a family business can produce, and a family that discovers this only after an oppression petition or a trust dispute has already been filed has discovered it at the most expensive possible moment.

The more useful question for a family setting up its governing documents is not whether to include an arbitration clause, which is almost always worth doing, but which specific disputes that clause can actually be relied upon to catch, and which ones need a different plan entirely because no clause, however well drafted, will route them away from a court or the NCLT. Working that out document by document, before a dispute forces the question, is considerably cheaper than litigating it afterward.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a dispute between family members over property be arbitrated in India?

Generally yes. A partition of family property, or a dispute over whether a family settlement is valid and should be enforced, is treated as a right in personam between specific family members and is usually arbitrable, as the Delhi High Court's January 2026 order in Pranav Gupta v. Adeesh Gupta illustrates by referring a combined family partition and business dispute to a sole arbitrator.

Can a family trust dispute be resolved through arbitration?

No, even if the trust deed itself contains an arbitration clause. The Supreme Court held in Vimal Kishor Shah v. Jayesh Dinesh Shah in 2016 that disputes arising out of a trust deed and the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 are not arbitrable, reasoning that the Act is a complete code and that beneficiaries are not parties to any arbitration agreement in the trust deed.

Can a shareholder oppression claim between family members be sent to arbitration?

Generally no. The Bombay High Court held in Rakesh Malhotra v. Rajinder Kumar Malhotra that a genuine oppression and mismanagement petition under Sections 241 and 242 of the Companies Act cannot be referred to arbitration merely because the shareholders have a shareholders' agreement with an arbitration clause, since the NCLT's jurisdiction over that specific relief is treated as exclusive. A petition that is merely a dressed-up contractual dispute can still be referred.

Does an allegation of fraud stop a family dispute from being arbitrated?

Not automatically. The Supreme Court in A. Ayyasamy v. A. Paramasivam confined the exception to cases of serious fraud going to the root of the contract and requiring extensive evidence to resolve. Ordinary fraud allegations that accompany a commercial or family dispute without that level of complexity generally do not defeat an otherwise valid arbitration clause.

Should a family business use one agreement or several for its succession and governance structure?

Several, generally. A Family Settlement Agreement for the division of property, a Shareholders' Agreement for company governance, and a trust deed where applicable are better drafted as separate documents, each with its own arbitration clause scoped to the disputes that document is actually likely to produce, rather than one document trying to cover partition, trust administration and company governance together.

What happens if a family dispute mixes an arbitrable claim with a non-arbitrable one, such as oppression?

Indian tribunals increasingly decline to split such disputes mechanically between forums. The NCLT has, in at least one reported matter, retained an entire mixed dispute rather than referring the arbitrable parts to arbitration separately, which means a broader non-arbitrable claim can end up pulling even the clearly arbitrable parts of the dispute along with it.

What interim protection is available while a family arbitration is pending?

Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 lets a party seek interim relief from a court before, during, or after arbitral proceedings, and Section 17 gives the arbitral tribunal itself similar powers once constituted. In family disputes this most often takes the form of an order restraining dealings with disputed shares or company control pending the outcome.
