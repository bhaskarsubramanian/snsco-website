Prenuptial and Postnuptial Agreements in India: What an HNI Marriage Can Actually Bind

A wealthy family asked to sign a prenuptial agreement before a wedding usually wants to know one thing: will a court actually enforce it. The honest answer in India today is that parts of it will, parts of it will not, and the dividing line is not where most people assume it is.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 15 November 2026 · Law stated as on 15 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Prenuptial agreements have moved, over the last decade, from a subject Indian family lawyers mentioned mainly to explain why they would not work to a document HNI families now routinely ask for before a wedding involving business wealth, inherited property, or a marriage across two countries. The honest position has not changed as fast as the demand: India has no statute that creates and enforces marital contracts the way a prenuptial agreement works in the United States, England or Goa's own Portuguese-derived civil code, and a court asked to give effect to one still has to fit it into the ordinary law of contract and the separate, mandatory law of maintenance that personal statutes impose. What falls out of that exercise is a document that can do real work for a family's asset planning, provided the family understands exactly which parts of it a court will actually hold the parties to.

Key points

  • India has no dedicated statute making prenuptial or postnuptial agreements enforceable as marriage contracts, outside Goa, where the Portuguese Civil Code of 1867 still governs marital property and gives couples a real choice of regime before marriage.
  • A prenuptial agreement is treated as an ordinary contract under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, which means it must satisfy Section 10's requirements of free consent and lawful consideration, and it must also clear Section 23, which voids any agreement whose object or consideration is opposed to public policy.
  • Courts have consistently treated a clause that purports to waive or cap a spouse's statutory right to maintenance as contrary to public policy and therefore void under Section 23, because maintenance is a right the applicable personal law and, for some claims, the Code of Criminal Procedure framework confers independently of any contract between the parties.
  • Clauses dealing with each party's own separately owned property, disclosed before marriage, and arrangements for dividing specific, identified assets on divorce have a more realistic prospect of being treated as a relevant factor by a court, even where the agreement as a whole is not enforced as a binding contract in the way a commercial agreement would be.
  • Goa remains the one part of India where prenuptial agreements operate as the Portuguese Civil Code intends, letting a couple choose between a communion-of-assets regime and a separation-of-assets regime before marriage, with the choice generally treated as binding and not freely revocable once the marriage takes place.
  • A will, rather than a prenuptial agreement, remains the more reliable tool for an HNI family wanting to fix how a spouse's inheritance rights interact with a planned succession, since testamentary freedom over self-acquired property is far more settled in Indian law than the enforceability of a marital contract.
  • Full financial disclosure, independent legal advice for both parties, and a genuinely voluntary execution well before the wedding date are not legal formalities for their own sake; they are the specific facts advisers and, where the question has reached a court, judges have pointed to when deciding how much weight an agreement actually deserves.

Why HNI Families Ask for This Now

A prenuptial agreement used to be associated, in the Indian imagination, with Hollywood divorces and with families who had decided in advance that the marriage would probably fail. That association has faded faster than the underlying law has caught up to it. Families with a business to protect, a significant inherited estate, or a marriage between an Indian partner and a foreign national with assets in more than one country now ask for a prenuptial agreement for reasons that have very little to do with pessimism about the marriage: clarity on which assets stay outside the marital pool, protection for a family business's shareholding from being drawn into a matrimonial dispute decades later, and a documented understanding both families can point to if a dispute ever arises about what was promised to whom.

The demand has also grown because more marriages now cross jurisdictions where prenuptial agreements are both common and generally enforceable, the United States, England and much of continental Europe among them, and a foreign spouse or a foreign-resident Indian spouse often arrives at the negotiation assuming the Indian legal position mirrors what prenups does at home. It does not, and the gap between that assumption and the actual Indian position is exactly where families most need a clear explanation before, not after, they sign something.

This piece sets out what Indian law will currently hold a couple to, what it will not, and why Goa remains the one part of the country where the answer is genuinely different. None of what follows should be read as advice against having a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement; a document that is honestly drafted around what Indian courts will actually enforce still does useful work, and often more useful work than a document copied from a foreign precedent and hoped into effect.

The gap between expectation and legal reality matters most at the moment a family is deciding how much time and legal fee to put into the document in the first place. A family that believes the agreement will operate as a complete, self-executing settlement of every financial question the marriage could ever raise is likely to under-invest in the parts that genuinely matter, the asset schedules and disclosure, while over-investing in maintenance and custody language that a court is simply not going to enforce. Setting realistic expectations at the outset, which is the purpose of this piece, tends to produce a better-drafted document, not a weaker one.

There Is No Indian Statute for This, So the Contract Act Does the Work

Unlike several personal laws that regulate marriage and divorce in detail, no Indian statute specifically creates, defines or enforces a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement as a distinct category of marital contract, outside the Goa Civil Code discussed separately below. Where a family executes one, it is treated, by default, as an ordinary contract governed by the Indian Contract Act, 1872, which means the agreement has to satisfy Section 10's conditions, free consent, lawful consideration, and a lawful object, before a court will give it any effect at all.

Satisfying Section 10 on paper is usually the easy part; most carefully drafted prenuptial agreements will recite consideration, confirm both parties signed freely, and describe a lawful object in terms that look unobjectionable. The harder test comes from Section 23 of the same Act, which voids an agreement, or any part of it, where the consideration or object is unlawful, or where the court regards it as opposed to public policy, even if nothing about the agreement looks unlawful on its face in the way a contract to commit a crime would. It is Section 23's public-policy limb, rather than anything in Section 10, that does most of the work in deciding how much of a prenuptial agreement an Indian court will actually enforce.

A separate point families sometimes raise is whether the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, the Special Marriage Act, 1954, or the personal law applicable to the marriage specifically forbids a prenuptial agreement. None of these statutes contains a provision expressly voiding such agreements. The real obstacle is not a specific prohibition in matrimonial legislation; it is that these statutes independently confer rights, principally the right to maintenance, that the courts have treated as incapable of being bargained away by private agreement, a distinction worth keeping clear because it changes what a family should actually expect the agreement to achieve.

The Maintenance Problem: Why That Clause Almost Never Survives

The clause most families actually want in a prenuptial agreement, a cap or a complete waiver of a spouse's right to maintenance if the marriage ends, is also the clause Indian courts have been most consistent in refusing to enforce. The right to maintenance under Hindu law, the Special Marriage Act, and the maintenance provisions available under the criminal procedure framework is treated as a statutory right conferred on the spouse independently of the marriage contract, a right the legislature intended to exist regardless of what the parties privately agreed, precisely because its purpose is to prevent a spouse, overwhelmingly though not always a wife, from being left without means after a marriage ends.

Early Indian authority on this point goes back more than a century. Courts examining separation deeds containing maintenance clauses have held, as far back as the beginning of the twentieth century, that an agreement by a spouse to give up the right to future maintenance lacks consideration moving the other way and is not enforceable in the manner an ordinary commercial contract would be, and later decisions applying the same reasoning have treated a clause purporting to extinguish that right outright as contrary to public policy under Section 23. A spouse who signs a prenuptial agreement waiving maintenance has not, as the law currently stands, actually given up the right to ask a court for maintenance later; the clause simply will not stop the claim from being heard on its merits.

This does not mean a maintenance-related clause in a prenuptial agreement is entirely without value. Courts examining a maintenance claim have, in some instances, treated an existing financial arrangement between the parties, where it is fair, properly disclosed, and supported by real consideration, as a relevant fact bearing on the amount of maintenance eventually awarded, rather than as a binding ceiling the court is required to apply. A family that wants a prenuptial agreement to actually influence a future maintenance outcome should treat the clause as persuasive material for the court to weigh, not as a number the agreement itself can lock in regardless of the spouse's actual needs at the time of the dispute.

Separation Clauses, No-Child Clauses and Other Terms Courts Read as Void

Beyond maintenance, Indian commentary and the limited reported decisions in this area treat certain other categories of clause as vulnerable to the same Section 23 public-policy objection. A clause that purports to fix in advance that the marriage will be treated as over, or that either party may unilaterally declare a separation, sits uneasily against the statutory grounds and procedure for divorce that personal law actually requires a court to apply, and a court is unlikely to treat a private contractual declaration of separation as having the legal effect of an actual decree.

A clause restricting whether the couple will have children, or dictating custody or parenting arrangements for children not yet born, runs into a different but related problem: custody and the welfare of a child are matters a court decides at the time of the dispute, based on the child's actual best interests as they then stand, and a court will not treat an agreement entered into years earlier, before the child existed, as binding on that later welfare determination. Families who include such clauses in a prenuptial agreement, often at the insistence of one side's family rather than the couple themselves, should understand that the clause is unlikely to carry legal weight if the question is ever actually litigated.

What tends to survive scrutiny, by contrast, is a clause that simply records facts rather than attempting to extinguish a statutory right: a schedule of each party's separately owned assets as they stood before the marriage, a description of how specific gifts or inheritances received during the marriage will be treated, and an agreed mechanism, such as a family arbitration or mediation step, for resolving disputes about property division if the marriage ends. These clauses do not ask a court to override a right the legislature created; they simply document an understanding the parties can point to as evidence of their intentions, which a court is considerably more willing to take into account.

Property Clauses: Where a Prenuptial Agreement Can Genuinely Help

India has no general matrimonial property regime of the kind that automatically pools spouses' assets on marriage and divides them on divorce, outside Goa. Outside Goa, each spouse generally retains ownership of property that is legally theirs, whether acquired before or during the marriage, and a court dividing assets on divorce works from the parties' actual legal titles and any specific statutory provision for alimony or settlement of property, rather than from a general community-of-property starting point. This actually makes a well-drafted property schedule in a prenuptial agreement more useful in India than it might be in a jurisdiction that already pools marital property by default, because the schedule gives the court, and the parties themselves, clear documentary evidence of what belonged to whom before the marriage and what was understood to remain separate.

For an HNI family, the most valuable use of a prenuptial agreement is usually not an attempt to override maintenance or custody law at all; it is this kind of asset-mapping exercise, identifying the family business shareholding, inherited property, and pre-marriage investments that the family wants kept clearly outside any future dispute, and documenting, at the time of the marriage, that both parties understood and accepted this. A court asked to divide property years later is considerably more likely to respect a party's claim that a particular asset was always separate where that claim is backed by a document both sides signed at the time, with full disclosure, than where the claim rests only on the party's own later assertion.

A related and often more effective tool for the same underlying goal is a will rather than a prenuptial agreement. Testamentary freedom over self-acquired property is a far more settled area of Indian law than the enforceability of a marital contract, and an HNI family planning how an estate will pass on death, including how a surviving spouse's share interacts with children from the marriage or from an earlier one, usually achieves more certainty through careful will drafting, read together with a prenuptial agreement's asset schedule, than through the prenuptial agreement alone.

Postnuptial Agreements and Settlement Deeds During the Marriage

Timing is the main thing that changes once the agreement is signed after rather than before the wedding. A postnuptial agreement faces broadly the same public-policy analysis as a prenuptial one where it deals with maintenance, custody or the existence of the marriage itself, but it has one practical advantage a prenuptial agreement does not: by the time the parties sign it, the marriage's actual financial position, each spouse's actual contribution, and any specific dispute already in the air are known facts rather than predictions, which tends to make a postnuptial settlement's property provisions easier to treat as a fair, considered bargain rather than a speculative attempt to fix outcomes in advance.

Separation deeds and family settlement arrangements entered into once a marriage has already broken down, even if the couple has not yet divorced, have a longer history before Indian courts than prenuptial agreements proper, and courts have sometimes given these agreements real weight on the specific property arrangements they contain, while still declining to treat a clause waiving future maintenance as binding for the reasons discussed above. A family drafting a postnuptial settlement at a point of genuine marital difficulty should expect the same split outcome a prenuptial agreement gets: the asset and property terms stand a reasonable chance of being respected, while any attempt to cap or extinguish a statutory maintenance claim does not.

Where a postnuptial agreement is tied to a specific event, a lump sum payment, a transfer of a particular property, or a defined division of jointly accumulated assets, rather than to an open-ended promise about future maintenance, the agreement tends to hold up considerably better in practice, because a court can examine the specific transfer that actually took place and ask whether it was fair and voluntary, rather than being asked to enforce an abstract promise about a right the law has already fixed independently.

Goa: The One Jurisdiction Where the Answer Is Genuinely Different

Goa is the one part of India where a prenuptial agreement functions in something close to the way families from common-law jurisdictions expect. The Portuguese Civil Code of 1867, kept in force in Goa after its 1961 integration into India by the Goa, Daman and Diu Administration Act, 1962, sets communion of assets, broadly, the pooling of both spouses' property into shared ownership on marriage, as the default regime that applies where a couple has not made a prenuptial agreement at all.

A couple marrying in Goa can instead execute a prenuptial agreement before the marriage choosing a different regime, most commonly total separation of assets, under which each spouse's property remains separately owned throughout the marriage, or a hybrid arrangement drawing on elements of both regimes. Once made, this choice is generally treated under the Code as fixed for the duration of the marriage rather than something either spouse can unilaterally revoke, which is precisely the feature, binding effect once executed, that a prenuptial agreement outside Goa currently lacks for its property provisions, let alone its maintenance provisions.

An HNI family with any genuine connection to Goa, a marriage actually solemnised there, or a willingness to structure the marriage and the couple's residence to bring the Goa Civil Code into play, has a meaningfully stronger prenuptial option available than a family marrying anywhere else in India under Hindu, Muslim, Christian or Special Marriage Act law. This is a narrow and somewhat artificial route for most families, since it depends on the Goa Code's own rules on when it applies rather than on the parties simply choosing Goa law by agreement, and a family seriously considering it should have the actual applicability question examined specifically rather than assuming that executing a document styled on the Goa Code, without the marriage or the parties having the necessary connection to Goa, achieves the same effect.

Drafting Practices That Give an Agreement the Best Chance of Being Respected

Full and honest financial disclosure by both parties before the agreement is signed is the single factor advisers and the limited judicial commentary in this area return to most consistently. An agreement where one party later shows that significant assets were concealed, or that the disclosed financial picture was materially misleading, gives a court an easy and well-established route to disregard the agreement entirely, independent of the Section 23 public-policy question discussed throughout this piece.

Independent legal advice for each party, rather than one lawyer drafting the agreement for both sides and the other party simply signing it, strengthens the argument that consent was genuinely free within the meaning of Section 10 of the Contract Act, and removes one of the more obvious grounds on which the weaker or less legally sophisticated party could later challenge the agreement as unconscionable or coerced.

Timing matters in a way families sometimes overlook: an agreement presented and signed only days or hours before the wedding, under the practical pressure of cancelling an already-arranged ceremony, is considerably more vulnerable to a claim of duress or lack of genuine free consent than one negotiated and signed with enough advance notice that either party could realistically have walked away from the marriage instead of signing. Families planning a prenuptial agreement should build the negotiation into the engagement period well ahead of the wedding date rather than treating it as a formality to be completed in the final week.

Finally, the agreement should be drafted around what this piece has described as actually enforceable, asset schedules, treatment of specific gifts and inheritances, and a dispute-resolution mechanism, rather than copied from a template built for English or American law that spends most of its text on maintenance waivers and custody provisions an Indian court is unlikely to give effect to. A shorter, more honest agreement focused on property documentation generally serves an HNI family better than a longer one modelled on a jurisdiction where the underlying law is genuinely different.

Cross-Border Marriages: A Foreign Spouse, a Foreign Prenup, and an Indian Divorce

A marriage between an Indian HNI and a foreign national, or between two Indian citizens where one or both live abroad, often arrives with a prenuptial agreement already drafted under the law of the foreign spouse's home jurisdiction, England, a US state, or wherever the couple expects to live. The agreement's own clause choosing that foreign law to govern it does not, by itself, guarantee an Indian court will apply that foreign law's more permissive approach to maintenance waivers or property division if the divorce itself is eventually litigated in India rather than abroad; the same public-policy analysis under Section 23 of the Contract Act applies regardless of which country's law the agreement says governs it, because an Indian court will not enforce even a foreign-law-governed clause where doing so would offend Indian public policy as the court understands it.

Where the marriage is solemnised in India under the Special Marriage Act, 1954, which is the route most cross-border couples use when one party is a foreign national, the maintenance and property provisions that Act and the connected procedural law make available to the parties operate independently of whatever the foreign-drafted prenuptial agreement says, for exactly the same reason that a Hindu Marriage Act maintenance claim cannot be waived by contract. A foreign spouse who signed a prenuptial agreement at home, confident it would govern an eventual Indian divorce exactly as it would at home, is often surprised to learn that confidence does not travel with the document once an Indian court is actually seized of the matter.

None of this means a foreign-drafted agreement is useless once an Indian forum is in play. Its property schedules and asset-disclosure provisions, the parts of any prenuptial agreement this piece has identified as the most durable, generally translate reasonably well regardless of which jurisdiction's lawyers drafted them, because they are factual records rather than attempts to override a statutory right. A cross-border family is usually better served by having the agreement reviewed by Indian counsel specifically for how an Indian court would treat each clause, rather than assuming a document valid at home needs no further attention simply because one spouse happens to be Indian or the couple happens to own property in India.

Prenuptial Agreements and the Family Business: Working Alongside, Not Instead of, the Shareholders' Agreement

Where an HNI family's wealth sits substantially in a family business, the prenuptial agreement is only one of several documents that actually protect the business from a future matrimonial dispute, and it is rarely the most important one. A shareholders' agreement or articles of association carrying a right of first refusal, a drag-along right, or a restriction on transferring shares to anyone outside the family without the other shareholders' consent does more practical work in keeping a divorcing spouse from acquiring an actual stake in the business than a prenuptial clause declaring the shares to be separate property, because the corporate document binds the company and its other shareholders directly, while the prenuptial agreement's effectiveness depends entirely on whether a court treats it as enforceable at the time of the dispute.

A prenuptial agreement's more useful role alongside these corporate protections is evidentiary: recording, at the time of the marriage, that the incoming spouse understood the shareholding was pre-existing family property, was not acquired using any contribution from the marriage, and was never intended to form part of the couple's joint assets. This kind of recital does not by itself stop a court from considering the shareholding's value when assessing an overall maintenance or settlement claim, since Indian courts have shown themselves willing to look at a spouse's overall financial position, including family wealth, when fixing maintenance, but it does help establish that the shares themselves, as distinct from income or dividends actually drawn during the marriage, were never joint property to begin with.

Families planning a wedding where a family business is involved should have the prenuptial agreement, the shareholders' agreement, and any family trust or will addressing the business's succession reviewed together by the same advisory team, rather than negotiated separately by different lawyers working from different assumptions about what the other document already covers. A gap between what the prenuptial agreement assumes the shareholders' agreement protects, and what the shareholders' agreement actually says, is exactly the kind of gap that surfaces, at the worst possible time, once a marriage is already in difficulty.

Where the business operates through a private family trust rather than, or in addition to, direct shareholding, the same coordination question arises in a different form: a spouse who later claims an entitlement against the marriage is not usually claiming against the trust itself, since the trust's assets belong to the trust and not to any individual beneficiary personally, but a court assessing an overall maintenance or settlement claim may still look at a spouse's beneficial interest or expected future benefit under the trust as part of that spouse's broader financial position. A prenuptial agreement recording that a beneficiary's interest in an existing family trust predates the marriage and was never intended to be shared does the same evidentiary work for a trust structure that it does for direct shareholding, and should be drafted with the trust deed's own terms specifically in view rather than in general language that does not actually engage with how the trust holds its assets.

How a Prenuptial Agreement Actually Reaches a Court

A prenuptial agreement does not enforce itself; it has to be placed before a court at some stage of a matrimonial proceeding, typically a divorce petition under the applicable personal law, an application for interim or permanent maintenance under that law or under the maintenance provisions of the criminal procedure framework, or a separate civil suit over a specific property. In practice, the party relying on the agreement usually introduces it as evidence supporting a particular factual claim, that an asset was always separate, that a sum was already paid in settlement, rather than asking the court to simply enforce the document as a free-standing contract the way a commercial court would enforce a supply agreement.

Family courts hearing these matters often also encourage, and in some proceedings are required to attempt, mediation and settlement discussions between the parties before the contested hearing, and a well-drafted prenuptial agreement frequently does its most practical work at this earlier, less formal stage, by giving both sides' lawyers a documented starting point for negotiating a consent settlement rather than litigating every asset from scratch. Many prenuptial agreements that would not survive a determined legal challenge if fully litigated still end up shaping the actual outcome of a divorce, simply because neither side wants to spend years and legal fees testing the document's enforceability in court when a negotiated settlement broadly along its lines is available instead.

This practical reality is worth keeping in view alongside the strict legal analysis in the rest of this piece. A family should not draft a prenuptial agreement purely on the assumption that a judge will one day read every clause and rule formally on its validity; in most cases that actually involve one, the agreement's influence is exercised earlier, in negotiation and settlement, where its reasonableness and the quality of its disclosure matter more than the precise legal category a court might eventually place each clause into.

A corollary of this is that the agreement's tone and apparent fairness matter almost as much as its strict legal content. An agreement that reads, on its face, as heavily one-sided, leaving one spouse with little or nothing regardless of the length of the marriage or that spouse's actual contribution to the family's wealth over the years, tends to attract more scrutiny and less sympathy from a court or from the other side's lawyers in settlement talks, even on the clauses that would otherwise have a reasonable claim to being enforced. A family that wants its prenuptial agreement to actually carry weight when it matters should resist the temptation to extract every possible advantage in the document and should instead aim for terms that look, to a neutral reader years later, like a fair bargain both parties could sensibly have agreed to at the time.

What Remains Unsettled

No Supreme Court ruling has squarely decided the general enforceability of a prenuptial agreement's property provisions outside Goa, and the position described in this piece rests on the public-policy reasoning applied in older maintenance-waiver cases, extended by commentary and practitioner analysis to the modern prenuptial agreement context, rather than on a single controlling authority addressing prenuptial agreements by that name. A family relying on a prenuptial agreement's property terms should treat that reliance as reasonably well-supported by the reasoning available, not as resting on a decided case directly on point, because no such case could be confirmed in this research.

Whether a prenuptial agreement's terms can bind a court at all when a maintenance claim is brought under the specific criminal procedure route available to a spouse, as distinct from a civil maintenance claim under personal law, is also not something this piece can state with confidence turns out any differently from the civil position described above; the research available treats the two routes together as subject to the same public-policy limitation, but a family with a live dispute on this specific point should have it checked against the latest authority on that route particularly, since maintenance law generally has moved quickly in recent years.

A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement in India today is not the binding marital contract it would be in many of the jurisdictions HNI families and their foreign-connected relatives are used to, and no clause waiving or capping maintenance, dictating custody, or declaring the marriage over in advance should be expected to hold up if it is ever actually tested in court. What the agreement can do, reliably, is document each party's separate assets, record an honest and disclosed understanding of how specific property and gifts will be treated, and give a family business or an inherited estate a documentary record that makes a later property dispute considerably easier to resolve on the family's own terms. Built around that narrower but genuinely useful purpose, and outside Goa's distinct regime, a prenuptial agreement remains worth drafting carefully; built around the broader promises a template from another jurisdiction makes, it is largely worth no more than the paper it is printed on.

The families that get the most out of this exercise tend to be the ones who treat the prenuptial agreement as one piece of a wider plan, alongside the shareholders' agreement, the trust deed and the will, rather than as a single document meant to settle every question a marriage's end could raise. Approached that way, and reviewed again whenever the family's own structure or asset base changes materially, the agreement earns its place in the family's papers regardless of how a court would eventually rule on any single clause within it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are prenuptial agreements legally valid in India?

There is no dedicated statute making them enforceable as marriage contracts, so they are treated as ordinary contracts under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Property-related clauses with full disclosure have a reasonable chance of being respected; clauses waiving maintenance, fixing custody in advance, or declaring the marriage over are generally treated as void under Section 23 of the Act.

Can a prenuptial agreement stop my spouse from claiming maintenance later?

No. Courts have consistently treated a clause waiving or capping statutory maintenance as contrary to public policy and therefore unenforceable. The claim can still be heard on its merits regardless of what the agreement says, though an existing fair arrangement may be considered as one factor in deciding the amount.

Is Goa the only place in India where prenuptial agreements work differently?

Yes. The Portuguese Civil Code of 1867, retained in Goa, lets a couple choose a matrimonial property regime, commonly separation of assets instead of the default communion of assets, before marriage, and that choice is generally treated as binding for the marriage's duration. No other part of India has an equivalent regime.

What can a prenuptial agreement actually protect for a family business?

A clear, disclosed schedule of pre-marriage assets, including a family business shareholding, gives a court documentary evidence that the asset was always separately owned if a property dispute arises later. This asset-mapping function, rather than any attempt to override maintenance or custody law, is where a prenuptial agreement does its most reliable work in India.

Does a postnuptial agreement work any differently from a prenuptial one?

The same public-policy limits apply to maintenance, custody and separation clauses. Postnuptial agreements tend to fare somewhat better on their property provisions because they are made once the marriage's actual financial position is known, which makes it easier for a court to treat the arrangement as a fair, considered bargain.

Should I use a will instead of a prenuptial agreement to protect my estate?

For succession specifically, often yes. Testamentary freedom over self-acquired property is more settled under Indian law than the enforceability of a marital contract, and a will addressing how a spouse's share interacts with the rest of the family is usually a more reliable tool, used alongside rather than instead of a prenuptial agreement's asset schedule.

Does signing a prenuptial agreement without a lawyer make it invalid?

It is not automatically invalid, but independent legal advice for each party strengthens the case that consent was genuinely free, which matters under Section 10 of the Contract Act. An agreement drafted by one side's lawyer with the other party simply signing is more exposed to a later challenge.

Can a prenuptial agreement signed under family pressure just before the wedding be challenged?

Yes. An agreement presented and signed only shortly before a wedding, under the practical pressure of an already-arranged ceremony, is more vulnerable to a claim that consent was not genuinely free than one negotiated with enough advance notice for either party to walk away.
