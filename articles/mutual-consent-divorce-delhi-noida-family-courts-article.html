Mutual Consent Divorce in Delhi and Noida: The Section 13B Procedure and the Fight Over Waiving the Wait

How the two-motion process under Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act actually runs in Delhi's family courts and Gautam Buddh Nagar's family court, and why the six-month and one-year waiting periods are being argued over, not simply applied

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 23 November 2026 · Law stated as on 23 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A couple who have agreed to end their marriage and settled every contested question between them, custody, maintenance, the division of assets, still have to pass through a statutory sequence before a Delhi or Noida family court will actually dissolve the marriage, and that sequence has become genuinely unsettled at exactly the point where most couples want speed: whether the waiting periods built into Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 can be shortened or skipped. Courts in the same city have gone different ways on that question within the last two years, and a couple should go into the process knowing that the answer depends heavily on which judge hears the case and how the facts are presented, not on a settled rule that applies uniformly across Delhi NCR.

Key points

  • Section 13B(1) requires the couple to have lived separately for one year immediately before filing a joint petition, and Section 13B(2) requires a second motion filed between six and eighteen months after the first, with the decree following only after that.
  • The Supreme Court held in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, (2017) 8 SCC 746, that the six-month period in Section 13B(2) is directory rather than mandatory and can be waived where listed factors are satisfied, and clarified in Amit Kumar v. Suman Beniwal, 2021 SCC OnLine SC 1270, that those factors are themselves not conditions that must each be separately proved.
  • Delhi's own courts had disagreed on how far that waiver power extends, with some orders restricting waiver to couples already separated for more than a year, but a Delhi High Court Full Bench resolved the broader question on 17 December 2025 in Shiksha Kumari v. Santosh Kumar, MAT.APP.(F.C.) 111/2025, holding that even the one-year separation precondition under Section 13B(1) is directory and can be waived under the proviso to Section 14(1), overruling contrary single-bench orders.
  • Either spouse can withdraw consent at any point before the decree is actually passed, which means a joint petition does not guarantee a decree even after both motions are filed and heard.
  • The Family Courts Act, 1984 governs the Delhi family courts and the Gautam Buddh Nagar family court, and Section 19 of that Act, not Section 28 of the Hindu Marriage Act alone, controls the right of appeal, including a flat bar on appealing a decree passed with the consent of both parties.
  • An appeal under Section 19 lies to a bench of two or more judges of the High Court within thirty days, and interlocutory orders passed during the proceeding are generally not appealable under that section at all.
  • Noida falls under the family court at Gautam Buddh Nagar, and while the same Hindu Marriage Act provisions apply there as in Delhi, the Allahabad High Court has not adopted identical positions to the Delhi High Court on every waiver question, so a Noida petition cannot simply assume the Delhi position will be followed.

What Section 13B actually requires before a joint petition can even be filed

Section 13B(1) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 lets a husband and wife present a joint petition for divorce where they have been living separately for a period of one year or more, have not been able to live together, and have mutually agreed that the marriage should be dissolved. All three conditions are read together as preconditions to filing, not as matters a court can simply overlook once the parties appear before it willing to separate. The separation has to immediately precede the presentation of the petition, and courts have repeatedly said that living separately does not necessarily mean living under different roofs; a couple occupying the same residence but functioning as separated individuals, having ceased to perform marital obligations toward each other, can in principle satisfy the requirement, though a petition built on that version of the facts invites closer scrutiny than one where the parties have genuinely been living apart.

A joint petition that is filed before the one-year mark is not automatically void, because Section 14 of the Act lets a court grant leave to present a petition before one year has elapsed on the ground that the case involves exceptional hardship to the petitioner or exceptional depravity on the part of the respondent, and a court that later finds those grounds made out can treat an early filing as validly entertained. What a couple cannot do is simply ignore the one-year requirement and ask the family court to waive it as a matter of convenience, since the weight of authority, including a Madhya Pradesh High Court ruling holding the one-year period mandatory and not waivable, treats this precondition differently from the later six-month gap between the two motions. A Delhi High Court Full Bench, however, settled the point for Delhi on 17 December 2025 in Shiksha Kumari v. Santosh Kumar, MAT.APP.(F.C.) 111/2025 (Chawla, Bhambhani and Bhatnagar JJ.), holding the Section 13B(1) condition directory and waivable under the proviso to Section 14(1), and overruling single-bench orders that had treated Section 13B as a complete code; because it is a Full Bench ruling it binds smaller Delhi benches going forward, though the Madhya Pradesh High Court's contrary, mandatory reading means the position is not uniform nationally, and the Full Bench itself cautioned against routine or wanton use of the Section 14(1) proviso.

The first motion and what the family court actually records

Once the one-year separation condition is met, the couple files a joint petition under Section 13B(1), supported by a joint statement recording the facts of the marriage, the date and manner of separation, the absence of collusion, and the terms the parties have agreed on for maintenance, the return or retention of stridhan, custody and visitation arrangements for any children, and the division of jointly held property. In Delhi, this petition goes before the concerned family court under the Pre-Decree Mediation and Conciliation framework that most Delhi family courts now follow, which typically refers the couple to a mediation centre attached to the court before the first motion is formally recorded, on the premise that a court should satisfy itself the consent is genuine and not the product of pressure from either side before it starts the statutory clock running.

At the first motion hearing, the presiding judge records statements from both spouses confirming the contents of the joint petition and their continuing consent to the divorce, and it is only after this recording that the second motion period begins to run. A spouse who has second thoughts at this stage, before the first motion is even recorded, can simply decline to go ahead, and no adverse order typically follows from withdrawing before the first motion, since the petition itself depends on both parties jointly maintaining their position throughout.

The gap between the two motions, and why it exists

Section 13B(2) requires the second motion to be filed not before six months have passed from the date of presenting the petition, and not after eighteen months from that date, and it is only on this second motion, after hearing the parties and making such inquiry as the court thinks fit, that the court passes a decree declaring the marriage dissolved. The purpose courts have consistently given for the six-month gap is to provide a cooling-off period during which an estranged couple might reconsider the decision, and the eighteen-month outer limit exists so that consent given at the first motion does not remain open-ended; a couple who let the eighteen months lapse without filing the second motion is generally taken to have let the joint petition lapse as well, on the reasoning that failing to move the matter forward within that window is itself evidence that one or both parties no longer want the divorce to proceed on that basis.

Either spouse retains the right to withdraw consent at any time up to the point the decree is actually passed, a position the Supreme Court settled well before the more recent waiver litigation and that has not been displaced by any of the cases discussed below. This means the second motion hearing is not a formality even where both spouses attended the first motion and nothing has visibly changed in the interim; the court is required to satisfy itself afresh, at the second motion, that consent continues, and a spouse who appears at the second motion and states that they no longer consent can defeat the petition even after the waiting period has fully run and even where the other spouse has relied on the process concluding.

Amardeep Singh and the case for waiving the six-month gap

The Supreme Court's decision in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, reported at (2017) 8 SCC 746, is the starting point for any argument that the six-month gap under Section 13B(2) can be shortened. The Court held that the period is directory and not mandatory, reasoning that where the object of the provision, giving the parties a real opportunity to reconsider, has already been substantially served by the time the parties approach the court, insisting on a further wait serves no purpose and only prolongs the parties' distress. The judgment set out a set of factors a court should weigh before waiving the period, including how long the parties have already been separated, whether attempts at mediation or reconciliation have genuinely failed, whether they have settled all disputes including alimony, custody and property, and whether the waiting period is likely to simply prolong their agony without any realistic prospect of reconciliation.

What the Court did not do, and what later confusion has centred on, is turn those factors into a rigid checklist that a petitioner must satisfy item by item before any waiver is granted. The Supreme Court addressed that confusion directly in Amit Kumar v. Suman Beniwal, 2021 SCC OnLine SC 1270, where it held that a family court and a High Court had misread Amardeep Singh by treating its illustrative factors as mandatory conditions precedent, clarifying that the factors remain guidance for the exercise of a discretion, not a fresh set of statutory prerequisites layered on top of Section 13B(2) itself. A petitioner asking a Delhi or Noida family court to waive the six-month gap should accordingly present the Amardeep Singh factors as supporting material for the court's discretion, with evidence of the length of separation and the completeness of the settlement reached, rather than treating the application as a formal compliance exercise that stands or falls on each listed factor being independently proved.

Where Delhi's own courts have pulled in different directions

Delhi family courts have not applied the waiver power uniformly even after Amardeep Singh and Amit Kumar. One widely reported Delhi High Court ruling directed a family court to waive the six-month period where the parties had already lived apart for a substantial time, treating that fact as sufficient on its own to justify dispensing with a further wait. Other Delhi orders have taken a narrower view, holding that the six-month period cannot be waived by the trial court or the High Court in the ordinary exercise of its jurisdiction, reserving waiver for genuinely exceptional circumstances rather than treating it as routinely available once a settlement is in place.

The Additional Principal Judge, Family Court, Dwarka, had in Shiksha Kumari v. Santosh Kumar (HMA No. 525/2025) refused to waive the six-month gap because the one-year separation period itself had not yet run, and that 10 March 2025 order was challenged in MAT.APP.(F.C.) 111/2025. A Delhi High Court Full Bench decided that appeal on 17 December 2025, holding that the one-year separation precondition under Section 13B(1) is itself directory and waivable under the proviso to Section 14(1), and that waiving the one-year period does not automatically mean the six-month gap under Section 13B(2) is also waived, since the two remain distinct exercises of discretion; this research did not find that the appeal addressed the eighteen-month outer limit. A couple filing in Delhi today can rely on this Full Bench authority to seek waiver of the one-year precondition in a genuine case, but should still treat waiver, of either period, as a discretionary request to be argued on the specific facts, supported by as complete a settlement record as possible, rather than as an entitlement that follows automatically from having already agreed on every contested issue between them, and the Full Bench itself warned against routine use of the Section 14(1) proviso.

A couple filing at the family court in Gautam Buddh Nagar, which covers Noida and Greater Noida, is bound by the same Supreme Court authority but not by Delhi High Court rulings as binding precedent, since the Allahabad High Court exercises appellate and supervisory jurisdiction over that court. The Allahabad High Court has applied Amardeep Singh in its own waiver orders, but a Noida petitioner should not assume that a position taken by a Delhi bench on a disputed question, such as whether the one-year separation precondition itself can be waived, will be mirrored by the Allahabad High Court, and should argue the application on the Supreme Court authority directly rather than relying on Delhi precedent as though it bound a Noida court.

What a waiver application actually needs to show in practice

A well-prepared waiver application filed alongside, or shortly after, the first motion typically documents the length of actual separation with something more concrete than a bare assertion, such as separate residential addresses on record, bank statements or utility bills showing independent households, or earlier correspondence between the parties or their counsel referring to the separation. It also sets out, in reasonable detail, the terms the parties have reached on maintenance, whether as a lump sum or periodic payment, on custody and visitation where children are involved, and on the division of any jointly owned property or joint accounts, since the completeness of the settlement is one of the clearest indicators courts look to under the Amardeep Singh factors.

Where mediation has already been attempted, whether through the court's own mediation centre or privately, a report or record of that attempt strengthens the application considerably, because it answers the factor asking whether reconciliation efforts have genuinely failed rather than simply been assumed to have failed. Counsel should also anticipate that the court may put each spouse through a short individual examination, separate from their joint statement, precisely to test whether the consent recorded on paper reflects an actual, continuing agreement rather than one party's unilateral pressure on the other, and a waiver application is far more likely to succeed where both spouses are able to answer that examination consistently and without apparent hesitation.

The Family Courts Act framework governing Delhi and Gautam Buddh Nagar

Both the Delhi family courts and the family court at Gautam Buddh Nagar operate under the Family Courts Act, 1984, which establishes family courts with jurisdiction, under Section 7, over matrimonial and related proceedings to the exclusion of the ordinary district courts and civil courts in the area for which they are constituted. This exclusivity matters practically because it means a divorce petition, whether contested or by mutual consent, cannot be filed before an ordinary civil court in an area where a family court has been established; filing in the wrong forum simply invites a return of the plaint for presentation before the correct court.

Section 19 of the Family Courts Act, not Section 28 of the Hindu Marriage Act standing alone, governs the right of appeal from a family court's orders, including orders passed in mutual consent proceedings. An appeal under Section 19 lies to a bench of two or more judges of the High Court having jurisdiction, and Section 19(3) prescribes a thirty-day limitation period for filing it. Two restrictions matter particularly for mutual consent cases. First, Section 19 expressly bars an appeal against a decree or order passed with the consent of the parties, which forecloses the most obvious route by which a party who has second thoughts after the decree is passed might otherwise try to unwind it through an ordinary appeal. Second, interlocutory orders passed during the pendency of a petition, such as an order declining to waive the statutory period, are generally treated as falling outside Section 19's appellate scope, leaving a party aggrieved by such an order to approach the High Court under its supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227 of the Constitution rather than by a statutory appeal, a route that carries a different, more limited standard of review.

Territorial jurisdiction: deciding whether to file in Delhi or in Noida

A separate Section 19, this one in the Hindu Marriage Act itself, fixes which district court, and by extension which family court, has territorial jurisdiction over a petition, and the provision matters more in Delhi NCR than in most parts of the country because a couple's marriage, their last shared residence and their current separate residences can easily sit across the Delhi and Uttar Pradesh borders at the same time. The section allows a petition to be presented where the marriage was solemnised, where the respondent resides at the time the petition is presented, or where the parties last resided together, giving a couple married in Delhi but now separated into Delhi and Noida households more than one lawful venue to choose between.

The Marriage Laws (Amendment) Act, 2003 added a further clause, now clause (iii-a) of Section 19, letting a wife file where she herself resides at the time of presenting the petition, a change introduced specifically to address cases where the husband had moved outside India and the wife would otherwise have struggled to establish jurisdiction anywhere she could practically litigate. Before that amendment, courts had dismissed petitions for want of jurisdiction where neither the marriage nor the parties' last shared residence connected the case to the forum chosen, and the amendment was a direct legislative response to that gap; a parallel amendment to Section 31 of the Special Marriage Act, 1954 extended the same protection to petitions filed under that Act.

In a mutual consent petition specifically, where both spouses are cooperating rather than contesting venue, jurisdiction rarely becomes a live dispute, but counsel should still confirm the chosen court's jurisdiction is defensible on the facts pleaded, particularly where one spouse has already relocated abroad or to another city, since an improperly filed petition can be returned for presentation before the correct court even where both parties consent to the divorce itself, adding delay that a straightforward jurisdiction check at the drafting stage would have avoided.

Alimony, custody terms and what the decree actually enforces

A mutual consent decree is not limited to dissolving the marriage; Section 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act empowers the court to order permanent alimony and maintenance at the time of passing the decree or at any point afterward, on an application by either party, with the amount assessed against the respondent's income and property and the applicant's own means and conduct, and Section 25(2) lets either party apply later to vary that order where circumstances have materially changed. Section 26 separately empowers the court to pass orders for the custody, maintenance and education of minor children, and courts have read this power broadly enough to cover a child's maintenance even in proceedings where Section 24's narrower pendente lite provision would not independently extend to the child.

For a mutual consent petition, the practical significance of Sections 25 and 26 is that the settlement terms the couple negotiates outside court do not become automatically binding or automatically enforceable simply because both spouses have signed a private memorandum of understanding; they take on the force of a court order, and become enforceable as such, only once they are actually incorporated into the decree itself, whether by the court recording the agreed terms verbatim or by reference to a settlement document filed with the petition. A couple who settle maintenance and custody privately but fail to have those terms specifically reflected in the decree risk discovering later that the decree, standing alone, dissolves the marriage but says nothing enforceable about the very terms they thought they had agreed, leaving the party owed maintenance or access to a child without a court order to enforce if the other side later reneges.

Once alimony or custody terms are properly recorded in the decree, they are enforceable in the same way as any other civil court order, through the ordinary execution machinery available for civil decrees, and a party who deliberately disobeys a court-recorded maintenance or custody direction also exposes themselves to contempt proceedings, a route Indian courts have used against a defaulting spouse separately from, and in addition to, ordinary execution. Counsel drafting the joint petition should therefore treat the decree's own language, not the private settlement document referenced in passing, as the operative text that will actually be enforced if a dispute arises later.

When one spouse lives abroad: appearance by video conferencing and power of attorney

Delhi NCR sees a steady stream of mutual consent petitions where one spouse, often having emigrated for work or marriage, is no longer resident in India at the time the petition is filed, and the question of whether that spouse must return for every hearing has produced a genuinely unsettled body of High Court authority rather than one settled national rule. The Supreme Court's decision in Santhini v. Union of India, (2018) 1 SCC 1, established that video conferencing can be used in family court proceedings generally where both parties consent to it, and the Supreme Court has itself, in at least one later matter, exercised its extraordinary jurisdiction under Article 142 of the Constitution to grant a mutual consent divorce through video conferencing where the facts warranted that course.

High Courts have since taken visibly different positions when asked to extend this to the conciliation or reconciliation stage that often precedes recording of the first motion. The Gujarat High Court, in a 2026 ruling, set aside a family court's refusal to let an NRI husband participate in conciliation by video conference, holding that compelling a party abroad to travel for conciliation alone was unreasonable where the substance of the proceeding did not require physical presence. The Madras High Court has gone further, holding that physical presence is not mandatory at all for a mutual consent divorce where a party resides abroad, and that the requirement in some video-conferencing protocols for a coordinator to be physically present at the remote end is primarily meant for criminal proceedings, not matrimonial ones, where identity can instead be verified through powers of attorney and supporting documents. The Andhra Pradesh High Court, by contrast, has taken a markedly more restrictive position, holding that video conferencing cannot be permitted at the reconciliation stage even where a spouse lives abroad, and that personal inconvenience, employment commitments, visa difficulties or foreign residence do not by themselves justify excusing physical appearance at that stage.

Because Delhi and Gautam Buddh Nagar fall under the Delhi High Court and the Allahabad High Court respectively, neither of which has yet taken as firm a position either way as Gujarat, Madras or Andhra Pradesh, a couple with one spouse abroad should not assume video appearance will simply be granted on request. The safer course is to file a specific, reasoned application early, ideally alongside the petition itself, setting out why physical appearance is impractical, confirming both parties' consent to appearing by video where the court permits it, and being prepared to support the application with a power of attorney and identity documents of the kind the Madras High Court has treated as adequate substitutes for in-person verification, rather than assuming the point will be conceded informally once the matter is before the court.

A power of attorney has real but limited use in this context. It can let an authorised representative file documents, receive notices, and in some courts make formal appearances on procedural dates, but the recording of a party's personal statement confirming consent at the first and second motions is generally treated as something the party must do personally, whether in person or by permitted video link, precisely because the entire mutual consent procedure exists to verify that consent is genuine and continuing; a power of attorney holder cannot supply that confirmation on the principal's behalf merely by producing a signed document.

Why withdrawal of consent remains a live risk even late in the process

Because either spouse can withdraw consent at any point before the decree is passed, and because a family court is required to satisfy itself of continuing consent at the second motion rather than simply ratifying what was agreed at the first, practitioners drafting mutual consent settlements should build in protections that do not depend on the divorce decree being passed on a particular timeline. A settlement that makes maintenance or property transfer conditional on the decree being granted by a specific date can leave a spouse without recourse if the other party withdraws consent close to, but before, that date, having already acted on the expectation that the decree was a formality.

A more defensible structure ties interim arrangements, temporary maintenance, continued residence in a shared property, access to children, to a separate, enforceable agreement or court order that stands independently of whether the Section 13B petition results in a decree at all, so that a withdrawal of consent by one spouse does not leave the other without any interim protection at all. Where a settlement involves transfer of immovable property, recording that transfer through a registered instrument once the terms are finalised, rather than treating it as a condition that will automatically take effect on the decree, avoids disputes later about whether an unregistered or informal arrangement survives a withdrawal of consent before the decree is passed.

Sequencing a mutual consent petition realistically in Delhi NCR

A couple approaching a Delhi or Noida family court for mutual consent divorce should plan on the statutory minimum of six months between the first and second motions unless they have a genuinely strong case for waiver supported by documented separation and a complete, written settlement, since an application for waiver that is thin on supporting material is likely to be refused and will simply add a further hearing to the timeline rather than shortening it. Where the one-year separation precondition under Section 13B(1) has not yet run, a couple can seek waiver under the proviso to Section 14(1) following the Delhi High Court Full Bench's ruling in Shiksha Kumari v. Santosh Kumar, MAT.APP.(F.C.) 111/2025 (17 December 2025), but should not treat it as a routine entitlement given the Full Bench's own caution against wanton use of that proviso, and should note the Madhya Pradesh High Court's contrary, mandatory reading means the position is not uniform outside Delhi.

Mediation referral, where a Delhi family court routes the matter to its attached mediation centre before recording the first motion, typically adds a few weeks rather than months to the front end of the process, and couples who arrive with a fully drafted settlement already in hand generally move through that stage faster than those still negotiating terms. Because the second motion requires fresh judicial satisfaction of continuing consent rather than a mechanical confirmation, counsel for both sides should prepare their clients for that hearing with the same seriousness as the first, rather than treating it as a formality once the waiting period has elapsed.

Inter-faith and civil marriages: the parallel route under the Special Marriage Act

A couple married under the Special Marriage Act, 1954, whether because one spouse is not Hindu or because they chose a civil ceremony regardless of religion, does not proceed under Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act at all. Mutual consent divorce for such couples is governed instead by Section 28 of the Special Marriage Act, which is structured almost identically: Section 28(1) requires the parties to have lived separately for one year or more and to be unable to live together, and Section 28(2) requires the second motion to be moved jointly, not earlier than six months and not later than eighteen months after the petition was first presented, with the decree following only on that second motion.

Courts have treated Section 28 as pari materia with Section 13B, applying the same Amardeep Singh reasoning to find the six-month gap under Section 28(2) directory rather than mandatory. An Orissa High Court decision and a line of Delhi High Court orders have both waived the Section 28(2) period where the parties satisfied the court, generally by affidavit, that reconciliation was no longer possible and that all ancillary issues had been settled. What the Delhi High Court has been careful to clarify is that the one-year separation period under Section 28(1) and the six-month gap under Section 28(2) are treated as distinct requirements, so a court waiving the latter does not automatically treat the former as waived as well, which mirrors the more cautious approach Delhi courts have taken toward the equivalent one-year requirement under Section 13B(1) of the Hindu Marriage Act.

For a Delhi or Noida couple married under the Special Marriage Act, often including a mixed-faith marriage or a marriage where one spouse holds foreign nationality or overseas citizenship, the practical procedure before the family court, joint petition, mediation referral, first motion, waiting period, second motion, is largely the same as under the Hindu Marriage Act, and the same documentary preparation, proof of separation, a written settlement covering maintenance and any children, and a record of failed reconciliation attempts, supports a waiver application under Section 28(2) in the same way it supports one under Section 13B(2).

Representation, documents and the practical mechanics of filing

Unlike proceedings before certain statutory tribunals where lawyers are kept out by design, parties before a family court are free to engage counsel, and in practice almost every mutual consent petition in Delhi and Noida is drafted and filed through an advocate, given how much turns on how the joint statement records the facts of separation and the terms of settlement. The joint petition is typically supported by affidavits from both spouses, a copy of the marriage certificate or proof of marriage where no formal certificate was issued at the time, identity and address proof for both parties, and, where children are involved, their birth certificates and a clear statement of the custody and visitation arrangement agreed between the parents.

Where the settlement involves a lump sum payment in lieu of ongoing maintenance, courts increasingly expect that amount to be either already paid and acknowledged, or secured through a mechanism such as a post-dated cheque, a bank guarantee, or deposit with the court, rather than left as an unsecured promise to be honoured after the decree is passed, since a decree of divorce does not itself enforce a separate maintenance promise unless that promise is incorporated into the decree or recorded in a separately enforceable instrument. Counsel drafting the settlement terms should accordingly treat the mode and timing of payment as being just as important as the amount itself, particularly where one spouse is relocating outside Delhi NCR, or outside India altogether, after the decree is granted, since enforcing an unsecured promise against a party who has since moved abroad is considerably harder than enforcing a decree term or a secured instrument executed before the decree.

Court fees for a mutual consent petition in Delhi and at Gautam Buddh Nagar are nominal compared to a contested matrimonial suit, since no separate valuation of relief is involved in the way it would be for a suit seeking possession or recovery of property, but parties should still budget for the incidental costs of the process, counsel's fees for both motions, any mediation centre charges, and the cost of obtaining certified copies of the decree once passed, which most banks, passport authorities and foreign consulates require as proof of the marriage's dissolution for a range of subsequent purposes, from remarriage to visa applications to updating bank and property records.

Section 13B gives a consenting couple a clear statutory sequence, one year of prior separation, a first motion, a gap of six to eighteen months, and a second motion before a decree follows, and Delhi's own courts have now gone further than most in opening up that sequence to waiver: a Full Bench of the Delhi High Court held on 17 December 2025, in Shiksha Kumari v. Santosh Kumar, MAT.APP.(F.C.) 111/2025, that even the one-year precondition under Section 13B(1) can be waived under the proviso to Section 14(1). A couple and their counsel should prepare for the process as it currently stands in their specific court, Delhi or Gautam Buddh Nagar, rather than assume a waiver will be granted as a matter of course simply because every substantive issue between them has already been resolved.

The two safeguards that matter most in practice are a settlement structured to survive a late withdrawal of consent, since either spouse retains that right up to the decree, and a clear understanding that an appeal under Section 19 of the Family Courts Act will not be available against a consent decree once it is passed. Getting both the substance of the settlement and the procedural sequencing right at the outset avoids the more common failure mode in these cases, a couple who assumed the paperwork would simply be rubber-stamped once consent was recorded, only to find the court unwilling to waive the statutory wait or one party no longer willing to proceed by the time the second motion comes up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the six-month waiting period for mutual consent divorce be waived in Delhi?

It can be, but Delhi courts have not applied the waiver power consistently. The Supreme Court in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, (2017) 8 SCC 746, held the six-month period under Section 13B(2) directory rather than mandatory, and some Delhi orders have waived it where the parties were already separated for a long time and had settled all issues. Other Delhi orders have declined to waive it except in exceptional circumstances, and a Delhi High Court Full Bench, in Shiksha Kumari v. Santosh Kumar, MAT.APP.(F.C.) 111/2025 (17 December 2025), has since gone further, holding that even the one-year separation precondition can be waived under the proviso to Section 14(1), while cautioning against routine use of that power; the outcome still depends on the specific facts and the bench hearing the application.

What is the minimum separation required before filing for mutual consent divorce?

Section 13B(1) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 requires the couple to have lived separately for at least one year immediately before filing the joint petition. This is generally treated as a precondition to filing, though a Delhi High Court Full Bench, in Shiksha Kumari v. Santosh Kumar, MAT.APP.(F.C.) 111/2025 (17 December 2025), held it directory rather than mandatory and waivable under the proviso to Section 14(1), so within Delhi the position now favours waiver in a genuine case, even though the Madhya Pradesh High Court takes a contrary, mandatory view.

Can one spouse withdraw consent after the first motion in a mutual consent divorce?

Yes. Either spouse can withdraw consent at any point before the decree is actually passed by the court, including after the first motion and even shortly before the second motion is due to be heard. The family court is required to satisfy itself of continuing consent at the second motion, and a withdrawal at that stage can defeat the petition even though the waiting period has fully run.

Which court handles mutual consent divorce for a couple living in Noida?

A couple residing in Noida or Greater Noida generally files before the family court at Gautam Buddh Nagar, which is governed by the same Family Courts Act, 1984 and the same Hindu Marriage Act provisions as Delhi's family courts, but falls under the appellate and supervisory jurisdiction of the Allahabad High Court rather than the Delhi High Court.

Can a consent divorce decree be appealed?

Section 19 of the Family Courts Act, 1984 expressly bars an appeal against a decree or order passed with the consent of both parties. A party dissatisfied after the decree is passed generally cannot use a statutory appeal to challenge it, though separate remedies may exist where the consent itself is alleged to have been obtained by fraud or coercion.

How long does mutual consent divorce take in Delhi or Noida if the waiting period is not waived?

Where the couple has already completed the one-year separation required before filing, and no waiver of the six-month gap between motions is sought or granted, the process typically takes at least six to seven months from the first motion to the decree, allowing for scheduling of both motions and any mediation referral, though the actual timeline depends on the specific court's calendar and how quickly the second motion is listed.

What documents support a waiver application for the six-month period?

Courts generally look for documented evidence of the length of actual separation, such as separate addresses, utility bills or bank records showing independent households, together with a complete written settlement covering maintenance, custody where applicable, and division of property, and any record of mediation or reconciliation attempts that did not succeed. The more complete and verifiable this material, the stronger the basis for a court to exercise its discretion in favour of waiver.
