Agricultural Land for Investors in Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal: Limits, Conversion and Compensation

What a business, an NRI or an agribusiness investor can and cannot do with farmland on the Noida-Greater Noida corridor and in rural West Bengal, and what a landowner actually recovers when the State decides to take it instead.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 13 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Agricultural land looks like the simplest asset in India to buy, and often turns out to be the hardest to actually use. Land reform law treats it differently from any other asset class on three separate fronts at once: who may acquire it, what it may be turned into, and what happens the day the State decides it wants the plot for a road, a corridor or an industrial estate. Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal answer each question through their own, independently amended statutes, and a buyer who assumes one state's rule applies in the other is usually wrong. This piece sets out what is settled today on each front, flags what current commentary genuinely disagrees about, and works through the separate federal bar that sits on top of state law whenever an NRI or foreign-linked investor is involved.

Key points

  • Whether a non-agriculturist may buy farmland without prior permission is not a settled, uniform answer in either Uttar Pradesh or West Bengal; current commentary conflicts, so the position should be confirmed for the specific tehsil or block before a purchase, not assumed from a general guide.
  • Both states cap how much agricultural land a single tenure-holder or raiyat's family may hold, computed in standard or converted hectares rather than plain acreage, and the cap applies across a buyer's aggregate holdings in the state, not plot by plot.
  • Agricultural land must be formally diverted to non-agricultural use before it can lawfully carry an industrial or commercial structure: under Section 80 of the Uttar Pradesh Revenue Code, 2006 in UP, and under Sections 4A to 4D of the West Bengal Land Reforms Act, 1955 in West Bengal, each with its own authority and documentation.
  • An NRI, OCI or foreign national cannot purchase, or receive as a gift, agricultural land, plantation property or a farmhouse anywhere in India under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019; inheritance remains the one route in, and even inherited land can only be sold onward to a resident Indian.
  • Where the State itself acquires the land, the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 fixes the market value and then applies a rural or urban multiplier under the First Schedule plus a mandatory 100% solatium, a formula that still leaves room for the kind of compensation disputes that recur around Noida and Greater Noida.
  • A bargadar's recorded cultivation rights in West Bengal, and recorded tenancy entries in Uttar Pradesh, survive a change of ownership and can block or complicate both a conversion application and a later sale, so title due diligence on agricultural land has to go further back than an ordinary encumbrance search.

Agriculture as a state subject with industrial consequences

Land and agriculture sit in the State List under the Seventh Schedule to the Constitution, which is why Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal each run their own land reform statutes, amended on their own schedules, rather than a single central code governing both. A structure built around one state's rule, however carefully drafted, cannot simply be repeated in the other without checking whether the equivalent provision even exists there, let alone whether it says the same thing.

Three distinct legal questions arise whenever agricultural land enters an industrial, agribusiness or investment transaction. The first is eligibility: who is actually permitted to acquire the plot. The second is use: what the land may lawfully be turned into once it changes hands. The third is permanence: what happens if the government, rather than a private buyer, decides it wants the land for a public project. Each question sits under a different statute, administered by a different authority, and a correct answer to one does not settle the others.

This piece works through those three questions for Uttar Pradesh, with particular attention to the Noida, Greater Noida and Yamuna Expressway corridor where industrial expansion keeps running into agricultural plots, and for West Bengal, before turning to the separate and non-negotiable federal bar that applies whenever an NRI, an Overseas Citizen of India or a foreign national is part of the ownership structure.

None of this is academic for the kind of transaction it actually affects. A manufacturer looking to expand a unit on the Yamuna Expressway, a logistics operator assembling a warehouse plot on Kolkata's eastern periphery, and an NRI family trying to work out what it can do with inherited farmland near Greater Noida are all, in effect, asking variations of the same three questions, and the answer for each of them turns out to depend on which authority they ask and when they ask it, which is precisely why a standard checklist is worth having before the first payment changes hands.

Who may buy: a question that is genuinely unsettled in current commentary

A good deal of publicly available guidance states flatly that a person who is not already an agriculturist cannot buy agricultural land in Uttar Pradesh without the Collector's prior permission. Other, more recent commentary takes the opposite position, saying that bar was relaxed by a 2014 amendment subject to a family landholding cap, and cites the same underlying law for a different conclusion. The same split exists for West Bengal: one line of guidance says only a certified agriculturist, defined by reference to a raiyat or bargadar who cultivates personally or through family labour, can buy without District Magistrate permission, while a more recent comparison places West Bengal among the states that impose no such agriculturist-only condition on the buyer, subject only to the land-reform ceiling.

Because the sources available to us do not agree, and each state's law on this point has moved more than once, we are not prepared to state a single rule for either state in this piece. What we would tell a client is this: confirm the current, applicable position for the specific tehsil in Uttar Pradesh or block in West Bengal before signing anything, using a fresh search of the live revenue record, the khatauni and khasra in UP or the record of rights through the Banglarbhumi portal in West Bengal, and, if there is any doubt at all, apply for the relevant permission before completing the purchase rather than relying on an assurance from the seller or a broker.

One point is settled on both sides of this uncertainty: a body corporate, trust or society buying agricultural land for a stated non-agricultural purpose has historically needed prior government sanction before the purchase, separately from any question about the buyer's agriculturist status, and that sanction requirement has been the subject of recent High Court rulings insisting on it as a precondition rather than a formality. A buyer that is itself a company should assume a sanction step is required and build the time for it into the transaction schedule.

The practical reason this uncertainty matters is that a defective purchase is not simply voidable at the buyer's option later; revenue authorities have, in reported cases, treated an unauthorised purchase by a non-agriculturist as void from the outset, which exposes a buyer who skipped the permission step to losing the benefit of the purchase price paid, not merely to a delay while the paperwork is sorted out retrospectively.

The ceiling that survives the sale

Uttar Pradesh has capped how much agricultural land a tenure-holder may hold since the Imposition of Ceiling on Land Holdings Act, 1960, and the ceiling has been reduced more than once since, most significantly by the 1973 amendment. Current secondary commentary puts the general ceiling for an ordinary tenure-holder's household at around 7.30 standard hectares of irrigated land, with the Act's own definitions converting unirrigated, grove and usar land to irrigated-land equivalents at set ratios, and separate additions permitted for adult sons and other family members within an overall cap. Because our own research turned up differing figures from different commentary on the history of this number, we would ask any client relying on a specific hectare figure to have it confirmed against the consolidated Act text or the local revenue office before it is used in a transaction document.

West Bengal's equivalent ceiling sits in Section 14M of the Land Reforms Act, 1955, inserted by a 1981 amendment with retrospective effect from 7 August 1969, which caps an ordinary raiyat's family holding, as reproduced in the texts available to us, at 7.00 standard hectares, scaling upward for larger families but not beyond that aggregate figure. A standard hectare is itself a defined term, generally one hectare of irrigated land or around one and four-tenths hectares of other land, so the acreage a given family may actually hold depends on how the land is classified, not on a flat figure. Land held in excess of the ceiling vests in the State, and a separate 2023 amendment to the Act has since allowed ceiling-surplus land held for certain specified purposes, including what the amendment itself describes as a township, to be transferred on a freehold basis in defined circumstances.

The ceiling matters to an industrial buyer even though the end use has nothing to do with farming, because an entity assembling several contiguous agricultural plots for a factory or warehouse site can run into the tenure-holder or raiyat ceiling well before it reaches the acreage the project needs, and the authorities can treat a chain of purchases routed through related individuals or group entities as a single acquisition for ceiling purposes. For an assembly of any real size, acquisition through the relevant state industrial development authority's own land bank, or a government-mediated land-pooling scheme, is usually a safer route than stitching the site together through private conveyances one plot at a time.

There is also a separate category of land that a ceiling calculation can end up excluding or treating specially: exemptions exist in both states for institutions such as agricultural research bodies, educational institutions and, under the Uttar Pradesh law, certain categories of trust and cooperative, each defined and capped on its own terms rather than by the general household figure. A buyer that believes it falls within one of these exemptions should treat that as a claim to be established with the revenue authority before the purchase, with supporting documentation, rather than as a default position it can assert only if the ceiling is later questioned.

Tenancy and bargadar rights that travel with the land

West Bengal's land reform law protects a bargadar, a recorded sharecropper, in a way that survives a change of ownership in the land itself. A bargadar's right to a defined share of the produce and to continued possession is not extinguished by a sale, and reported guidance on the conversion process notes that a plot under recorded bargadar cultivation generally cannot be approved for non-agricultural use except in specified circumstances. A bargadar, like certain other co-sharers under the Act, can also hold a pre-emptive right to purchase land offered for sale, which can defeat an outside buyer's completed transaction if that right was not cleared or waived before the sale closed.

Uttar Pradesh's revenue law creates its own version of this problem through recorded tenancy and sub-tenancy entries, and separately through the practical reality that long, uninterrupted cultivation by someone other than the recorded owner tends to generate disputes about bhumidhar status even where no formal tenancy was ever registered. A buyer who checks only the ownership column of the khatauni, without checking for tenancy or cultivation entries, is checking half the record.

Due diligence on an agricultural parcel earmarked for industrial use therefore has to reach further back and wider than an ordinary title search would for urban property. These rights are created and protected by the land reform statute itself, not by private contract, so a purchaser cannot simply extinguish them by taking a clean-looking sale deed from the recorded owner. Clearing them, whether by negotiated settlement, surrender or a formal finding that no such right attaches to the plot, has to happen before the land is diverted or built upon, not after a dispute surfaces at the construction stage.

Converting the land in Uttar Pradesh: Section 80 and what replaced the old Section 143

Section 143 of the former Uttar Pradesh Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act, 1950 used to govern the declaration a landholder had to make before putting agricultural land to a non-agricultural use. That 1950 Act, along with more than thirty other enactments, was repealed by the Uttar Pradesh Revenue Code, 2006, and the equivalent declaration requirement now sits in Section 80 of the Revenue Code: where a bhumidhar wants to use the holding for an industrial, commercial, residential or other non-agricultural purpose, a declaration to that effect has to be made and sanctioned before the use changes.

In practice, this means an application to the Sub-Divisional Magistrate supported by the current khatauni and khasra extracts, identity proof and a statement of the intended use, followed by an inspection and an order from the SDM recording the land as diverted, which is then entered in the khatauni, commonly described in practice as showing the land as gair-krishi, meaning non-agricultural. The State Cabinet approved an ordinance in 2026 intended to streamline this process by combining conversion with building-plan approval in certain development areas; whether that combined process has actually been notified and brought into force for a particular district is something to confirm before relying on it.

For an industrial buyer or lender, the consequence is straightforward but frequently overlooked: executing a sale deed for agricultural land does not, by itself, make the land fit for a factory or warehouse. The diversion order has to be obtained and recorded in the khatauni before construction begins, and a lender financing the project will typically want that order in hand before treating the plot as clean security, because an undiverted plot carries its own separate risk of objection at the mutation stage, independent of anything wrong with the sale deed itself.

Converting the land in West Bengal: Sections 4A to 4D and the Banglarbhumi process

West Bengal regulates the change of land use under Sections 4A to 4D of the Land Reforms Act, 1955, administered through a tiered structure set out in Rule 5A of the West Bengal Land Reforms Rules, 1965. Reported guidance places conversion of smaller plots, up to a tenth of an acre, with the Block Land and Land Reforms Officer, and conversion of plots between a tenth of an acre and one acre with the Sub-Divisional Land and Land Reforms Officer, with applications increasingly routed through the Banglarbhumi portal rather than filed only on paper.

As with a sale, conversion in West Bengal runs into the bargadar question discussed above: a recorded bargadar interest is itself an obstacle to approval unless it has been cleared first. Beyond that, the fee payable and the exact set of supporting documents, typically an updated record of rights, a mutation certificate and a site plan, are set by departmental circular rather than by the Act itself, and both have changed over time, so they are worth confirming with the local BL&LRO office rather than assumed from an earlier transaction.

Around Kolkata's peripheral industrial belt, the land-reforms conversion order is only one of two approvals an agribusiness or logistics investor typically needs, the other being a separate land-use or planning clearance from the Kolkata Metropolitan Development Authority or the relevant local planning body. The two processes do not run on the same timeline, and treating the land-reforms conversion order as the only clearance required is one of the more common planning mistakes we see in this kind of transaction.

Plots that exceed the one-acre threshold under Rule 5A, which is common for an industrial or logistics site rather than a homestead, move to a higher authority within the Land and Land Reforms department, and the time such an application takes tends to lengthen accordingly. Investors planning a project timeline around a notified conversion period should treat that period as the floor, not the ceiling, of how long the step will actually take, and build in a buffer for site inspection, any bargadar clearance that turns out to be needed, and queries raised by the sanctioning authority before an order is actually issued.

The federal wall: why an NRI or foreign investor cannot simply buy the land

Whatever the state-law position on who may buy agricultural land, a separate and non-negotiable federal bar sits on top of it for an NRI, an Overseas Citizen of India or any other foreign national. Under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, read with the Reserve Bank's Master Direction on acquisition of immovable property, an NRI or OCI may acquire any immovable property in India by purchase other than agricultural land, plantation property or a farmhouse. The same exclusion applies to acquisition by way of gift, whether the gift comes from a resident or from another NRI or OCI relative.

Inheritance remains the one route in. An NRI or OCI may acquire agricultural land, plantation property or a farmhouse by inheritance from a person resident in India, but the restriction does not simply disappear once the land is inherited: inherited agricultural land can only be sold onward to a person resident in India, not to another NRI or OCI, the heir cannot use the inheritance as a basis to operate a farming business in their own right, and sale proceeds of such inherited agricultural property can be repatriated out of India only with the Reserve Bank's prior approval.

A genuine foreign or NRI-backed investor who wants agricultural or agri-processing land in practice structures the holding through an Indian resident company or LLP, itself subject to the applicable entry route and sectoral conditions under the foreign direct investment framework, which then acquires the land as a resident entity, or leases the land from a resident owner or cooperative rather than attempting to hold farmland personally. This is also where the choice between an outright purchase and a long-term lease or a contract-farming arrangement, discussed in our companion piece on agricultural trade and mandi compliance, tends to make the most commercial sense for a foreign-backed structure.

Spouses need their own check. A spouse of an NRI or OCI who is personally a foreign national, and neither an NRI nor an OCI, can acquire one residential property jointly with the NRI or OCI spouse under conditions set out in the applicable rule, but agricultural land, plantation property and farmhouses are excluded from that joint-acquisition route as well, so a family cannot work around the farmland bar simply by putting the purchase in the non-NRI spouse's name.

When the State wants the land instead of you: the RFCTLARR framework

Where the government itself acquires agricultural land for a public purpose, whether a road, an industrial corridor or an authority's own development scheme, the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 now governs the process, having replaced the Land Acquisition Act, 1894. The Act runs through a preliminary notification, a social impact assessment for larger acquisitions, a declaration, and finally an award fixing compensation, and a landowner's practical leverage is almost entirely concentrated at the stage the award is being computed and at any later reference or appeal against it.

Section 26 of the 2013 Act sets the method for determining market value, generally the higher of the value indicated in nearby registered sale deeds, the average of the higher half of comparable registered transactions in the preceding three years, or a consented amount agreed for the acquisition. Section 26(2), read with the First Schedule, then applies a multiplication factor to that market value, 1.00 for urban areas and a factor between 1.00 and 2.00 for rural areas depending on distance from the urban area, as notified by the state government, and Section 30, again read with the First Schedule, adds a mandatory solatium equal to 100% of the resulting amount, on top of separate compensation for standing crops, trees and damage from severance. Before this Act, the 1894 law gave only a 30% solatium, so the change is substantial even before any further enhancement.

This formula has not stopped compensation disputes from recurring around Noida and Greater Noida, where the local development authorities have for years combined a cash component with a share of developed land rather than relying solely on the statutory multiplier, and disagreement over whether that package or an enhanced cash award is the better outcome has reached the Supreme Court more than once. Greater Noida's authority revised the rate it pays for acquired land again in 2026, reported in the press at a little above Rs 6,400 per square metre under one compensation option, though the figures reported for the earlier and revised rates vary depending on which compensation category is being compared, so a landowner negotiating with the authority should ask for the notification itself rather than relying on a reported figure.

Compensation under the 2013 Act is also not limited to the cash award. The statute's rehabilitation and resettlement provisions contemplate additional entitlements for an affected family beyond the market-value-plus-solatium calculation, including, depending on the scheme actually notified for the project, assistance for loss of livelihood where the family depended on the acquired land for its income. These entitlements are frequently the subject of separate negotiation or litigation from the market-value dispute itself, and a landowner's advisers should treat the two as distinct claims rather than assuming a generous cash award has automatically resolved the resettlement side of the file.

Structuring an acquisition without buying everything

Before signing a sale deed, an investor should weigh the alternatives against an outright purchase. A long-term lease from the recorded owner avoids both the ceiling and the non-agriculturist eligibility question, since no transfer of title occurs, though the lease itself may need registration beyond a certain term and has to be drafted to stay outside the protected-tenancy provisions discussed earlier, or it can create exactly the tenancy risk it was meant to avoid. A contract-farming or supply arrangement that never takes possession of the land at all, discussed in our companion piece on agricultural trade compliance, is a further step removed from any of the ownership-side problems covered in this piece. And acquisition through a state industrial authority's own land bank substitutes a lease from the Authority, governed by its own lease deed, for private-law title risk, which is in substance how NOIDA, GNIDA and YEIDA already allot plots to businesses rather than selling agricultural land outright.

Where an outright purchase does go ahead, the due diligence should include a land-record search reaching back further than the usual limitation period for a suit challenging a mutation, a specific search for bargadar or tenancy entries, written confirmation from the relevant revenue officer that the holding, aggregated with the buyer's other holdings in the state, does not exceed the applicable ceiling, and, wherever an NRI or foreign national sits anywhere in the ownership chain, a FEMA compliance opinion obtained before money changes hands rather than after.

None of this is unique to agricultural land as such, but the consequence of skipping it is more severe than for ordinary urban property, because a defect in agricultural title is just as likely to be enforced by the State itself, through ceiling-surplus vesting or a refused conversion, as by a private rival claimant, and a State-enforced defect is far harder to negotiate around once construction has already started.

A staged structure is often the most workable compromise for a project of any scale: an option or agreement to lease with a defined right to convert the arrangement into a purchase once the eligibility, ceiling and conversion questions have been cleared for the specific plot, rather than a single conveyance signed on the assumption that each of those questions will resolve favourably afterwards. This also gives the investor a natural point to walk away, at comparatively low cost, if the diligence turns up a bargadar claim, a ceiling conflict or a conversion refusal that cannot be resolved on acceptable terms.

Financing against the land: the SARFAESI gap lenders already know about

Lenders taking agricultural land as security for a project loan run into a limit that has nothing to do with title and everything to do with the land's revenue classification. Section 31 of the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 excludes agricultural land from the enforcement mechanism the Act otherwise gives a secured creditor, so a bank or financial institution cannot use SARFAESI's summary possession and sale procedure against land that is still classified as agricultural in the revenue record, however industrial the building standing on it may actually be.

This exclusion is well established and has generated its own body of litigation over what counts as agricultural land for the purpose of the exemption, including cases where a mortgaged plot's revenue entry had not been updated to reflect an actual change of use, or where a borrower argued the exemption to resist enforcement after the land had in substance already become part of an industrial unit. The practical effect for a lender financing a conversion project is that the diversion or conversion order discussed earlier in this piece is a condition for lawful construction, and it is frequently, separately, the difference between security a lender can enforce under SARFAESI and security it has to pursue, slower and more expensively, through an ordinary civil suit or a Debt Recovery Tribunal reference instead.

Lenders who understand this tend to build it into the facility structure directly: disbursement staged to completion of the diversion order, a covenant requiring the borrower to apply for diversion promptly after acquiring the land rather than after construction has started, and in some cases a requirement that the land be diverted before it is even accepted as primary security rather than only before the loan is disbursed in full. A borrower negotiating project finance against agricultural land should expect these conditions and plan the diversion timeline accordingly, rather than treating it as a formality to be dealt with after financial closure.

Dispute forums: the revenue side first, civil and writ jurisdiction after

Most disputes of the kind described in this piece begin in the revenue hierarchy rather than in a civil court. In Uttar Pradesh, the Tehsildar, Sub-Divisional Magistrate and Collector have jurisdiction over ceiling determination, conversion applications and mutation objections; in West Bengal, the equivalent authorities are the Block Land and Land Reforms Officer, the Sub-Divisional Land and Land Reforms Officer and the Collector, acting under the Land Reforms framework. A civil suit filed without first using the available revenue remedy risks being dismissed for want of jurisdiction, since the land reform statutes generally treat these as matters for the revenue authorities to decide in the first instance.

Appeals from revenue-authority orders generally run up through the Commissioner and, for some categories in West Bengal, to a Land Reforms Tribunal, before a party can approach the High Court. Compensation disputes under the RFCTLARR Act follow their own separate route entirely, through a reference to the authority constituted for that purpose at the district level and an onward appeal, rather than through the general revenue hierarchy used for ceiling or conversion disputes.

Writ jurisdiction under Article 226 becomes realistically available once the statutory remedies have been exhausted, or where the challenge goes to the authority's jurisdiction or process itself, such as bias, want of notice or acting beyond what the statute permits. Both the Allahabad High Court and the Calcutta High Court have in practice entertained writ petitions against revenue and acquisition action on that basis, but both have also declined to entertain a writ where a genuinely disputed question of fact, such as who actually cultivated a plot or whether a sale exceeded the ceiling once aggregated with other holdings, needs to be tried on evidence rather than decided on affidavits alone.

Limitation is worth flagging separately because it catches more agricultural-land disputes than almost any other single defence. A challenge to a mutation, a ceiling-surplus declaration or a conversion refusal usually has a short window set either by the specific statute or by the general law of limitation applicable to the proceeding, and that window tends to run from the date of the order rather than from the date the affected party actually learns of it, particularly where the order was published or recorded in the normal course rather than served personally. Anyone who discovers a problem with agricultural land they already hold, whether an adverse mutation entry, an old conversion order they did not apply for, or a ceiling declaration made years earlier, should get the limitation position checked immediately rather than after deciding on a strategy, since the strategy itself may no longer be available by the time it is chosen.

A due diligence checklist before any agreement is signed

Before an investor or business signs anything, five confirmations are worth getting in writing rather than taking on trust. First, the current, block or tehsil-specific position on whether the intended buyer may acquire the land without special permission, given how unsettled the general commentary on this point turned out to be. Second, a ceiling computation that aggregates the buyer's other holdings in the state, not just the plot in question. Third, a dedicated search for recorded bargadar, tenancy or sub-tenancy entries, separate from the ordinary ownership and encumbrance search. Fourth, written confirmation of the land's current revenue classification and the status of any conversion or diversion application, rather than an assumption that a later application will be a formality. Fifth, wherever an NRI, OCI or foreign national features anywhere in the buyer's or seller's structure, a FEMA compliance opinion addressing the agricultural land exclusion specifically.

Where the transaction instead involves land that is, or may become, the subject of a government acquisition, a sixth check matters just as much: whether a preliminary notification or social impact assessment has already been initiated for the area, since that can change both the commercial terms a private buyer should be willing to pay and the compensation route available to the current owner.

A seventh item belongs on the list wherever the plot is going to secure project finance rather than simply be bought outright: confirmation, obtained before the facility documents are finalised, of where the diversion application actually stands, since a lender relying on SARFAESI as its enforcement route will treat an undiverted plot very differently from a diverted one when pricing and structuring the facility, for the reasons set out earlier in this piece.

Agricultural land in Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal carries three separate sets of rules that a buyer, lender or investor has to clear, not one: a rule on who may acquire it that current commentary itself does not agree on, a ceiling on how much any one holder's family may accumulate, and a separate conversion procedure before the land can carry anything other than a crop. An NRI or foreign-backed structure adds a fourth rule that neither state can override. Treating any one of these as settled without a fresh, plot-specific check is the most common way these transactions go wrong.

The practical answer, in most cases we see, is not to force an outright purchase through every one of these hurdles but to ask first whether a lease, a land-bank allotment from the relevant development authority, or a contract-farming arrangement would get the business to the same commercial outcome with far less exposure to title, ceiling and conversion risk. Where a purchase genuinely is the right structure, the checklist in this piece is the minimum, not the full extent, of what should be confirmed before the sale deed is signed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a company buy agricultural land in Uttar Pradesh or West Bengal to build a factory on it?

Usually yes, but not directly and not without prior steps. Current guidance on whether a non-agriculturist buyer needs special permission conflicts across sources for both states, so this should be confirmed for the specific tehsil or block first. Separately, a company acquiring land for a non-agricultural purpose has historically needed prior government sanction for the purchase itself, and the land then has to be formally diverted to non-agricultural use, under Section 80 of the UP Revenue Code or Sections 4A to 4D of the West Bengal Land Reforms Act, before it can lawfully carry a factory.

Is there a limit on how much agricultural land one buyer can hold?

Yes, in both states, though expressed in standard or converted hectares rather than a flat acreage figure, and applied across a buyer's aggregate holdings in the state rather than plot by plot. Uttar Pradesh's ceiling sits under the Imposition of Ceiling on Land Holdings Act, 1960, and West Bengal's under Section 14M of the Land Reforms Act, 1955. The applicable figure for a given family size and land type should be confirmed from the current consolidated Act or the local revenue office rather than assumed from a general guide.

Can an NRI or OCI buy a farmhouse or agricultural land in India?

No, not by purchase or by gift. The Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019 exclude agricultural land, plantation property and farmhouses from what an NRI or OCI may acquire by purchase or gift anywhere in India. The one route in is inheritance from a person resident in India, and even inherited agricultural land can only be sold onward to a resident Indian, not to another NRI or OCI.

What happens if the plot has a bargadar or recorded tenant on it?

The bargadar's or tenant's rights generally survive a sale and have to be dealt with before conversion or construction, not after. In West Bengal, a plot under recorded bargadar cultivation is generally not approved for non-agricultural conversion unless that interest has been cleared, and a bargadar can also hold a pre-emptive right to purchase the land. In Uttar Pradesh, recorded tenancy or long, uninterrupted cultivation by someone other than the owner can generate its own disputes about bhumidhar status that a buyer needs to clear before relying on the title.

How is compensation calculated when the government acquires agricultural land for an industrial project?

Under the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, market value is determined under Section 26 by reference to comparable registered sale deeds or a consented amount, then multiplied by a factor of 1.00 for urban land and between 1.00 and 2.00 for rural land as notified by the state government, with a further 100% solatium added under Section 30, on top of compensation for crops, trees and severance. Authorities such as Greater Noida have also used developed-land or hybrid compensation packages alongside this statutory formula, which is where most of the recurring litigation in that area comes from.

Can agricultural land simply be leased for industrial use instead of bought?

Often, yes, and it can be the more straightforward route, since a lease does not transfer ownership and so avoids both the ceiling and the buyer-eligibility questions discussed in this piece. A lease still needs careful drafting to avoid inadvertently creating a protected tenancy interest and may need registration depending on its term, and the land will still need to be diverted to non-agricultural use before it can lawfully carry an industrial structure.

Does converting agricultural land to non-agricultural use happen automatically once it is sold for that purpose?

No. A sale deed transfers title; it does not change the land's revenue classification. In Uttar Pradesh, a separate declaration and order under Section 80 of the Revenue Code, 2006 is required, and in West Bengal a separate application under Sections 4A to 4D of the Land Reforms Act is required, each with its own authority, documentation and, in West Bengal, a precondition that any bargadar interest has first been cleared.
