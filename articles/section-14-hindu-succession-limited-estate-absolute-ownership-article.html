Section 14 of the Hindu Succession Act: When a Woman's Limited Estate Becomes Absolute

A gift deed or a will can try to give a Hindu woman only a life interest in property, but a 2026 Delhi High Court ruling shows how a pre-existing right can still turn that limited estate into full ownership

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 26 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Family settlements and gift deeds drafted decades ago often tried to give a daughter, a widow or a grandmother only a life interest in property, meaning to keep it within the male line after her. Section 14 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 can override exactly that intention, and a Delhi High Court ruling handed down on 30 January 2026 shows how far the provision still reaches today.

Key points

  • Section 14(1) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 converts property held by a Hindu woman, whether acquired before or after the Act, into her absolute property if she was 'possessed' of it when the Act commenced or acquires it afterwards, overriding any earlier document that tried to limit her to a life interest.
  • Section 14(2) is the carve-out: where property is acquired by a Hindu woman for the first time under a gift, will, instrument, decree, order or award that itself prescribes a restricted estate, that restriction survives and Section 14(1) does not enlarge it.
  • The dividing line the courts have drawn between the two sub-sections is whether the woman already had some pre-existing right, under Hindu law or otherwise, to be maintained out of or supported by the property before the restrictive document was executed; where she did, Section 14(1) prevails even though the document itself used life-interest language.
  • In Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh v. Simranjit Singh Grewal & Ors., the Delhi High Court held on 30 January 2026 that an obligation to maintain a woman under Hindu law can itself be the pre-existing right that lets Section 14(1) enlarge what a gift deed described as only a life estate.
  • The Court reached this conclusion at the threshold stage, dismissing an application under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure that sought to reject the partition suit outright on the strength of the gift deed's restrictive wording.
  • Whether a document creates a fresh, independent grant, which Section 14(2) can protect, or merely formalises an existing right the woman already had, is a question of substance that depends on the facts and the document's own recitals, not on whether the document uses the words 'life interest' or 'absolute owner'.
  • This distinction matters well beyond litigation over old family settlements. It directly affects whether a woman's limited estate can later be sold, mortgaged or bequeathed as full ownership, and whether a family settlement drafted today, intending to limit what a female family member receives, will actually achieve that limitation.

A provision written to undo a very old kind of limitation

Before the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, Hindu law in most of its schools recognised something called the Hindu woman's estate: a form of ownership under which a woman, typically a widow inheriting from her husband, held property for her lifetime with fairly wide powers of use and management, but without the power to pass it on by will, and with the property reverting on her death to whoever would have inherited from the last full male owner rather than to her own heirs. It was ownership in a practical sense and something considerably less than ownership in a legal sense.

Section 14 of the 1956 Act set out to end this particular form of limited holding, and it did so in sweeping terms. Section 14(1) provides that any property possessed by a female Hindu, whether acquired before or after the commencement of the Act, shall be held by her as full owner and not as a limited owner, and an Explanation to the sub-section makes clear that property includes both movable and immovable property acquired by a female Hindu by a wide range of means, including inheritance, partition, maintenance, gift, her own skill or exertion, purchase, prescription, or in any other manner, as well as property held by her as stridhan before the Act came into force.

If Section 14 had stopped there, every restriction on a woman's ownership created by any document at all would have been swept away the moment the Act commenced. The legislature did not intend quite that much, and Section 14(2) draws the provision back in for a specific category of case.

Section 14(2): the exception for a fresh, restricted grant

Section 14(2) provides that nothing in Section 14(1) applies to property acquired by way of gift, or under a will or any other instrument, or under a decree or order of a civil court, or under an award, where the terms of the gift, will or other instrument, decree, order or award prescribe a restricted estate in such property. In plain terms, if a document gives a woman property for the first time and expressly limits her to something less than full ownership, that limitation is not overridden by Section 14(1); it survives on its own terms.

Read together, the two sub-sections create a structure that looks, at first glance, straightforward: Section 14(1) is the rule, and Section 14(2) is the exception for a document that creates a restricted estate. In practice, the two sub-sections have generated a large body of litigation, because the dividing line between them is not simply whether a document uses restrictive words. The courts have consistently held that Section 14(2) applies only where the document in question confers a new title on the woman for the first time. If the woman already had some pre-existing right to the property, and the document in question merely recognises, formalises or gives effect to that pre-existing right while attempting to restrict it, the restriction does not survive, because the case then falls within Section 14(1) rather than Section 14(2).

This distinction between a document that creates a right and a document that merely recognises an existing one is easy to state and often difficult to apply, since the same words, a gift of a life interest, or a bequest for maintenance during the woman's lifetime, can describe either situation depending on what rights the woman held before the document was executed. The Supreme Court's early decisions on Section 14, and the large body of High Court authority that followed them, have mostly been occupied with working out exactly what counts as a pre-existing right sufficient to pull a case out of Section 14(2) and into Section 14(1).

What counts as a pre-existing right

The paradigm case the courts have recognised for decades is a Hindu widow's right to maintenance out of her deceased husband's estate. Even before the 1956 Act, Hindu law recognised that a widow had a right to be maintained from her husband's property, a right that existed independently of any document and that a family member holding the property was legally bound to respect. Where a family settlement, a partition deed, or an arrangement among the husband's heirs purported to give the widow a life interest in a defined portion of the property in satisfaction of that maintenance right, courts have held that the document was not creating a new right but was instead quantifying and giving a specific shape to a right the widow already possessed. On that reasoning, Section 14(1) enlarges the life interest into absolute ownership, and the restrictive language in the document does not survive under Section 14(2).

The same logic has been extended, in more recent decisions, to categories of women beyond widows, including daughters and other female relatives who can be shown to have had an independent, pre-existing right to maintenance or support under Hindu law before the document limiting their interest was executed. The critical question in every case is not the relationship itself but whether that specific relationship carried, under the applicable Hindu law at the relevant time, an enforceable right to maintenance or support from the property in question, separate from and prior to whatever the restrictive document says.

Where no such pre-existing right can be shown, and the document is instead the sole and original source of whatever interest the woman holds, Section 14(2) operates as the legislature plainly intended, and a woman who receives property for the first time under a will or gift that expressly limits her to a life interest holds exactly that: a life interest, not full ownership, and the property will pass on her death according to the terms the document itself lays down rather than according to her own personal law of succession.

Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh v. Simranjit Singh Grewal: the 2026 Delhi High Court ruling

The Delhi High Court applied this framework on 30 January 2026 in Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh v. Simranjit Singh Grewal & Ors., a decision on an application under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure seeking to reject a partition suit at the threshold, without a trial on the merits. The defendants' case for rejection rested on a gift deed that, on their reading, had given the plaintiff only a life estate in the property in question, with the result, they argued, that she had no right to seek partition of it as if she were its absolute owner.

The defendants invoked Section 14(2) directly, arguing that the gift deed was the instrument that created the plaintiff's interest in the first place, that it expressly restricted her to a life estate, and that Section 14(2) therefore protected that restriction from being overridden by Section 14(1). The Court's task at the Order VII Rule 11 stage was not to finally decide whether the plaintiff held an absolute or a limited interest, but to decide whether the plaint, taken on its own averments, disclosed no cause of action at all, which is the narrow test that provision applies.

The Court held that the plaint did disclose a cause of action and declined to reject it, reasoning that an obligation to maintain the person described in the gift deed could itself amount to a pre-existing right under Hindu law, which, if established at trial, would bring the case within Section 14(1) rather than Section 14(2), regardless of the gift deed's own restrictive language. The defendants' argument that Section 14(2) settled the matter conclusively was, in other words, treated as exactly the kind of disputed question of fact and law that a trial, not a threshold rejection application, exists to resolve.

Commentary on the decision has summarised its core holding as recognising that a pre-existing maintenance obligation can let a limited estate created by a gift deed enlarge into absolute ownership under Section 14(1), a formulation consistent with the older line of authority on widows' maintenance rights but applied here to the gift deed's own beneficiary. The judgment leaves the final factual determination, whether the maintenance obligation genuinely pre-existed the gift deed on the facts of this particular family, to be decided once the suit is tried on its merits.

Why an Order VII Rule 11 application was the wrong tool here

Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure allows a court to reject a plaint without going to trial, but only on narrow grounds, chiefly that the plaint does not disclose a cause of action, that the relief claimed is undervalued or insufficiently stamped and not corrected within the time allowed, or that the suit appears from the plaint's own statements to be barred by law. Courts have repeatedly held that this exercise must be confined to the averments in the plaint itself, read as a whole and assumed to be true for this limited purpose, without looking at the defendant's written statement or weighing contested evidence.

A Section 14 dispute of the kind raised in Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh is almost always unsuited to resolution at the Order VII Rule 11 stage, precisely because the question of whether a pre-existing right existed before a restrictive document was executed is a mixed question of fact and law that depends on evidence about the family's history and the relationships between the parties at the relevant time, not on the bare wording of the document alone. A defendant who wants to rely on Section 14(2) to defeat a claim to absolute ownership is making a substantive defence that belongs in the trial, not a jurisdictional or threshold objection that can dispose of the suit before evidence is led.

This procedural point has a practical consequence for anyone defending or bringing this kind of claim. A plaintiff whose case rests on a pre-existing maintenance right needs to plead, clearly and with supporting facts, what that pre-existing right was and when it arose, rather than simply asserting that Section 14(1) applies as a conclusion. A defendant relying on Section 14(2) should expect to have to prove, at trial, that the document was genuinely the first and only source of the woman's interest, rather than assuming the document's own wording will carry the point without contest.

Drafting around Section 14 today: what actually works and what does not

Families drafting a gift deed, a will or a family settlement today, who genuinely intend to give a female relative only a limited interest, need to understand that the words used in the document are necessary but not sufficient to achieve that result. A document that says 'for her lifetime only, remainder to her sons' will be read as creating a restricted estate under Section 14(2) only if the woman had no pre-existing right to the property independent of that document. If she already had, for instance, a right to be maintained from a joint family's property, or an existing share as a coparcener or co-owner that the document is merely restating in restrictive language, courts will look past the document's wording to the substance of what it actually did.

The practical lesson for drafting is to be explicit, in the document itself, about what it is doing: a settlement or gift that is genuinely creating a new interest for the first time, where the woman had no pre-existing claim at all, should say so and should avoid language that could later be read as merely formalising a right she already had. Where the family's actual intention is to satisfy an existing maintenance obligation through a defined grant, and the family wants that grant to remain only a life interest, the document should be drafted with the clear understanding that this intention may not survive judicial scrutiny under the Section 14(1) and Section 14(2) framework, however carefully the words are chosen, because the underlying legal character of a pre-existing right cannot be redefined away by the document that later gives it shape.

This is not a reason to avoid family settlements that provide for women's maintenance through defined property grants, which remain a legitimate and common way of organising family arrangements. It is a reason to go into such drafting with realistic expectations about what a restrictive clause can and cannot achieve, and, where the stakes are significant, to document contemporaneously whether a pre-existing right existed and what the settlement intends to do about it, since that contemporaneous record is often the only evidence available decades later when the question is litigated by the next generation.

What this means for a family's succession and estate planning

The practical stakes in a Section 14 dispute are rarely abstract. Where a woman's interest is held to be an absolute Section 14(1) estate rather than a restricted Section 14(2) grant, she can sell, mortgage, gift or bequeath the property by her own will, and on her death without a will it will pass according to the ordinary rules of intestate succession applicable to her, typically favouring her own children and, in their absence, other heirs under her personal law, rather than reverting to whoever the restrictive document named as the remainderman.

This has a direct bearing on estate planning within families where older gift deeds, wills or family settlements gave a grandmother, mother or aunt what the document calls a life interest. Before assuming that the property will pass as the old document intended once that woman dies, the family should establish, as a matter of fact rather than assumption, whether she held a pre-existing right to the property independent of that document. Where she did, the property is very likely hers absolutely under Section 14(1) regardless of what the old document says, and her own will, if she has one, or the ordinary rules of intestacy if she does not, will govern its passing rather than the remainder clause in the decades-old settlement.

Families who want certainty on this point rather than litigating it after the fact, as happened in Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh, are better served addressing the question directly while the woman in question is still alive: establishing clearly, through contemporaneous documentation or a fresh family settlement if needed, whether her interest is absolute or restricted, rather than leaving the next generation to fight over the same question the courts have been working through since 1956.

A note on coparcenary property and the overlap with daughters' rights

Section 14 operates alongside, but is conceptually distinct from, the separate line of authority on daughters' coparcenary rights under the 2005 amendment to the Hindu Succession Act, which gave daughters an equal birthright share in joint Hindu family property by birth, on the same footing as sons. A daughter's coparcenary share, once it exists, is her own property from the moment it vests, and Section 14 has comparatively little work to do in that context because there is no separate restrictive document purporting to limit what a coparcener holds; her share is simply her share.

It becomes relevant to daughters, granddaughters and other female relatives in a different and narrower setting instead: where a document, rather than the coparcenary rules themselves, is the source of the interest, such as a family settlement that allocates a defined portion of property to a daughter or granddaughter in terms that look restrictive. The Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh case itself illustrates this overlap, since the dispute concerned a gift deed's treatment of an individual's interest rather than a claim framed directly as a coparcenary right. Families and practitioners should keep the two frameworks separate in their analysis: a coparcenary share question looks to the 2005 amendment and to when the relevant right vested, while a Section 14 question looks to whether a specific document creating or recognising an interest should be read as an absolute grant or a protected restricted estate.

Stridhan and the special position of a woman's own property

Section 14's Explanation specifically brings stridhan, property traditionally recognised as a Hindu woman's own, held by her absolutely even under the pre-1956 law, within its sweep, which might look unnecessary since stridhan was never subject to the same reversionary limitation as a widow's estate in most schools of Hindu law. The provision's drafters included it anyway, most likely to foreclose any argument that a particular category of stridhan, such as property received at marriage or as gifts from relatives, might be read as subject to some lesser form of ownership than Section 14(1) otherwise confers.

The practical effect is that any ambiguity about whether a particular item of property counts as stridhan, and therefore already fully owned, or as something else subject to a document's restriction, is resolved in the woman's favour by Section 14(1) in any event, provided she was possessed of the property at the relevant time. This makes the stridhan characterisation argument less critical in modern litigation than it might once have been, since Section 14(1) tends to reach the same result, full ownership, through its own general language regardless of whether the property is separately classified as stridhan.

Where this characterisation still matters is in disputes that predate the woman's possession of the property, for instance whether an item given to her at marriage was intended as her personal stridhan or as a conditional gift to the family unit as a whole. That question is usually resolved under the general law of gifts and the parties' intention at the time, rather than under Section 14, which only comes into play once it is established that the woman holds, or is possessed of, the property in question.

Evidence that actually decides a Section 14 dispute

Because the dividing line between Section 14(1) and Section 14(2) turns on whether a pre-existing right existed before the restrictive document was executed, the evidence that actually decides these cases looks quite different from what a reader might expect from a dispute that, on its face, is about interpreting a single document. Courts have looked at the family's conduct both before and after the document was executed, any earlier arrangements or payments that suggest a maintenance obligation was already being honoured informally, and the document's own recitals, where they sometimes candidly acknowledge that the grant is being made in satisfaction of an existing obligation rather than as a fresh bounty.

Oral evidence from family members about the understanding at the time the document was executed is frequently led in these disputes, and its weight depends heavily on how far it is corroborated by contemporaneous conduct, such as the woman having lived on or received income from the property for years before the document was formally executed, which tends to support the existence of a pre-existing right rather than a fresh grant. A woman who can show she was already being supported from the property's income for a meaningful period before a gift deed was signed is in a materially stronger position than one whose claim rests only on the gift deed itself plus an assertion, unsupported by other evidence, that a maintenance right must have existed in principle.

On the other side of the same dispute, a family defending a restrictive document under Section 14(2) is well served gathering evidence that the grant was genuinely the first time the woman received any interest in or benefit from the property, with no prior arrangement, formal or informal, under which she was maintained from it. The cleanest Section 14(2) cases, from a defendant's perspective, are ones where the document itself, and the surrounding record, show a grant made to someone who had no previous connection to or dependence on the property at all.

A related but separate question: what happens on intestacy once the estate is absolute

Once Section 14(1) establishes that a woman holds property as full owner, the next question families usually ask is what happens to it if she dies without a will. The answer depends on which personal law applies to her and, for a Hindu woman, on Section 15 of the Hindu Succession Act, which lays down a scheme of heirs distinct from the scheme that applies to a male Hindu under Section 8. Section 15 generally prefers her own children and husband first, then her husband's heirs, then her own parents, then her father's heirs, and finally her mother's heirs, with the source of the property, whether it came from her father's side, her husband's side, or was acquired independently, affecting the order of succession in specific circumstances under Section 15(2).

This matters directly to the Section 14 analysis because families sometimes assume that property a woman holds as a Section 14(1) absolute owner will necessarily pass to her own children in every case, in the same straightforward way a son's self-acquired property would. Section 15's source-based rules mean that is not always so, particularly where the property originated from her husband's side and she dies without children, in which case it can revert toward her husband's heirs rather than her own natal family. A family settlement or a will, made once the woman's ownership is established as absolute under Section 14(1), is often the only reliable way to ensure the property passes according to what she actually wants, rather than according to whichever branch of Section 15's hierarchy happens to apply on the particular facts of her family and the property's own history, a branch of the statute she may well not have turned her own mind to at all before the question happens to be raised with her directly by someone in the family.

This is also why establishing whether a woman's interest is a Section 14(1) absolute estate or a Section 14(2) restricted one is not merely an academic exercise even where no immediate dispute exists. If her estate is absolute, she needs her own succession planning, a will if she wants to direct where the property goes, because Section 15's default rules may not match her actual wishes. If her estate is genuinely restricted under Section 14(2), no will is of any use for that property at all, since it will pass strictly according to the original document's own remainder clause regardless of anything she might wish or write.

How this plays out in a partition suit

A partition suit is the setting in which Section 14 disputes most often surface, because partition requires the court to first determine exactly what share, and what quality of ownership, each party holds before it can divide the property at all. A co-owner who holds only a Section 14(2) restricted life interest cannot insist on a partition that treats her share as freely alienable in the same way an absolute owner's share would be, and the remaindermen named in the restrictive document typically have an interest in resisting any partition that would let her deal with the property as though she owned it outright.

This is precisely the posture the Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh dispute took: a partition suit met at the threshold with an objection that the plaintiff's interest was, on the defendants' reading of the gift deed, nothing more than a life estate incapable of supporting a partition claim framed in terms of absolute ownership. Where a court declines to reject such a suit at the threshold, as happened there, the substantive question of whether Section 14(1) or Section 14(2) governs becomes the central issue the trial must resolve before partition itself can proceed, since the nature of each party's share is a necessary predicate to dividing the property.

Practitioners handling a partition suit involving an older gift deed, will or family settlement that uses limiting language about a female co-owner's interest should treat the Section 14 question as a threshold issue to be pleaded and proved early, with the relevant family history, any evidence of pre-existing maintenance arrangements, and the document's own recitals assembled well before the matter reaches trial, rather than left to be addressed reactively once the opposing side raises Section 14(2) as a defence. A partition suit that proceeds for years on an unstated assumption about the quality of a co-owner's title, only for that assumption to be challenged late in the proceedings, risks significant delay and cost that earlier, deliberate pleading on this specific point would have avoided.

Where the generational gap creates the hardest cases

The hardest Section 14 disputes tend to involve documents executed well before the woman asserting absolute ownership, or the family resisting her claim, had any personal knowledge of the circumstances in which they were signed. A gift deed executed in the 1960s or 1970s, long before the current generation of family members was born, often leaves no one alive who can give direct evidence of whether a maintenance obligation genuinely existed at the time, forcing courts to reconstruct the position from secondary evidence: old revenue records, rent receipts, correspondence, and the conduct of the parties over the decades that followed.

Families holding onto old family settlements, partition deeds or gift deeds involving female relatives would do well to treat these documents, and any surrounding papers that illuminate the circumstances of their execution, as permanent records rather than as paperwork that can be discarded once the immediate transaction is complete. Old revenue records, municipal tax receipts naming the woman as the person in occupation, and correspondence between family members discussing how the property's income was to be shared are all the kind of material that, individually, looks unremarkable but, taken together over a long enough period, can establish exactly the pattern of pre-existing support a Section 14(1) claim needs, in a way that no single document, however formally drafted, can ever quite manage on its own. A rent receipt from fifty years ago showing that a grandmother was being paid a share of agricultural income before a gift deed was ever drawn up can be the difference between a successful and an unsuccessful Section 14(1) claim brought by her descendants long after her death, precisely because it supplies the contemporaneous evidence of a pre-existing right that oral testimony alone, given decades later, rarely carries the same weight to establish, however sincerely and consistently that testimony is given by relatives who were not themselves alive to witness the events they are describing at second hand.

This generational gap is also why disputes of this kind so often surface only when the next transaction forces the question, a proposed sale, a bank's title search, or, as in Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh, a partition suit, rather than being resolved while the woman whose interest is in question is still alive to explain the circumstances herself. Addressing the Section 14 characterisation while direct evidence and direct testimony are still available is consistently the more reliable course across the pattern of disputes described in this piece, even though it requires a family to confront a question that often feels unnecessary until a dispute actually arises, and even though the woman at the centre of the question is rarely the one pressing for it to be resolved while she is still able to speak to it herself.

Section 14 has been part of Indian succession law for seven decades, and the distinction it draws between a document that creates a restricted estate and one that merely gives shape to a pre-existing right is still being worked out case by case, as Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh v. Simranjit Singh Grewal shows. The provision's basic purpose, to stop old documents from permanently confining a Hindu woman to less than full ownership where she already had a right to more, remains exactly as relevant to a family settlement drafted last year as it was to one drafted in 1956.

Anyone relying on, or trying to draft around, a restrictive clause in a gift deed, will or family settlement involving a female relative should treat the document's wording as the start of the analysis rather than the end of it, and should expect a court to look behind that wording to whatever right the woman held before the document was ever signed.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Section 14(1) of the Hindu Succession Act actually do?

It converts property a Hindu woman possesses, whether acquired before or after the Act commenced in 1956, into her absolute property, overriding any earlier limitation that would otherwise have confined her to a life interest or similar restricted holding under pre-1956 Hindu law.

When does Section 14(2) protect a restriction on a woman's property instead?

Where a gift, will, other instrument, decree, order or award gives the woman property for the first time and itself prescribes a restricted estate, that restriction survives under Section 14(2) rather than being enlarged by Section 14(1), provided the document is genuinely the original source of her interest rather than merely formalising a right she already had.

What is a 'pre-existing right' in this context?

It is a right the woman already held under Hindu law or otherwise, independent of the document being examined, most commonly a right to maintenance from the property, such as a widow's traditional right to be maintained from her husband's estate. Where such a right existed before the restrictive document, the document is treated as having merely given it shape, and Section 14(1) applies instead of Section 14(2).

What did the Delhi High Court decide in Mrs. Ajit Inder Singh v. Simranjit Singh Grewal?

Deciding only whether to reject the suit at the threshold under Order VII Rule 11 CPC, the Court held on 30 January 2026 that a pre-existing maintenance obligation can let a gift deed's life estate enlarge into absolute ownership under Section 14(1), and that this was a triable issue rather than something the gift deed's wording could settle on its own. The final factual determination was left for trial.

Can a woman sell or will away property she holds under a 'life interest' document?

Not if the document genuinely creates a restricted estate protected by Section 14(2); her interest ends on her death as the document provides. If, however, she held a pre-existing right that Section 14(1) enlarges into absolute ownership despite the document's wording, she can deal with the property as full owner, including by sale, mortgage or will.

Does Section 14 apply to daughters claiming a coparcenary share?

A daughter's coparcenary share under the 2005 amendment to the Hindu Succession Act arises by birth and is not usually a Section 14 question, since no restrictive document is the source of that share. Section 14 becomes relevant to a daughter or granddaughter only where a separate document, such as a family settlement or gift deed, is the actual source of a disputed interest.

Can a family settlement drafted today successfully limit a woman to only a life interest?

Yes, but only where she has no pre-existing right to the property independent of that settlement. If she already held an existing claim, such as a right to maintenance, that the settlement is merely restating in restrictive terms, courts can look past the wording and treat her interest as absolute under Section 14(1) regardless of how the settlement is drafted.
