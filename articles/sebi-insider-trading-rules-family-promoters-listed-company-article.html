SEBI's Insider Trading Rules for Promoters of a Family-Run Listed Company

What the 2024 and 2025 amendments to the PIT Regulations changed, and why family offices are often the weakest link

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 21 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A promoter family that treats SEBI's insider trading framework as a listed company's problem, rather than the family's own problem, usually discovers the gap only after a trade by a relative or a family office has already happened. Three rounds of amendments between 2024 and 2025, and a Supreme Court ruling in August 2026, have widened who counts as connected, what counts as price sensitive, and how little room there is to argue motive.

Key points

  • The Securities and Exchange Board of India (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015 treat a promoter, and anyone connected to the promoter, as an insider whenever that person is in possession of unpublished price sensitive information, regardless of whether they ever intended to misuse it.
  • A 2025 amendment, effective from June 2025, deemed many of the events a listed company must disclose under Regulation 30 of the LODR Regulations, such as a change in credit rating or a change in key managerial personnel, to also be unpublished price sensitive information under the PIT Regulations.
  • Amendments effective from December 2024 widened the definitions of 'connected person' and 'relative', and placed a rebuttable presumption on the connected person to show they were not in possession of unpublished price sensitive information, reversing what used to be SEBI's burden to prove.
  • A SEBI circular from April 2025 extended automated trading window closure to the immediate relatives of designated persons, and introduced a quarterly requirement to update the PAN details of designated persons and their relatives.
  • The Supreme Court held in SEBI v Rajeev Vasant Sheth (2026 INSC 826, 11 August 2026) that possession of unpublished price sensitive information coupled with trading is enough to presume insider trading, and that the trader's financial need or the use made of the proceeds is not a defence.
  • A structured digital database must record every disclosure of unpublished price sensitive information, including information that originates outside the company, though a March 2025 proviso now allows externally sourced information to be logged within two calendar days rather than at the moment it is received.
  • Penalties under Section 15G of the SEBI Act, 1992 range from a floor of ten lakh rupees to twenty-five crore rupees or three times the profit made, whichever is higher, and family offices that trade through multiple related entities can multiply this exposure across each entity that traded.

Why a promoter family is inside the regulation, not outside it

Many Indian promoter families still think of SEBI's insider trading rules as a compliance department's responsibility, sitting inside the listed company and answerable to the company secretary. That reading misses how the Prohibition of Insider Trading Regulations, 2015 actually work. Regulation 2(1)(g) defines an insider as any person who is a connected person, or who is in possession of or has access to unpublished price sensitive information, and it does not matter whether that person holds any office in the company at all.

A promoter who sits on the board is an obvious insider. A promoter's sibling who holds no shares and no title, but who regularly hears about the company's quarterly numbers before they are released, is just as much an insider under the Regulations. So is the family office that manages the promoter's personal investments, if anyone in that office has seen a board presentation or a draft financial statement before it became public, and so, in principle, is a distant relative who happens to overhear the same information at a family gathering and then places a trade on the strength of it.

The practical consequence is that a family's insider trading exposure runs wider than the listed company's own compliance list, and three sets of amendments since 2024, together with a Supreme Court ruling handed down in August 2026, have made that wider exposure considerably easier for SEBI to establish.

This matters more for a family-run listed company than for one with widely dispersed, institutional shareholding, simply because information in a family business travels through channels a compliance officer cannot realistically monitor or control: a conversation at a family dinner, a message on a family group chat, a casual mention by a relative who sits on the board to another who does not. None of that informality changes the legal position in any respect that matters to SEBI. The Regulations ask whether a person was, in fact, in possession of UPSI or connected to someone who was, not how formally or casually that information happened to be conveyed between family members.

Unpublished price sensitive information: a list that just grew

Unpublished price sensitive information, or UPSI, is information that is not generally available and that, if it became generally available, would be likely to materially affect the price of the company's securities. Regulation 2(1)(n) lists examples such as financial results, dividends, changes in capital structure, mergers and acquisitions, and changes in key managerial personnel, but the list was never meant to be exhaustive, and sensitivity has always been judged on the facts.

A SEBI amendment notified on 11 March 2025, with an effective date roughly ninety days later in June 2025, changed this picture by deeming most of the events that a listed company must disclose to the stock exchanges under Regulation 30 of the LODR Regulations to also count as UPSI, including a change in credit rating and a change in key managerial personnel other than through superannuation or the ordinary completion of a term. For a promoter family, this means a far longer list of board-level and company-level events now trigger insider status automatically, rather than requiring a case-by-case sensitivity judgement.

The same amendment carried a practical concession in the other direction. Where UPSI originates outside the listed company altogether, for example in a potential acquirer's own deal team during a transaction the target company has not yet learned of, the company need not close its trading window on that account, and a new proviso allows the company to log that externally sourced UPSI in its structured digital database within two calendar days of receiving it, rather than at the exact moment of receipt.

Family businesses in sectors where credit ratings and banking relationships matter, such as real estate, infrastructure and financial services, should pay particular attention to the credit rating limb of this change. A rating agency's provisional view, shared with the company ahead of a formal announcement, is now UPSI in the hands of anyone at the company who sees it, and in the hands of any family member they discuss it with, from the moment that view is formed rather than from whatever later point a compliance officer might otherwise have judged it sensitive.

Connected persons and relatives: a wider net and a reversed burden

Regulation 2(1)(d) defines a connected person broadly, covering anyone who has a connection with the company that would reasonably be expected to put them in possession of UPSI, and the Regulations have long treated the immediate relatives of a connected person as themselves connected persons. An amendment effective from 6 December 2024 widened both definitions further, and, more significantly for enforcement, strengthened the presumption that a relative of a connected person is also connected and in possession of UPSI.

This presumption is rebuttable, but the practical effect is that the burden shifts to the family member to show they were not in possession of UPSI, rather than requiring SEBI to prove access from scratch. A promoter's adult child who trades the family company's shares through a personal demat account, or a promoter's spouse who runs an independent business but shares a household with someone on the board, now starts any SEBI inquiry from a weaker position than before December 2024.

Family offices sit at the sharper end of this change. An office that manages investments for several branches of an extended family, and that also happens to employ someone who previously worked inside the listed company's finance team, can find every trade it places in the company's shares treated as presumptively informed, unless the office can show a genuine separation between whatever that employee knew and the office's own trading decisions, with that separation documented rather than assumed.

Trading window closure now follows the family, not just the designated person

Listed companies have long been required to close the trading window for designated persons, typically senior management and anyone with regular access to UPSI, whenever the compliance officer determines that the person can reasonably be expected to have UPSI, and to reopen it only after the information becomes public. A SEBI circular issued in April 2025, following a consultation paper from February 2025, extended automated trading window closure to the immediate relatives of designated persons as well, and introduced a requirement that designated persons and their relatives have their PAN details updated with the company on a quarterly basis.

For a promoter family, this closes a gap that enforcement had long targeted informally: a designated person who could not trade during a closed window, but whose spouse or adult child could, at least on paper, continue trading in the same securities through a separate account. The quarterly PAN update requirement also gives the company, and through it SEBI, a cleaner trail to match trades against the list of relatives whose window should have been closed.

'Immediate relative' is itself a defined term under the Regulations, generally reaching a spouse, parent, sibling and child who is either financially dependent on the designated person or who consults the designated person in taking trading decisions. A promoter family with adult children who manage their own finances independently, and who genuinely do not discuss company matters with the designated parent, has an argument that the dependency or consultation element is absent, but that argument needs to be supported by how the family actually conducts itself, not merely asserted after the fact once a trade is questioned.

The compliance officer, trade relaxations and the structured digital database

The compliance officer appointed under the PIT Regulations is responsible for the company's overall compliance with the Regulations, including determining when the trading window should close, maintaining the list of designated persons, and overseeing the structured digital database that records every instance of UPSI being shared, with the names of the persons who received it, their relationship to the company, and the date and, where relevant, the purpose of the sharing.

A compliance officer can, in defined circumstances, grant a relaxation allowing a designated person to trade even during a closed window, typically for a bona fide reason such as financial hardship, but must record the reasons in writing, and that relaxation cannot itself be used to put the Regulations' core prohibition out of effect. A promoter family dealing with a compliance officer who seems reluctant to grant a relaxation should recognise that the officer is personally exposed if the relaxation is later found to have allowed trading while the person was realistically in possession of UPSI, which is one reason relaxation requests are scrutinised closely rather than granted as a matter of course.

The structured digital database is not simply an internal record for the company's own comfort. It is the document SEBI reaches for first in any insider trading inquiry, because it shows, in the company's own words, who was told what and when. A family business whose database entries are sparse, late, or written in vague terms that do not identify the specific information shared, makes its own defence harder to mount later, since the absence of a clear record tends to be read against the company rather than in its favour.

Trading plans: a more usable route after the 2024 changes

Regulation 5 lets an insider pre-commit to a trading plan, disclosed in advance to the compliance officer and then to the public, so that trades executed under that plan are shielded from a later insider trading allegation even if UPSI happens to exist at the time of execution, because the plan was adopted before that information came into being. For years, promoter families found the trading plan route too rigid to use in practice, chiefly because of a twelve-month minimum coverage period and a black-out period between disclosure and the first trade that made the plan commercially unattractive for anything but very long-term liquidity needs.

Amendments made through 2024 removed the twelve-month minimum coverage requirement and revised the cooling-off period between public disclosure of the plan and the first trade under it, making the route usable for shorter-term and more specific liquidity needs, such as funding a known future expense, rather than only for a multi-year diversification programme. A promoter family that regularly needs to sell a small percentage of holdings for predictable reasons, school fees abroad, an unrelated business investment, or a scheduled loan repayment, should now look at a properly disclosed trading plan as a genuine alternative to waiting for windows to open, rather than dismissing the route as impractical.

A plan, once approved and disclosed, is not freely revocable, and the compliance officer must independently verify that the person proposing it is not currently in possession of UPSI before approving it, so a trading plan is not a way to launder an otherwise improper trade through a formal label. What it does offer a promoter family is certainty: once the plan is public, trades executed strictly on its terms are not later reopened as an insider trading question merely because UPSI happened to exist on the execution date, which is a meaningfully different position from relying on an after-the-fact explanation of why a particular trade was innocent.

SEBI v Rajeev Vasant Sheth: why 'I needed the money' is not a defence

On 11 August 2026, the Supreme Court decided SEBI v Rajeev Vasant Sheth, reported as 2026 INSC 826, in a bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and N. Kotiswar Singh. The respondents, promoters of a listed company, had traded in its shares while in possession of UPSI and argued before the Securities Appellate Tribunal that they had done so out of financial necessity and had not profited from the information itself. The Tribunal had accepted that defence and set aside SEBI's finding of insider trading.

The Supreme Court reversed that view. It held that the note to Regulation 4(1) of the PIT Regulations proceeds on the assumption that a trade executed while in possession of UPSI was motivated by that information, so that mere possession of UPSI coupled with trading during that period is sufficient to attract the presumption of insider trading. The Court held that neither the absence of profit nor the use the trader made of the sale proceeds altered that conclusion, restored SEBI's finding, restored disgorgement of roughly 1.38 crore rupees, and reduced the penalty on one respondent from twenty-five lakh rupees to ten lakh rupees while leaving the underlying finding intact.

For a promoter family, the lesson sits squarely inside the pattern this article describes. A family member who trades while in possession of UPSI, and who later explains the trade by pointing to an unrelated financial need, should not expect that explanation to answer the charge. The defence that matters is showing the absence of UPSI, or the absence of a connection that would give rise to its presumed possession, and that defence is best built before the trade happens, not after SEBI asks about it.

Penalties: how exposure multiplies across a family

Section 15G of the SEBI Act, 1992 sets the penalty for insider trading at not less than ten lakh rupees, extending up to twenty-five crore rupees or three times the profit made from the trade, whichever is higher. SEBI's adjudicating officer retains some discretion in fixing the amount within this range, guided by the factors set out in Section 15J, such as the amount of loss caused and the repetitive nature of the conduct, though how much discretion that section actually gives to go below the statutory floor is itself a point commentators read differently.

What a promoter family should take from the provision is less the exact figure in any one order and more the multiplying structure of the exposure. If three family members, trading through three separate accounts, each traded on the same piece of UPSI, each of them faces a separate penalty calculation, and the disgorgement SEBI seeks is calculated against the gains or avoided losses of each trade, not against the family's position as a whole. A family that treats a single relative's trade as an isolated, low-stakes decision is usually undercounting how many separate exposures a single leak of information can create.

A SEBI order under Section 15G is appealable to the Securities Appellate Tribunal, and from there, on a question of law, to the Supreme Court, as the Rajeev Vasant Sheth litigation itself shows. That appeal route matters to a family weighing whether to settle an adjudication proceeding or contest it, since a Tribunal that is sympathetic to a hardship argument, as it was at first instance in that case, is no longer a reliable route to a different outcome once the Supreme Court has spoken directly on what the hardship argument is worth.

Family offices, HUFs and the people promoters often forget

Succession structures deserve the same scrutiny. A Hindu Undivided Family, a private trust set up for succession planning, and a family office that executes trades on behalf of several family members are all capable of being connected persons or insiders in their own right, depending on who within that structure has access to UPSI and whose instructions actually drive the trade. Promoter families that have built sophisticated succession structures for tax and estate planning purposes sometimes fail to extend the same care to insider trading compliance within those structures, treating the trust or the HUF as a tax vehicle rather than as a trading entity that SEBI will examine on its own facts.

Family office employees deserve particular attention for the same reason, since staff who move between the listed company and the family office, or who sit on calls with both, are exactly the kind of connected persons the widened December 2024 definitions were written to catch. Documenting, in writing, who within the family office has access to what information, and keeping that group as small as the business genuinely requires, does more to manage this risk than any disclaimer in the office's investment mandate. Rotating a family office employee out of a listed company role and straight into managing that same company's shares, without any gap or information barrier in between, is one of the clearer patterns SEBI's surveillance systems are built to flag.

Trusts set up for succession planning raise a related but distinct question: whose instructions actually move the trust's trading decisions. A discretionary trust whose trustee acts entirely independently of the settlor and beneficiaries, on professional investment advice unconnected to the family business, sits in a different position from a trust where the settlor continues, informally, to direct what the trustee buys and sells. Families that set these structures up years ago for tax and succession reasons should revisit whether the trust's actual practice still matches the independence the trust deed describes on paper, because SEBI's inquiry will look at practice, not just the deed.

Continual disclosure: the trade itself has to be reported

Separately from the question of whether a trade was made while in possession of UPSI, Regulation 7(2)(a) requires every promoter, every member of the promoter group, every director and every employee to disclose to the company any acquisition or disposal of securities where the value of the trade, taken together with other trades in the same transaction or series of transactions, crosses ten lakh rupees within a calendar quarter. That disclosure must reach the company within two trading days of the trade, and the company is then required under Regulation 7(2)(b) to pass the particulars on to the stock exchanges within a further two trading days of receiving the disclosure or otherwise becoming aware of it.

A January 2019 amendment extended this disclosure duty to the promoter group as a whole, beyond just the promoters named as such in the company's filings, which means cousins, in-laws and holding entities who sit within the promoter group as the company has defined it can trigger a disclosure obligation even if they personally hold no office and have never been near a board meeting. SEBI has, in an informal guidance addressed to Kotak Mahindra Bank, read this disclosure requirement purposively, as aimed at persons likely to be in possession of UPSI rather than as a mechanical reporting exercise divorced from the Regulations' underlying concern.

Since a 2020 circular on system-driven disclosures, much of this reporting now happens automatically through the depositories and stock exchanges rather than through a manually filed form, which reduces the chance of a missed disclosure but does not reduce the underlying obligation. A promoter family member who trades through a demat account not linked to the company's system-driven disclosure mechanism, perhaps an account opened through a family office or a newly formed holding company, can fall outside that automatic net and still be squarely within the disclosure obligation, with no system in place to catch the omission until an audit or a SEBI inquiry does.

Promoter group disclosures and the overlap with LODR Regulation 30

Because the 2025 amendment ties UPSI directly to the list of events a listed company must disclose under LODR Regulation 30, a promoter family's own compliance calendar should now track that disclosure list as closely as the company's investor relations team does. A board decision on a related party transaction, a change in a key managerial person, or a revision to a credit rating are all now presumptively UPSI from the moment the relevant decision is taken until the company discloses it, not from some later point when a compliance officer decides the information has become sensitive.

A promoter who also serves as a director receives this information in the boardroom and is bound by the trading window regardless of the LODR link, but a promoter who does not sit on the board, and who instead hears about these decisions informally through family channels, is now on notice that the same information is treated as UPSI the moment the board decides it, whatever route it travelled to reach that promoter.

This overlap also affects how a family should plan the timing of personal transactions that touch the company, such as a promoter pledging shares to raise a personal loan, or a family member negotiating a related party transaction on the family's behalf. Both kinds of transaction can themselves generate UPSI before they are disclosed, and a family member who is both the source of that information and a potential trader in the company's shares sits on both sides of the Regulations at once, which calls for separating the decision to transact from any decision to trade, even where the same family members are involved in both.

Building, or fixing, a family business's insider trading code

Every listed company is required to adopt a code of conduct for designated persons, modelled on the minimum standards SEBI has prescribed in Schedule B to the PIT Regulations, covering trading window closure, pre-clearance of trades above a threshold value, and the maintenance of the structured digital database. A code that was last updated before the 2024 and 2025 amendments is very likely out of date on at least three points: the definition of designated persons' relatives, the trading window obligations of those relatives, and the trading plan provisions in Regulation 5.

A promoter family reviewing its own code should ask the company's compliance officer three direct questions: whether the list of designated persons' relatives has been updated to reflect the widened definitions, whether the quarterly PAN update process required since April 2025 has actually been implemented rather than only adopted on paper, and whether the structured digital database captures externally sourced UPSI within the two-day window the March 2025 proviso allows, rather than missing it altogether because no one owns that specific entry.

The code's pre-clearance mechanism deserves the same scrutiny. Most codes require a designated person to seek clearance before any trade above a set value, supported by an undertaking that the person is not in possession of UPSI, and a family member who is a designated person but has never actually sought pre-clearance before trading is building a record that will not help if SEBI later asks why the company's own code was not followed. The same pre-clearance habit should extend to relatives who are not themselves designated persons but who are now within the trading window closure net, since an informal practice of checking with the compliance officer before trading protects the relative even where the Regulations do not formally require pre-clearance from that specific person.

Common mistakes this firm sees in promoter families

Assuming that only board members and officers of the company count as insiders is the most common mistake, and the one the widened 2024 relative and connected-person definitions were specifically written to close. A family member with no title and no shareholding can still be an insider, and can still be presumed to have been trading on UPSI once a connection to someone who had it is shown.

Treating a trading plan as too rigid to bother with is a second mistake, one that the 2024 amendments addressed directly by removing the old twelve-month minimum and easing the cooling-off period, yet many family offices still have not revisited a trading plan structure they last looked at years before those changes.

Running family office trades through a single operations team without separating who has seen company information from who places the trade is a third mistake, and it is the one most likely to turn an individual's access into the whole office's exposure, since a connected person's knowledge can taint every trade the office places in that company's securities for as long as the information remains unpublished.

Assuming that financial hardship, or simply not having profited from a trade, will answer a SEBI inquiry is a fourth and increasingly costly mistake after the Supreme Court's ruling in SEBI v Rajeev Vasant Sheth. The defence has to be built around the absence of UPSI or the absence of a qualifying connection, not around the reason the trade was placed.

Leaving the Regulation 7(2) continual disclosure obligation to the company's secretarial team to track alone, without a family member checking that their own trades above the threshold value have actually been reported, is a fifth and often overlooked mistake. A late or missed disclosure is a separate violation from any underlying insider trading question, and SEBI has pursued continual disclosure lapses on their own facts even where no UPSI issue was ever raised.

A sixth mistake, specific to succession planning, is setting up a trust or a family office structure for tax reasons without asking how the structure will be treated under the PIT Regulations once it holds, or trades, the listed company's shares. A structure drafted purely by tax counsel, without input from securities counsel, can leave the trustee's or the office's actual trading practice inconsistent with the independence the paperwork describes, which is precisely the gap the widened 2024 presumption is designed to expose. None of these six mistakes requires sophisticated wrongdoing to become a live SEBI matter; each one is an ordinary family practice that the 2024 and 2025 changes simply made easier for a regulator to notice and harder for the family to explain away after the fact.

A practical checklist for the next twelve months

List every family member, trust, HUF and family office entity that holds or trades the listed company's securities, and map each of them against the widened connected-person and relative definitions rather than assuming the old, narrower list still applies.

Confirm with the company's compliance officer that the quarterly PAN update process for designated persons and their immediate relatives, required since the April 2025 circular, is actually running, and ask to see evidence of the most recent update cycle.

Review any existing trading plan, or the absence of one, against the post-2024 Regulation 5 framework, and consider whether a plan now serves a genuine, previously unmet liquidity need within the family.

Check that the structured digital database captures externally sourced UPSI within the two-calendar-day window the March 2025 proviso allows, and ask who in the organisation is responsible for making that specific entry, since it is an easy one to miss.

Treat every family office employee who has ever worked inside the listed company, or who regularly interacts with its management, as a potential connected person, and document in writing what information that person does and does not have access to.

Cross-check every family member's and family entity's trading records against the Regulation 7(2) threshold value for the relevant quarter, and confirm with the company that disclosures were actually filed for every trade that crossed it, rather than assuming the depository's system-driven process caught every account.

Ask the company's compliance officer to walk the family through the current code of conduct in person, rather than circulating it as a document to be signed, since a family member who can explain in their own words when the trading window closes and what pre-clearance requires is considerably less likely to trade at the wrong moment than one who has only filed a signed acknowledgement.

The 2024 and 2025 amendments to the PIT Regulations did not change the basic structure of India's insider trading law, but they closed several gaps that promoter families had, in practice, relied on: a narrower reading of who counts as connected, a trading window that stopped at the designated person's own account, and a trading plan regime too rigid to use. The Supreme Court's ruling in SEBI v Rajeev Vasant Sheth closed a fourth gap, the idea that a sympathetic reason for trading might soften the consequence of trading while in possession of UPSI.

A family that updates its own list of connected persons, its trading plans and its family office practices to match these changes protects itself considerably more cheaply than one that waits for a SEBI notice to do the same work under pressure, since the review described in this article takes a few weeks of attention now against months of adjudication proceedings later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a promoter's family member be liable for insider trading even without a formal role in the company?

Yes. Under Regulation 2(1)(g) of the PIT Regulations, an insider is any person who is a connected person or who is in possession of or has access to UPSI, regardless of any formal title. Amendments effective from December 2024 also strengthened the presumption that a relative of a connected person is themselves connected and in possession of UPSI, shifting the burden to that relative to show otherwise.

What changed about what counts as unpublished price sensitive information in 2025?

A SEBI amendment notified in March 2025, effective from around June 2025, deemed most events that a listed company must disclose under LODR Regulation 30, including changes in credit rating and changes in key managerial personnel, to also count as UPSI under the PIT Regulations.

Does the trading window now apply to a designated person's relatives as well?

Yes. A SEBI circular from April 2025 extended automated trading window closure to the immediate relatives of designated persons, and introduced a quarterly requirement to update the PAN details of designated persons and their relatives.

Is financial need a defence to an insider trading charge in India?

No. The Supreme Court held in SEBI v Rajeev Vasant Sheth (2026 INSC 826, 11 August 2026) that possession of UPSI coupled with trading is enough to presume insider trading, and that the trader's financial necessity or the use made of the proceeds does not answer the charge.

What is a trading plan, and has it become easier to use?

A trading plan under Regulation 5 of the PIT Regulations lets an insider pre-commit to specific future trades, disclosed in advance, so that execution under the plan is shielded from a later insider trading allegation. Amendments made through 2024 removed the earlier twelve-month minimum coverage period and eased the cooling-off period before the first trade, making the route usable for more specific, shorter-term liquidity needs.

How large can an insider trading penalty be in India?

Section 15G of the SEBI Act, 1992 sets the penalty at not less than ten lakh rupees, extending up to twenty-five crore rupees or three times the profit made, whichever is higher. Each person who traded is assessed separately, so a family in which several members traded on the same information can face several separate penalty calculations.

Can a family office be treated as an insider in its own right?

Yes, where the office itself, or a person within it who influences its trading decisions, is a connected person or has access to UPSI. Family offices that employ staff who previously worked at, or regularly interact with, the listed company carry particular exposure on this point.

What is the structured digital database, and does it need to capture information from outside the company?

The structured digital database records every disclosure of UPSI, including the names of recipients and the date and purpose of sharing. A March 2025 proviso allows UPSI that originates outside the company to be logged in the database within two calendar days of receipt, rather than at the exact moment it is received.
