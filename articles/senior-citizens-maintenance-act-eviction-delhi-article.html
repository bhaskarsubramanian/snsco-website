When Can a Senior Citizen Evict a Child From Their Own Property in Delhi?

How the Senior Citizens Act 2007 and the Delhi Rules let a parent remove a son, daughter or other legal heir from self-acquired property, where that power ends against a daughter-in-law's right of residence, and what the Delhi High Court has settled and left open

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 23 November 2026 · Law stated as on 23 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An elderly parent in Delhi who owns the flat their son or daughter still lives in, and who finds that living arrangement has turned into neglect or outright ill-treatment, has a remedy that does not require filing a civil suit for possession and waiting years for a decree: an application to the District Magistrate under the Delhi Rules framed for the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007. The Delhi High Court has firmly upheld this eviction power in recent years, but the Supreme Court has just as firmly drawn a line around it where the person facing eviction is a daughter-in-law asserting an independent right to stay in the house, and a parent considering this route needs to understand both sides of that line before filing.

Key points

  • The Delhi Rules framed under the 2007 Act let a senior citizen approach the District Magistrate directly for eviction of a son, daughter or other legal heir from the senior citizen's property, on the specific grounds of non-maintenance or ill-treatment.
  • This eviction route under Rule 22 is distinct from the maintenance claim route under Section 5 of the Act, which goes to a separate Maintenance Tribunal headed by an officer of at least Sub-Divisional Magistrate rank and is capped at a modest monthly figure.
  • The Delhi High Court has repeatedly upheld the District Magistrate's power to order eviction under the Rules, most recently in Punam v. Divisional Commissioner, GNCT of Delhi, 2026 DHC 8543 (DB), rejecting the argument that property disputes of this kind must go to a civil court instead.
  • Before ordering eviction, the District Magistrate must issue a written show-cause notice to the person facing removal and record a finding of non-maintenance or ill-treatment; the matter is usually preceded by an inquiry report from the Sub-Divisional Magistrate.
  • An order of the District Magistrate can be appealed to the Divisional Commissioner under Rule 22(4) of the Delhi Rules.
  • The Supreme Court's ruling in S. Vanitha v. Deputy Commissioner, Bengaluru Urban District, (2020) 12 SCR 1057, held that the Senior Citizens Act does not override a woman's right to reside in a shared household under Sections 17 and 19 of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, which limits how far an eviction order can be used against a daughter-in-law.
  • Section 23 of the Act offers a separate remedy for a senior citizen who transferred property on the understanding that the transferee would provide care: the Tribunal can declare that transfer void if the care promised was not actually provided.
  • The Supreme Court has made clear in two 2025 rulings, Urmila Dixit v. Sunil Sharan Dixit, (2025) 2 SCC 787, and Samtola Devi v. State of Uttar Pradesh, 2025 INSC 404, that eviction under the Act is a discretionary remedy to be exercised only after recording specific reasons of necessity or expediency, not an order that follows automatically once non-maintenance or ill-treatment is alleged, and that a genuine, pending civil dispute over title is a reason to pause eviction rather than override it.

A remedy outside the ordinary civil court system

A property owner who wants someone removed from their premises normally has to file a suit for possession, serve the defendant, go through written statements, evidence and arguments, and wait, often for years, for a decree that is itself subject to appeal before it becomes final. The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007, and the rules Delhi has framed under it, give a senior citizen a materially faster alternative where the person to be removed is their own child or another legal heir and the ground is non-maintenance or ill-treatment, by routing the application to the District Magistrate rather than a civil court.

This is not a general eviction power available to every property owner against every occupant; it is confined by its own terms to a narrow but commonly arising family situation, where a parent who owns or has a legal right to the property finds that the adult child living with them, often along with that child's own spouse and family, is not providing the care and support the relationship would ordinarily be expected to provide, and the parent wants that person out of the house rather than simply seeking a maintenance order to be paid while the arrangement continues unchanged.

The context in which this remedy arises is familiar to anyone who has handled property disputes within Delhi families: a parent who bought or inherited a flat decades ago, who allowed a son or daughter and their spouse to move in, often on the understanding that the child would look after them in old age, and who now finds that understanding has broken down, sometimes with active hostility, sometimes simply with the child's family treating the parent as an inconvenience in their own home. A civil suit for possession in that situation forces the parent to litigate, often for years, against their own child while continuing to live under the same roof, a prospect that by itself deters many elderly litigants from ever filing. The 2007 Act was specifically designed to spare a senior citizen that burden by placing the decision in the hands of an executive authority empowered to act relatively quickly on a focused inquiry into non-maintenance or ill-treatment, rather than a civil court working through the full machinery of pleadings, discovery and trial.

The statutory source: Section 4, Section 5 and why eviction sits outside both

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 is built primarily around a maintenance obligation. Section 4 fixes the obligation on children and relatives to maintain a senior citizen unable to maintain themselves from their own earnings or property, and Section 5 lets a senior citizen or parent apply to a Maintenance Tribunal, constituted for each sub-division under Section 7 and headed by an officer not below the rank of Sub-Divisional Magistrate, for a maintenance order against the children or relatives liable to pay it. The amount a Tribunal can award under this route is capped by rule at a modest monthly figure, commonly cited as ten thousand rupees, though the exact ceiling within that outer limit is set by each state's own rules and should be confirmed against the current Delhi notification rather than assumed.

Eviction is not something Section 5 or the maintenance Tribunal itself grants. The power to order a son, daughter or other legal heir out of a senior citizen's property comes instead from the Delhi Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Rules framed under the Act, specifically through a provision commonly referred to as Rule 22, which empowers the District Magistrate, not the Maintenance Tribunal, to pass an eviction order on a senior citizen's application. This split matters practically: a parent who only wants a maintenance order files before the Tribunal, while a parent who wants the occupant physically removed from the property files a separate application before the District Magistrate, and the two proceedings run on different tracks even though they arise from the same underlying relationship and the same underlying facts.

What the Delhi Rules actually require before an eviction order can be passed

Under the Delhi Rules, a senior citizen seeking eviction applies to the District Magistrate having jurisdiction over the property, setting out the relationship with the person to be evicted, the senior citizen's ownership or other right over the property, and the specific facts amounting to non-maintenance or ill-treatment. The District Magistrate is required to be satisfied on one of those two grounds, non-maintenance or ill-treatment, before an eviction order can be made; either ground on its own is enough, and a senior citizen does not have to establish both simultaneously.

In practice, the application is usually first examined by the Sub-Divisional Magistrate, who conducts an inquiry into the facts alleged and submits a report to the District Magistrate before any final order is passed. Courts reviewing these proceedings have treated this inquiry report as an important part of the record, and a District Magistrate's decision, whether to evict or to decline eviction, is expected to engage with what that inquiry actually found rather than proceeding on the senior citizen's application alone.

Before an eviction order is actually made, the District Magistrate must issue a written notice calling on the person facing eviction, and any other person claiming an interest in the property, to show cause why the order should not be passed. This show-cause requirement gives the occupant a formal opportunity to contest the application, whether by disputing the facts of non-maintenance or ill-treatment, by asserting an independent right to remain in the property, or by raising a defence of the kind the Supreme Court recognised in S. Vanitha, discussed below.

The Delhi High Court has upheld this power repeatedly

A recurring argument made by children facing these applications is that a dispute over who is entitled to possess or occupy a specific property is inherently a civil dispute, properly resolved by a civil court applying ordinary principles of title and possession, and that a summary proceeding before a revenue or executive officer like the District Magistrate is not an appropriate forum for what amounts to a property dispute dressed up as a welfare measure. The Delhi High Court has consistently rejected this argument where the underlying facts fit within the Act's own scheme, holding that the District Magistrate does have the power to order eviction of an adult child or other legal heir from a senior citizen's self-acquired or ancestral property where non-maintenance or ill-treatment is made out, and that this power is not ousted simply because the property happens to be immovable property that a civil court could, in theory, also adjudicate upon.

The most recent reported illustration is Punam v. Divisional Commissioner, GNCT of Delhi, 2026 DHC 8543, a Division Bench ruling that upheld the validity of the Rules empowering the District Magistrate to order eviction in cases of non-maintenance or ill-treatment, reinforcing a line of earlier Delhi High Court decisions taking the same position. The practical effect for a senior citizen in Delhi today is that an eviction application under the Rules is a genuinely available and judicially endorsed route, not a provision whose validity remains in serious doubt, though, as the next sections explain, that does not mean every application will succeed on its facts or that every person the parent wants removed can actually be reached by this particular remedy.

Who counts as a "child" or "relative" who can be evicted

Section 2(a) of the Act defines children to include a son, daughter, grandson and granddaughter, but expressly excludes a minor, which means an eviction or maintenance application under the Act cannot be directed against a grandchild who has not yet attained majority, whatever the facts of neglect might otherwise suggest. This also means that, read strictly, the defined term does not on its face extend to a son-in-law or daughter-in-law, who are not listed among the categories the definition covers, which is part of why the daughter-in-law cases discussed below have had to be analysed through a different lens than a straightforward application against a son or daughter.

Separately, Section 2(g) defines relative, a category relevant where the senior citizen has no children, to mean a legal heir of a childless senior citizen who is not a minor and who is in possession of, or would inherit, the senior citizen's property after their death. A Kerala High Court ruling applying this definition has held that it carries four distinct conditions, the senior citizen must be childless, the person must actually be a legal heir under the applicable personal law, that person must be an adult, and that person must either possess the property or stand to inherit it, and that a person who is not a legal heir cannot be treated as a relative under the Act merely because they happen to be in possession of the property. For a Delhi applicant whose case turns on the relative category rather than the children category, this means establishing the respondent's status as a legal heir under the governing personal law is a necessary first step, not something that can be assumed from possession alone.

The Supreme Court's 2025 caution: eviction is discretionary, not automatic

Two Supreme Court decisions from 2025 have added an important qualification to the eviction power that a senior citizen and their counsel in Delhi need to build into any application. In Urmila Dixit v. Sunil Sharan Dixit, (2025) 2 SCC 787, the Court held that a Tribunal or competent authority under the Act may order eviction, but that doing so is not mandatory or automatic in every case where an application is made; the power is discretionary, and the authority exercising it is expected to weigh the specific facts rather than treat eviction as the default outcome whenever non-maintenance or ill-treatment is alleged.

The Court went further in Samtola Devi v. State of Uttar Pradesh, [2025] 3 SCR 1235, decided on 27 March 2025, upholding a High Court decision that had set aside an eviction directive against a son where genuine civil disputes over title and over the validity of certain property transfers were already pending between the parties. The Court held that the power to evict under the Act must be exercised only after recording specific reasons demonstrating necessity or expediency, and that proceedings under the 2007 Act are not the forum for resolving a disputed question of title, which belongs instead before a civil court; the senior citizen in that matter was left free to pursue the pending civil remedies rather than having the Act's summary mechanism used to settle an underlying ownership dispute by the back door.

Reading these two decisions together with the Delhi High Court's own line of authority upholding the Rule 22 eviction mechanism in principle, the current legal position as on this date is best understood as two-layered: the power to evict under the Rules is validly conferred and the Delhi High Court will not strike it down as beyond the District Magistrate's competence, but the District Magistrate exercising that power is expected to actually engage with the facts, record why eviction specifically, rather than a lesser remedy such as a maintenance order, is necessary, and pause or decline eviction where a genuine, independently pending dispute over title or over the validity of a transfer exists between the parties rather than resolving that dispute itself in the course of an eviction proceeding. A senior citizen whose application rests on a straightforward, undisputed title and clear facts of neglect is in a considerably stronger position under this framework than one whose application is really an attempt to use the Act's faster procedure to shortcut an underlying property dispute that a civil court has not yet decided.

Timelines a senior citizen and a respondent should expect

The Act itself builds in some, though not complete, procedural speed. Section 5(4) requires the Tribunal to dispose of an application for a monthly allowance within ninety days from the date the notice of the application is served on the respondent, reflecting Parliament's intent that a senior citizen's maintenance claim should not be allowed to drag on the way an ordinary civil suit might. A separate, narrower time limit appears in the proviso to Section 5 dealing with enforcement: where a monthly allowance has been ordered and instalments fall due but remain unpaid, an application to the Tribunal to levy the amount due, enforceable in the manner of a fine under the Code of Criminal Procedure, has to be made within three months from the date the amount became due, a limitation that governs recovery of arrears rather than the filing of the original maintenance or eviction application itself.

This research did not locate an express limitation period within the Act or the Delhi Rules specifically governing how long after the alleged ill-treatment or non-maintenance a senior citizen must file an eviction application under Rule 22, and that gap should not be read as settled law that no limitation applies; a senior citizen with a long-delayed complaint should still expect the District Magistrate to ask why the application was not brought earlier, and should be prepared to explain that delay, particularly where the respondent is likely to argue that a stale complaint undermines the credibility of the allegations themselves, for instance by pointing to years of apparently amicable cohabitation on the available record after the ill-treatment is now said to have begun.

Appeal to the Divisional Commissioner, and what happens after

A party aggrieved by the District Magistrate's order, whether the senior citizen whose application was refused or the child or relative ordered to vacate, can appeal under Rule 22(4) of the Delhi Rules to the Divisional Commissioner. This appellate layer sits entirely within the executive hierarchy rather than the ordinary court system, which is part of what makes the overall process faster than civil litigation but also means the standard of review and the procedure followed at this stage differ from what a party would encounter in an appeal to a District Court or the High Court under the Code of Civil Procedure.

An order of the Divisional Commissioner can itself be challenged before the Delhi High Court, ordinarily by way of a writ petition invoking the High Court's jurisdiction under Article 226 of the Constitution rather than a further statutory appeal, since the Act and the Rules do not themselves provide a second tier of appeal beyond the Divisional Commissioner. A party contemplating this route should be prepared for the more limited scope of judicial review a writ court typically applies to an executive authority's factual findings, as opposed to the fuller re-examination of facts and law available on a first appeal from a civil court's decree.

Where the Supreme Court has drawn a firm limit: a daughter-in-law's right of residence

The Act's eviction mechanism has been used, in a number of reported cases including outside Delhi, by in-laws seeking to remove a daughter-in-law from a shared household, often in the backdrop of a strained or breaking-down marriage with their son. The Supreme Court addressed exactly this situation in S. Vanitha v. Deputy Commissioner, Bengaluru Urban District, (2020) 12 SCR 1057, decided on 15 December 2020 by a bench of Justices D.Y. Chandrachud, Indu Malhotra and Indira Banerjee, where the appellant's in-laws had used the Senior Citizens Act to obtain her eviction from a shared house, with the Assistant Commissioner and then the Deputy Commissioner and the Karnataka High Court all upholding that eviction.

The Supreme Court set the eviction order aside, holding that the Senior Citizens Act does not have an overriding effect on the separate statutory right of a woman to reside in a shared household under Sections 17 and 19 of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and explaining that this right of residence does not necessarily depend on the woman having ownership or any other proprietary interest in the property. The Court's reasoning was not that the Senior Citizens Act has no application wherever a daughter-in-law is involved, but that the two statutes protect different interests, a senior citizen's welfare and freedom from ill-treatment on one side, and a woman's statutory protection against being rendered homeless from a household she has shared on the other, and that an authority acting under the Senior Citizens Act cannot simply bypass the second protection by treating an eviction application as though the Domestic Violence Act did not exist.

For a Delhi senior citizen considering an eviction application where a daughter-in-law is among the people to be removed, S. Vanitha means the application has to be approached differently than one directed only at an adult son or daughter. Where a daughter-in-law has a pending or potential claim to reside in the shared household under the Domestic Violence Act, whether because proceedings under that Act are already underway or because the facts would plainly support one, a District Magistrate is expected to weigh that competing claim rather than evict her purely on the strength of the senior citizen's application, and the Supreme Court made clear she can still separately pursue remedies, including interim protection, under the Domestic Violence Act even where an eviction proceeding under the Senior Citizens Act is in motion against her.

Section 23: voiding a property transfer made in exchange for a broken promise of care

A separate and sometimes overlapping remedy exists where a senior citizen has already transferred property, by gift, sale or otherwise, to a child or other person on the understanding, express or implied, that the transferee would provide the senior citizen with basic amenities and physical needs in return. Section 23(1) of the Act lets the Tribunal declare such a transfer void at the senior citizen's instance where the transferee subsequently fails to provide that care, treating the promise of maintenance as effectively a condition of the transfer rather than an unenforceable moral obligation. Section 23(2) separately allows the Tribunal, where it does not void the transfer outright, to instead order the transferee to pay maintenance to the senior citizen in lieu of the care that was promised but not delivered.

This is a materially different remedy from the Rule 22 eviction power discussed above, because Section 23 addresses a situation where the senior citizen has already given up legal ownership of the property and is asking for that transfer to be undone, whereas Rule 22 addresses a situation where the senior citizen still owns the property and simply wants an occupant removed from it. A senior citizen who gifted a flat to a son on the understanding that the son would look after them, and who now finds that care is not forthcoming, has a potential Section 23 claim to have the gift declared void, which is a different, and in some ways more powerful, remedy than an eviction order, since voiding the transfer restores the senior citizen's ownership itself rather than simply restoring possession.

A practical complication with Section 23 claims is proving the condition attached to the transfer, particularly where the original gift deed or sale document is silent on any obligation to provide care and the understanding was oral rather than written into the instrument itself. Tribunals and courts examining such claims have looked at surrounding circumstances, whether the transfer was made shortly before or after a specific commitment to care for the senior citizen, whether consideration was actually paid where the document records a sale rather than a gift, and whether the transferee's subsequent conduct is consistent with having accepted such an obligation, to decide whether the statutory condition can fairly be read into the transaction. A senior citizen contemplating a future property transfer to a child, rather than one already made, is well advised to record any expectation of continued care explicitly in the transfer document itself, since a written condition is considerably easier to enforce under Section 23 than an understanding that has to be reconstructed after the relationship has already broken down.

Eviction with a safety net: why courts often order alternative accommodation alongside removal

A pattern visible across several recent Delhi High Court rulings is that eviction under the Act is frequently granted together with a direction addressing where the displaced family will go, rather than leaving that question unaddressed once the order to vacate is passed. In a 2026 ruling upholding the eviction of a daughter-in-law and her son, the Delhi High Court held that shared household rights under the Domestic Violence Act do not amount to proprietary rights and cannot override a senior citizen's rights over their own property, but went on to direct that the daughter-in-law and her children would be entitled either to alternate accommodation from among other properties owned by the senior citizen, where such property existed, or to reasonable rental accommodation in the same locality, funded by the family member liable to provide it.

Where no alternative property is available, Delhi courts have instead ordered a monthly sum to secure rental accommodation, with one 2024 decision directing a payment of twenty-five thousand rupees a month credited directly to the displaced family's bank account, and another directing a monthly payment of seventy-five thousand rupees specifically to support a daughter-in-law's accommodation needs after eviction. These figures are case-specific, turning on the particular property values and family finances involved, and should not be treated as a standard rate, but the underlying pattern, eviction paired with a funded housing alternative rather than eviction standing alone, is consistent enough across these decisions that a senior citizen's application and a respondent's defence should both anticipate the District Magistrate or a reviewing court raising the question even where neither side has specifically pleaded it.

At the same time, the same body of Delhi High Court decisions has been clear that this balancing exercise does not mean a senior citizen can be compelled to continue living alongside the person being evicted as a condition of the order, with one ruling specifically holding that a court cannot impose a forced living arrangement on a senior citizen against their will where that arrangement has itself been found to be the source of their suffering. The alternative accommodation direction protects the displaced family's immediate housing need; it does not dilute or postpone the senior citizen's underlying right to actually regain exclusive possession of their own property within a reasonable, court-specified timeframe.

Practical drafting and evidentiary points for an application in Delhi

An application under Rule 22 is considerably stronger where the senior citizen can document ownership of the property clearly, through a registered sale deed, a will, a succession certificate or similar title document, since a dispute over whether the applicant actually owns or holds a legal right in the property can itself become a preliminary obstacle the District Magistrate has to resolve before reaching the question of eviction at all. Where title is genuinely contested, rather than merely asserted by the person facing eviction as a delaying tactic, the District Magistrate may be more cautious about proceeding to eviction without that question being settled, which is one of the few situations where a parallel or prior civil suit for declaration of title can actually strengthen, rather than substitute for, the Rule 22 application.

Evidence of ill-treatment or non-maintenance should be as specific and contemporaneous as possible, dated complaints to the police or to a resident welfare association, medical records documenting injury or neglect, witness statements from neighbours or domestic help, and any written communication in which the occupant acknowledges the dispute, rather than a general, retrospective narrative prepared only once the application is filed. A senior citizen should also keep a simple, dated record of day-to-day incidents as they occur, since memory of specific dates and sequences tends to fade well before a hearing actually takes place, and a District Magistrate weighing whether ill-treatment is made out generally gives more weight to contemporaneous notes, even informal ones, than to a reconstructed timeline produced only after the application has already been filed. Where the occupant is a daughter-in-law, counsel should specifically address, in the application itself, whether any Domestic Violence Act proceeding exists or is contemplated, and should anticipate that the District Magistrate will expect that question to be confronted directly rather than left for the daughter-in-law alone to raise by way of defence, given the Supreme Court's reasoning in S. Vanitha.

Finally, because the Divisional Commissioner's appellate order is likely to be the practical end of the road before a writ petition becomes the only further avenue, a senior citizen and their counsel should treat the District Magistrate's initial hearing, not the appeal, as the stage at which the strongest possible evidentiary record needs to be placed, rather than assuming gaps in the original application can simply be filled in on appeal.

Choosing between the Tribunal, the District Magistrate and a civil suit

A senior citizen facing a difficult living situation with an adult child often has more than one available route, and choosing correctly at the outset avoids wasted time on a forum that cannot grant the relief actually needed. Where the goal is simply financial support, and the senior citizen is content for the current living arrangement to continue, an application to the Maintenance Tribunal under Section 5 is the appropriate route, since the District Magistrate's eviction power under Rule 22 exists to remove a person from the property, not to fix a monthly payment while that person continues to reside there.

Where the goal is to regain exclusive possession and the relationship has deteriorated to the point that continued cohabitation is not wanted, the Rule 22 application before the District Magistrate is the more direct route, provided the senior citizen's ownership or legal right in the property is not seriously in doubt. Where ownership itself is genuinely contested, where, for instance, the occupant claims an independent share in the property through inheritance, a prior gift, or a joint purchase, a civil suit for declaration of title, possibly combined with a prayer for possession, may be the more appropriate first step, since the Supreme Court's reasoning in Samtola Devi indicates that the District Magistrate and the Tribunal are not expected to resolve a genuine title dispute within the Act's own summary procedure.

A senior citizen is not necessarily confined to one route to the exclusion of the others over time. It is common, for example, for an initial maintenance application to be followed later by a Rule 22 eviction application once a previously tolerable arrangement deteriorates further, or for a Section 23 application to be filed where it later emerges that a property transfer was made on a promise of care that was never honoured. What matters at each stage is matching the specific relief sought, payment, removal, or reversal of a transfer, to the forum actually empowered to grant it, rather than filing a single composite application asking for all three remedies at once in a forum equipped to grant only one of them.

Counsel advising a senior citizen in Delhi should also factor in the comparative speed of each route when the client's priority is urgency rather than the theoretical strength of the claim. A Rule 22 application, resting on a straightforward title and well-documented facts, can realistically move faster through the District Magistrate's office than a civil suit would move through the ordinary court system, simply because the forum is designed around a focused inquiry rather than a full trial. Where the facts are genuinely contested, however, that apparent speed advantage can evaporate quickly once the respondent raises a title dispute that the District Magistrate is, per Samtola Devi, not meant to resolve, and a senior citizen who files under Rule 22 expecting a quick order in a case that actually turns on disputed ownership may find the application stalled rather than accelerated, with the civil suit route having to be pursued in any event.

The Senior Citizens Act and the Delhi Rules give a parent a genuinely faster route than civil litigation to remove a child or other legal heir from their own property on proven grounds of non-maintenance or ill-treatment, and the Delhi High Court's repeated endorsement of this power, most recently in Punam v. Divisional Commissioner, means a senior citizen in Delhi is not filing into legally uncertain territory by using it, though the Supreme Court's 2025 rulings in Urmila Dixit and Samtola Devi mean the application still has to be built on solid, undisputed facts rather than treated as a formality once filed. What the remedy is not, however, is an unqualified eviction power that defeats every competing claim an occupant might raise, and a daughter-in-law's statutory right to reside in a shared household under the Domestic Violence Act is the clearest example of a claim the Supreme Court has required a District Magistrate to actually engage with rather than override.

A senior citizen weighing this option should start by identifying precisely which remedy fits the facts, a maintenance order from the Tribunal where the goal is support rather than removal, an eviction application under Rule 22 where the goal is to regain possession from a child or relative who still holds no independent claim to the property, or a Section 23 application where property has already been transferred away on a promise of care that was not honoured, since filing the wrong one, or filing an eviction application without anticipating a daughter-in-law's Domestic Violence Act defence, is the most common way these otherwise fast proceedings end up delayed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a senior citizen in Delhi evict their son from their own flat without a civil suit?

Yes, where the senior citizen owns or holds a legal right in the property and can show non-maintenance or ill-treatment by the son. The application is made to the District Magistrate under the Delhi Rules framed under the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007, a faster route than a civil suit for possession, though a civil suit remains necessary where title to the property is genuinely disputed rather than merely asserted by the occupant.

Is eviction under the Senior Citizens Act different from a maintenance claim?

Yes. A maintenance claim under Section 5 of the Act goes to a Maintenance Tribunal headed by an officer of at least Sub-Divisional Magistrate rank and results in a monthly payment order capped at a modest figure. Eviction is a separate power under the Delhi Rules, exercised by the District Magistrate, and results in the occupant being asked to vacate the property rather than simply pay maintenance.

Can an eviction order under the Senior Citizens Act be used against a daughter-in-law?

It can be sought, but the Supreme Court in S. Vanitha v. Deputy Commissioner, Bengaluru Urban District, (2020) 12 SCR 1057, held that the Act does not override a woman's statutory right to reside in a shared household under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005. A District Magistrate is expected to weigh that competing right rather than order eviction without considering it, particularly where Domestic Violence Act proceedings exist or are likely.

What has to be shown before the District Magistrate orders eviction?

The District Magistrate must be satisfied that the person to be evicted has either failed to maintain the senior citizen or has ill-treated them, and must issue a written show-cause notice to the person facing eviction before passing an order. The application is usually preceded by an inquiry report from the Sub-Divisional Magistrate into the facts alleged.

Can an eviction order under the Senior Citizens Act be appealed?

Yes. Rule 22(4) of the Delhi Rules allows an appeal to the Divisional Commissioner against the District Magistrate's order. A further challenge beyond that is generally by way of a writ petition to the Delhi High Court, since the Act and Rules do not provide a second statutory appeal.

What if a senior citizen already gave the property away to their child?

Section 23 of the Act offers a separate remedy in that situation. Where the transfer was made on the understanding that the recipient would provide the senior citizen with care and that care was not provided, the Tribunal can declare the transfer void, or order the recipient to pay maintenance in lieu of the promised care, instead of or in addition to any eviction proceeding over the property itself.

Does the law automatically order eviction once a senior citizen complains of neglect?

No. The Supreme Court held in Urmila Dixit v. Sunil Sharan Dixit, (2025) 2 SCC 787, that eviction under the Act is discretionary, not mandatory, and in Samtola Devi v. State of Uttar Pradesh, 2025 INSC 404, that it must be ordered only after recording specific reasons of necessity or expediency, with a genuine pending civil dispute over title being a reason to pause eviction rather than override it.

If a daughter-in-law is evicted, is she entitled to alternative accommodation?

Delhi High Court decisions have frequently paired an eviction order against a daughter-in-law with a direction for alternative accommodation, either from another property owned by the senior citizen's family or through a monthly payment to secure rented accommodation in the same area, rather than leaving her and any children without housing. The specific amount or arrangement depends on the facts and finances of each case rather than a fixed statutory figure.
