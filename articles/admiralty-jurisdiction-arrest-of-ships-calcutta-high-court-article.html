Arrest of Ships in India: Admiralty Jurisdiction and the Calcutta High Court's Practice Under the 2017 Act

Why a cargo claimant, a bunker supplier or a shipowner dealing with a vessel calling at Haldia or Kolkata needs a different playbook from an ordinary civil suit

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 25 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A vessel does not have to be registered in India, or its owner to have any other presence here, for an Indian court to arrest it the moment it enters Indian waters. For a foreign shipowner, charterer, cargo claimant or bunker supplier with business running through Kolkata and Haldia, that one fact under the Admiralty Act, 2017 changes how a maritime dispute is actually fought.

Key points

  • The Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, in force from 1 April 2018, gives admiralty jurisdiction to eight High Courts, including Calcutta, and lets any of them order the arrest of a vessel physically present within its jurisdiction, regardless of where the vessel is registered or where its owner is based.
  • Before the 2017 Act, admiralty jurisdiction rested on the Supreme Court's 1992 ruling in M.V. Elisabeth v. Harwan Investment and Trading, which held that jurisdiction was not frozen at the colonial-era Admiralty Court Act, 1861 and that coastal High Courts beyond Bombay, Calcutta and Madras had inherent admiralty powers.
  • Section 5 lets the Calcutta High Court arrest a vessel present at Haldia Port or anywhere else within its jurisdiction to secure a maritime claim listed under Section 4, and Section 5(2) allows arrest of a sister vessel instead of the one the claim actually arose from.
  • In Gram Marine Pte Ltd v Owners of MV Pelagos, the Calcutta High Court ordered the arrest of a vessel berthed at Haldia under Section 4(1)(n), vacating the order on a security deposit of over Rs 1.75 crore being furnished to the Registrar, Original Side.
  • In the MT TSM Pollux matter over damage to the Haldia Oil Jetty, the court first fixed security of around Rs 88 crore for release, then revised that figure on the owner's application, holding that security for a maritime claim must be supported by evidence and should not be harsh or oppressive.
  • In Jindal ITF Limited v I-Marine Infratech (India) Pvt Ltd, the Calcutta High Court's Appellate Side held that admiralty jurisdiction does not extend to a vessel meant exclusively for inland waterways and that never goes out to open sea.
  • Sections 9 and 10 rank maritime liens ahead of registered mortgages and charges, which rank ahead of other maritime claims, so a cargo claimant or supplier needs to know where its claim sits in that order before assuming an arrest will actually recover its money.
  • Whether a foreign-seated arbitration clause bars an Indian court from entertaining a ship-arrest suit to secure the underlying claim remains an unsettled question under the 2017 Act, and pre-Act authority suggests it does not, which keeps arrest available as a parallel remedy to arbitration rather than a substitute for it.

A remedy built for a defendant that will not stay still

An ordinary civil suit assumes the defendant can be served, has assets that can be traced, and will still be within the court's reach by the time a decree is ready for execution. None of that holds for a ship. A vessel owned by a company with no office in India, no bank account in India and no other connection to India can still load and discharge cargo at Haldia or Kolkata, take on bunkers, and sail out again within days, often before an ordinary suit would even have been admitted for hearing.

Admiralty jurisdiction exists precisely to solve that problem. It lets a claimant arrest the vessel itself while it is physically present in Indian waters, converting the ship into security for the claim regardless of who owns it or where that owner is otherwise found. For a cargo owner whose goods arrived damaged at Kolkata port, a bunker supplier who delivered fuel at Haldia and was never paid, or a charterer caught in a dispute over hire, the vessel sitting at berth is often the only practical leverage available, and it disappears the moment the ship clears port.

This is why admiralty practice runs on a different clock from the rest of commercial litigation. A claimant who hears that a vessel connected to its dispute is due at an Indian port within days needs its application, supporting affidavit and proposed order ready before the vessel arrives, not after, because an admiralty court asked to act will usually be asked to act within hours rather than weeks. Counsel unfamiliar with this tempo, treating it as just another commercial suit to be filed when convenient, routinely lose the one window the remedy exists to create.

From three colonial courts to eight High Courts: how the jurisdiction got here

Admiralty jurisdiction in India did not start as a general power available to any court with a coastline nearby. Under the Admiralty Court Act, 1861, the Colonial Courts of Admiralty Act, 1890 and the Colonial Courts of Admiralty (India) Act, 1891, only the Bombay, Calcutta and Madras High Courts, successors to the old chartered High Courts, held statutory admiralty jurisdiction, and a 1997 Calcutta decision described these three as colonial admiralty courts with unlimited admiralty jurisdiction over India's coastal waters.

That narrow position was overturned by the Supreme Court in M.V. Elisabeth v. Harwan Investment and Trading, a 1992 ruling arising from the arrest of a Greek-owned vessel. The Court rejected the argument that Indian admiralty jurisdiction was frozen as it stood under the 1861 Act, held that a High Court could draw on the 1952 Arrest Convention's principles even though India had not ratified it, and found that coastal High Courts beyond the original three had inherent admiralty jurisdiction of their own.

The Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, which came into force on 1 April 2018, finally codified this into statute, replacing the old colonial-era Acts and extending admiralty jurisdiction by name to the High Courts of Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Gujarat, Karnataka, Kerala and Orissa, and to the High Court at Hyderabad, which serves both Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. The statement of objects behind the Act expressly traces back to the Elisabeth line of reasoning, aiming to codify and clarify what had until then been built case by case.

For a firm advising on disputes that touch Kolkata and Haldia, the practical effect of this history is simple even if the path to it was not: a party does not need to look for some separate maritime tribunal or specialised forum, the ordinary Calcutta High Court, sitting on its admiralty side, is the court, and the same judges who handle commercial and company matters on the Original Side also hear admiralty applications. That matters for forum planning, since there is no separate filing window or registry to locate, only the admiralty-specific rules that govern how an application within the same court is made and heard.

What counts as a maritime claim, and who can arrest a vessel for it

Section 4 of the 2017 Act lists the categories of maritime claim over which a High Court can exercise admiralty jurisdiction, a list modelled closely on Article 1 of the 1999 International Convention on Arrest of Ships. It covers the claims one would expect from shipping practice: damage caused by a vessel, loss of or damage to cargo, claims for necessaries such as fuel, stores and repairs supplied to a vessel, charterparty and bill of lading disputes, salvage, general average, towage, pilotage, crew wages, disbursements made on a vessel's behalf, and disputes over ownership, mortgage or co-ownership of the vessel itself.

Section 5 then gives the High Court the power to order arrest of a vessel within its jurisdiction to secure such a claim in an admiralty proceeding. The vessel named in the arrest application does not even have to be the one that gave rise to the claim: Section 5(2) allows arrest of a sister vessel owned by the same person in its place, which matters where the offending vessel itself has already sailed on but a related vessel in the same fleet happens to call at an Indian port.

Procedurally, Calcutta's own rules under Section 16(3) of the Act permit an ex parte arrest order on the plaintiff's application, which is what makes the remedy workable in practice: a claimant who had to give the vessel's owner notice before arrest would simply watch the ship leave before any order could be passed. The arrest itself typically comes with a condition for release, usually a deposit or a bank guarantee calculated against the claim, rather than keeping the vessel detained indefinitely once adequate security is on the table.

A claim that does not fall within one of Section 4's listed categories simply cannot support an arrest, however genuine the underlying grievance. A dispute over a shore-based agency agreement connected only loosely to a vessel, or a claim better characterised as an ordinary contractual dispute between two shore-based companies, is not converted into a maritime claim merely because a ship happens to be mentioned in it, and a claimant should test its facts against the Section 4 list carefully before an application is drafted rather than assume the court will stretch the category to fit. Cargo loss or damage, claims for necessaries such as fuel, stores and repairs, charterparty and bill of lading disputes, collision damage, salvage, general average, crew wages and disputes over ownership or mortgage of the vessel remain the categories that appear most often in Calcutta's own reported matters, and a claimant whose facts fit comfortably within one of these has a far more straightforward path than one trying to fit an unusual grievance into the list.

The Haldia cases: how the Calcutta High Court has actually applied this

Two reported Calcutta matters, both arising from vessels calling at Haldia, show the mechanics in practice. In Gram Marine Pte Ltd v Owners of MV Pelagos, 2018 SCC OnLine Cal 5834, the court allowed arrest of a vessel berthed at Haldia Port under Section 4(1)(n) of the Act, and set the order to stand vacated if the owner or interested parties deposited Rs 1,75,20,037 with the Registrar, Original Side, as security. The order shows the two-step structure that typically follows an admiralty claim in Calcutta: an arrest to secure the claim, followed by release once security matching the claim's value is furnished.

A more recent dispute over the tanker MT TSM Pollux, a Liberian-flagged vessel said to have damaged the Haldia Oil Jetty, saw the court first fix security of roughly Rs 88 crore as the price of release or arrest. On a subsequent application by the owner, the court revised that figure, holding that security demanded for a maritime claim must be supported by evidence of the claim's actual value and should not be set at a level that is harsh or oppressive on the shipowner. Read together, the two matters show Calcutta courts treating the security amount as a genuinely contested question, not a formality the claimant can set unilaterally by naming a large figure in its application.

Both disputes also share a feature that is easy to miss on a first read: the underlying damage in each case was to port infrastructure at Haldia itself, not to a third party's cargo. That pattern reflects how much of Calcutta's admiralty docket is now generated by the port's own operations and the heavy tanker and bulk traffic calling there, rather than by the cargo-cover disputes that dominate admiralty practice in some other jurisdictions, and a claimant or respondent new to the Haldia trade should expect port-damage and jetty-related claims to be a recurring category rather than an unusual one.

Where the jurisdiction stops: inland vessels and other limits

Admiralty jurisdiction under the 2017 Act is not unlimited, and the Calcutta High Court's own Appellate Side has drawn one of the clearest boundaries on the point. In Jindal ITF Limited and Anr v I-Marine Infratech (India) Pvt Ltd, a judgment dated 29 January 2021, the Division Bench held that the Act's admiralty jurisdiction applies to vessels generally but does not extend to a vessel that is exclusively meant for inland waterways and never goes out to the open sea, a category separately governed by the Inland Vessels Act, 1917 rather than the admiralty regime.

That distinction carries real weight on the Hooghly and in the Sundarbans waterways around Kolkata, where barges and lighters moving goods between river ports and larger vessels are a routine part of cargo operations. A claimant dealing with a dispute involving such a vessel needs to work out, before filing, whether the vessel in question is genuinely a sea-going ship that happens to be in inland waters at the time, which keeps the admiralty route open, or a vessel built and used only for inland plying, which does not.

In the Jindal ITF matter itself, the Division Bench treated the vessel's registration under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 as dispositive: once so registered, the vessel ceased to be an 'inland vessel' under the 1917 Act for this purpose, so a claimant can often resolve the question precisely by pointing to that registration rather than needing to litigate the vessel's actual day-to-day use. Where the position is genuinely unclear, a cautious claimant files the admiralty application in the alternative alongside an ordinary civil suit for the same claim, rather than betting the entire case on admiralty jurisdiction being available, since a finding that the vessel falls outside the Act's reach does not revive the limitation period that may have run while the admiralty route was being tried.

Who gets paid first: maritime liens, mortgages and ordinary claims

An arrest secures a claim; it does not by itself decide who gets paid, or in what order, if the vessel is eventually sold and several claimants are competing for the proceeds. Sections 9 and 10 of the Act address that ranking. Certain categories of claim, defined as maritime liens under Section 2(1)(g) and including claims such as crew wages and salvage, rank with an inter se priority set out in Section 9, and Section 9(4) specifically excludes certain categories of claim from ever attaching as a maritime lien in the first place.

Section 10 then places the overall hierarchy in order: maritime liens rank highest, registered mortgages and charges on the vessel rank next, and all other maritime claims, the ordinary cargo-damage or necessaries claim that most commercial disputes actually involve, rank below both. A cargo claimant or a bunker supplier arresting a vessel should go into the process aware that its claim likely sits at the bottom of this ladder, which matters most where the vessel's value, once liens and mortgages are satisfied, may not stretch to cover everyone below them.

This ranking rarely matters where the vessel is simply released on security and the dispute proceeds to settlement or judgment against that security fund, because the security is usually set at a level covering the arresting claimant's own claim rather than the vessel's entire value. It becomes important mainly where the vessel is actually sold by the court to satisfy multiple claims, a less common but not unheard-of outcome, and a party assessing whether arrest is worth pursuing for a modest necessaries or cargo claim should factor in that a mortgagee bank, if one exists on the vessel, will usually have first call on sale proceeds ahead of it.

What an arrest actually achieves: the vessel, not just its owner

Admiralty practice draws a basic distinction between an action in rem, proceeded against the vessel itself, and an action in personam, proceeded against the owner personally. Arrest is fundamentally a feature of the action in rem: the claimant is not required to first establish that it can serve the owner, obtain jurisdiction over the owner's person, or enforce a decree against the owner's other assets, because the remedy treats the vessel in Indian waters as the thing answering for the claim.

That has a practical consequence worth flagging rather than assuming. If the vessel is released on adequate security, cash deposit or a bank guarantee, the claimant's practical recovery is generally limited to that security, and the claimant does not automatically gain a wider right to chase the owner's other assets in India simply because an arrest once took place. A claimant who expects the arrest to function as a stepping stone to a broader recovery against the owner should plan for that separately, typically by converting or combining the admiralty claim with an action in personam where the facts support one, rather than assuming the in rem remedy alone will get there.

Letters of undertaking from P&I Clubs: not a right in India

Internationally, the fastest and most common way to release an arrested vessel is for the owner's Protection and Indemnity Club to issue a letter of undertaking, a club guarantee that the claimant accepts in place of a court-ordered cash deposit or bank guarantee. Most major maritime jurisdictions treat a club LOU as standard security once its terms are agreed, because the clubs are well capitalised and their undertakings are routinely honoured.

Indian practice has historically taken a more cautious position. Reported Calcutta High Court authority, including a Division Bench decision in SB International v MT Zaima Wavard, has declined to treat a P&I Club LOU as security the court must accept, and later Indian admiralty commentary describes the position as continuing under the 2017 Act: an LOU is not security as of right, and the court or the claimant can insist on a cash deposit or bank guarantee instead. A plaintiff remains free to accept an LOU voluntarily, and many do for practical reasons, but a shipowner negotiating release should not assume an Indian court will treat its club's undertaking the same way a London or Singapore court would. Building in time and a fallback cash or guarantee option, rather than relying on the LOU being accepted, avoids losing days while the vessel sits arrested.

The commercial consequence is that negotiations over release in Calcutta tend to take a slightly different shape than they do in London market practice. A claimant's proctor can reasonably insist on a bank guarantee from an Indian bank or a cash deposit with the Registrar, Original Side, as the price of agreeing to release, and an owner's correspondent should go into that negotiation expecting to arrange one of those rather than assuming its club's standard-form LOU will simply be accepted on the strength of the club's international standing. Arranging a bank guarantee from a scheduled Indian bank in advance, before a dispute reaches the arrest stage, is a sensible precaution for any owner or charterer with vessels regularly calling at Haldia or Kolkata.

Arrest and arbitration: a remedy that runs alongside, not instead of

Shipping contracts are heavily arbitration-driven, and charterparties routinely provide for a foreign seat, London and Singapore being the most common. A natural question is whether a foreign arbitration clause stops a party from going to an Indian court to arrest a vessel, rather than pursuing the claim only in arbitration. Commentary on the 2017 Act notes that this is not fully settled under the new statute, which contains no provision addressing the point directly, unlike the position under some of the international Conventions India has not ratified.

What pre-Act Indian authority does support is the view that an arbitration agreement, even one providing for a foreign seat, does not by itself bar a suit to arrest a ship in order to secure the underlying maritime claim. On that reasoning, arrest functions as a security measure available in parallel with arbitration, not a forum the claimant has to choose instead of arbitration, which is consistent with how interim relief in aid of arbitration generally works in Indian practice. A party relying on this should still expect the point to be argued in any arrest application where the underlying contract carries a foreign arbitration clause, since the statute itself leaves the question open rather than answering it.

A more recent Calcutta ruling, reported in October 2026, shows the same tension playing out on the arbitration side once security has already been furnished: the court rejected a charterer's attempt to invoke Section 45 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 after a consent decree had already been recorded in the admiralty suit, holding that the consent decree bound the charterer. That sequence illustrates why a party should treat its position in the admiralty suit, including any consent terms it agrees to, as something that can later limit what it is able to argue in a parallel or follow-on arbitration, rather than assuming the two tracks will stay neatly separate.

What this means for the three parties who most often end up here

A cargo owner whose goods arrive damaged at Kolkata or Haldia should treat the arrest application as time-critical in a way an ordinary cargo-damage claim is not; once the vessel sails, the practical leverage the arrest would have given is largely gone, and chasing a foreign shipowner through its home jurisdiction afterward is slower and less certain. A bunker or necessaries supplier left unpaid should similarly move quickly once a vessel from the same ownership calls at an Indian port, using Section 5(2)'s sister-vessel provision if the vessel that actually received the supplies is no longer available.

A shipowner or charterer facing an arrest application, on the other hand, has real room to contest the security figure rather than simply pay whatever amount the claimant's application names, as the MT TSM Pollux revision shows. Evidence of the claim's actual likely value, rather than the claimant's opening figure, is what the Calcutta High Court has looked for in setting a fair security amount, and an owner who engages promptly on that question, instead of focusing only on resisting the arrest itself, tends to do better than one who contests jurisdiction alone.

All three parties share one practical constraint that is easy to underestimate: the commercial cost of a vessel sitting idle at berth runs into real money every day, in demurrage, charter hire, crew costs and lost earning time, which puts pressure on both sides to resolve the security question quickly rather than litigate it to a finish. That pressure cuts in the claimant's favour at the moment of arrest, since the owner usually wants the vessel released fast, but it can reverse just as quickly once security is furnished and the vessel sails, because the claimant then has little left to press with beyond the security itself and whatever claim it can make good on the merits.

The 2017 Act gave India a codified admiralty statute roughly a century and a half after the Admiralty Court Act, 1861 first extended the remedy here, but much of what the Calcutta High Court is now doing with it, fixing security against actual evidence of value, drawing a line around inland vessels, leaving the arbitration question open, builds directly on the expansive reading the Supreme Court gave the jurisdiction in M.V. Elisabeth more than three decades ago.

For a port as busy as Haldia, feeding cargo and bunker disputes into the Calcutta High Court's admiralty docket on a regular basis, that continuity matters more than the statute's age might suggest. A party on either side of a maritime claim is better served by looking at how the court has actually priced security and drawn jurisdictional lines in recent Haldia matters than by assuming the bare text of the Act answers every question on its own.

The gaps that remain, whether a foreign-seated arbitration clause can block an arrest suit, exactly how far the in rem remedy reaches against an owner with no other Indian presence, and whether an LOU will ever be treated as security as of right, are not failures of the 2017 Act so much as questions Parliament left for the courts to work out case by case, in the same tradition M.V. Elisabeth itself set. Anyone with a live dispute touching an Indian port should treat that body of case law, not the statute's text alone, as the real source of the governing rule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an Indian court arrest a foreign-owned ship that has no other connection to India?

Yes. Admiralty jurisdiction under the Admiralty Act, 2017 attaches to the vessel itself once it is physically present within the jurisdiction of a High Court that has admiralty powers, regardless of where the vessel is registered or where its owner is based or incorporated.

Which High Courts in India can order the arrest of a ship?

The 2017 Act extends admiralty jurisdiction to the High Courts of Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Gujarat, Karnataka, Kerala and Orissa, and to the High Court at Hyderabad, which serves Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. A High Court without this jurisdiction, such as Delhi's, cannot order an arrest under the Act.

Can a vessel other than the one involved in the dispute be arrested?

In some cases, yes. Section 5(2) of the Admiralty Act allows arrest of a sister vessel, one owned by the same person as the vessel that actually gave rise to the claim, where the original vessel is not available to be arrested.

How is the security amount for releasing an arrested vessel decided?

The Calcutta High Court has held, in the MT TSM Pollux matter, that security for a maritime claim must be supported by evidence of the claim's likely value and should not be set at a harsh or oppressive level. A shipowner can apply to have an initially fixed security figure revised if it is not justified by the evidence.

Does admiralty jurisdiction cover river barges and other inland vessels in West Bengal?

Generally, no. The Calcutta High Court held in Jindal ITF Limited v I-Marine Infratech (India) Pvt Ltd that admiralty jurisdiction under the 2017 Act does not extend to a vessel meant exclusively for inland waterways that never goes out to open sea; such a vessel falls instead under the Inland Vessels Act, 1917.

If a vessel is sold to satisfy claims, who gets paid first?

Sections 9 and 10 of the Admiralty Act set the order: maritime liens, such as claims for crew wages and salvage, rank highest and carry their own internal priority under Section 9; registered mortgages and charges on the vessel rank next; and other maritime claims, including most cargo-damage and necessaries claims, rank below both.

Does a foreign arbitration clause in a charterparty stop a party from arresting a ship in India?

This is not fully settled under the 2017 Act, which does not address the point directly. Pre-Act Indian authority supports the view that an arbitration agreement, even one with a foreign seat, does not by itself bar a suit to arrest a vessel to secure the underlying claim, so arrest is generally treated as available alongside arbitration rather than barred by it.

Will an Indian court accept my P&I Club's letter of undertaking to release an arrested vessel?

Not automatically. Indian courts, including the Calcutta High Court, have historically treated a club letter of undertaking as acceptable only if the claimant agrees to it, rather than as security the court must accept. A shipowner should be prepared to offer a cash deposit or a bank guarantee from an Indian bank as the more reliable route to release.
