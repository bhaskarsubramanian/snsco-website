Industrial and Commercial Plots in Noida and Greater Noida: A Business Buyer's Guide to the Lease

What a business actually owns when it takes an industrial or commercial plot from NOIDA, GNIDA or YEIDA, and what happens when it wants to mortgage, sublet, transfer or change how the plot is used.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A manufacturer that wins an industrial plot in Greater Noida through an e-auction, or a retailer that picks up a commercial footprint plot along the Yamuna Expressway, has not bought land in the ordinary sense. What it has bought is a long leasehold interest from a statutory development authority, governed by a lease deed and a scheme document that dictate, often in more restrictive terms than a private landlord would impose, when the plot can be mortgaged, when it can be sublet, when it can be sold on, and what happens the day a payment is missed or a factory is never actually built.

Key points

  • A plot from NOIDA, GNIDA or YEIDA is almost always leasehold, typically for ninety years from execution of the lease deed, and the business holds a lessee's interest rather than freehold title, which shapes every later dealing with the plot.
  • Mortgaging the plot to raise finance needs the Authority's prior permission to mortgage, obtained for a fee, and a lender takes its charge subject to the Authority's own first claim for unpaid dues and its power to cancel the underlying lease.
  • Sub-letting a plot itself is generally restricted or barred outright until the allottee has built and commissioned a functional unit; renting out built-up space within a completed building is treated differently from sub-letting the land.
  • Transferring the plot to a new owner usually requires the first allottee to have a functioning unit and a completion or functional certificate, payment of a transfer charge calculated on the premium, and execution of a fresh transfer deed within a fixed window after the Authority issues its transfer memorandum.
  • YEIDA has in recent years restricted transfers of industrial and institutional plots altogether until construction is completed and operations begin, treating an indirect transfer through a change in shareholding as a violation carrying the same risk of cancellation.
  • Cancellation for non-payment, non-construction or breach of lease conditions is the Authorities' standard response to default, and while courts generally expect notice and a hearing before an executed lease is cancelled, they have upheld cancellation without separate notice in some circumstances, particularly where dues were repeatedly acknowledged or no lease deed had yet been executed.
  • A lessee aggrieved by cancellation typically has to work through a representation to the Authority before approaching the Allahabad High Court under Article 226, since the Authorities are treated as State instrumentalities whose allotment and cancellation decisions are reviewable as administrative action; a straightforward civil suit is often not the right first step.
  • A leasehold interest that has already been cancelled by the Authority has been held, in at least one reported insolvency matter, to fall outside the assets available for a corporate debtor's resolution plan, a point that matters to lenders financing a business against such a plot.

What a business actually buys: a long lease, not land

NOIDA, the Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority and the Yamuna Expressway Industrial Development Authority are statutory bodies constituted under the Uttar Pradesh Industrial Area Development Act, 1976, charged with planning and developing their respective areas and, as part of that function, allotting plots to businesses rather than selling land outright in the way a private developer would. The allotment is followed by a lease deed, and the brochures and scheme documents these Authorities publish for successive allotment rounds consistently describe the term as ninety years running from the date the lease deed is executed, not from the date of allotment itself, which matters because the gap between an allotment letter and an executed lease deed can run to months and is treated differently for several purposes described later in this piece.

Because the underlying interest is leasehold, the lease deed and the scheme document governing the particular allotment, not the general law of sale that would apply to freehold property, are what actually decide what a business can and cannot do with an industrial or commercial plot. Payment terms typically combine an upfront premium with an annual lease rent calculated as a percentage of that premium, commonly reported at around two and a half per cent with GST, and liable to a scheduled increase, often reported as a fifty per cent step-up every ten years under some schemes. A business evaluating a plot should treat these figures as scheme-specific rather than fixed across every allotment, since different schemes and different years have carried different rates, and should have the actual allotment letter and lease deed checked rather than relying on a brochure for an older or different scheme.

The lease deed also attaches construction and operational obligations that a private freehold purchase would not carry: a timeline to complete construction, a requirement to get the unit actually running, and an obligation to obtain a functional or completion certificate from the Authority confirming that the plot is being used for the purpose it was allotted for. Most of the restrictions on mortgaging, sub-letting, transferring or changing the use of the plot, discussed in the sections that follow, turn on whether this functional stage has actually been reached.

Plots reach a business through different routes depending on category and Authority: an e-auction for larger or more commercially attractive industrial and commercial plots, where the premium is whatever the auction settles at rather than a fixed scheme price, and a scheme-based allotment, often by draw of lots or on a first-come basis within published eligibility criteria, for smaller industrial plots, institutional plots and certain categories such as nursery school or specific footprint commercial plots. The route by which a plot was obtained affects the paperwork a buyer or lender should expect to see: an e-auctioned plot's file will show the auction terms and the winning bid, while a scheme-allotted plot's file will show the application, the draw or eligibility determination, and the specific scheme brochure in force at the time, which is also the document that fixes many of the mortgage, transfer and sub-letting conditions discussed below for that particular plot.

Raising finance against the plot: permission to mortgage

A lessee that wants to borrow against an industrial or commercial plot, whether to fund construction or for working capital, needs the Authority's prior permission to mortgage before creating any charge in favour of a bank or financial institution. Scheme brochures describe this as a defined administrative step attracting its own fee, reported in some Greater Noida schemes at a flat one thousand rupees and in some Yamuna Expressway schemes at five thousand rupees plus GST; the figure varies across schemes and years, so a lessee should confirm the current fee for its specific allotment rather than assume either figure applies across the board.

A lender financing against one of these plots needs to understand that its charge sits behind the Authority's own position. The lease deed typically reserves the Authority's right to re-enter and cancel the lease for breach of its conditions, including non-payment of lease rent, independently of whatever arrangement the lessee has made with its lender, and a mortgage does not by itself protect the lender from the consequences of the Authority exercising that right. Courts examining disputes of this kind have treated a lender's charge and attachment as a matter essentially separate from the Authority's own power to cancel the lease deed for the lessee's default, meaning a lender cannot assume that its mortgage, however properly registered with the Authority's permission, will survive a cancellation triggered by the borrower's own failure to pay the Authority what it is owed.

This has a direct consequence for a business raising finance against such a plot: the loan documentation and the lender's due diligence should treat continuing compliance with the lease's payment and construction obligations as a condition of the facility in its own right, separate from the usual mortgage covenants, precisely because a lapse on that front can extinguish the security itself rather than merely triggering an event of default under the loan agreement.

Where the borrower does default on the loan itself, a lender holding a registered mortgage over the leasehold interest can generally still proceed under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002, including taking symbolic or physical possession and, where resistance is met, seeking the District Magistrate's assistance under Section 14 of that Act, in the same way it would for any other mortgaged property; this piece's companion article on recovery in Noida and the district's wider SARFAESI practice addresses that enforcement process in more detail. What is specific to an Authority-leased plot is the risk already described above: enforcement under SARFAESI assumes the mortgage itself survives, and a lender needs to have satisfied itself, before relying on the security at all, that the Authority has no live or imminent cancellation claim against the lease that could extinguish what the lender is attempting to enforce against.

Sub-letting: the plot itself versus built-up space within it

Sub-letting an industrial or commercial plot before it has been developed is treated far more restrictively than letting out space within a completed building that stands on it. The underlying premise across the schemes reviewed for this piece is that the Authority allotted the plot to a specific allottee on the basis of that allottee's own stated use, construction timeline and, in many industrial schemes, its specific line of business, so letting the bare plot to a third party before any of that has happened defeats the purpose of the allotment and is accordingly restricted or barred outright under most scheme terms.

Once a functional unit exists on the plot, the position changes. A business that has built out a commercial or industrial facility can generally let out built-up space within that facility to tenants, subject to the terms of the specific scheme under which the plot was allotted and, in some schemes, subject to a defined policy on sub-leasing built-up space that the Authority publishes separately from its plot allotment rules. A tripartite sub-lease arrangement, involving the Authority, the original lessee and the sub-lessee, has also been used in Greater Noida for certain categories of built-up space, and disputes over an Authority's refusal to execute or recognise such a tripartite sub-lease have reached the Allahabad High Court in more than one reported matter.

A business planning to generate rental income from a plot it has been allotted should work out, before signing any sub-lease or rental arrangement, which category its situation falls into: sub-letting the undeveloped plot itself, which is likely to be restricted and risks a cancellation if done without approval, or letting out space within a building already constructed and certified, which is the more workable route under most schemes. Getting this distinction wrong at the drafting stage is one of the more common ways a business loses an otherwise straightforward rental arrangement to an Authority's objection.

Selling the plot on: transfer, change in shareholding, and change of product

A lessee that wants to exit, by selling the leasehold interest to a new owner, cannot do this as freely as a freehold seller could. Scheme documents reviewed for Greater Noida's industrial allotments condition a transfer on the existing allottee having become what they describe as a bona fide lessee, generally understood to mean the unit is functional and not under any outstanding default, with cancelled allotments made ineligible for transfer altogether. Once the Authority approves a transfer, it issues a transfer memorandum, and the transferor and transferee are then required to execute a transfer deed within a fixed window, reported in at least one Greater Noida scheme as ninety days from the date of that memorandum; after execution, both the assets and the liabilities attaching to the unit pass to the transferee, so a buyer taking an assignment of an existing lease needs to check the transferor's compliance history and outstanding dues with real care before completing the deal, since those liabilities do not simply disappear on transfer.

The fee for a transfer is calculated as a percentage of the premium, reported for the Yamuna Expressway Authority's schemes at around five per cent of the prevailing premium or the total bid premium, whichever is higher, with applicable GST, though exact figures again vary by scheme and should be confirmed against the specific allotment letter rather than assumed from a brochure for a different scheme or year. A change in the shareholding of the allottee company, rather than an outright transfer of the plot, is treated by at least one Authority's scheme as attracting its own charge, reported at ten per cent on a full change in shareholding and proportionately less for a partial change, reflecting the Authorities' consistent position that an indirect change of control over the entity holding the lease is economically equivalent to a transfer and should not be used to avoid the transfer process and its fee.

A completed transfer still has to be registered like any other conveyance of an interest in immovable property in Uttar Pradesh, attracting stamp duty and registration charges assessed on the transaction value or the applicable circle rate, whichever the registering authority treats as higher, a process this site's companion guide on Uttar Pradesh stamp duty and the registry process addresses separately. A buyer should budget for the Authority's transfer charge and for stamp duty and registration as two distinct costs, since paying one does not substitute for the other, and should confirm that the transfer deed itself, once executed, is actually presented for registration within the time the Registration Act, 1908 allows, rather than treating the Authority's transfer memorandum as the end of the paperwork.

A change in the product or activity carried on at an industrial plot, as distinct from a change in ownership, also typically needs the Authority's approval, since the original allotment was usually made for a specific category of industrial or commercial use, and switching to a materially different activity without approval risks the same consequences as an unauthorised transfer. YEIDA has gone further than most in recent years, reportedly introducing a policy that bars transferring industrial and institutional plots altogether until construction is actually completed and operations have begun, treating a change in shareholding structured to pass on ownership indirectly as a violation of that policy and exposing the allottee to cancellation; allottees have pushed back publicly against this restriction, particularly where the delay in reaching the operational stage was itself caused by the Authority's own slow handover of possession or infrastructure, but the policy as reported remains the current position for the Authority's newer industrial and institutional schemes and should be checked directly before a business plans an exit through sale.

Changing how the plot is used: conversion and change of land use

A business that finds its industrial plot more valuable for a commercial use, or that wants to add a use not covered by the original allotment, such as a showroom or office component on a plot allotted for manufacturing, is dealing with a change of land use rather than simply a change of product within the same broad category, and the Authorities treat this as a more significant departure from the original allotment than the product-category change described above. Change of land use ordinarily requires a specific application to the Authority, is assessed against the master plan and zoning for the sector in which the plot sits, and, where permitted at all, typically carries a conversion charge calculated with reference to the difference between the premium rates applicable to the original and the proposed use, reflecting the fact that commercial land is usually priced well above industrial land in the same location.

A lessee should not assume that a change of land use will be granted merely because a neighbouring plot already carries a mixed or commercial use, since zoning permissions are decided plot by plot and sector by sector under the applicable master plan, and an unauthorised change of use, carried out without the Authority's approval, exposes the lessee to the same cancellation risk as an unauthorised transfer or an unauthorised product change. Where a business's real commercial objective is a different use rather than a different owner, applying for change of land use through the Authority's own process, however slow, is the safer route compared with attempting to achieve the same result informally through how the plot is actually operated.

Default and cancellation: the Authorities' standard remedy

Non-payment of the premium instalments or the annual lease rent, failure to complete construction within the time the lease deed allows, and failure to get the unit functional within the stipulated period are the recurring grounds on which NOIDA, GNIDA and YEIDA cancel allotments and lease deeds. A cancellation is typically preceded by a demand or reminder notice calling on the lessee to clear the dues, and reported Allahabad High Court decisions have both upheld and set aside cancellations depending heavily on the specific facts: a cancellation following years of acknowledged, unpaid dues and repeated reminders has been upheld even against an argument that the allottee deserved one further opportunity, with courts unwilling to let what one 2025 Division Bench decision called deliberate, repeated default by an allottee be rewarded with indefinite forbearance; conversely, cancellations made without any real notice or hearing, or made on grounds the allottee was never given a chance to answer, have been quashed or sent back for reconsideration.

Whether the full requirements of natural justice, meaning a specific show cause notice and a personal hearing before cancellation, apply with the same force at every stage of the relationship is not uniformly answered across the decisions reviewed for this piece. At least one summarised Allahabad High Court ruling treats cancellation of an allotment for non-payment as valid without a separate prior notice where the lease deed itself had not yet been executed, suggesting that the allotment stage, before a lease deed exists, is treated somewhat differently from cancellation of an already executed lease. A business facing a cancellation should not assume either outcome automatically applies to its own situation; the stage the allotment has reached, whether a lease deed has been executed, and the specific wording of the notice actually issued all affect how a court is likely to approach the case.

Before cancellation is even considered, most lease deeds build in an interest or penal charge for late payment of an instalment or the annual lease rent, calculated for a defined grace period before the Authority treats the default as serious enough to issue a cancellation notice. A business that is genuinely short of funds for a particular instalment is usually better served by paying what it can promptly and writing to the Authority to explain and request time, creating a record of good faith that a court will weigh favourably if cancellation is later challenged, than by staying silent until a cancellation notice actually arrives; several of the cancellations upheld in the decisions reviewed for this piece turned on the allottee's prolonged silence and repeated unexplained default rather than on the default itself being incapable of excuse.

A separate and increasingly significant ground of dispute is the Authorities' delay in delivering what the sanctioned plan promised, such as an approach road of the width shown in the layout. The Supreme Court's 2026 decision in New Okhla Industrial Development Authority v. Sunshine Trade Tower Private Limited, arising from an Allahabad High Court judgment of 19 July 2024, dismissed NOIDA's appeal and upheld relief for a lessee under the Authority's own Zero Period Policy where a sanctioned forty-five-metre front road had never actually been made available, rejecting NOIDA's argument that such relief should be confined to cases of a total absence of physical access. A business whose construction timeline has been affected by the Authority's own failure to deliver promised infrastructure should examine whether a Zero Period claim, which effectively pauses the construction clock for the period the Authority was itself in default, is available before assuming a resulting delay in meeting construction milestones will count against it.

After cancellation: restoration, and how narrow the window usually is

A cancelled allotment is not always the end of the matter, but the route back is narrow and time-bound. In at least one reported insolvency proceeding, counsel for an Authority described its restoration policy as allowing an application for restoration only within ninety days of the cancellation itself, and in that case a request made after roughly three hundred days was rejected as plainly out of time. A lessee facing cancellation, or a buyer considering taking on a cancelled allotment through some later arrangement with the original lessee, should treat this kind of short window as the likely norm across these Authorities' restoration policies rather than an unusual feature of a single case, and should move for restoration, if that is the chosen course, immediately rather than after attempting other remedies first.

Separately, where an allottee can show that it had in fact made real progress, such as completing a defined share of the construction before the cancellation, Authorities have in specific instances restored plots on that basis; one reported account describes a batch of plots being restored after their owners produced proof of having completed more than ten per cent of construction. This should be read as an illustration of the kind of evidence that has worked in a specific policy exercise rather than as a universal rule, since no single consolidated restoration policy applicable across all categories of plots and all three Authorities was found in the sources examined for this piece.

Courts have occasionally stepped into the restoration process directly rather than simply reviewing an Authority's decision after the fact, including by directing an Authority's chief executive to constitute a committee to hear the parties and attempt an amicable resolution of a restoration dispute, and, in at least one matter concerning a housing society's cancelled lease, by setting up a committee headed by a retired judge to examine the scope for partial restoration. These examples show that the restoration process can sometimes be reopened through judicial intervention even after an Authority's own internal window has closed, though a lessee should not count on this happening and should treat the Authority's own ninety-day-style window as the primary opportunity to act.

Remedies against an adverse decision: representation first, then the High Court

A lessee aggrieved by a cancellation, a refusal to permit a transfer or sub-lease, or a demand it considers wrongly calculated, generally starts by making a written representation to the Authority itself, addressed to the Chief Executive Officer or the department actually handling the file, setting out the specific facts and the specific relief sought. Several reported disputes examined for this piece show Authorities and courts alike expecting this step to have been taken, and a representation that is ignored or rejected without adequate reasons strengthens a subsequent writ petition considerably more than going straight to the High Court without first giving the Authority a chance to reconsider on the facts actually in dispute.

Because NOIDA, GNIDA and YEIDA are statutory authorities performing public functions and are treated as instrumentalities of the State, their allotment, cancellation and transfer decisions are reviewable by the Allahabad High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution as administrative action, and this writ route, rather than an ordinary civil suit, has been the recurring forum in the reported disputes gathered for this piece, including the Sunshine Trade Tower litigation and the Allahabad High Court's review of the Sector 1 institutional plot cancellation referenced in this site's companion article on Noida real estate disputes. The Supreme Court has itself observed, in litigation concerning these very Authorities, that allotment of land in planned townships of this kind has generated what it called innumerable controversies and extensive litigation before the Allahabad and Delhi High Courts and the Supreme Court itself, which reflects just how central the writ route has become to disputes of this kind rather than being an unusual choice of forum.

A civil suit is not entirely closed off, but it tends to be the right vehicle only where the real grievance is a straightforward contractual claim, such as a dispute over money owed between the lessee and a third party, or a claim for damages that does not require quashing or reviewing the Authority's own administrative decision; where the grievance is actually about the fairness or legality of what the Authority itself did, in cancelling, refusing or demanding something, the writ route is the one courts have consistently used. A business should decide this question deliberately with counsel rather than defaulting to whichever forum its lawyer happens to be more familiar with, since choosing the wrong one wastes the limited time available to challenge an administrative decision effectively.

What happens to the leasehold interest in insolvency

A business that has financed itself or a group company against one of these plots, or that is itself a lender assessing security over such a plot, needs to factor in how a later corporate insolvency resolution process under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 treats a leasehold interest that an Authority has already cancelled. In a matter involving a cancelled Noida lease, reported before the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal in 2024 and discussed in commentary through 2025, the cancelled leasehold rights were excluded from the assets available to the corporate debtor's resolution plan, with the tribunal also rejecting reliance on the 'tenant holding over' protection under the Transfer of Property Act, since the Authority had neither accepted rent from the company after cancellation nor otherwise assented to its continued possession.

The practical lesson for a lender or an investor is that a cancelled lease, whatever its earlier value to the business, cannot simply be assumed to remain part of the pool of assets available to satisfy creditors once an Authority has validly exercised its cancellation power, and the point at which cancellation actually took effect, relative to the commencement of any insolvency process, can decide whether the plot is treated as part of the debtor's estate at all. A business or lender with exposure to one of these plots should keep its own compliance with the lease current as a matter of real priority, independent of whatever is happening elsewhere in the business, precisely because the consequence of losing the plot through cancellation reaches well beyond the plot itself once insolvency proceedings are in the picture.

A practical sequence before taking, financing or buying into a plot

A business considering a fresh allotment should read the specific scheme document and the draft lease deed for that allotment round in full before bidding or applying, rather than relying on a general understanding of how these Authorities work, since premium payment schedules, lease rent percentages, transfer conditions and construction timelines vary meaningfully from scheme to scheme and from year to year. The second step, once allotted, is calendaring the construction and functional-certificate deadlines the lease deed actually sets, since missing them is one of the most common paths to a cancellation notice, and building in a buffer for the possibility that the Authority itself may be slow to hand over promised infrastructure, in which case a Zero Period claim of the kind recognised in the Sunshine Trade Tower case may become relevant.

A business planning to raise finance against the plot should apply for permission to mortgage early rather than waiting until a lender is ready to disburse, and should build loan covenants that track the lease's own payment and construction obligations rather than treating the mortgage as a self-contained arrangement that survives regardless of what happens between the lessee and the Authority. A business buying into an existing lease through a transfer should insist on seeing the transferor's full payment history, any prior notices or default correspondence from the Authority, and confirmation that the unit is genuinely functional and not merely described as such, since the liabilities attaching to the plot pass to the transferee once the transfer deed is executed.

Finally, a lessee that finds itself facing a cancellation notice should treat the first few weeks after receiving it as the period that matters most: making a clear, specific representation to the Authority promptly, preserving every record of payments made and construction progress achieved, and getting advice on whether a writ petition, a restoration application within whatever window the Authority allows, or both together, is the right combination of steps for its particular facts, rather than waiting to see whether the Authority relents on its own.

A business already in dispute with one of these Authorities, whatever the specific issue, should also keep an eye on its own internal corporate housekeeping: a shareholding change, a merger, or a restructuring carried out for unrelated business reasons while an allotment dispute is live can itself be read by the Authority as an attempt to transfer control of the lease without going through the proper process, inviting an additional and entirely avoidable cancellation ground on top of whatever the original dispute was about. Clearing any such internal changes with the Authority in advance, even where the change has nothing to do with the plot itself, is a precaution worth taking for any business holding one of these leases.

Where the position remains unsettled

Several points in this area do not have a single settled answer across all three Authorities and deserve a flag rather than a firm statement. The exact fee for permission to mortgage, the exact transfer charge, and the exact restoration window are all reported differently across different schemes and different years in the sources gathered for this piece, and a business should always confirm the figure that applies to its own specific allotment letter and lease deed rather than relying on any number given here as a general rule.

The extent to which natural justice requires a separate show cause notice and hearing before every cancellation, as against the more limited protection apparently available at the pre-lease-deed allotment stage, is also not uniformly settled across the decisions reviewed, and turns heavily on the specific facts of each case, including how many reminders were given and whether the dues were ever disputed by the allottee. YEIDA's reported policy barring transfers of industrial and institutional plots until operations begin is relatively recent and has already drawn public pushback from allottees citing the Authority's own delays; whether that policy survives in its current form, or is modified in response to that pushback, is worth checking against YEIDA's current circulars before relying on it for a transaction being planned now.

An industrial or commercial plot from NOIDA, GNIDA or YEIDA comes with a lease deed that does far more work than a typical private lease, dictating not just rent and term but whether a business can mortgage, sublet, transfer or repurpose the plot at all, and what happens the moment it falls behind on payment or construction. Treating the lease deed and the specific scheme document as the primary source of truth, rather than a general sense of how these Authorities operate, is the difference between a transaction that goes smoothly and one that ends in a cancellation notice.

Where a dispute does arise, the pattern across the reported cases gathered for this piece is consistent: a prompt representation to the Authority, followed if necessary by a writ petition to the Allahabad High Court rather than an ordinary civil suit, is the route that has actually worked for lessees in these disputes. Businesses that build this sequence, and the Authority's own short restoration windows, into their planning from the outset are in a far stronger position than those that discover the rules only after a cancellation notice has already arrived.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a business own the land outright once it is allotted an industrial plot in Noida or Greater Noida?

No. The Authority grants a long leasehold interest, typically reported at ninety years from execution of the lease deed, not freehold ownership. The lease deed and the scheme document under which the plot was allotted continue to govern what the lessee can do with the plot for the life of that lease.

Can a business mortgage a NOIDA, GNIDA or YEIDA plot to raise a loan?

Yes, but only after obtaining the Authority's prior permission to mortgage, for a fee that varies by scheme and Authority. A lender's charge remains subject to the Authority's own right to cancel the lease for the lessee's default on payment or construction obligations, so continuing compliance with the lease matters as much to the lender as to the borrower.

Can a business sublet an industrial plot it has been allotted?

Generally not before it has built and commissioned a functional unit; subletting the bare plot is restricted or barred under most scheme terms. Once a unit is operational, letting out built-up space within the completed building is treated more liberally, subject to the specific scheme's terms, and in some cases a tripartite sub-lease arrangement involving the Authority may be used.

What does a buyer need to check before taking a transfer of an existing industrial plot lease?

The transferor's full payment history and any outstanding dues, since liabilities attaching to the unit pass to the transferee once the transfer deed is executed; confirmation that the unit is genuinely functional, since transfers are usually conditioned on the existing allottee being a bona fide, non-defaulting lessee; and the transfer charge, calculated on the premium, that the specific scheme requires.

What happens if a business misses its construction deadline because the Authority itself delayed handing over infrastructure?

It may be able to claim relief under the Authority's own Zero Period Policy, which effectively pauses the construction clock for the period the Authority was itself in default. The Supreme Court's 2026 ruling in New Okhla Industrial Development Authority v. Sunshine Trade Tower upheld such relief where a sanctioned access road had never been made available, rejecting the Authority's argument that the policy should apply only where there was no access at all.

Can a cancelled lease be restored?

Sometimes, but the window to apply is usually short, reported in at least one case as ninety days from the date of cancellation, after which a restoration request risks being rejected as out of time. Some Authorities have also restored plots where the allottee could show meaningful construction progress before cancellation, but there is no single consolidated restoration policy confirmed across all three Authorities.

Should a lessee file a civil suit or a writ petition against a cancellation or a refusal to permit a transfer?

In the great majority of reported disputes, a writ petition to the Allahabad High Court under Article 226 has been the route used, since these Authorities are treated as State instrumentalities whose allotment and cancellation decisions are reviewable as administrative action. A civil suit tends to be appropriate only where the real grievance is a straightforward contractual claim that does not require reviewing the Authority's own decision.

Does a lender lose its security if the Authority cancels the underlying lease?

It can. Courts have treated a lender's mortgage and any attachment under it as distinct from the Authority's own power to cancel the lease deed for the lessee's default, and at least one reported insolvency matter excluded an already-cancelled leasehold interest from the assets available to a corporate debtor's resolution plan. Lenders financing against these plots should treat the lessee's ongoing compliance with the lease as part of their own risk monitoring, not just the borrower's problem.
