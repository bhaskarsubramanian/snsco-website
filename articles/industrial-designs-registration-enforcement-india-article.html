Registering and Enforcing Industrial Designs in India: A Guide for Foreign Brand Owners

Design registration is the cheapest, fastest intellectual property right available in India, and the one foreign companies entering the market most often skip, right up until a local manufacturer starts selling a near-identical product.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 17 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company bringing a product into India, whether a shoe, a packaging format, a consumer appliance or a user interface, tends to reach first for a trademark filing and treat the product's actual appearance as an afterthought. The Designs Act, 2000 protects exactly that appearance, registers faster and costs less than either a patent or a trademark, and lasts up to fifteen years. It also interacts with trademark law in ways that have produced some of the most litigated intellectual property disputes in India over the last decade, including one still running through the courts against a well-known footwear brand.

Key points

  • A registered design protects only how a product looks, through its shape, pattern, configuration or ornamentation, never how it works
  • Novelty is assessed on a worldwide basis, so a design already published or sold anywhere, including by the applicant's own earlier launch, can defeat an Indian application
  • A foreign applicant can claim priority from a home-country filing made within the preceding six months under the Paris Convention route
  • Protection runs for ten years and can be extended once by five more, for a maximum of fifteen years, with no further renewal available after that
  • Enforcement is entirely civil; there is no criminal sanction for design piracy, unlike trademark counterfeiting or copyright infringement
  • A registered design can be cancelled if its owner starts using it as a trademark, because the Act's own definition of design excludes anything that is a trademark
  • Since a 2018 Delhi High Court ruling, a design-infringement claim and a passing-off claim for the same product can be brought in one composite suit rather than two separate proceedings
  • DPIIT has proposed, but not yet enacted, amendments that would let India join the Hague Agreement for a single international design filing and raise statutory damages to Rs 50 lakh

The Right Foreign Companies Keep Treating as an Afterthought

Of the three registered intellectual property rights available in India, a patent, a trademark and a design, the design registration is consistently the one foreign companies file latest, or skip entirely, when they bring a new product into the Indian market. That is the reverse of what the economics suggest. A design application is examined and typically registered within months, costs a fraction of a patent filing, and gives the owner an exclusive right over the product's visual appearance for up to fifteen years without the applicant ever having to prove an inventive step or defend a specification against an examiner's objections the way a patent applicant must.

The gap shows up most clearly once a local manufacturer starts selling a close copy of a successful foreign product. At that point the foreign company usually finds it has a trademark on its brand name and logo, which does nothing to stop someone else from making a product that looks identical but carries a different name, and it has no design registration either, because nobody filed one when the product first launched in India. What follows is an attempt to improvise a remedy out of passing-off law, built on proving that the product's shape itself has become distinctive of the brand in the mind of the Indian consumer, which is a considerably harder and slower case to win than simply having registered the design in the first place.

The most visible illustration of exactly this gap, and of how long it takes to resolve once missed, is the ongoing litigation between Crocs Inc. and several Indian footwear manufacturers, discussed later in this article, which has run for more than a decade through the Delhi High Court and the Supreme Court over the same underlying question: what protection survives for a product's shape once reliance on design registration alone turns out to be not enough.

This matters more for a foreign entrant than for a domestic manufacturer launching in its home market, because the foreign company is typically bringing a product that has already been copied, or is already being watched for copying, in other jurisdictions before it ever reaches India. A product successful enough abroad to justify an Indian launch is also, by the same token, successful enough to attract an Indian manufacturer's attention once it appears, which makes the usual advice to "wait and see how the market responds before spending on registration" considerably riskier in India than it might be for a brand with no track record anywhere else.

What a Design Registration Actually Protects

Section 2(d) of the Designs Act, 2000 defines a design narrowly and deliberately. It covers only the features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament or composition of lines or colours applied to an article, whether in two dimensions, three dimensions or both, and judged solely by the eye. It does not protect how the article works, what it is made of, or any feature dictated purely by the function the article has to perform. A kettle's silhouette can be a design; the mechanism that makes it boil water cannot be, and has to look elsewhere in intellectual property law, typically to a patent, for protection.

Registration requires the design to be new and original, and India assesses that requirement on a worldwide basis, not merely against what has already been seen or sold in India. A design already published, used or sold anywhere in the world before the Indian filing date defeats novelty, which includes, awkwardly for an unwary applicant, a foreign company's own earlier public launch of the same product in another country if that happened too long before the Indian filing. The Patent Office's own practice looks at the article as a whole for novelty, rather than requiring the applicant to point to one specific new feature.

Once registered, applications are classified under the Locarno system used internationally for industrial designs, following the Designs Rules, 2001 as amended. A registration lasts ten years from the date of registration under Section 11, and can be extended once, by a further five years, on an application made before the first ten years run out, for a combined maximum of fifteen years. There is no mechanism to extend beyond fifteen years. A registration that is allowed to lapse can, within a limited window, still be restored under Section 12, but that restoration route is not a substitute for simply renewing on time.

There is no substantive pre-grant opposition in the Indian design system, which makes the examination stage comparatively quick, and which puts the real contest, where one actually occurs, after the right is already granted. The Design Office does run both a formal and a substantive novelty check against its own database of earlier Indian applications, registrations and publications before granting a registration, but it has no practical way to search every product launched anywhere in the world, which is why the global novelty standard described above is enforced mainly through post-grant cancellation rather than at the examination stage itself. A foreign applicant should not read a smooth, unopposed registration as proof the design would survive a determined challenge later; it mainly reflects the limits of what the examiner could find in the time available, not a final word on worldwide novelty.

Filing From Abroad: Claiming Priority Under the Paris Convention

A foreign company does not have to file its first design application in India to preserve its rights here. Section 44 of the Act gives an applicant who has already filed in a Paris Convention country, which includes virtually every country with which an Indian-facing business is likely to deal, a window of six months from that first foreign filing date within which to file the corresponding Indian application and claim the earlier date as the priority date for novelty purposes. Filed within that window as a Reciprocity Application under the Rules, the Indian filing is treated, for novelty, as though it had been made on the original foreign filing date rather than the later Indian one.

Missing that six-month window is not a minor administrative lapse. Once it closes, the foreign company's own earlier filing and any publication that followed from it become prior art against its own later Indian application, which can defeat the very registration the company is trying to obtain. A foreign brand owner planning an India launch should treat the design filing calendar as running from the date of the first filing anywhere in the world, not from the date the product actually reaches Indian shelves, and should have the certified priority documentation, with an English translation where needed, ready well before the Indian filing is due.

A foreign applicant with no place of business in India also has to act through an agent for the filing itself. Section 43 of the Act requires an applicant who does not reside or carry on business in India to appoint an agent located in India for the purpose of the application, which in practice means a registered patent and design agent or an advocate handles the actual filing, the Patent Office's correspondence, and any objections the examiner raises, on the foreign company's instructions. Choosing that agent early, before the six-month clock is close to running out, avoids the scramble that follows when a foreign company only starts looking for Indian counsel once the priority deadline is already days away.

The Design-Trademark Overlap: Why a Registration Can Be Cancelled for the Wrong Kind of Success

The Designs Act's own definition in Section 2(d) excludes from "design" anything that is, in substance, a trademark. The Trade Marks Act has no corresponding exclusion running the other way, which creates a one-directional trap: a product shape can start out as a pure design, used only to make the product look distinctive, and over time, as the shape itself comes to signal the brand to consumers, shift into functioning as an unregistered trademark as well. Once that shift happens, Indian courts have held that the design registration itself becomes vulnerable to cancellation, because what is actually being used is a trademark masquerading as a design, which the Act's own definition does not permit.

The Delhi High Court worked through this problem across a sequence of rulings culminating in Carlsberg Breweries A/S v. Som Distilleries & Breweries Ltd., decided by a five-judge special bench on 14 December 2018. The earlier position, set by a three-judge full bench in Mohan Lal v. Sona Paint & Hardwares in 2013, had held that a design-infringement claim and a passing-off claim for the same product could not be combined in one suit, because the two causes of action rested on inconsistent foundations, design law protecting pure appearance and passing-off law protecting a trademark-like reputation in that same appearance. The special bench in Carlsberg Breweries overruled that position, holding that where the two claims share a common question of law and fact, they can and should be tried together in a single composite suit under the ordinary civil procedure rules on joinder of causes of action.

What the Mohan Lal line did leave standing, and what the later Carlsberg Breweries bench did not disturb, is the underlying substantive point: a passing-off claim cannot simply restate the registered design as though it were a trademark. Only features beyond the registered design itself, shown to have independently acquired goodwill as a badge of origin, such as a distinctive strap pattern, a particular colourway, or packaging get-up layered on top of the basic shape, can support a trademark-style claim. The registered design gets you the design right; a separate trademark case still has to be built and proved on its own evidence.

The Crocs Litigation: What Happens When the Strategy Gets Tested in Practice

Crocs Inc.'s long-running dispute with Bata India, Liberty Shoes and Relaxo over its clog-style footwear shows exactly how this plays out when a foreign brand owner relies on both design and passing-off claims at once. A single judge of the Delhi High Court dismissed Crocs' passing-off suits at a preliminary stage on 18 February 2019, reasoning that where the product's appearance was already the subject of a registered design, a separate passing-off claim over the same appearance was not maintainable, relying on the Mohan Lal and Carlsberg Breweries line of authority.

A Division Bench of the same Court reversed that dismissal in M/S Crocs Inc. USA v. M/S Bata India & Ors., decided on 1 July 2025, holding that neither Mohan Lal nor Carlsberg Breweries actually excluded a passing-off claim merely because a design-infringement claim was also pleaded, and that a plaintiff did not have to point to something entirely separate from the subject matter of its design claim before a passing-off case could proceed. The suits were restored for trial. Bata India and Liberty Shoes then challenged that revival before the Supreme Court, which dismissed their petitions, leaving the passing-off claims to be tried on the merits in the Delhi High Court, with the Court's own order making clear it had done no more than restore the suits for the trial court's consideration.

Two features of this seven-year run of litigation are worth drawing out for any foreign brand owner weighing the same strategy. First, having both a registered design and an independent trade-dress case gave Crocs a second route to relief once the design-based claim ran into difficulty, rather than leaving the company with nothing. Second, none of this resolved quickly: a decade of proceedings, across three levels of the court system, has so far produced a right to proceed to trial, not a final finding of infringement either way, which is the realistic timeline a foreign company should expect from contested design and trade-dress litigation in India rather than the faster outcome a registered right might suggest on paper.

Enforcing a Registered Design: Civil Remedies Only

Design piracy under Section 22 of the Act is a purely civil wrong. Unlike trademark counterfeiting or copyright infringement, which can attract criminal prosecution in appropriate cases, there is no criminal sanction anywhere in the Designs Act, 2000, which means a foreign company cannot ask the police to raid a factory copying its registered design the way it might for counterfeit branded goods; the only recourse is a civil suit.

Section 22(2) gives the registered proprietor two alternative routes once piracy is established. The first is a statutory, no-proof-of-damage route: a sum, commonly described as up to Rs 25,000 recoverable as a contract debt for each contravention, subject to an overall ceiling per design, which is quick to claim but modest in amount and rarely reflects the actual commercial harm of a sustained copying campaign. The second is an ordinary suit for damages and an injunction, uncapped in amount but requiring the proprietor to prove its actual loss, or an equivalent account of the infringer's profits, in the usual way. Neither route may be pursued in a court below the level of a District Judge, which in practice means these cases are filed in a District Court's commercial division or in a High Court exercising original civil jurisdiction, depending on the city and the value of the claim.

In practice, the remedy that actually stops the copying before a trial concludes years later is an interim injunction, sought under Order 39 of the Code of Civil Procedure at the same time the suit is filed. A court granting one weighs the usual three factors, a prima facie case on the registration's validity and infringement, the balance of convenience between letting the copy continue to sell and shutting down a competitor's product before trial, and whether the proprietor would suffer irreparable harm without interim relief. Acting quickly matters more in design cases than it might elsewhere, because a proprietor who sits on obvious infringement for months or years before suing risks a delay or acquiescence defence that can itself defeat interim relief, regardless of how strong the underlying registration is.

Defending a Registration: Cancellation Under Section 19

India's design system has no pre-grant opposition procedure. A competitor cannot challenge an application before it is registered the way it could oppose a trademark application; the only route to challenge a registered design is a post-grant cancellation petition before the Controller under Section 19, which any interested person can file at any time after registration, with no limitation period of its own.

The grounds are five, and all five turn on facts that existed before or at registration rather than on anything that happened afterward: the design was already registered in India before the later registration; it had already been published, in India or abroad, before the registration date; it was not new or original; it is not registrable under the Act at all; or it does not meet the Section 2(d) definition of a design in the first place, which includes the trademark exclusion discussed earlier. Appeal from the Controller's decision on a cancellation petition lies to the High Court.

The same five grounds double as a complete defence to an infringement suit under Section 22. If a defendant raises one of them in defence, Section 22(4) requires the suit itself to be transferred to the High Court having jurisdiction, since a District Court is not the forum for deciding the validity of the registration itself. For a foreign proprietor, the practical lesson is to keep dated, verifiable records of the design's own development and its first publication anywhere in the world, since it is exactly this evidence that will need to be produced, potentially years after filing, if a defendant in an infringement suit challenges the registration's validity on novelty or prior-publication grounds rather than simply denying infringement.

The Gap That Catches Software and Device Makers: Screen Displays and Icons

A product's physical shape is not the only appearance a modern consumer brand wants to protect. A distinctive app icon, a dashboard layout, an on-screen interface animation or a smart device's display screen can be just as central to a brand's identity as the housing it sits inside, and the current Act was not written with any of this clearly in mind. The Patent Office has, in limited circumstances, accepted applications covering a screen display or icon as part of an article having a physical form, such as a mobile handset or a dashboard panel shown with the screen as part of it, but the position is inconsistent, decided case by case rather than under any clear statutory rule, and offers nothing close to the certainty a foreign software or device company gets when registering the same interface design in jurisdictions that explicitly recognise graphical user interfaces as registrable subject matter.

A foreign company whose product value sits substantially in its screen-based interface, rather than its housing, should not assume Indian design law currently gives it the same protection it may have secured elsewhere. Until the law changes, the safer course is to register whatever physical article the interface actually appears on, with the screen display included as part of that article's own appearance, rather than attempting to register the interface as a stand-alone subject matter the current Act does not clearly recognise.

What Is Changing: The Proposed 2026 Amendments and Hague Accession

On 23 January 2026 the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade issued a concept note proposing a substantial overhaul of the Designs Act, 2000, and invited public comments until 22 February 2026. As of today, these remain proposals under consultation; no bill implementing them has been introduced in or passed by Parliament, and the Act described in the rest of this article is the one that continues to govern every application, registration and suit in the meantime.

The headline proposal is India's accession to the Hague Agreement, the international treaty that lets a single application, filed through WIPO, extend protection into multiple member countries at once. India is not currently a member, which is why a foreign brand owner protecting a product shape across several markets today has to file a separate national application in India in addition to wherever else it files. The concept note proposes a dedicated new chapter to implement Hague filings, along with a revised term structure commonly described as five years plus five years plus five years, intended to track Article 17 of the Hague Agreement, and an option for deferred publication of up to thirty months, which would let an applicant keep a new design confidential for longer before it becomes public.

Alongside Hague accession, the note also proposes India's accession to the Riyadh Design Law Treaty, explicit coverage for virtual and graphical user interface designs, which the current Act does not clearly address, a twelve-month grace period that would let an applicant's own prior disclosure within that window no longer defeat its own later application, provision for divisional applications, and a sharp increase in statutory damages for piracy, reported at up to Rs 50 lakh against the current Rs 25,000 figure. A related proposal would also revisit the overlap between design protection and Section 15 of the Copyright Act, which currently withdraws copyright protection from an article once it has been reproduced more than fifty times by an industrial process, a provision that interacts awkwardly with the design regime in its own right. None of this is law yet, and a foreign brand owner should plan its current filings on the Act as it stands, while watching this consultation for whether and when it actually reaches the statute book.

A Practical Filing and Enforcement Strategy for a Foreign Brand Owner

Four steps reduce most of the risk this article describes. File the Indian design application within six months of the first filing anywhere else in the world, under the Section 44 reciprocity route, rather than waiting until the product itself reaches India; the six-month clock runs from the first filing date regardless of where the product is actually sold. Register separately for each distinct product variant that the company intends to sell or license in India, since a single registration covers the specific article and configuration actually shown in the application, not every later variation of it. Build and document a trademark or trade-dress case for any feature that goes beyond the registered design itself, such as a signature colour combination, surface pattern or packaging layout, from the earliest point that feature starts being used, so that evidence of its independent reputation exists well before any dispute requires it. And budget enforcement around the civil-only, District-Judge-or-above forum structure Section 22 actually provides, rather than assuming a criminal remedy exists the way it does for trademark counterfeiting.

A fifth step is worth treating separately because it is the one most often overlooked by a group company managing filings across several jurisdictions through one central trademark docket: keep the India design file distinct from the group's trademark strategy, with its own renewal calendar, rather than folding it into whatever system tracks trademark renewals. A design registration's ten-year initial term and single five-year extension run on a different clock from a trademark's indefinite, repeatedly renewable life, and a design allowed to lapse by mistake, because a renewals team assumed it followed the same renewal rhythm as the brand's trademarks, cannot be brought back after the fifteen-year ceiling regardless of how valuable the product still is.

None of these steps is expensive or slow relative to the alternative of discovering the gap only after a competing product has already reached the Indian market. The design registration itself, filed on time and renewed before the fifteen-year ceiling, remains the cheapest exclusive right available under Indian intellectual property law, and the one most easily lost to a missed deadline rather than to any substantive weakness in the underlying product.

A registered design gives a foreign brand owner a fast, inexpensive and genuinely exclusive right over how a product looks in India, for up to fifteen years, with none of the examination burden a patent carries. What the Crocs and Carlsberg Breweries litigation shows is that the right works best as part of a considered strategy rather than as a stand-alone filing made and forgotten: a design registration can itself be cancelled if it strays into functioning as a trademark, and a company that waits until a copy appears before thinking about trademark or trade-dress protection for the same product's distinctive features finds itself litigating a much harder case than the one it could have built from the start.

The proposed 2026 reforms, Hague accession above all, would eventually make a foreign company's life simpler by letting one international filing cover India alongside other markets. Until that happens, filing promptly within the six-month priority window, registering each product variant separately, and keeping the trademark and design strategies distinct but complementary remains the more reliable path than waiting for the law to make things easier. A foreign brand entering India today should plan around the Act as it stands now, treat the consultation on Hague accession as a reason to watch rather than to delay, and accept that the fifteen-year ceiling on design protection means the registration decision, once made correctly at the outset, rarely needs revisiting until the product itself is retired from the Indian market.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a foreign company register an industrial design in India without having manufactured or sold the product here yet?

Yes. Registration does not require the article to have been made, used or sold in India. What matters is that the design itself is new and original on a worldwide basis as of the Indian filing date, and that the applicant files within the Section 44 priority window if relying on an earlier foreign filing.

How long does design protection last in India?

Ten years from the date of registration under Section 11, extendable once by a further five years on an application made before the first ten years expire, for a maximum of fifteen years. There is no further extension after that, though a lapsed registration can sometimes be restored within a limited window under Section 12.

Can a registered design be cancelled after it has already been granted?

Yes, at any time, through a cancellation petition before the Controller under Section 19, on one of five grounds: prior Indian registration, prior publication anywhere, lack of novelty or originality, non-registrability, or the subject matter not meeting the Section 2(d) definition of a design, which includes designs that have in substance become trademarks.

Is copying a registered design a criminal offence in India?

No. Design piracy under Section 22 is a civil wrong only. There is no criminal sanction under the Designs Act, 2000, unlike trademark counterfeiting or copyright infringement, which can attract criminal prosecution in appropriate cases.

Can a company sue for both design infringement and passing off in the same case?

Yes, since the Delhi High Court's special bench ruling in Carlsberg Breweries v. Som Distilleries in December 2018, which overruled the earlier Mohan Lal v. Sona Paint position and allowed both claims to be combined in one composite suit where they share common questions of law and fact.

Will India join the Hague Agreement for international design registration?

It has been proposed, not yet enacted. The Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade issued a concept note in January 2026 proposing India's accession to the Hague Agreement and the Riyadh Design Law Treaty, among other reforms, but as of now no bill implementing these proposals has been introduced or passed, and a foreign applicant must still file a separate Indian application rather than relying on a single international registration.

What can a design owner actually recover if someone copies the design?

Either a statutory sum, commonly up to Rs 25,000 per contravention subject to an overall ceiling, recoverable as a contract debt without proving actual loss, or an ordinary suit for damages and an injunction with no statutory cap, which requires proof of the actual harm suffered. Neither route can be filed below the level of a District Judge's court.

Does owning a trademark on a product's brand name also protect the product's shape?

No, not on its own. A trademark registered only for a name or logo does not stop a competitor from copying the product's physical appearance under a different name. Protecting the shape itself requires a separate design registration, and if that shape has independently become a signal of the brand to consumers, a further trademark or passing-off case built on evidence of that separate reputation.
