Structuring an HNI Family Office: Private Trust, LLP or a GIFT City Family Investment Fund

Comparing the onshore routes against the IFSCA Family Investment Fund in GIFT City, as on 22 November 2026

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An Indian family with wealth spread across property, listed shares, a family business and investments abroad eventually asks the same question every such family asks: should the holding structure be a private trust, an LLP, or something newer built for this purpose in GIFT City. Each answer carries a different registration step, a different tax position and a different degree of control the family keeps over its own money.

Key points

  • A private family trust remains the default onshore vehicle for succession planning and asset protection, governed by the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, with no separate regulator and no minimum corpus, but it does not by itself offer the pooled-investment tax treatment a fund structure can.
  • An LLP or a family holding company gives operational flexibility and limited liability for running an active family business, but neither is a ready-made investment vehicle for a diversified securities portfolio the way a trust or a fund is.
  • The IFSCA (Fund Management) Regulations, 2025, notified on 19 February 2025, replaced the 2022 regulations and carried forward the Family Investment Fund category, a self-managed, IFSCA-registered pooling vehicle open only to a single family.
  • A Family Investment Fund does not need a separate, independently licensed fund manager the way an ordinary Alternative Investment Fund does; the family's own entity registers directly with IFSCA as an Authorised FME for its own Family Investment Fund.
  • A Family Investment Fund must reach and then maintain a minimum corpus of USD 10 million within three years of its registration certificate, under the 2025 regulations, a threshold meant to keep the category genuinely reserved for large family pools rather than ordinary retail money.
  • The definition of 'single family' for this purpose has been widened to include a family's holding entities, such as a company, LLP, partnership or trust, where family members hold a significant economic interest, with a mandated risk disclosure for any non-family holder of up to 10 percent economic interest.
  • GIFT City's income-tax concessions for IFSC funds, long described under Sections 10(4D) and 10(4E) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, now sit in the renumbered provisions of the Income-tax Act, 2025, and whether a given family's income actually qualifies depends on conditions, including non-resident unit holding, that a family should verify for its own fact pattern rather than assume.

The question every growing family wealth eventually asks

A family that has built wealth across two or three generations typically ends up holding it in a mix of forms that grew organically rather than by design: property bought as opportunities arose, shares accumulated through the family business and personal investment, an insurance portfolio, and increasingly, investments routed abroad by children who studied or settled overseas. At some point, usually prompted by a succession conversation or an advisor's suggestion, the family asks whether all of this should sit inside a single, deliberately chosen structure instead.

The honest answer is that no single structure does everything. A trust is built for succession and asset protection, not for running an actively traded investment book. A company or an LLP is built for running a business, not for the tax-neutral pooling of family money the way a fund is designed to. The newer GIFT City Family Investment Fund route is built specifically to let a family manage its own pooled investments under a lighter version of India's fund management regulation, in a jurisdiction with its own tax concessions, but it brings a minimum scale requirement most families do not meet until their wealth has grown well past the point of needing a trust for succession in any event.

What follows sets out each route on its own terms, and where they can be combined rather than treated as exclusive alternatives, since most large Indian family structures in practice use more than one of them side by side. The sequence moves from the oldest and most familiar route, the private trust, through the company and LLP forms most businesses already have, to the two fund-based options, the mainland AIF and the GIFT City Family Investment Fund, before setting out a practical way to decide among them for a specific family's actual mix of assets.

The private family trust: still the default for succession

A private trust under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 lets a settlor transfer assets to trustees to hold for named beneficiaries, on terms the settlor fixes in the trust deed. There is no minimum corpus, no separate regulator approving the structure, and no requirement that the assets be financial instruments at all; a trust can hold property, shares in the family company, insurance policies or anything else the settlor chooses to settle into it.

What a trust is particularly good at is exactly what a fund is not built for: keeping a family's succession plan out of the delay and publicity of probate, ring-fencing assets from an individual beneficiary's personal creditors or a failed marriage, and letting the settlor set conditions, such as an age at which a beneficiary becomes entitled to distributions, that a will's probate process does not easily replicate. These are estate-planning functions, not investment-management functions.

Where a trust is used to actually hold and trade a securities portfolio, the trustees face the ordinary tax and regulatory consequences of being the registered holder of those securities in their own right, without the specific concessions that a registered fund vehicle in GIFT City or under the SEBI Alternative Investment Fund regime can access. A trust used purely as a holding and succession vehicle, with investment management delegated to a separately structured fund or discretionary portfolio manager, usually gets the best of both features.

An LLP or family holding company: good for the business, limited for a portfolio

Many family groups already run their operating business through a private limited company, and some use a Limited Liability Partnership as a holding layer above it, because an LLP's internal arrangements are far more flexible than a company's, governed by the partners' own LLP agreement rather than the Companies Act's more prescriptive rules on board composition and shareholder rights.

Both vehicles work well for holding operating businesses, real estate and strategic stakes the family intends to keep for the long term, and both offer limited liability that a simple partnership does not. Neither, however, gives a tax-efficient pass-through for a portfolio of listed securities and mutual fund units bought and sold frequently, since gains inside a company are taxed at the company level before any further tax on distribution, and an LLP's own tax treatment, while a pass-through for many purposes, still was not built around the fund-specific exemptions available to a registered AIF or an IFSC fund vehicle.

Families sometimes default to a holding company for a securities portfolio simply because one already exists for the operating business, without working out whether a separate trust or fund structure would actually be more tax-efficient for that specific pool of money. The two decisions, how to hold the operating business and how to hold the liquid investment portfolio, are worth separating rather than defaulting to the same vehicle for both.

The AIF route: India's general-purpose pooled investment vehicle

Before going further offshore, in jurisdictional terms, to GIFT City, most Indian families already have access to a mainland route for pooling their own investments under a single, tax-efficient vehicle: the SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012. An AIF registered in Category I or II gets statutory pass-through taxation under Section 115UB of the Income Tax Act, with the fund's income taxed in the hands of investors rather than the fund itself, at a minimum commitment typically set at Rs 1 crore per investor.

A family can, and often does, set up its own single-family AIF on the mainland for exactly this purpose, with a licensed manager running the fund on the family's behalf, or with the family's own entity seeking the manager registration. This route carries its own compliance burden, including SEBI's manager eligibility and net worth requirements, and it operates in rupees under ordinary Indian tax and foreign exchange law for any investment the AIF itself makes outside India.

The Family Investment Fund in GIFT City is best understood as a parallel, not a replacement, route: it is built for a family that specifically wants an IFSC-based, foreign-currency-denominated structure, whether because it has NRI or foreign family members, wants exposure structured for offshore assets, or wants the lighter self-managed regime IFSCA offers that a mainland AIF manager registration does not.

The IFSCA Family Investment Fund: what actually changed in 2025

IFSCA, the single regulator for GIFT City's financial services, notified the IFSCA (Fund Management) Regulations, 2025 on 19 February 2025, replacing the 2022 regulations that had first introduced the Family Investment Fund category and consolidating several circulars issued under the earlier regime directly into the text of the new regulations.

A Family Investment Fund, under both the earlier and the current regime, is a self-managed fund pooling money only from a single family, through one or more investment vehicles. The 2025 regulations widened the definition to recognise that a 'single family' structure can itself sit across more than one legal entity, provided family members retain a significant economic interest across those entities, rather than requiring every family member to invest directly and individually into the fund.

The defining feature that distinguishes a Family Investment Fund from every other fund category IFSCA regulates is that it does not need a separate, independently capitalised and licensed Fund Management Entity. The family's own entity applies to IFSCA and is registered as an Authorised FME specifically for managing its own Family Investment Fund, with the usual FME legal-form and net worth conditions switched off for this category, because the fund is not being sold or managed for third parties.

The minimum corpus and who counts as the single family

A Family Investment Fund has to reach a minimum corpus of USD 10 million within three years of the date its certificate of registration is granted, and the regulations require that level to be maintained after it is reached, not merely touched once and allowed to fall. This is the single biggest practical filter on the category: a family whose investable wealth does not comfortably clear that threshold, after accounting for the assets it intends to keep outside the fund, is better served by a mainland AIF or a straightforward trust-and-portfolio-manager arrangement.

The 'single family' definition that IFSCA applies, broadened by a 2023 circular and carried into the 2025 regulations, extends beyond individuals who share blood or marriage to include a sole proprietorship, partnership firm, company, LLP, trust or other body corporate through which family members hold their collective economic interest, so long as that interest is genuinely held by the family rather than by unrelated co-investors using the family as a nominal front.

Where a small number of non-family persons hold an economic interest in one of these holding entities, up to 10 percent, IFSCA's framework does not disqualify the structure outright but requires those non-family holders to be given a specific risk disclosure about investing alongside, and effectively inside, a family-controlled vehicle that is not managed by an independent, arm's-length manager.

IFSCA has reported registering the first foreign family office as a Family Investment Fund under the 2025 regulations, which signals that the category is being used by families outside India too, and is not limited to Indian-resident families looking for an IFSC-based structure for their offshore wealth.

Tax treatment: genuinely favourable, but conditional and still moving

GIFT City's tax appeal for funds has rested for several years on exemptions for a Category III IFSC AIF's unit holders, long found in Sections 10(4D) and 10(4E) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, covering income accruing to non-resident unit holders and gains on transfer of units, subject to conditions including that units other than those held by the sponsor or manager are held by non-residents.

The Income-tax Act, 2025 has renumbered large parts of the 1961 Act's scheme, and GIFT City's fund-related exemptions now sit under different section numbers in the new Act. A family evaluating a Family Investment Fund today should have its own tax advisor confirm the current section numbers and the exact conditions attached to them, rather than relying on the 1961 Act citations that still circulate in a great deal of secondary commentary written before the renumbering took effect.

Even where the exemption applies cleanly, it answers only the Indian tax question. A tax exemption inside India does not mean the income is tax-free everywhere; an NRI family member's home country of residence, or the country where the fund itself books its activity for regulatory purposes, may still tax the same income under its own law, and a family with members spread across several tax jurisdictions needs each of those positions checked separately before assuming GIFT City's concession is the end of the analysis.

GIFT City is regularly compared, in this respect, to the longer-established family office hubs in Singapore and Mauritius, and the comparison is useful rather than settled in anyone's favour. Singapore's family office schemes carry their own minimum asset and local-spending conditions, and Mauritius has its own treaty-based advantages for holding African and Asian investments. GIFT City's pitch is that a family keeps its structure onshore, in Indian rupee and foreign currency accounts within the same jurisdiction as the rest of its wealth, under a regulator that speaks directly to India's own tax authorities, rather than adding a further layer of cross-border compliance that an entirely offshore hub would bring.

Registration mechanics: what the process actually involves

An application for Family Investment Fund registration is filed with IFSCA through its Single Window IT System, the common portal IFSCA uses for most registrations and approvals in GIFT City, rather than through any separate paper process. IFSCA reviews the application for compliance with the Fund Management Regulations and grants a certificate of registration on satisfaction of the conditions, after which the three-year clock to reach the USD 10 million minimum corpus begins to run.

The family's chosen GIFT City entity, typically a company or an LLP incorporated or registered in the IFSC, is the applicant and becomes the Authorised FME for its own fund; the fund itself is then structured as one or more schemes under that FME, which is the layer IFSCA actually licenses and supervises. A bank account and custody arrangements within the IFSC's banking and depository ecosystem follow registration, since the fund is expected to operate through GIFT City's own financial infrastructure rather than through ordinary mainland banking channels for its fund-level transactions.

Families should treat the application as a genuine regulatory filing, not a formality: IFSCA has shown, through periodic amendments to the Fund Management Regulations, including changes tightening aspects of the fund management regime during 2025 and 2026, that it continues to actively supervise and adjust this framework, and an application that treats the family-office concessions as a loophole rather than a purpose-built, conditions-attached category risks closer scrutiny than one that engages with the conditions directly.

The timeline from a family's first engagement with its advisors to an actual certificate of registration typically runs to several months, covering the incorporation or registration of the GIFT City entity itself, drafting the scheme documents and the FME application, and IFSCA's own review period, before the three-year corpus clock even starts running. Families planning a transition from an existing mainland structure, such as moving a portfolio currently held through a trust or a company into the new fund, should sequence the tax and FEMA consequences of that transfer carefully, since moving assets into the fund is itself a transaction with its own tax and exchange control footprint, separate from the fund's own ongoing tax treatment once it is operating.

FEMA and residency considerations for the family's Indian members

GIFT City sits, for most regulatory purposes, outside the ordinary domestic tariff area and is treated as a foreign jurisdiction for several exchange control purposes, which is precisely why it can offer IFSC-specific concessions in the first place. An Indian-resident family member investing into a Family Investment Fund does so under the Reserve Bank of India's Liberalised Remittance Scheme or another applicable outward investment route, subject to whatever annual limit and reporting that route currently carries, rather than under some separate GIFT-City-specific exemption from India's ordinary capital account rules.

An NRI or OCI family member, by contrast, generally invests through the fund without the LRS limit applying, since that limit is specific to resident individuals, though the family should still confirm the exact FEMA characterisation of the investment with its advisors, since the position can differ depending on whether the investing entity is the individual directly or one of the family's holding vehicles.

A family that includes both resident and non-resident members investing into the same Family Investment Fund should expect to track each member's contribution against the FEMA route applicable to that member individually, rather than treating the fund as a single undifferentiated pool for exchange control purposes; the fund's own compliance team, not the family informally, should own this tracking once the structure is operational.

Governance and ongoing compliance once the fund is running

Registration is the start of an ongoing relationship with IFSCA, not a one-time event. An Authorised FME managing a Family Investment Fund has to maintain prescribed books and records, file periodic reports on the fund's activities and corpus, and respond to IFSCA's supervisory queries the same way any other regulated entity in GIFT City does, even though the fund serves only the family that owns it.

The family typically appoints its own directors or designated partners to run the FME entity, supported by a compliance officer who may be a family member with the right qualifications or an external professional engaged for the role, and the fund's scheme documents, whether structured as a single scheme or as separate schemes for different asset classes or family branches, need to be kept current as the family's investment strategy evolves.

A custodian and, depending on the assets held, a GIFT City-based banking unit complete the operational picture. IFSCA requires fund assets above a prescribed threshold to be held with an independent custodian rather than directly by the FME, a safeguard that exists even for a self-managed family vehicle precisely because the family members who are not actively running the fund are still entitled to independent assurance that the assets are where the fund's records say they are.

What happens to a family member's interest on death or exit

Pooling the family's money does not erase individual ownership within the fund: each contributing member or branch typically holds a distinct, identifiable interest in the fund's schemes, recorded the way units or a capital account would be in any pooled vehicle. When a contributing family member dies, that interest passes under the same succession law that would govern any other asset in the estate, a will, intestate succession, or a registered family settlement, and the fund's own records then need to be updated to reflect the new holder.

This is a different problem from the trademark and patent transmission questions that arise for family businesses generally, discussed at length elsewhere on this site, but it rhymes with them: a fund interest does not automatically re-register itself in an heir's name, and the family's own FME, as the entity administering the scheme, should have a documented process for accepting probate, letters of administration or a succession certificate and updating its unit or capital account records accordingly, ideally specified in the scheme documents from the outset rather than worked out for the first time when a family member actually dies.

Families setting up a Family Investment Fund specifically to hold assets for the next generation should also decide, at the structuring stage, whether a deceased member's interest is meant to pass to that member's own heirs directly, or to be bought out by the remaining family members at a valuation fixed in the scheme documents, since the two approaches lead to very different outcomes if a contributing branch of the family later wants to exit the structure altogether, whether on death or otherwise.

Choosing between the routes: a practical framework

A family whose primary concern is succession, keeping a family business or a property portfolio out of probate, and controlling how and when the next generation receives it, should start with a private trust, whether or not it also eventually sets up a fund structure for the liquid investment portion of its wealth.

Running an active operating business is best kept inside a company or LLP chosen for its operational and governance needs, separately from the family's personal investment portfolio, rather than mixing operating risk and personal wealth inside the same legal entity.

Below the GIFT City Family Investment Fund's effective scale threshold, a diversified securities and alternative investment portfolio is usually better served by a mainland Category I or II AIF, its own or a third party's, or by a discretionary portfolio management arrangement under a trust, than by forcing a smaller pool into an IFSC structure built for a different scale of wealth.

Where the family genuinely clears the USD 10 million corpus threshold, has meaningful NRI, OCI or foreign-resident membership, or specifically wants self-management of its own pooled wealth without engaging a third-party licensed manager, it is the natural candidate for the GIFT City Family Investment Fund route, ideally layered underneath, rather than instead of, a trust that continues to handle succession.

Cost is the factor families most often underweight at the outset. Setting up and running a GIFT City FME, even a self-managed one, carries registration fees, annual charges, custodian fees and the cost of local directors, a compliance officer and an auditor familiar with IFSCA's reporting formats, none of which a plain domestic trust holding a portfolio through a bank-managed account incurs. A family should weigh that recurring cost against the tax and structuring benefits for its own actual corpus and cross-border profile, rather than choosing GIFT City because the structure has become fashionable among comparable families.

What remains unsettled or worth rechecking before relying on this

The exact Income-tax Act, 2025 section numbers that now carry the exemptions historically described under Sections 10(4D) and 10(4E) of the 1961 Act should be confirmed against the current consolidated statute and the fund's own tax opinion before a family relies on any specific figure or condition described in secondary commentary written before the renumbering.

Fee figures for IFSCA registration and annual charges, and the precise current wording of the 'significant economic interest' and risk-disclosure conditions for non-family holders, should be checked against IFSCA's own regulations and circulars in force on the date of filing, since this framework has been amended more than once since 2022 and commentary written even a year earlier can understate how much the conditions have moved.

There is no single right structure for a family's wealth, only a right structure for what that family is actually trying to achieve with each part of it. Succession planning, running a business and managing a pooled investment portfolio are three different problems, and India now offers purpose-built answers to each: a trust for the first, a company or LLP for the second, and either a mainland AIF or, once the family's scale and cross-border profile justify it, a GIFT City Family Investment Fund for the third.

Families that try to solve all three problems with one inherited structure, usually whichever vehicle was set up first for an unrelated reason, tend to discover the mismatch only when a succession event, a dispute or a tax assessment forces the question. Working through the choice deliberately, with each route's actual conditions in hand rather than its reputation, costs far less than discovering the mismatch later, and a review carried out every few years, as the family's wealth, residence and membership change, keeps the structure matched to the family it actually serves rather than the family it was built for at the outset.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Family Investment Fund in GIFT City?

A Family Investment Fund is a self-managed pooling vehicle registered with the International Financial Services Centres Authority under the IFSCA (Fund Management) Regulations, 2025, open only to money contributed by a single family, directly or through the family's own holding entities. It does not need a separate, independently licensed fund manager the way an ordinary Alternative Investment Fund does.

What is the minimum size required to set up a Family Investment Fund?

The fund must reach a minimum corpus of USD 10 million within three years of the date of its certificate of registration, and the regulations require that level to be maintained afterward, not merely reached once. Families well below this threshold are generally better served by a mainland AIF or a trust-based structure.

Is a private family trust still useful if a family also sets up a GIFT City fund?

Yes. A trust handles succession, keeping assets out of probate and letting the settlor set conditions on when beneficiaries receive distributions, which a fund structure does not do. Large family structures commonly use a trust for succession and a separate fund, mainland or GIFT City, for pooled investment management.

Can an NRI family member invest in a GIFT City Family Investment Fund without using the Liberalised Remittance Scheme?

Generally yes, because the LRS annual limit applies specifically to resident individuals, not to non-resident investors. An Indian-resident family member, by contrast, typically invests through the LRS or another applicable outward investment route, subject to whatever limit and reporting currently applies to that route.

Does GIFT City fund income escape Indian tax entirely?

Certain income of non-resident unit holders in qualifying IFSC funds has historically been exempt under what were Sections 10(4D) and 10(4E) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, now renumbered in the Income-tax Act, 2025, but the exemption is conditional and fund-specific, and it answers only the Indian tax position, not any tax the investor's own country of residence may separately impose.

Who counts as a 'single family' for a Family Investment Fund?

IFSCA's definition, widened by a 2023 circular and carried into the 2025 regulations, extends beyond individuals related by blood or marriage to a company, LLP, partnership, sole proprietorship or trust through which family members hold a significant economic interest, with a risk-disclosure requirement for any non-family holder of up to 10 percent economic interest.

Does a Family Investment Fund need to hire an external fund manager?

No. Its defining feature is that it is self-managed: the family's own GIFT City entity registers directly with IFSCA as an Authorised FME for managing its own fund, and the usual legal-form and net-worth conditions that apply to a fund manager serving third-party investors are switched off for this category.

Should a family choose GIFT City over a mainland SEBI-registered AIF?

It depends on scale and cross-border profile. A mainland Category I or II AIF under the SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012 suits a family that wants an onshore, rupee-denominated structure at a lower minimum commitment. GIFT City suits a family that clears the higher USD 10 million threshold, has significant NRI, OCI or foreign-resident membership, or specifically wants the self-managed IFSC regime.
