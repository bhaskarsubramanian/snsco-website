Specific Performance and Injunctions After the 2018 Amendment: Where Discretion Ended and Where It Did Not

The Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018 made performance the rule rather than the exception, but readiness and willingness, time, liquidated damages and interim relief still decide most cases

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

For more than a century, an Indian court asked to order specific performance of a contract began from the premise that it would rather not, and would do so only if damages were plainly inadequate. The Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018 turned that premise around. This piece sets out what actually changed, what the Supreme Court has since said about which contracts the change reaches, and why readiness and willingness, time, and the choice between damages and an injunction remain as important as they always were.

Key points

  • Section 10, as substituted in 2018, requires a court to enforce specific performance, subject only to Sections 11(2), 14 and 16, replacing the earlier wide judicial discretion.
  • Section 20, also substituted in 2018, lets an aggrieved party have the contract performed by a third party or its own agency after notice, and recover the cost; a party who takes that route cannot also sue for specific performance.
  • The Supreme Court held in 2022 that the amendment is prospective and does not reach contracts executed before 1 October 2018; a 2024 review of that decision left commentators divided, but in October 2025, in Annamalai v. Vasanthi, the Supreme Court confirmed that the amendment has no retrospective effect and does not reach suits or transactions arising before that date.
  • Readiness and willingness to perform, required under Section 16(c), remains a live issue in every specific performance suit and is usually the ground on which such suits are actually won or lost.
  • Time is still not presumed to be of the essence in a contract for the sale of immovable property, though a court can find on the specific wording and conduct of the parties that the parties intended it to be essential.
  • Where a contract fixes a sum as liquidated damages, Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 caps what a court can award as reasonable compensation at that sum, and proof of actual loss remains a precondition for any award at all.
  • Interim injunctions in aid of a specific performance claim are governed by Order 39 CPC and by the restrictions in Section 41 of the Specific Relief Act, which bars an injunction to restrain breach of a contract that could not itself be specifically enforced.
  • The Allahabad High Court has treated post-2018 specific performance as effectively mandatory once a registered agreement and the buyer's continuing readiness are shown, narrowing the room for a seller to resist merely by pointing to inconvenience or a rise in property value.
  • A specific performance suit must be filed within three years under Article 54 of the Limitation Act, 1963, running from the date fixed for performance or, where no date is fixed, from the date the plaintiff had notice that performance was refused; getting the trigger date wrong in the plaint can defeat an otherwise strong claim.

The old discretion and why Parliament removed it

Before October 2018, Section 10 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 told a court that it may, in its discretion, enforce specific performance in two broad situations: where there was no standard for ascertaining the actual damage caused by non-performance, or where monetary compensation would not afford adequate relief. Everything after that opening word, may, was discretionary, and decades of judgments built up a body of equitable considerations, hardship on the defendant, delay by the plaintiff, unfairness in the bargain, that a court could weigh before deciding whether to grant the remedy at all, even where the plaintiff had otherwise proved breach.

The difficulty this created in commercial practice was predictable. A seller who had entered a contract to sell land, taken part payment, and then found a better buyer when prices rose, had every incentive to breach and absorb a damages award rather than hand over the property, particularly where damages were difficult to quantify or where delay in litigation eroded their real value. The Law Commission and the Government's own review of ease-of-doing-business concerns identified this as a structural problem: a remedy that exists on paper but that a defendant can reliably avoid by accepting a damages exposure is not much of a deterrent to breach. The Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018 was Parliament's response, built around three provisions: a rewritten Section 10 making performance the rule, a rewritten Section 14 narrowing the exceptions, and a new Section 20 creating substituted performance as an alternative remedy for the aggrieved party to pursue without going to court at all.

Section 10 after 2018: performance as the rule

The substituted Section 10 now provides that the specific performance of a contract shall be enforced by the court, subject to the provisions contained in sub-section (2) of Section 11, Section 14 and Section 16. The change in verb, from may to shall, is deliberate and is the change courts have leaned on most heavily since 2018: specific performance is no longer something a plaintiff has to persuade a court to grant as a matter of grace once breach is proved; it is something the court must grant unless the contract falls within one of the specific statutory exceptions. The old open-ended discretion to weigh equitable factors beyond those exceptions has, correspondingly, narrowed a great deal, though it has not disappeared altogether, since a court retains a residual discretion in applying equitable doctrines such as delay, acquiescence, and the plaintiff's own conduct, which continue to inform whether the statutory conditions, including readiness and willingness, are actually satisfied on the facts.

The Allahabad High Court applied this directly in Tushar Agrawal v. Ganesh Prasad, decided in July 2026, concerning a registered agreement to sell a house in Bhadohi for thirty lakh rupees dated December 2019. The Court held that, after the 2018 amendment, it is now obliged to enforce specific performance of a contract, subject only to Sections 11(2), 14 and 16, and decreed performance in the buyer's favour, directing that the sale deed be executed through the court on deposit of the balance consideration. The decision reflects a broader pattern in post-2018 rulings: once a registered agreement is shown, and the buyer's continuing readiness and willingness to perform is established, a seller resisting performance has a materially harder task than before 2018, when a court could simply decline the remedy as a matter of discretion even where breach and readiness were both proved.

Which contracts the amendment actually reaches: the prospective-application problem

A threshold question in nearly every post-2018 specific performance suit is whether the amended, more plaintiff-friendly Section 10 applies at all, which depends on when the underlying contract was made, not merely on when the suit was filed. The Supreme Court addressed this in Katta Sujatha Reddy v. Siddamsetty Infra Projects Pvt. Ltd., decided on 25 August 2022 by a three-judge bench, in a dispute arising from agreements for sale entered into in March 1997. The Court held that the 2018 amendment is prospective in operation and does not reach transactions entered into before 1 October 2018, even where the suit itself is filed, or remains pending, after that date; for such older contracts, the pre-amendment discretionary standard continued to apply, while specific performance was treated as an enforceable right for contracts made after the cut-off, no longer dependent on the older equitable considerations.

That position was not the end of the matter. A review petition against the August 2022 decision was heard, and in November 2024 the Supreme Court revisited its earlier ruling, reinstating the view taken by the High Court in the underlying dispute. Commentary on the review was divided on how far it unsettled the 2022 prospective rule, but the Supreme Court has since resolved the uncertainty. In Annamalai v. Vasanthi, decided on 29 October 2025 (2025 INSC 1267), a bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra held that the 2018 amendment has no retrospective effect and does not govern suits or transactions arising before 1 October 2018, clarifying that the 2024 review had not held otherwise. For a contract made before the cut-off, the pre-amendment position under Section 20 as it then stood, requiring the court's discretion to be exercised judicially rather than arbitrarily, continues to govern, whatever the date on which the suit itself was filed.

Section 14 and the contracts that remain outside specific performance

Even after 2018, not every contract can be specifically enforced. The substituted Section 14 continues to exclude, among other categories, a contract whose performance involves a continuous duty that the court cannot supervise, such as an ongoing service or maintenance obligation stretching over time, and a contract that is so dependent on the personal qualifications of a party that the court cannot enforce specific performance of its material terms, a category courts have long applied to contracts for personal service built on mutual trust and confidence between the parties, such as employment or engagement of a particular individual's skill. A contract that is in its nature determinable, meaning either party can terminate it as of right, also falls outside specific performance, on the logic that there is little point decreeing performance of an obligation the defendant could simply terminate the moment after.

The new addition in 2018 is the exclusion in Section 14(a): a contract cannot be specifically enforced where the party seeking relief has already obtained substituted performance of that contract under Section 20. This dovetails with the corresponding bar in Section 16(a), which disqualifies a plaintiff who has obtained substituted performance from then also seeking specific performance of the same contract; the two remedies are structured as alternatives, not as a sequence a plaintiff can run through one after the other on the same breach.

Substituted performance under Section 20: an alternative worth understanding

Section 20, as it stood before 2018, gave courts a separate discretion to refuse specific performance even where the statutory conditions were met, essentially preserving an equitable safety valve. The amendment replaced this altogether with a different idea: substituted performance, under which a party aggrieved by breach of contract has the option, instead of suing for specific performance, to have the contract performed by a third party or by its own agency, after giving written notice of not less than the period prescribed, and to recover the costs, expenses and other sums incurred from the party in breach. This is a self-help remedy in the sense that it does not require the aggrieved party to go to court before acting, though any dispute over the amount recoverable can itself become the subject of subsequent litigation.

The trade-off written into the Act is important for anyone advising a client on which route to take. Once a party has obtained substituted performance under Section 20, Section 14(a) and Section 16(a) together bar that same party from subsequently claiming specific performance of the same contract. A business that has already arranged for a substitute supplier or contractor to complete the work, and has paid the extra cost, has made an election; it cannot then turn around and also insist that the original defaulting party specifically perform. The choice between suing for specific performance and exercising substituted performance should therefore be made early and deliberately, with an eye to which remedy actually serves the client's commercial interest, rather than treated as something that can be revisited once one route turns out to be slower or more expensive than hoped.

Readiness and willingness: the condition that decides most disputed cases

Section 16(c) of the Act, both before and after 2018, requires a plaintiff seeking specific performance to prove that they have performed, or have always been ready and willing to perform, the essential terms of the contract that were to be performed by them, other than terms the defendant has prevented or waived. This requirement survived the 2018 amendment essentially intact, and in practice it is the issue most specific performance suits actually turn on, since breach by the defendant is often not seriously disputed; what is disputed is whether the plaintiff was genuinely ready and able to pay the balance consideration or otherwise perform their own side of the bargain throughout the period between the contract and the suit, not merely at the moment they filed it.

Readiness and willingness is a question of conduct and capacity, not merely of intention stated in court. A plaintiff does not need to keep the entire balance consideration physically in hand throughout the relevant period, but does need to show the financial capacity and the continuing intention to perform, and an unexplained failure to tender payment, to respond to the seller's communications, or to take any visible step toward completion over an extended period can defeat the claim even where the seller's own breach is otherwise clear. Where the seller's conduct itself has made tender impossible, for instance by refusing to accept any payment or by denying the existence of the contract altogether, the plaintiff is not expected to make a futile tender, but should still be able to show that they were in a position to perform had the seller allowed it.

Time as essence: the rule that the 2018 amendment left untouched

A persistent misunderstanding in practice is that the 2018 amendment, by making performance the rule, also changed the law on whether time is of the essence in a contract for sale of immovable property. It did not; that question continues to be governed by ordinary contract principles rather than by the Specific Relief Act's provisions on discretion. The settled position, restated by the Supreme Court's Constitution Bench in Chand Rani v. Kamal Rani, reported at (1993) 1 SCC 519, is that in a contract for sale of immovable property there is no presumption that time is of the essence, but a court can still find, from the specific wording of the contract and the surrounding circumstances, that the parties intended time to be essential for a particular obligation, such as payment of the balance consideration by a fixed date.

What this means practically is that a seller who wants a firm completion date to be treated as a hard deadline, after which the buyer forfeits the right to specific performance, needs the contract to say so clearly and specifically, rather than relying on a generic time period mentioned in passing. Conversely, a buyer who has missed a date mentioned in the contract is not automatically out of court; if the contract's language and the parties' conduct do not support treating that date as essential, delay alone, short of amounting to the kind of conduct that defeats readiness and willingness or attracts a separate limitation bar, does not necessarily forfeit the remedy.

Liquidated damages and Section 74: the ceiling, not the floor

Where a plaintiff seeks damages instead of, or in addition to, specific performance, or where a contract names a sum payable on breach, Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 governs what a court can actually award. The Supreme Court's decision in Kailash Nath Associates v. Delhi Development Authority, reported at (2015) 4 SCC 136, decided on 9 January 2015, remains the leading authority on this provision. The Court held that where a contract names a sum as liquidated damages, the injured party can recover that sum as reasonable compensation only if it is found by the court to be a genuine pre-estimate of the loss the parties contemplated at the time of contracting; in every case, the named sum operates as an upper limit beyond which the court cannot award reasonable compensation, not as a sum the plaintiff is automatically entitled to regardless of actual loss.

The Court also held that proof of loss or damage caused by the breach remains a precondition for any award under Section 74 at all; the section does not dispense with the need to show that the breach actually caused some loss, even though it dispenses with the need to prove the precise extent of that loss once a genuine pre-estimate is found reasonable. A separate line of authority, tracing back to Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Dass and affirmed in Kailash Nath Associates itself, treats forfeiture of a reasonable amount paid as earnest money as standing somewhat apart from Section 74's reasonable-compensation framework, on the basis that earnest money serves a different function, as security for performance, rather than as a pre-agreed measure of damages. Anyone drafting a liquidated damages clause, whether in a sale agreement, a construction contract, or a commercial supply agreement, should treat the named figure as a ceiling to be justified against a realistic estimate of likely loss at the time of drafting, not as a number that can simply be inflated to deter breach, since an unrealistically high figure invites exactly the scrutiny Kailash Nath Associates requires.

Interim injunctions in aid of a specific performance claim

A plaintiff who sues for specific performance of an agreement to sell land, and who is worried that the defendant will sell or encumber the property to a third party while the suit is pending, will typically also seek a temporary injunction under Order 39, Rules 1 and 2 of the Code of Civil Procedure, restraining the defendant from alienating or creating third-party rights over the property until the suit is decided. The ordinary three-part test applies: a prima facie case on the underlying claim, a balance of convenience favouring the plaintiff, and the likelihood of irreparable injury if the injunction is refused, with courts generally alert to the risk that an uninjuncted sale to a bona fide third party for value, without notice of the pending suit, could defeat the plaintiff's remedy altogether even if the suit is later decided in their favour.

Section 41 of the Specific Relief Act imposes its own limits on when an injunction, interim or final, can be granted, independent of the Order 39 test. Clause (e) of Section 41 bars an injunction to prevent the breach of a contract the performance of which would not itself be specifically enforceable, which means a plaintiff cannot use an injunction to achieve indirectly what Section 14 would prevent them from achieving directly through a decree of specific performance. A plaintiff whose underlying contract falls within one of the excluded categories, such as a contract involving a continuous duty the court cannot supervise, cannot sidestep that exclusion by framing the relief sought as an injunction rather than as specific performance, since the Section 41 bar looks at the substance of what is being restrained, not the label on the prayer.

Specific performance claims that arise alongside arbitration

A fair proportion of the contracts most likely to generate a specific performance dispute, share purchase agreements, joint venture agreements, agreements for sale of commercial property with a development component, carry an arbitration clause. This raises a question that is easy to overlook in the rush to protect a client's position: if the agreement itself is to be arbitrated, does a party seeking specific performance sue in civil court at all, or does the claim have to go before the arbitral tribunal, with court involvement limited to interim protection under Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 pending constitution of the tribunal? The general position is that specific performance, like most contractual remedies, is arbitrable, and where a valid arbitration agreement covers the dispute, a civil suit for specific performance is liable to be referred to arbitration on an application under Section 8, leaving the tribunal, not the civil court, to decide whether to decree performance.

This matters for strategy in two concrete ways. First, a party anxious about the other side alienating the subject property while an arbitration is being set in motion should not wait for the tribunal to be constituted before seeking protection; Section 9 allows an application for interim measures, including an order restraining alienation, to be made to a court even before arbitral proceedings have formally commenced, and the urgency that justifies an interim injunction under Order 39 in ordinary civil litigation applies with equal force here. Second, the 2018 amendment's substantive changes to Sections 10, 14, 16 and 20 bind an arbitral tribunal applying Indian substantive law just as they bind a civil court; an arbitrator deciding whether to award specific performance of a sale agreement governed by Indian law works from the same post-2018 statutory framework, including the readiness-and-willingness requirement and the Section 41 limits on restraining a party from doing something a court could not itself decree, even though the forum and the procedural rules around evidence and interim relief differ from a civil suit.

Limitation: three years, but from which date

A specific performance suit filed too late fails regardless of how strong the merits are, and the limitation rule here has a trap built into it. Article 54 of the Limitation Act, 1963 gives three years from the date fixed for performance, or, where no such date is fixed, from the date the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused. These are two separate triggers, not a single three-year window running from the contract date, and which one applies depends entirely on how the contract itself is worded.

Where the contract names a specific calendar date for completion, that date starts the clock, and the Supreme Court has read the word date in Article 54 narrowly, as referring to an actual, identifiable point on the calendar rather than a vague or conditional milestone, such as completion upon the happening of some other event. Where the contract instead makes performance conditional on steps that do not resolve to a fixed calendar date, for instance completion once existing tenants vacate, the first limb may not apply at all, and limitation instead runs from the date the plaintiff had notice that the other side was refusing to perform, a date that itself has to be established on the facts, often through a notice of refusal or an unequivocal communication denying the contract. A plaintiff who is vague, in the plaint itself, about which limb applies and when the relevant date or notice actually fell, risks a limitation objection succeeding before the substantive merits are ever reached, which is why pleading the specific trigger date, and the evidence for it, needs the same care as pleading readiness and willingness itself.

Drafting and litigation strategy in light of the current position

For anyone drafting a contract for sale of property or a significant commercial agreement after 1 October 2018, a few practical points follow from the current state of the law. If a firm completion date genuinely matters, say so explicitly, rather than leaving the question to be inferred later from surrounding circumstances a court may read differently than the drafter intended. If liquidated damages are included, set the figure with reference to a realistic estimate of likely loss, documented where possible at the time of drafting, since an inflated figure invites challenge and, even where it survives, operates only as a ceiling rather than a guarantee of recovery. And where substituted performance under Section 20 is a realistic alternative to litigation, for instance because a replacement supplier or contractor is readily available, weigh that option early, since exercising it forecloses a later specific performance claim on the same breach.

For a party already in a specific performance dispute, the practical priorities are different. Establish and preserve, from the earliest possible point, a clear record of readiness and willingness, correspondence, proof of funds, any tender actually made or attempted, since this is the ground on which the case is most likely to be fought regardless of how the prospective-application question for the contract's date is eventually resolved. Where the contract predates October 2018, the Supreme Court's October 2025 decision in Annamalai v. Vasanthi confirms that the pre-amendment, discretionary standard governs; plead and prepare evidence accordingly rather than assuming the 2018 standard will apply. And where urgent protection of the property is needed, move for interim relief promptly, since delay in seeking an injunction is itself a factor courts weigh against the applicant, and a third-party sale completed while the plaintiff waited can be far harder to undo than to prevent.

The 2018 amendment genuinely changed the starting point for specific performance litigation in India, from a remedy a plaintiff had to persuade a reluctant court to grant, to one the court must grant once the statutory conditions are met. What it did not change is the practical reality that most such suits are won or lost on readiness and willingness, on whether time was truly made essential, and on how carefully the underlying contract was drafted in the first place.

The Supreme Court's October 2025 decision in Annamalai v. Vasanthi has since confirmed that the amendment does not reach contracts made before 1 October 2018, resolving the uncertainty left by the 2024 review of Katta Sujatha Reddy. A contract made close to that cut-off still needs careful, date-specific advice, but litigants and drafters no longer need to treat the applicable standard as unsettled.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is specific performance now automatic whenever a contract is breached?

No. Section 10, as substituted in 2018, requires a court to enforce specific performance subject to the exceptions in Sections 11(2), 14 and 16, and the plaintiff must still prove readiness and willingness under Section 16(c). It is the rule rather than a matter of open discretion, but statutory conditions still have to be satisfied.

Does the 2018 amendment apply to a contract signed before October 2018?

The Supreme Court held in 2022, in Katta Sujatha Reddy, that the amendment is prospective and does not apply to contracts entered into before 1 October 2018. A 2024 review of that decision left the point in some doubt, but in October 2025, in Annamalai v. Vasanthi, the Supreme Court confirmed that the amendment has no retrospective effect and does not govern suits or transactions arising before that date.

What is substituted performance under Section 20, and can I still sue for specific performance after using it?

Substituted performance lets an aggrieved party, after notice, have the contract performed by a third party or its own agency and recover the cost from the defaulting party. Once exercised, Sections 14(a) and 16(a) bar that party from also seeking specific performance of the same contract.

Is time always of the essence in a contract to sell property?

No. The Supreme Court's position in Chand Rani v. Kamal Rani is that there is no presumption that time is of the essence in a contract for sale of immovable property, though a court can find, from the contract's specific wording and the parties' conduct, that time was intended to be essential for a particular obligation.

Can I recover more than the liquidated damages figure stated in my contract?

Generally no. Under Section 74 of the Contract Act, as explained in Kailash Nath Associates v. DDA, a named liquidated damages figure operates as an upper limit on reasonable compensation, not a guaranteed sum, and the court can award less than that figure, or nothing, if no actual loss is proved.

Can I get an injunction to stop a breach even if I could not get specific performance of the same contract?

Generally no. Section 41(e) of the Specific Relief Act bars an injunction to prevent breach of a contract that could not itself be specifically enforced, so the exclusions in Section 14 cannot be sidestepped by seeking an injunction instead of a decree of specific performance.

What do I need to prove to show I was 'ready and willing' to perform?

Continuing financial capacity and genuine intention to perform your side of the contract throughout the relevant period, evidenced by conduct such as attempts to tender payment, correspondence, and the absence of unexplained long delay, rather than a bare statement of willingness made only once the suit is filed.

Does the 2018 amendment affect contracts for personal services, like an employment agreement?

No. Section 14, as amended, continues to exclude contracts that are dependent on the personal qualifications of a party or that involve a continuous duty the court cannot supervise, a category that has long included personal service contracts.

How long do I have to file a specific performance suit?

Three years under Article 54 of the Limitation Act, 1963, running from the date fixed for performance in the contract, or, if no such date is fixed, from the date the plaintiff had notice that performance was refused. Which trigger applies depends on the specific wording of the contract.
