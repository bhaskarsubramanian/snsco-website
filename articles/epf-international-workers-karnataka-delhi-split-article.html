EPF for International Workers in India: Mandatory Enrolment and a Live Split Between Two High Courts

Delhi says a foreign employee must contribute on full salary. Karnataka says that rule is unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has not yet said which view wins.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 15 November 2026 · Law stated as on 14 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company posting an executive to its Indian subsidiary, or an Indian company hiring a foreign national directly, runs into a provident fund rule that surprises almost everyone the first time they meet it: there is no wage ceiling. An Indian employee's provident fund contribution is capped once monthly pay crosses a threshold that has stayed at fifteen thousand rupees for years. A foreign employee classified as an International Worker contributes on full salary, however large that salary is, unless a specific exemption applies. Two High Courts have now looked at whether that distinction is lawful and reached opposite conclusions, and the Supreme Court has the question in front of it without having decided it. Any employer with foreign staff in India needs to know where that leaves its payroll obligations today.

Key points

  • An 'International Worker', under the provision now carried forward in the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme, 2026 (previously paragraph 83 of the EPF Scheme, 1952, which the 2026 Scheme replaced with effect from 1 July 2026), contributes to the EPF on full monthly salary, with no wage ceiling, unless the person qualifies as an 'excluded employee'.
  • The main route to excluded-employee status is holding a Certificate of Coverage from a country with which India has a Social Security Agreement, which keeps the worker's social security contributions in the home country instead.
  • On 25 April 2024, a single judge of the Karnataka High Court, in Stone Hill Education Foundation v Union of India, struck down paragraph 83 of the EPF Scheme and the corresponding provision of the Employees' Pension Scheme as unconstitutional, holding that the full-salary rule for foreign workers was arbitrary when measured against the wage-ceiling protection given to Indian employees.
  • On 4 November 2025, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court, in the SpiceJet and LG Electronics writ petitions, expressly declined to follow the Karnataka ruling and upheld the International Worker regime as a valid classification, not a discriminatory one.
  • On 12 March 2026, the Supreme Court issued notice on a Special Leave Petition filed by LG Electronics India Limited against the Delhi High Court's ruling, so the question is now before the apex court without a final decision as of this writing.
  • No stay has been reported on either High Court's order, which leaves employers operating in a genuinely unsettled area of law, with the Karnataka ruling's effect understood to be confined to parties before that court pending its own appeal and the Delhi ruling standing as the more recent appellate pronouncement nationally.
  • India has social security agreements with close to twenty countries, including Belgium, Germany, Switzerland, France, Luxembourg, Denmark, South Korea, the Netherlands and Hungary, with a new agreement with the United Kingdom signed in February 2026; an employee from a country without such an agreement gets no exemption route at all.
  • Withdrawal of an International Worker's provident fund balance before retirement age is restricted in ways that do not apply to an Indian employee, and the precise withdrawal conditions depend on whether the worker's home country has a Social Security Agreement with India.

A rule that catches foreign secondees by surprise

Multinational groups that second an executive to an Indian subsidiary, or that hire a foreign national directly into an Indian payroll, usually arrive with a mental model of provident fund contributions built around the wage ceiling that applies to the rest of the Indian workforce. Under that general rule, an employer's and employee's provident fund contributions are calculated on a wage figure capped at fifteen thousand rupees a month, regardless of how much the employee actually earns, unless the employer and employee have voluntarily opted to contribute on the full salary. For a foreign employee classified as an International Worker, that ceiling simply does not apply. The contribution is calculated on the full monthly salary, which for a senior expatriate executive can mean a provident fund contribution running into lakhs of rupees a month rather than a few thousand.

This is not a drafting accident or an oversight in the scheme. It follows from a definition first introduced into the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme, 1952 through paragraph 83, which created the category of International Worker specifically to bring foreign nationals employed in India, and Indian nationals employed abroad in countries with which India has reciprocal arrangements, within the provident fund net on a different contribution basis from the general workforce. With effect from 1 July 2026, the Ministry of Labour and Employment notified the Employees' Provident Funds Scheme, 2026 under the Code on Social Security, 2020, replacing the 1952 Scheme; commentary on the new scheme describes it as carrying the International Worker framework forward in substance, with existing International Workers continuing as members without a fresh enrolment, so the analysis and litigation discussed in this article remain live even though the governing scheme's number has changed. The policy reasoning behind the rule, as far as it can be reconstructed from the regulatory history, was to protect India's own nationals working abroad under reciprocal social security arrangements by extending a comparable obligation to foreign nationals working in India, and to prevent foreign employees from treating a short Indian posting as a way to avoid social security contributions altogether.

Whatever the original policy logic, the rule has now become one of the more litigated corners of Indian employment law, precisely because it treats a defined class of employees differently from everyone else on a basis, nationality combined with the absence of a bilateral treaty, that invites a constitutional challenge. That challenge has now produced two High Court judgments going in opposite directions, with the Supreme Court watching from the sidelines rather than having stepped in yet.

Who counts as an International Worker

The scheme's definition works through a two-step test. The first step asks whether a person falls within the definition of International Worker under paragraph 2 of the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme, which broadly covers a foreign national employed in an establishment in India to which the scheme applies, and separately covers an Indian employee who has worked or is working in a country with which India has a Social Security Agreement and is eligible to avail of benefits under that country's social security programme by virtue of the agreement. The definition is not limited to senior executives or to any particular salary band; a junior foreign employee on an Indian payroll is, in principle, caught just as much as a chief executive sent on secondment.

The second step asks whether the person, having met the first test, is nonetheless an 'excluded employee' under the scheme's separate definition of that term. An excluded employee is taken out of the mandatory full-salary contribution regime, and the most commonly used route to that status is holding a valid Certificate of Coverage issued by the home country's social security authority under a Social Security Agreement between that country and India. A person who clears the first test but does not clear the second, in other words a foreign national from a country with no Social Security Agreement with India, or one who has not obtained the Certificate of Coverage, remains a full International Worker for contribution purposes and is brought onto the Indian provident fund rolls on full salary from the date of joining.

Employers sometimes assume that a short posting, or a posting where the employee continues to be paid partly or wholly by the foreign parent, takes the arrangement outside the scheme altogether. It generally does not. If the person is working in an establishment in India to which the Employees' Provident Fund and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952 applies, the International Worker classification is triggered by the fact of employment in India, not by the mechanics of how or from where the salary is paid.

The Certificate of Coverage and India's Social Security Agreements

A Social Security Agreement, commonly abbreviated as an SSA, is a bilateral treaty under which India and a partner country agree to avoid requiring the same employee to contribute to social security in both countries for the same period of work, and typically also agree to count periods of contribution in either country toward eligibility for benefits in the other, a feature usually called totalisation. Reporting on India's treaty position suggests the country has entered into such agreements with a number of partners, including Belgium, Germany, Switzerland, France, Luxembourg, Denmark, South Korea, the Netherlands and Hungary, with the precise current count reported differently across sources, somewhere in the high teens, and with a further agreement with the United Kingdom signed in February 2026 and reported as moving toward operation alongside the India-UK trade agreement. An employer should treat any list of SSA partner countries, including the one in this article, as a starting point to be checked against the EPFO's own current list rather than a final answer, because the roster changes as new agreements are signed and brought into force.

Where an SSA is in force, an employee seconded from the partner country to India, who continues to be covered by that country's social security system and who obtains a Certificate of Coverage confirming that coverage, can be treated as an excluded employee in India and kept out of the mandatory full-salary EPF contribution. The Certificate of Coverage is issued by the home country's social security authority, not by the EPFO, and is typically sought before or shortly after the posting begins, since the exemption generally operates from the date the certificate is obtained and does not automatically apply retrospectively to a period before the employer held it.

An employee from a country with no Social Security Agreement with India, which still covers a large share of the countries from which foreign executives are posted to India, including (on the sources reviewed for this piece) major sending countries outside the current treaty list, has no equivalent exemption route open to them at all. For that employee, the International Worker classification and the full-salary contribution apply without a Certificate of Coverage escape valve, which is precisely the group of employees whose treatment the Karnataka High Court found constitutionally troubling.

Why the full-salary rule became a constitutional question

An Indian employee earning above the wage ceiling contributes to the provident fund only on the capped figure, by default, unless the employer and employee have jointly opted for a higher contribution base. A foreign employee with no Certificate of Coverage contributes on the entire salary from the first rupee. For a highly paid expatriate, the gap between what an equivalent Indian employee would contribute and what the International Worker must contribute can be substantial, and the foreign employee typically cannot access that larger accumulated balance until retirement age or certain other specified events, which is a liquidity and planning issue quite apart from the fairness question.

The argument that eventually succeeded before the Karnataka High Court was that this difference in treatment, foreign nationals contributing on uncapped salary while Indian nationals enjoy a capped default, was not a rational classification serving a legitimate purpose but an arbitrary one, particularly once the state's own stated justification, protecting the social security interests of Indian workers posted abroad through reciprocal treaties, is tested against a class of foreign workers who have no treaty-based reciprocal protection to speak of and are simply paying more because the treaty their home country might have signed does not exist.

The Union of India's position, which later persuaded the Delhi High Court in the later case, was that nationality-based classification in this specific context is not inherently suspect, because the scheme is implementing India's own international social security policy and is entitled to treat nationals of treaty countries differently from nationals of non-treaty countries as a matter of reciprocity, a rationale the Delhi High Court accepted as a valid basis for the classification rather than an arbitrary one.

The Karnataka High Court strikes down the regime

In Stone Hill Education Foundation v Union of India, a writ petition numbered WP No. 18486 of 2012, a single judge of the Karnataka High Court delivered a ruling on 25 April 2024 striking down paragraph 83 of the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme and the corresponding paragraph 43A of the Employees' Pension Scheme insofar as they applied to International Workers. The court's reasoning, as reported, centred on the absence of any wage ceiling for International Workers while Indian employees retained that protection, treating this as offending the guarantee of equality under Article 14 of the Constitution because the differential treatment could not be justified as a rational classification connected to a legitimate purpose.

The EPFO's own response, recorded in a press release reported shortly after the judgment, acknowledged the ruling and indicated the organisation was evaluating its options, including an appeal, which signalled from the outset that the Karnataka decision was unlikely to be the last word on the question even within Karnataka itself. That expectation has been borne out: the ruling is reported to be under appeal before a Division Bench of the same High Court, and no stay of the single judge's order had been reported as granted as of the most recent commentary reviewed for this piece.

A single judge's ruling striking down a central scheme provision as unconstitutional is a significant decision, but its immediate legal effect, pending the Division Bench appeal, is generally understood to bind the parties before that court and to carry persuasive, not binding, weight elsewhere in India. Employers with operations only in Karnataka, and employees who were parties to or are directly covered by that litigation, are in a different practical position from employers elsewhere in the country, a distinction that matters a great deal for anyone trying to work out what to do about current payroll deductions.

The Delhi High Court takes the opposite view

Roughly a year and a half after the Karnataka ruling, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court, comprising Chief Justice Devendra Kumar Upadhyaya and Justice Tushar Rao Gedela, delivered judgment on 4 November 2025 in writ petitions filed by SpiceJet Limited, numbered W.P.(C) 2941 of 2012, and by LG Electronics India Limited, numbered W.P.(C) 6330 of 2021. The Division Bench dismissed both writ petitions and upheld the validity of the notifications requiring International Workers who are not excluded employees to mandatorily enrol in and contribute to the provident fund, including on salary above the wage ceiling that applies to Indian employees.

On the constitutional challenge, the Delhi High Court is reported to have held that there are valid reasons connected to India's international social security obligations and policy to treat foreign workers as a distinct category from domestic workers, and that the classification was accordingly not arbitrary or discriminatory in the sense Article 14 prohibits. The bench is also reported to have rejected a separate argument that the scheme's International Worker provisions exceeded the rule-making power delegated under the parent Act.

The judgment goes out of its way, on the reporting available, to address the Karnataka ruling directly rather than simply ignoring it: the Delhi High Court expressly declined to follow the Stone Hill Education Foundation decision, treating it as a single-judge ruling whose correctness remains open to further appellate scrutiny, and noted that its own view aligned with a position the Bombay High Court had separately taken. That makes the current position, on the reporting reviewed, a two-against-one split in substance, with Delhi and Bombay upholding the regime and Karnataka alone having struck it down, though the Karnataka ruling's own appeal is still pending.

Where the question stands today

LG Electronics India Limited, having lost in the Delhi High Court, carried the question to the Supreme Court. On 12 March 2026, the Supreme Court issued notice on the Special Leave Petition, which means the apex court has agreed to examine whether the Delhi High Court's ruling upholding the International Worker regime was correctly decided, without having yet ruled on the merits. As of the date of this article, no final Supreme Court decision on that petition had been reported.

Put together, the live position as on 14 November 2026 is that the International Worker contribution regime stands upheld by the Delhi High Court, a ruling that aligns with the Bombay High Court's position, while a single judge of the Karnataka High Court has struck the regime down in a decision currently under appeal before that court's own Division Bench, and the Supreme Court has the Delhi ruling under examination through a pending Special Leave Petition. No authoritative pan-India resolution exists yet, and an employer cannot responsibly treat either High Court ruling as having settled the question nationally.

This is precisely the kind of situation in which this article's authors would ordinarily caution against treating any single ruling as the final word, and that caution applies here with particular force because the two rulings are not reconcilable readings of the same facts; they are a direct disagreement on whether the underlying rule is constitutional at all. An employer operating across states covered by different High Courts, which describes most multinational employers with more than one Indian office, cannot assume a uniform answer applies to all its Indian establishments.

What an employer should actually do with payroll right now

Pending a Supreme Court resolution, the safer and more defensible compliance posture for an employer outside Karnataka is to continue complying with the International Worker regime as it stands, deducting and remitting provident fund contributions on full salary for employees who do not qualify as excluded employees, because that is what the currently binding appellate authority, the Delhi High Court's Division Bench ruling, requires, and because continuing to comply avoids the much larger exposure of an interest and penalty assessment if the regime is eventually confirmed by the Supreme Court.

An employer with operations in Karnataka, or with employees who are themselves parties to or directly covered by the Stone Hill Education Foundation litigation, faces a genuinely harder judgment call, because the Karnataka High Court's ruling, while under appeal and not binding beyond its own parties and jurisdiction in the ordinary course, is a live order from a court whose decisions bind establishments within that state absent a stay. This is a fact-specific question that should be put to counsel with the actual litigation record in hand rather than resolved generically, because the scope of a single-judge order's binding effect, and whether the EPFO has sought or obtained any stay pending the Division Bench appeal, can change the answer.

What an employer should not do, whatever its view on the merits, is stop deducting International Worker contributions unilaterally on the strength of the Karnataka ruling for an establishment located outside Karnataka. Doing so creates a direct exposure if the Supreme Court eventually upholds the Delhi High Court's position, which would leave the employer liable for the unremitted contributions together with interest and, potentially, damages under the Act's enforcement provisions, for a period during which the employer chose not to comply with what was, at the relevant time, the position taken by the court whose ruling actually binds it.

Common mistakes employers make with International Worker compliance

The most frequent error is treating a Certificate of Coverage as optional paperwork to be sorted out eventually rather than a precondition to the exemption. An employee from an SSA country who has not yet obtained the certificate is not automatically excluded merely because their home country has an agreement with India; the exemption follows the certificate, and a gap between the posting's start date and the date the certificate is actually issued can leave the employer liable for full-salary contributions during that gap.

A second common error is assuming that because an employee is paid from a foreign payroll, or paid partly offshore, the Indian establishment has no International Worker obligation at all. The test turns on employment in an establishment in India covered by the Act, not on the mechanics of salary payment, and structuring the payment offshore does not, by itself, take the employee outside the scheme's reach.

A third error, more relevant after the two High Court rulings than before them, is assuming that because one High Court struck the regime down, the question is settled everywhere. It is not settled anywhere yet in the sense of a final, binding, nationwide answer; it is contested, with the more recent and geographically broader appellate pronouncement, Delhi's, currently favouring continued compliance, and the Supreme Court yet to weigh in.

What a foreign company should check before seconding staff to India

A foreign company planning to second an executive, or several, to an Indian subsidiary or branch should start by checking whether its home country has a Social Security Agreement with India that is actually in force, not merely signed, since a signed-but-not-yet-operational agreement, such as the position reported for the UK agreement signed in February 2026, does not yet confer excluded-employee status. Where an agreement is in force, the company should apply for the Certificate of Coverage from the home country's social security authority before or as close as possible to the start of the posting, to minimise any gap period during which full contributions apply.

Where no agreement exists, the company should build the full-salary provident fund contribution into the cost of the secondment from the outset, rather than discovering it as an unplanned payroll cost once the posting is underway. For a senior executive on a substantial package, the contribution can be a material annual cost, and treating it as a budgeting surprise rather than a planned cost is the kind of avoidable friction that strains the relationship between the seconding company's finance function and its Indian subsidiary.

The company should also confirm, separately from the contribution question, what the employee's practical access to the accumulated balance will look like on completion of the assignment, since withdrawal before retirement age is restricted for International Workers in ways that differ from the rules applying to Indian employees, and an executive who expects to withdraw the balance on leaving India at the end of a three-year posting may be disappointed to learn that withdrawal is tied to retirement age or other specified conditions rather than to the end of Indian employment as such.

Withdrawal and pension entitlements: the part that gets less attention

Discussion of the International Worker regime tends to focus on the contribution obligation, because that is the part that shows up immediately on a payroll run. The withdrawal side deserves equal attention, because it is where an employee's actual expectations are most likely to be disappointed. Commentary on the scheme's withdrawal conditions is not fully consistent in the detail, with some sources describing withdrawal as available broadly on the same triggers that apply to any member, such as retirement at the specified age, permanent incapacity, or departure from India under the terms of an applicable Social Security Agreement, and other sources describing more restrictive conditions tied to length of service and totalisation of contribution periods across India and the home country.

Because the sources reviewed for this article did not converge cleanly on a single statement of the withdrawal rule, the responsible position is to flag the uncertainty rather than state a specific rule as settled. What is consistent across the material is that an International Worker's access to the accumulated balance is not automatically the same as an Indian employee's access on resignation, and that the terms of any applicable Social Security Agreement with the employee's home country can materially change the answer. An employer managing the end of a secondment should treat the withdrawal question as needing its own specific check against the current EPFO guidance and, where relevant, the text of the applicable agreement, rather than assuming the general resignation-withdrawal rule that applies to Indian employees carries over.

The pension scheme runs alongside the provident fund question

Paragraph 43A of the Employees' Pension Scheme extends a parallel International Worker treatment to the pension component, and the Stone Hill Education Foundation ruling struck this down alongside paragraph 83 of the provident fund scheme, while the Delhi High Court's ruling upheld both provisions together as part of the same regime. An employer tracking the litigation should treat the provident fund and pension questions as a single package for compliance purposes, since both High Court rulings addressed them jointly rather than reaching separate conclusions on each.

For an International Worker without excluded-employee status, pension contributions follow the same uncapped salary basis as the provident fund contribution, which compounds the cost difference relative to an Indian employee's capped pension contribution. An employer modelling the total cost of an expatriate secondment should include both components in the same calculation rather than treating the provident fund figure as the whole of the International Worker cost.

The International Worker regime sits in an unusual position for Indian employment law: a central government scheme provision, applied to every multinational employer with foreign staff in the country, currently has one High Court saying it is unconstitutional and another saying it is perfectly valid, with the Supreme Court having agreed to look at the question but not yet having answered it. Employers do not get the luxury of waiting for that answer before running payroll this month.

The pragmatic response is not to pick a side in the constitutional argument but to comply with whichever ruling currently binds the specific establishment in question, document the basis for that compliance decision, and revisit it the moment either the Supreme Court rules or the Karnataka Division Bench disposes of the pending appeal. Employers who have already obtained Certificates of Coverage for eligible employees should keep that documentation current and renew it on schedule; employers who have not checked whether their foreign employees' home countries have an operative Social Security Agreement with India should do that now, because that single check determines which of the two paths, full-salary contribution or exemption, actually applies to each individual employee on the roll.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is every foreign employee working in India classified as an International Worker for EPF purposes?

Broadly yes, for a foreign national employed in an establishment covered by the Employees' Provident Fund and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952, unless the employee qualifies as an excluded employee, most commonly by holding a Certificate of Coverage from a country with which India has a Social Security Agreement in force.

Does the Karnataka High Court's ruling mean foreign employees no longer have to contribute on full salary?

Not nationally. The ruling struck down the relevant provisions as applied in that case, is reported to be under appeal before a Division Bench of the same court, and the more recent Delhi High Court ruling of November 2025 reached the opposite conclusion and upheld the regime. Pending the Supreme Court's decision on the pending Special Leave Petition, employers outside the specific Karnataka litigation should continue complying with the regime as currently binding authority requires.

What is a Certificate of Coverage and who issues it?

It is a document issued by the social security authority of an employee's home country, confirming that the employee continues to be covered by that country's social security system during an overseas posting under a Social Security Agreement with India. It is obtained from the home country's authority, not from the EPFO, and should be applied for before or at the start of the Indian posting.

Which countries have a Social Security Agreement with India?

India has such agreements with close to twenty countries, reported to include Belgium, Germany, Switzerland, France, Luxembourg, Denmark, South Korea, the Netherlands and Hungary, with an agreement with the United Kingdom signed in February 2026. The exact current list and which agreements are actually in force, as opposed to signed, should be confirmed against the EPFO's current published list for the specific employee's home country.

Can an International Worker withdraw their provident fund balance when leaving India at the end of a posting?

Not automatically in every case. Withdrawal rules for International Workers differ from the rules for Indian employees and are tied to conditions such as retirement age, permanent incapacity, or the terms of an applicable Social Security Agreement, rather than simply to the end of Indian employment. The specific conditions should be checked against current EPFO guidance for the employee's particular circumstances before an employer or employee assumes funds can be withdrawn on departure.

Does the International Worker rule apply only to high-salary expatriates?

No. The classification applies to any foreign national employed in a covered establishment in India, regardless of salary level, subject to the excluded-employee exception. A junior foreign employee without a Certificate of Coverage is just as much an International Worker as a senior expatriate executive.

What happens if an employer stops deducting International Worker contributions because it believes the regime is unconstitutional?

That is a risky position outside the specific litigation in which a court has actually ruled in the employer's favour. If the Supreme Court eventually upholds the Delhi High Court's position, an employer that stopped complying would face liability for the unremitted contributions together with interest and possible damages for the non-compliant period, regardless of its own view on the constitutional merits.

Does the pension scheme follow the same rule as the provident fund for International Workers?

Yes. Paragraph 43A of the Employees' Pension Scheme extends comparable treatment to pension contributions, and both High Court rulings discussed in this article addressed the provident fund and pension provisions together rather than separately.
