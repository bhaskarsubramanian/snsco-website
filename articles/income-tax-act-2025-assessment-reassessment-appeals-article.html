Income Tax Disputes Under the Income-tax Act, 2025: Assessment, Reassessment and Appeals From 1 April 2026

The new Act renumbers nearly every provision a tax litigator relies on day to day, moves the reassessment time limits in opposite directions at once, and leaves every pending matter exactly where the 1961 Act left it.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 5 November 2026 · Law stated as on 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

For sixty-four years, every notice, appeal and recovery proceeding in direct tax litigation traced back to a single statute. That changed on 1 April 2026, when the Income-tax Act, 2025 took over from the Income-tax Act, 1961, and reorganised the sections a tax litigator reaches for most often under new numbers. The substance of most provisions has carried across largely intact, but the detail has not: one reassessment time limit has lengthened, another has shortened, a transitional rule decides which Act actually governs a given matter, and more than one point of real practical consequence is still being argued over in professional commentary rather than settled by any reported decision. This piece works through assessment, reassessment, appeals and stay of demand as they stand under the new Act, flagging where the renumbering is settled and where it is not.

Key points

  • The Income-tax Act, 2025 came into force on 1 April 2026 and repealed the 1961 Act, but Section 536(2) keeps any proceeding, right or liability arising before that date governed by the old Act until it is finally disposed of.
  • "Tax year" now replaces both "previous year" and "assessment year," collapsing two overlapping twelve-month labels the 1961 Act used into one.
  • Reassessment, now called "income escaping assessment," sits in Sections 279 to 286; the ordinary notice window is reported at four years from the end of the tax year, against three years under the old Act, while the extended window for escaped income of fifty lakh rupees or more is reported at six years, against ten years under the old Act.
  • No reassessment notice can be issued within one year of the end of a tax year, and the sanction of a specified authority remains mandatory before a notice issues.
  • Faceless assessment is reported to have moved from a scheme the government notified under a general delegation into a dedicated statutory provision, while schemes already notified before 1 April 2026 continue to operate under the transitional savings clause.
  • The first appeal now runs to either the Joint Commissioner (Appeals) or the Commissioner (Appeals), depending on the seniority of the officer who passed the order under challenge, with the thirty-day filing period unchanged; the second appeal to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal is reported at Section 362.
  • Filing an appeal does not by itself stop recovery. An assessee must still apply to be treated as not in default on the disputed amount, and the Tribunal's own power to stay recovery remains capped at 180 days at a time and 365 days in aggregate.

A second income tax code after sixty-four years

The Income-tax Act, 2025 received the President's assent on 21 August 2025 and took effect on 1 April 2026, replacing the Income-tax Act, 1961 as the governing statute for direct taxes in India. The project behind it was presented from the outset as a rewrite for clarity rather than a change in tax policy: removing redundant provisos and explanations, shortening long sections into tables and sub-clauses, and renumbering the Act from scratch rather than continuing the patchwork of inserted sections, sub-sections and lettered provisos the 1961 Act had accumulated over six decades of amendment.

That framing matters for how a litigator should approach the new Act. Tax rates, exemptions and the broad computation of income have, on the weight of early commentary, carried across largely as they stood, so a dispute about whether a particular receipt is taxable or a particular deduction is available will usually turn on the same substantive question it always did, now located under a different section number. Procedure is a different matter. Assessment, reassessment, appeals and recovery are exactly the areas where a renumbering exercise of this scale tends to produce real changes, sometimes deliberate and sometimes apparently incidental, and those are the areas this piece works through.

A caution belongs at the very start. The Act is new enough that very little of what follows has yet been tested by a reported court decision interpreting the 2025 Act's own text. Much of the detail below, particularly exact section numbers and time limits, is drawn from professional tax commentary rather than from the Act as directly consulted, because the government's own consolidated text was not reachable while researching this piece. Every figure used here is one on which at least two independent commentary sources agree; where sources disagreed and the disagreement could not be resolved, the point is flagged rather than stated as settled.

Which Act actually governs your matter: the Section 536 transition rule

Section 536 of the 2025 Act is the repeal and savings clause, and it is the single most important provision for anyone with an open tax matter in late 2026. Sub-section (1) repeals the Income-tax Act, 1961 outright with effect from 1 April 2026. Sub-section (2) then preserves what the repeal would otherwise disturb: any right, liability, obligation or proceeding that arose, accrued or was pending under the 1961 Act before that date continues to be governed by the 1961 Act, including its procedure for assessment, reassessment, appeal and recovery, until that specific matter is finally disposed of.

The practical test that follows from this is straightforward to state, even if applying it to a specific file takes care. If the tax year under dispute falls before the new Act's own first tax year, that is, any year that the 1961 Act would have called a previous year ending on or before 31 March 2026, the entire 1961 Act procedure continues to apply to that year's assessment, reassessment, appeal or recovery, regardless of the actual calendar date on which the notice, order or hearing takes place. A notice issued in 2027 for the previous year ending March 2025 is still a Section 148 notice in substance, tested against Section 149's time limits, Section 246A's appeal structure and Section 254's Tribunal powers, not their 2025 Act equivalents. Only tax year 2026-27 onward, the Act's own first full year, is assessed and litigated under the new numbering from the start.

Where the 2025 Act is silent on a particular transitional question that Section 536 does not expressly answer, commentary points to Section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 as the fallback, on the general principle that a repeal does not revive anything not expressly revived and does not affect anything already done or any right already acquired under the repealed enactment unless a different intention appears. A Finance Bill, 2026 proposal has also been reported as seeking to amend Section 536 itself, to clarify how carried-forward deductions, exempt income and MAT credit from years before the transition are to be treated going forward. Whether that amendment has actually been enacted could not be confirmed from the sources available, and a litigator relying on any clarification to Section 536 should check its current, enacted form before assuming it applies.

A worked example makes the test easier to apply in practice. Take an assessee whose income for the period April 2023 to March 2024, a previous year under the 1961 Act's own terminology, is sought to be reassessed through a notice issued in, say, October 2026. Because the year under dispute ended well before 1 April 2026, Section 536(2) keeps that entire proceeding under the 1961 Act: the notice is tested against Section 148 and Section 149, any appeal from the resulting order goes through Section 246A and Section 253, and any stay application is argued under Section 220(6) or Section 254(2A), not their 2025 Act counterparts, even though the notice itself physically issues after the new Act has already come into force. Only an assessee whose relevant year is tax year 2026-27, the period from April 2026 to March 2027, or a later tax year, is assessed, reassessed and appealed entirely under the new Act's numbering from the outset.

One tax year instead of two overlapping years

The 1961 Act worked with two distinct twelve-month labels for essentially linked purposes. Income was earned in a previous year, and that income was then assessed in the following assessment year; a return for income earned between April 2025 and March 2026, for instance, would be filed and assessed during assessment year 2026-27. The distinction served a purpose in the statute's own logic, but it also produced a recurring source of confusion in pleadings and even in departmental orders, where the two labels were used interchangeably or, worse, inconsistently within the same document.

The 2025 Act replaces both labels with a single concept, the tax year, defined as a twelve-month period running from 1 April to 31 March. Numerically, a tax year under the new Act corresponds to what the old Act would have called the previous year, the year in which the income was actually earned, rather than the year in which it is assessed. Tax year 2026-27, in other words, is the period from 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027, during which income is earned and which will itself be assessed in the following period. The separate assessment year label has been retired for this purpose, though the financial year label continues to appear elsewhere in the statute for other reckonings, such as notification and rule-making dates, that do not depend on when income was earned.

For a litigator reading a notice or order issued after 1 April 2026, the number that matters is which tax year the department is actually referring to, and whether that tax year is itself one governed by the new Act under the Section 536 test described above. Treating a reference to a tax year as if it followed the old assessment year's numbering convention, rather than the previous year's, risks a miscalculated limitation period in exactly the kind of dispute where limitation is often the entire case.

Faceless assessment becomes a statutory default rather than a scheme

Faceless assessment under the 1961 Act operated through a general scheme-making power, originally exercised under Section 143(3A), that let the Central Government notify a scheme eliminating the physical interface between an assessee and an assessing officer. The Faceless Assessment Scheme, 2019 and its 2021 successor were both products of that delegation, which meant the procedural detail a taxpayer actually had to comply with sat in a notification rather than in the Act itself, and challenges to that procedure often had to grapple with the scheme's own status as delegated legislation.

Commentary on the 2025 Act reports that faceless assessment has now been written directly into the statute as its own dedicated provision, within the chapter on assessment procedure, rather than left to a scheme issued under a general power. A separate, general scheme-making provision is reported to survive alongside it, letting the Central Government continue to notify modifications and adaptations needed to operate faceless and other technology-driven procedures. The practical effect for a taxpayer should be limited: the underlying workflow of document-based assessment through the e-filing portal, without a face-to-face hearing in the ordinary course, remains the default mode of assessment rather than an exception.

What should not be assumed is that this change starts every pending faceless assessment matter over from nothing. Section 536(2)'s savings provision is reported to treat schemes and actions already taken under the 1961 Act's faceless framework as continuing in effect, so a pending challenge to a faceless assessment order passed before 1 April 2026 does not become infructuous merely because the underlying statutory basis for the scheme has itself been repealed. The procedural grounds that have been argued under the old scheme, most commonly a denial of a personal hearing that the scheme itself promised, or an assessment order passed without first circulating a draft order for comment, rest on natural justice principles that do not depend on which Act happens to authorise the scheme, and should remain available in substance even where the citation changes.

Income escaping assessment: the reassessment chapter renumbered

The 1961 Act's reassessment machinery, built around Sections 147 to 153, reappears in the 2025 Act as a cluster of provisions generally described in commentary as running from Section 279 to Section 286, under the heading income escaping assessment. Section 279 is reported to carry the AO's basic power to assess or reassess income that has escaped assessment for a tax year, in substance the same power Section 147 of the 1961 Act conferred. Section 280 is reported to be the provision under which the notice itself issues once the Assessing Officer has information suggesting that income has escaped assessment, occupying the role Section 148 played under the old Act.

Before that notice can issue, Section 281 is reported to require the Assessing Officer to carry out a preliminary inquiry and to give the assessee a show-cause opportunity on whether a notice should be issued at all, a sequence that mirrors the procedure the 1961 Act introduced through Section 148A with effect from April 2021. That 2021 reform was itself a response to widespread litigation over reassessment notices issued without any prior opportunity to the assessee, and its reappearance in the new Act as a distinct procedural step, rather than being folded back into the notice provision itself, suggests the drafters treated the pre-notice inquiry as settled practice worth keeping as a separate safeguard.

A further safeguard carries across in the form of a sanction requirement. Section 284 is reported to require the approval of a specified authority before a notice under the new reassessment provisions can be issued, corresponding to the sanction the 1961 Act required under Section 151. Reassessment notices issued without the required sanction, or issued by an authority below the rank the provision specifies, have been a recurring and often successful ground of challenge under the 1961 Act, and there is no reason apparent from the sources available to expect that line of argument to lose its force simply because the sanction requirement now sits under a new section number.

What actually counts as grounds for reopening a case is itself defined rather than left to an Assessing Officer's unguided discretion, at least under the 1961 Act's post-2021 framework that Section 281's pre-notice procedure is expected to continue in substance. Explanation 1 to Section 148 of the 1961 Act treats certain categories as "information" sufficient to suggest escapement: material flagged for a given assessment year under a risk management strategy the CBDT formulates and periodically updates, a final objection raised by the Comptroller and Auditor General pointing to an assessment that has not been made in accordance with the Act, and information required to give effect to a finding or direction in an order of a Tribunal or court. A February 2025 CBDT office memorandum has also been reported as treating information arising from a search, requisition or survey as information the Assessing Officer is deemed to possess for this purpose without needing separate risk-management flagging. Courts have already begun testing the boundaries of this definition, including a Madras High Court observation describing the categories of information in the 1961 Act as exhaustive rather than illustrative, and a Delhi High Court ruling that receiving risk-management information on an assessee does not prevent the department from separately relying on search-related material found in a related group concern. None of the commentary consulted confirms whether the 2025 Act restates this definition under an identical sub-clause structure, so a practitioner should treat the underlying categories as a guide to what a notice is likely to rely on, while checking the new Act's own definitional provision directly before arguing that a notice falls outside it.

The time limits: longer at the ordinary end, shorter at the extended end

Section 282 is reported to be the 2025 Act's time-limit provision for reassessment, corresponding to Section 149 of the 1961 Act. The ordinary limitation period under Section 282 is reported at four years from the end of the relevant tax year, which is one year longer than the three-year ordinary period Section 149(1)(a) of the 1961 Act prescribed after the 2021 reform. An extended period is reported to apply where the Assessing Officer has evidence that income escaping assessment, represented in the form of an asset, expenditure on a transaction or event, or an entry in the books of account, amounts to fifty lakh rupees or more; that extended period under the 2025 Act is reported at six years from the end of the tax year, in place of the ten-year outer limit Section 149(1)(b) of the 1961 Act allowed for the same fifty-lakh threshold.

Read together, these two figures move in opposite directions from where the 1961 Act left them. The window available for an ordinary reassessment, below the fifty-lakh threshold, has lengthened by a year. The window available for a high-value reassessment, at or above that threshold, has shortened by four years. For a department used to relying on the old ten-year outer limit in asset-linked cases, and for an assessee used to treating anything beyond three years as presumptively safe unless the fifty-lakh threshold is clearly met, both assumptions need resetting once the dispute concerns a tax year governed by the new Act.

Two further rules are reported alongside the main time limits. No notice under Section 280 or Section 281 may be issued within one year of the end of the relevant tax year, a floor that did not have a direct equivalent stated this way under the 1961 Act's own structure. And Section 283 is reported to allow a notice to be issued at any time, notwithstanding the limits in Section 282, where the notice is needed to give effect to a finding or direction in an order passed in appeal, reference, revision or by a court, again mirroring a carve-out the 1961 Act recognised for giving effect to appellate directions.

None of this body of commentary yet rests on a reported decision actually interpreting Section 282 itself, since a tax year governed by the new Act could not have produced a reassessment dispute reaching final appellate scrutiny by the time of writing. The extensive case law built up around Section 149(1)(b) of the 1961 Act, including disagreement among High Courts on whether the fifty-lakh threshold can be reached by aggregating income across more than one year and on whether the extended period applies to years that had already become time-barred before the 2021 reform took effect, remains directly relevant to any tax year that the Section 536 transition rule leaves under the old Act, which in practice will be most of the reassessment disputes actually being litigated for some years yet.

The first appeal: a two-track structure by officer rank

Appeals, revisions and alternate dispute resolution are grouped in the 2025 Act under a chapter commentary generally numbers from around Section 356 onward. Section 356 is reported to provide for an appeal to the Joint Commissioner (Appeals) against orders passed by officers up to a certain rank, while Section 356(2) is reported to bar that route specifically where the order under challenge was passed by, or with the prior approval of, an income-tax authority above the rank of Deputy Commissioner; in that situation the appeal is reported to lie instead to the Commissioner (Appeals) under Section 357.

This two-track structure, splitting what the 1961 Act's Section 246A handled as a single consolidated list of appealable orders into two parallel appellate authorities distinguished by the seniority of the officer who passed the order, continues a distinction the 1961 Act itself had already introduced through the Finance Act, 2023 creation of the Joint Commissioner (Appeals) tier. A litigator filing a first appeal under the new Act should therefore check, as a first and basic step, exactly which officer, and at what rank, actually passed the order under challenge, since filing before the wrong one of the two authorities is the kind of avoidable error that can cost real time to correct.

The filing window itself is reported to be unchanged across this transition. Section 358 is reported to set a thirty-day limitation period running from the date the order is communicated to the appellant, matching the thirty-day period the 1961 Act's Section 249(2) prescribed, with a discretionary power to condone delay on sufficient cause shown. Section 359 is reported to carry over the first appellate authority's procedural powers, including the power to adjourn a hearing, in substantially the same terms as before.

Second appeal to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal

An order passed by either the Joint Commissioner (Appeals) or the Commissioner (Appeals) can be carried further on appeal to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, a route reported to sit at Section 362 of the 2025 Act, corresponding to Section 253 of the 1961 Act. The Tribunal's basic jurisdiction, to hear and decide an appeal on both fact and law from the first appellate authority's order, is reported to carry across without substantive change.

The limitation period for filing that second appeal is one of the few points on which the available commentary genuinely disagrees rather than merely varying in emphasis. One line of commentary treats the familiar sixty-day period, running from the date the order is communicated, as unchanged from the 1961 Act's own practice. Another describes the period as recast in terms of two months from the end of the month in which the order is communicated, which produces a materially different, and in most cases longer, deadline than a straight sixty-day count would. Given that disagreement, and the absence of any reported decision resolving it, the safer course for a litigator is to calculate the deadline both ways and file by whichever date comes first, rather than rely on either reading until the point is settled by the Tribunal or a court.

The Tribunal's order is reported to be final under Section 362's governing sub-section, save as otherwise provided in a separate section dealing with rectification or reference that this piece could not independently confirm the content of from the sources available, and accordingly does not describe further. That finality, subject to whatever narrow exceptions the Act preserves, mirrors the 1961 Act's own treatment of a Tribunal order as the last word on fact and the practical end of the ordinary appellate road short of a substantial question of law reaching the High Court.

Stay of demand: an appeal is not itself a shield against recovery

A principle that carries across unchanged, and that is worth stating plainly because taxpayers routinely assume otherwise, is that the tax demanded by an assessment order remains payable notwithstanding that an appeal against that order is pending. A provision reported at Section 369 of the 2025 Act, corresponding to Section 265 of the 1961 Act, states this directly: filing an appeal does not, by itself, suspend the obligation to pay.

What an assessee actually needs, to avoid recovery proceeding while an appeal is heard, is a specific stay, and the mechanism for that is reported to sit at Section 411, corresponding to Section 220 of the 1961 Act, which sets the time allowed for payment after a notice of demand and the consequences of default. Within that provision, a sub-section reported at Section 411(12), corresponding to Section 220(6), lets the Assessing Officer, usually on the assessee's application and typically subject to conditions, treat the assessee as not in default in respect of the amount in dispute for so long as the first appeal remains pending. This is a discretionary, administrative form of relief granted by the same officer whose order is under challenge, not an automatic consequence of having filed the appeal, and it is commonly, though not invariably, conditioned on a partial payment or deposit.

A separate provision reported at Section 415, corresponding to Section 225 of the 1961 Act, governs the Tax Recovery Officer's own power to stay recovery proceedings once they have begun. Under the 1961 Act, departmental instructions setting a commonly cited deposit figure, often reported as twenty per cent of the disputed demand, have themselves been the subject of litigation where field officers were found to be demanding more than the instruction actually permitted. Nothing in the sources consulted for this piece confirms whether an equivalent instruction has been reissued to apply specifically to the 2025 Act's renumbered provisions, and that is a point worth checking against the Central Board of Direct Taxes' current instructions before assuming any particular percentage in a live matter.

Stay before the Tribunal: the 180-day and 365-day limits

Where a stay of recovery is sought not from the Assessing Officer but from the Tribunal itself, pending the second appeal, the governing provision is reported at Section 363 of the 2025 Act. Commentary describes the Tribunal as empowered to grant a stay for a period not exceeding 180 days, conditioned on the assessee either depositing at least twenty per cent of the disputed tax or furnishing security of an equivalent amount, with the expectation that the appeal itself will be disposed of within that period.

Where the appeal cannot be disposed of in time, the stay may be extended, but the aggregate of the original period and any extension is reported to be capped at 365 days in total, a limit the 1961 Act's Section 254(2A) had itself carried since the Finance Act, 2020 amendment removed an earlier open-ended extension power. If the appeal is still not disposed of once the permitted period, original or extended, runs out, the stay is reported to stand vacated automatically, regardless of why the appeal remains pending.

That automatic vacating has generated real friction under the identically structured 1961 Act provision, because a Tribunal's own listing backlog, rather than any default by the assessee, is frequently the actual reason an appeal is not disposed of within 365 days. The Supreme Court, in DCIT v. Pepsi Foods Ltd, decided 6 April 2021, struck down as unconstitutional the part of Section 254(2A) of the 1961 Act that caused a stay to vacate automatically on completion of 365 days, holding that the 365-day outer limit is not absolute where the delay in disposing of the appeal is not attributable to the assessee; the Allahabad High Court has since applied that reasoning to orders under the same provision. Whether that reasoning carries over to Section 363 of the 2025 Act is, at the time of writing, untested, since no reported decision yet interprets the new provision directly, and a practitioner relying on it for a tax year governed by the new Act should treat the point as arguable rather than settled until a court actually says so.

An assessee seeking a stay before the Tribunal, in the meantime, gains little by waiting for that question to be settled elsewhere. The more reliable course is the one that worked under the 1961 Act's own Section 254(2A): apply early, before the bulk of the 180-day period has run; be ready to deposit, or to offer security for, the twenty per cent figure rather than arguing against it as a threshold matter; and press the Tribunal itself to list the appeal for final hearing well inside the stay period, since a stay order secured but allowed to lapse through inattention to the Tribunal's own cause list achieves nothing once the 365-day ceiling is reached, however the attribution-of-delay argument might eventually be resolved for someone else's case.

Matching the right Act to the right tax year: a practical sequence

Given the transitional structure described above, the first question in any matter touching a tax year on either side of the 1 April 2026 boundary should be which Act actually governs it, not which Act happens to be in force on the date the notice or order was issued. If the dispute concerns a tax year that the 1961 Act would have called a previous year ending on or before 31 March 2026, the full 1961 Act machinery, its own reassessment time limits, its own appeal structure through Sections 246A and 253, and its own Section 220 and Section 254(2A) stay powers, continues to apply by force of Section 536(2), whatever the calendar date of the actual proceeding.

Only once that threshold question is settled does it make sense to turn to the specific provisions this piece has worked through. For a reassessment notice concerning a tax year governed by the new Act, the limitation calculation should start from Section 282's reported four-year ordinary period or six-year extended period, not from the superseded three-year and ten-year figures that remain correct only for years still under the old Act. For an appeal, the rank of the officer who passed the order under challenge decides whether Section 356 or Section 357 is the correct forum, and the thirty-day filing clock runs the same way it always did. For recovery, the working assumption should be that an appeal alone buys no protection, and that a specific application, to the Assessing Officer under Section 411(12) or to the Tribunal under Section 363, is needed to actually stop the department's hand.

Because several of the figures involved, the Tribunal limitation period chief among them, remain genuinely contested in the available commentary, a cautious practitioner should treat any single source's statement of a section number or a time limit as a starting point for verification against the Act's own text, rather than as a settled fact, until the body of reported decisions interpreting the 2025 Act directly has had time to grow.

A simple drafting habit helps while that body of case law is still thin. A notice, reply, appeal memo or stay application concerning a tax year that straddles the two regimes is easier to defend, and easier for a bench unfamiliar with the new numbering to follow, if it cites both the 2025 Act's reported section and its 1961 Act equivalent together, at least until a settled cross-reference becomes common knowledge at the bar. A short internal table mapping the sections discussed in this piece, Section 279 to 147, Section 280 to 148, Section 281 to 148A, Section 282 to 149, Section 356 and 357 to 246A, Section 362 to 253, Section 363 to 254(2A), Section 369 to 265, Section 411 to 220 and Section 415 to 225, costs little to keep on file and avoids the kind of citation confusion that a bench, used to sixty-four years of a single numbering convention, is unlikely to forgive easily in the first few years of the new one.

The Income-tax Act, 2025 was presented as a simplification exercise, and for a reader concerned only with substantive tax liability, much of it probably is. For a litigator, the renumbering carries real consequences that go beyond finding the right section to cite. A reassessment window that used to run as long as ten years in high-value cases now appears capped at six, while the ordinary window has grown by a year; an appeal that used to follow a single track now splits by the seniority of the officer who passed the order; and a Tribunal stay that already carried a hard 365-day ceiling under the old Act carries the same ceiling, with the same unresolved tension between that ceiling and delay that is nobody's fault, under the new one.

None of that matters, in a given file, until the transition question is answered first. Section 536 decides which Act's limitation periods, appeal structure and stay powers actually apply to a specific tax year, and getting that threshold question wrong, by assuming the new Act's numbers apply simply because the calendar has moved past 1 April 2026, is the single most avoidable error this transition creates.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Act applies to my pending income tax matter now that the Income-tax Act, 2025 is in force?

It depends on the tax year involved, not on today's date. Section 536(2) of the 2025 Act preserves the Income-tax Act, 1961 for any right, liability or proceeding that arose before 1 April 2026, so a dispute concerning a year the 1961 Act would have called a previous year ending on or before 31 March 2026 continues to be governed by the 1961 Act's own reassessment, appeal and recovery provisions, even if the actual notice, order or hearing takes place well after that date.

What is a tax year under the new Act, and how is it different from an assessment year?

A tax year is a single twelve-month period from 1 April to 31 March, and it replaces both the previous year and the assessment year labels the 1961 Act used. Numerically, a tax year corresponds to what the old Act called the previous year, the year income was actually earned, rather than the following year in which that income used to be assessed. Confusing the new tax year numbering with the old assessment year numbering risks miscalculating a limitation period that runs from the end of the tax year.

Has faceless assessment been removed under the Income-tax Act, 2025?

No. Faceless assessment is reported to have moved from a scheme the government issued under a general delegated power into a dedicated provision within the Act itself, but the underlying document-based assessment process, without an ordinary face-to-face hearing, remains the default mode. Schemes and actions already taken under the 1961 Act's faceless framework before 1 April 2026 are reported to continue under the Section 536 savings clause, so a pending challenge does not lapse merely because the statutory basis changed.

How long does the department have to issue a reassessment notice under the new Act?

For a tax year governed by the 2025 Act, the ordinary limitation period is reported at four years from the end of the relevant tax year, one year longer than the three-year period that applied under the 1961 Act after its 2021 reform. An extended period, reported at six years, applies where the escaped income represented as an asset, expenditure or book entry is fifty lakh rupees or more, which is shorter than the ten-year outer limit the 1961 Act allowed for the same threshold. For a tax year still governed by the 1961 Act under the Section 536 transition rule, the old three-year and ten-year figures continue to apply instead.

Do I appeal to the Joint Commissioner (Appeals) or the Commissioner (Appeals) first?

That depends on who passed the order you are challenging. The appeal generally goes to the Joint Commissioner (Appeals), but it is reported to be barred specifically where the order was passed by, or with the prior approval of, an officer above the rank of Deputy Commissioner, in which case the appeal lies instead to the Commissioner (Appeals). Checking the rank of the officer who actually signed the order is the first thing to do before filing.

If I file an appeal, do I still have to pay the disputed tax in the meantime?

Yes, unless you obtain a specific stay. A provision reported at Section 369 states that tax demanded remains payable notwithstanding a pending appeal, and filing the appeal does not by itself suspend recovery. Relief from recovery has to be sought separately, either from the Assessing Officer under the provision reported at Section 411(12), or from the Tribunal under Section 363 once the matter reaches that stage, and is usually conditioned on a partial deposit or security.

Can the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal grant a stay of recovery for as long as my appeal takes?

Not indefinitely. The Tribunal's stay power, reported at Section 363, runs for up to 180 days at a time, conditioned on a deposit or security of at least twenty per cent of the disputed tax, and the total of the original period plus any extension cannot exceed 365 days. Under the identically structured provision of the 1961 Act, at least one High Court has held that this outer limit is not absolute where the delay in deciding the appeal is not the assessee's fault, though whether that reasoning will be applied to the new Act's provision has not yet been tested in a reported decision.

Does the new Act change whether I actually owe the tax, or only where I file my case?

For most disputes, it changes procedure rather than substance. The computation of income, the availability of deductions and the applicable tax rates have, on current commentary, carried across largely unchanged, with the real differences concentrated in assessment procedure, reassessment time limits, the appellate structure and recovery and stay provisions, which is why this article focuses on those areas rather than on substantive tax questions.
