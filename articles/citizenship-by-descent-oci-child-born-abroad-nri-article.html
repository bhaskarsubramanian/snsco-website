Citizenship by Descent or OCI? What an NRI Parent Must Do for a Child Born Abroad

Section 4 of the Citizenship Act sets a one-year clock running from the day a child is born outside India, and missing it can turn a simple registration into a lifetime of paperwork

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Chandrayee Roy, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 27 November 2026 · Law stated as on 26 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An NRI couple in London or Toronto who has just had a child rarely thinks of a hospital birth certificate as the start of a legal deadline. Under the Citizenship Act, 1955, it often is. Whether that child becomes an Indian citizen by descent, or is instead left with Overseas Citizen of India status as the only practical option, turns on a one-year consular registration window that most new parents never hear about until it has already closed.

Key points

  • A child born outside India to an Indian citizen parent is a citizen of India by descent only if the birth is registered at an Indian consulate within one year of birth, or later with the Central Government's permission under Section 4(1) of the Citizenship Act, 1955.
  • For a birth on or after 3 December 2004, the parents must also give a written undertaking that the minor does not hold the passport of another country at the time of registration.
  • Missing the consular window does not merely delay the paperwork. The Gujarat High Court held in Dolly Khilankumar Vadalia v. Union of India (judgment dated 30 April 2026) that a woman born in Mozambique in 2000 to Indian parents could not claim citizenship by descent decades later because her birth was never registered at a consulate, whatever the hardship behind that omission.
  • If the one-year window and the later permission route are both closed, the child's practical status in India is Overseas Citizen of India, not Indian citizenship, and the two carry materially different rights.
  • An OCI card gives most of the economic, residential and educational rights an NRI enjoys, but it does not carry voting rights, eligibility for government or constitutional office, or the right to acquire agricultural land, farmhouse or plantation property.
  • The Citizenship (Amendment) Rules require specific documentary proof of the parents' Indian origin for a minor's OCI application, and the chain of proof becomes harder to assemble the longer a family waits.
  • Divorced or separated parents face an added layer: OCI applications for a minor need either a joint consent or a custody order that names the applying parent as the one with legal custody.
  • Registering a child's birth at the nearest Indian consulate within the first year remains the only step that keeps every future option, citizenship and OCI alike, open without a fight.

A deadline new parents do not know exists

Most Indian-origin parents living abroad assume that their own citizenship simply passes to a child born in a foreign hospital. In some legal systems that assumption would be correct. Indian law does not work that way. Citizenship by descent under the Citizenship Act, 1955 is conditional, and the condition is procedural rather than biological: the parent's citizenship status matters, but so does a registration step that has nothing to do with the birth certificate the hospital issues.

A recurring pattern follows from this gap in awareness. Families typically address the question only years after a child's birth, usually when applying for an Indian passport, a school admission that asks for proof of citizenship, or a property transaction that needs the child's status settled. By then the one-year window referred to in Section 4(1) of the Act has long closed, and the options left are narrower and slower than they would have been at the time of birth.

This piece sets out what Section 4 actually requires, what the Central Government's discretionary permission route looks like once the one-year period has passed, and what Overseas Citizen of India status offers when citizenship itself is no longer realistically available. The two tracks, citizenship by descent and OCI, are frequently confused with each other. They are governed by different parts of the Act, carry different rights, and call for different paperwork.

None of this is academic for a family weighing where to settle, where to educate a child, or how to structure property that will eventually pass to the next generation. A child's citizenship status touches banking, schooling, inheritance and the right to hold certain kinds of property long before anyone in the family thinks of it as a legal question at all, which is exactly why getting it addressed early, rather than only when a specific transaction forces the point, saves a great deal of difficulty later.

Citizenship by birth and citizenship by descent are not the same door

Section 3 of the Citizenship Act deals with citizenship by birth, and it applies to a person born in India. A child born in a hospital in New Jersey or Dubai never enters through that door at all, however Indian the parents' documents may be. The relevant provision for such a child is Section 4, titled citizenship by descent, and it has its own, separate set of conditions.

Section 4(1) provides that a person born outside India on or after 26 January 1950 is a citizen of India by descent if, at the time of birth, either parent was a citizen of India (the rule requiring specifically the father's citizenship, rather than either parent's, applied to births before 10 December 1992 and was changed by amendment). That sounds straightforward until the next layer appears: a proviso that restricts how far descent can run.

Where the parent through whom citizenship is claimed was themselves a citizen of India only by descent, the child does not get citizenship by descent at all unless that parent's own birth was itself registered at an Indian consulate within one year, or the parent was, at the time of the child's birth, in Indian government service, or the Central Government otherwise certifies the child as a citizen. In plain terms, descent-based citizenship does not automatically run on indefinitely from generation to generation abroad. A family that has been outside India for two generations needs to check which generation actually holds citizenship otherwise than by descent, because that is usually the link the next generation's claim depends on.

This generational limit explains a pattern we see in second-generation NRI families more often than in first-generation ones. Parents who themselves moved abroad as adults, while already Indian citizens in their own right rather than by descent, generally pass an unrestricted claim to their children. Parents who were themselves born abroad to Indian parents, and who hold citizenship by descent, need to look closely at whether their own birth was properly registered before assuming their children's claim is automatically available on the same footing.

The one-year consular registration clock

Even where a parent's own citizenship is not itself limited by descent, Section 4(1) carries a second, independent condition that catches far more families than the generational proviso does. The birth of the child must be registered at an Indian consulate within one year of the date of birth, or, for a child born on or after the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003 came into force, within one year of that commencement date if that is later. After that window, registration is possible only with the permission of the Central Government.

This is not a formality that can be completed at leisure once the family happens to visit the consulate for an unrelated matter. It is a limitation period in substance, even though the Act does not use that word. The Gujarat High Court's decision in Dolly Khilankumar Vadalia v. Union of India illustrates how unforgiving the provision can be in practice. The petitioner was born in Xai-Xai, Mozambique in February 2000 to Indian citizen parents, and the family left Mozambique within weeks because of political and economic conditions there, returning to India on an emergency travel document. She grew up in Rajkot, held an Aadhaar card, a PAN and a voter identity card, and only discovered decades later, when she applied for an Indian passport, that her birth had never been registered at an Indian consulate. In its judgment dated 30 April 2026, the Gujarat High Court held that this omission meant she could not establish citizenship by descent, however long she had lived in India and however complete her other identity documents appeared.

For a child born on or after 3 December 2004, the Act adds a further condition at the registration stage itself: the parents must give a written undertaking, typically on the prescribed Form, that the minor does not hold the passport of another country. Registration under Section 4(1) is therefore not simply a matter of walking into the consulate with a birth certificate. It needs the parents' passports, proof of their own Indian citizenship or origin, the child's foreign birth certificate, and, where applicable, the no-other-passport declaration, all assembled within a single calendar year of the birth.

Families sometimes ask whether the one-year period runs from the date of birth shown on the foreign certificate or from the date the certificate is actually issued, since hospitals and civil registries abroad can take weeks to produce the final document. The statute is clear that the period runs from the date of birth itself, not from the date of any paperwork about it, which is precisely why delay in obtaining the foreign birth certificate is not treated as a separate excuse for missing the consular deadline.

Missing the window: the Central Government's permission route

Section 4(1) does allow registration after the first year, but only with the Central Government's permission, through the Ministry of Home Affairs or the mission concerned acting on its behalf. This is a discretionary gateway, not a right, and the Act does not set out a fixed list of grounds on which permission must be granted. In practice, missions tend to ask for a detailed explanation of the delay, supporting evidence of the parents' continuous Indian citizenship and connection to India, and often evidence that the family did not deliberately allow the child to acquire or retain another country's citizenship in the interim.

The Ministry of Home Affairs has, in communications reported in litigation on this point, continued to take the position that consular registration remains a necessary step for a citizenship-by-descent claim, delayed permission included, rather than something that can be dispensed with once a family can show long residence in India or possession of other Indian identity documents such as Aadhaar or a PAN card. Those documents establish identity for their own statutory purposes. They do not, by themselves, establish citizenship.

A family that has missed the one-year window should not assume the position is hopeless, but should also not assume that documents like a long-held Indian passport issued in error, a voter card, or school records will carry the argument on their own. The application for delayed registration needs to be built around the two things the Act actually asks for: proof of the parent's citizenship at the relevant time, and a credible account of why consular registration did not happen within the first year. Where that permission is refused, the practical alternative is OCI, which does not look at the one-year window at all.

It is worth being realistic about timelines here as well. A delayed-registration application routed through the Ministry of Home Affairs does not move on a fixed statutory clock the way the one-year window itself does. Unlike ordinary consular registration, there is no prescribed turnaround time once discretion comes into play, and the file can take materially longer where it needs to be checked against old records or where an earlier, unrelated passport application created inconsistencies that now need to be explained before the Ministry will act on it.

When citizenship is out of reach: Overseas Citizen of India status

Overseas Citizen of India status is governed by Sections 7A to 7D of the Citizenship Act, inserted after the earlier Person of Indian Origin card scheme was merged into it, and by the Citizenship (Amendment) Rules made under the Act. It is not, and has never been, a form of dual citizenship. India does not recognise dual citizenship for any person, and an OCI card is better understood as a long-term, multiple-entry immigration status layered with a defined set of economic and civil rights, available to a person of Indian origin or, in the case of a minor, to the child of an OCI cardholder or of an Indian citizen.

For the family whose child has missed the Section 4 consular window, and whose application for delayed registration is refused or simply not pursued, OCI registration is the practical route that remains. It is also, for many families, a deliberate choice rather than a fallback: a child who will hold a foreign passport throughout childhood and may never permanently relocate to India often does not need the heavier administrative obligations that come with Indian citizenship, such as the requirement to surrender a foreign passport on turning 18 if citizenship were ever claimed.

What an OCI minor gets is substantial. The card confers a lifelong, multiple-entry visa to India, exemption from the foreigner registration requirements that apply to other visa holders, and parity with non-resident Indians on most financial and property matters, including the right to acquire residential and commercial immovable property. What it does not confer is equally important to set out plainly, because families sometimes discover the limits only when a specific transaction runs into them.

Eligibility for a minor's OCI registration generally follows the parents' own status: a child is eligible if either parent is already an Indian citizen, or if either parent is already an OCI cardholder in their own right, or if a grandparent was an Indian citizen and the parent is eligible for OCI on that basis even if the parent has not yet taken out a card. Eligibility is not open-ended, however. The Act excludes persons who were, or whose parents or grandparents were, citizens of Pakistan or Bangladesh at any time, and a handful of other categories the Central Government may specify, so a family with a Pakistani or Bangladeshi branch in its ancestry should check this exclusion carefully before assuming OCI is automatically available.

What OCI does not give a child, and why it matters later

An OCI cardholder, minor or adult, cannot vote in Indian elections, cannot hold a seat in Parliament or a state legislature, and is not eligible for appointment to constitutional posts or, with limited exceptions, to public services and posts under the Government of India. These restrictions rarely matter while the child is young, but they surface later if the family starts thinking about the child eventually working in government service in India or standing for any public office.

Property is the other area where the gap shows up concretely. An OCI cardholder can buy and hold residential and commercial property on broadly the same footing as an NRI, but cannot purchase agricultural land, a farmhouse or plantation property. Inheritance works differently from purchase: an OCI cardholder can inherit agricultural land from a resident Indian relative, though selling that inherited land generally requires transferring it to a resident Indian buyer rather than to another non-resident or OCI holder. Families who expect an OCI-holding child to eventually inherit ancestral agricultural land should plan around this restriction well before the question of a will or a family settlement actually arises.

A further practical point, often missed, is that OCI status is not immune from larger policy shifts. The scheme has been narrowed by notification in the past for specific categories of applicants, and an OCI card can be cancelled under the Act on grounds that include fraud in the original application, conduct prejudicial to India's sovereignty or security, or conviction for a serious offence within a prescribed period of registration. None of this is a reason to avoid OCI where it is the available option. It is a reason to keep the underlying documentation, including the proof of the parents' Indian origin on which the grant rested, in good order rather than treating the card as a closed file once issued.

There is also a renewal step that catches some families by surprise. An OCI card issued to a minor, unlike a citizenship grant, is linked to the minor's passport, and the re-issue rules around a new passport have changed more than once in recent years, most recently with an e-OCI card introduced in mid-2026. Parents who treat the original card as a one-time, lifetime document sometimes discover later, usually when the child is about to travel on a renewed passport, that the current rule needs to be checked afresh rather than assumed from what applied when the card was first issued.

Documents and process for a minor's OCI registration

An OCI application for a child born abroad is built around proving two separate facts: that the child was born to parents connected to India, and that the parents themselves are, or were, Indian citizens or persons of Indian origin. The core document set, drawn from the checklists Indian missions and VFS Global centres publish for OCI services, includes the child's foreign birth certificate naming both parents (a hospital-issued certificate is typically not accepted on its own; the civil registration certificate is what is required), current passport copies of both parents, and documentary proof of the parents' Indian origin.

That proof of Indian origin can be an Indian passport held at any time, a domicile certificate, a nativity certificate from the competent authority in India, or the parents' own PIO or OCI cards if they hold them. Where none of these is available, missions will generally accept a documented family-lineage chain establishing the connection to India, though an application built entirely on lineage evidence, without any of the primary documents, is noticeably harder to get through and takes longer to process.

A signed parental authorisation, usually notarised and executed by both parents, is a standard requirement, since a minor cannot sign the application. Where the parents are divorced or separated, missions typically ask for the decree of dissolution together with a custody order or arrangement that specifically names the parent applying for OCI as the parent with legal custody of the child; where custody is joint, a notarised consent letter from both parents, or an order that addresses the point expressly, is usually needed instead. Name discrepancies between documents, which are common where a child's name has been recorded differently on a foreign birth certificate and on a parent's passport, are generally resolved with a court-issued name-change order or a notarised affidavit of identity, rather than left unaddressed and hoped past.

The apostille and document-authentication layer

Documents executed or issued abroad, whether for a delayed citizenship registration application or for an OCI application, generally need the same authentication treatment that applies to any foreign document being used for an Indian legal purpose: apostille where the country of origin is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, or consular legalisation where it is not. Missions have, in recent practice, been specific about the form that authentication must take, with at least one consular checklist expressly stating that only a paper apostille is accepted and that an eApostille will not be.

A second, equally practical point is that missions usually ask for self-attested copies of the apostilled documents rather than the apostilled originals themselves, reserving the originals for the applicant to retain and produce only if specifically asked. Families who send original apostilled documents by courier, assuming that is what is wanted, sometimes create an avoidable complication when those documents need to be tracked, retrieved or resubmitted.

None of this authentication work can be done at the last minute if the family is also racing the one-year registration clock under Section 4(1). A birth certificate that needs to be apostilled in a foreign jurisdiction can itself take weeks to process depending on the country, and that delay eats directly into the window available for consular registration. Families who are even considering a return to India, or who are simply unsure whether they want the child to hold Indian citizenship eventually, are better served starting the authentication and registration process within the first few months after birth rather than waiting to decide.

Deciding between pursuing delayed citizenship and simply taking OCI

Where the one-year window has already closed, families sometimes ask whether it is worth pursuing the Central Government's delayed-registration permission for citizenship by descent, rather than accepting OCI as the practical outcome. The honest answer depends on what the family actually needs the status for. If the underlying motivation is property rights, ease of travel, or the ability to study and work in India without a separate visa, OCI delivers almost everything that matters, and it is a far faster and more predictable process than a discretionary permission application that can be refused without a detailed right of appeal.

If the underlying motivation includes something OCI structurally cannot provide, most commonly a future in government service, eligibility for public office, or the ability to hold agricultural land in the child's own name rather than only by inheritance, then pursuing the delayed registration route, difficult as it is, may be the only way to get there. Families in this position should go in with realistic expectations: the Vadalia decision shows that courts read the one-year requirement strictly, and sympathy for the family's circumstances, including the fact that the omission happened when the child was an infant and not through any fault of the child, has not so far been treated as a basis to set the requirement aside.

A middle path some families adopt is to secure OCI promptly, so the child's practical rights are not left in limbo for years while a citizenship application is argued, and to pursue the delayed registration separately and without urgency. The two are not mutually exclusive at the application stage, though a grant of citizenship by descent would eventually supersede the need for an OCI card, which exists only for a person who is not an Indian citizen.

A practical roadmap for NRI parents

The single most useful thing a new NRI parent can do, as a matter of ordinary family practice rather than crisis management, is to register the child's birth at the nearest Indian consulate within the first few months, well inside the one-year limit the Act allows. This preserves citizenship by descent as a live option without foreclosing anything else, and it avoids the later, harder conversation about whether a discretionary permission application is worth the time and expense.

Where more than one year has already passed, the family should first establish, calmly and with documents rather than assumptions, exactly which parent's citizenship the claim would run through, and whether that parent holds citizenship by descent only or in some other capacity, since that affects whether the generational proviso in Section 4(1) is even in play. Only once that is clear does it make sense to weigh a delayed-registration application against simply proceeding with OCI.

Families who are uncertain, or who are dealing with a mix of circumstances such as a parent's own citizenship status being unclear, a divorce, or a child who already holds a foreign passport obtained without the no-other-passport declaration being considered at the time, are better served getting a considered opinion before filing anything. An application that is filed on an incomplete or mistaken legal basis does not simply get refused and leave the position unchanged; it can create a paper trail that makes the next application, whichever route is chosen, harder to run.

It is also worth keeping a simple family file from the outset rather than relying on memory years later: the child's foreign birth certificate and its apostille, both parents' passports as they stood at the time of birth, any consulate acknowledgment of registration (or, if registration did not happen, a note of why), and copies of whatever was eventually filed for OCI or citizenship. None of this is required by the Act itself as a standing obligation, but a status dispute that surfaces years later is almost always harder to resolve precisely because this basic paper trail was never kept together in one place from the start.

A family with one Indian-origin parent and one parent of no Indian origin

The analysis changes in degree, though not in its basic structure, where only one parent is an Indian citizen or of Indian origin and the other is not. Section 4(1) still looks at the Indian parent's citizenship status at the time of birth in the same way, and the one-year consular registration requirement applies regardless of the other parent's nationality. The practical difficulty in these families is usually documentary rather than legal: proving the Indian parent's own citizenship and, where relevant, their parents' citizenship, can take longer when records sit across two countries and two naming conventions.

Where the family eventually decides that OCI, rather than citizenship by descent, is the right fit for the child, the same rule applies as for a child of two Indian-origin parents: the application needs to establish Indian origin through at least one parent, and the documents listing both parents on the foreign birth certificate remain central to that proof even where one parent's side of the family has no connection to India at all.

A separate, frequently overlooked point concerns the child's name. Many countries, and the United States in particular, place few restrictions on how a child's name is recorded, while Indian documentation practice is comparatively exacting about matching names across the birth certificate, the passport application and any later citizenship or OCI filing. Families with a parent of non-Indian origin, where naming conventions between the two cultures differ, are well advised to settle the child's name formally and consistently across every document created in the first year, rather than adjusting it later once a citizenship or OCI file is already open.

Banking, schooling and the practical gap while status is unresolved

Parents often ask what a child can and cannot do in India while citizenship or OCI status remains unresolved, typically because an unrelated transaction, such as a bank account, a school admission, or inheritance under a grandparent's will, has forced the question into the open. The honest answer is that very little can be done cleanly. A minor who is neither an Indian citizen nor an OCI cardholder is treated as a foreign national for most purposes, which affects everything from the kind of bank account that can be opened in the child's name to the fees and category under which a school admission is processed.

This is one of the more common triggers for families to finally address a citizenship or OCI application years after the child's birth, and it is also why the gap matters in practice rather than remaining a theoretical compliance point. A grandparent who wants to leave property or funds to a grandchild who has neither status will usually still be able to do so as a matter of succession law, but the grandchild's ability to deal with that inheritance, open an account to receive it, or register property in their own name, depends on first resolving whether they hold Indian citizenship, OCI, or neither.

Families who are planning a longer stay in India with a child who holds neither status, for example around a work posting or a sabbatical, should also check the visa the child will need to enter and remain in India, since OCI's chief practical advantage over an ordinary visa is precisely that it removes the need for this separate immigration step on every visit. A tourist or entry visa obtained for a single trip will not substitute for OCI if the family's plans change midway and the stay becomes longer than originally intended, and extending or converting that visa from within India is its own separate process with its own delays.

How this differs from an adult giving up Indian citizenship

It helps to keep this discussion separate from a question that looks related but runs on entirely different legal machinery: what happens when an adult Indian citizen takes up a foreign citizenship and moves to OCI status later in life. Under Section 9(1) of the Citizenship Act, an Indian citizen who voluntarily acquires another country's citizenship ceases to be an Indian citizen from the date that foreign citizenship is acquired, and that person then typically applies for a renunciation certificate and an OCI card afterwards.

A child born abroad to NRI parents has never held Indian citizenship in the first place, so Section 9 has no application at all. The child's position is governed only by Section 4, and the question is not whether citizenship was given up, but whether it was ever acquired. This distinction matters practically because advice correctly given to an adult renouncing citizenship, for instance about surrendering an Indian passport or timing a renunciation certificate, does not transfer across to the question of a newborn's status, and families sometimes conflate the two simply because both end at the same OCI card.

The timing also cuts the other way in the child's favour compared with an adult's position. An adult who has already built a life around Indian citizenship, often with property, bank accounts and official documents issued on that basis, faces a genuinely difficult unwinding exercise on taking foreign citizenship. A newborn child has no such history to unwind. The one-year window under Section 4 is, in that sense, a much smaller and more manageable task than anything an adult in the renunciation process has to go through, provided the family acts on it promptly rather than discovering the deadline only when it has already passed.

The hardest part of this area of law is not any single provision; it is the fact that families rarely know, at the moment a child is born abroad, that a clock has started running. Section 4 of the Citizenship Act gives a generous enough window, one full year, for the overwhelming majority of NRI families to register a child's birth and keep citizenship by descent available without any difficulty at all.

Where that window has already closed, the law still leaves a path through either a discretionary delayed-registration application or Overseas Citizen of India status, and the right choice between them depends on what the family actually needs the status to do for the child, not on which one sounds more prestigious.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a child born abroad to Indian citizen parents automatically an Indian citizen?

Not automatically. Citizenship by descent under Section 4 of the Citizenship Act, 1955 depends on the parent's citizenship status at the time of birth and, critically, on registering the birth at an Indian consulate within one year, or later only with the Central Government's permission. Without that registration, the child does not acquire Indian citizenship even if a parent is unquestionably an Indian citizen.

What happens if we miss the one-year consular registration deadline?

Registration is still possible, but only with the permission of the Central Government, which is discretionary rather than automatic. Missions typically want a credible explanation for the delay and documentary proof of the parents' citizenship. If permission is refused, Overseas Citizen of India status is usually the remaining practical route for the child.

Can a child have both Indian citizenship and a foreign passport?

No. India does not recognise dual citizenship. For a birth on or after 3 December 2004, the parents must declare in writing, as part of the consular registration, that the minor does not hold another country's passport. A child who already holds a foreign passport and wants Indian citizenship by descent generally needs that position resolved before or as part of the registration.

Is OCI the same as dual citizenship?

No. Overseas Citizen of India status is a long-term, multiple-entry status under Sections 7A to 7D of the Citizenship Act. It gives most of the economic and residential rights an NRI has, but not voting rights, eligibility for government or constitutional office, or the right to buy agricultural land, a farmhouse or plantation property.

Can an OCI-holding child inherit agricultural land in India?

Yes, an OCI cardholder can inherit agricultural land from a resident Indian relative, even though they cannot purchase such land directly. Selling inherited agricultural land generally requires transferring it to a resident Indian buyer rather than to another non-resident or OCI holder.

What documents does a minor's OCI application actually need?

Typically the child's civil birth certificate naming both parents, both parents' current passport copies, proof of the parents' Indian origin such as an Indian passport, domicile or nativity certificate, a notarised parental authorisation, and, where the parents are divorced or separated, a custody order or joint consent addressing the OCI application specifically.

Do documents issued abroad need to be apostilled for these applications?

Generally yes, where the country of birth or issue is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention; otherwise consular legalisation is needed instead. Missions commonly ask for self-attested copies of the apostilled documents rather than the apostilled originals, and some will not accept an eApostille in place of a paper apostille.

If we missed the consular deadline years ago, is it ever too late to fix?

It is not automatically too late, because the Central Government can still permit late registration, but courts have read the one-year requirement strictly. The Gujarat High Court's 2026 ruling in Dolly Khilankumar Vadalia v. Union of India refused a citizenship-by-descent claim decades after birth precisely because consular registration had never been completed, regardless of the family's long residence in India on other documents.
