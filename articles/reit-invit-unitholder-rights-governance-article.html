REITs and InvITs in India: Unitholder Voting, Board Nomination Rights and Exit After SEBI's 2026 Reforms

A unitholder in one of these trusts is not a shareholder in the usual sense, and the rights that come with holding units have been rewritten in stages since 2023 rather than fixed once and left alone.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 18 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Real estate investment trusts and infrastructure investment trusts hold units, not shares, and a unitholder's rights come from the SEBI regulations governing these trusts rather than from company law. Those regulations have moved considerably since the first Indian REIT listed, most recently with a 2023 reform letting a sizeable unitholder nominate a director, and a cluster of 2026 amendments that changed how a key approval threshold is measured, narrowed who counts as a dissenting unitholder, and built in an exit mechanism for unitholders left behind when a sponsor leaves a multi-sponsor trust. A foreign or domestic investor sizing up a REIT or InvIT stake today needs to work from the current version of this framework, not the one that applied when the first of these trusts listed, because several of the rights that actually matter were added years after listing began.

Key points

  • An 'eligible unitholder', holding at least 10% of a REIT or InvIT's total outstanding units individually or collectively with others, has had the right since 2023 to nominate one director to the board of the trust's manager or investment manager, a right the original 2014 regulations did not provide.
  • A unitholder who exercises this nomination right is bound by a Stewardship Code that SEBI inserted into the REIT and InvIT regulations alongside the nomination right itself, which sets out how that unitholder is expected to engage with the trust's governance rather than simply holding a board seat passively.
  • A listed REIT or InvIT whose public unitholding falls below 25% has three years from listing to bring it back up to that level, through a public offer, sponsor sales within prescribed limits, an offer for sale, or specific other routes SEBI's 2023 circulars set out, and stock exchanges report non-compliance to SEBI every quarter.
  • SEBI's InvIT (Second Amendment) Regulations, 2026, reported as notified in October 2026 following an August 2026 consultation paper, moved several unitholder approval thresholds, including the headline 75% approval requirement, from a share of total unitholding value to a share of votes actually cast, excluding votes cast by parties related to the transaction in question.
  • The same reforms are reported to have narrowed the definition of a 'dissenting unitholder' to a person who expressly votes against a resolution, rather than, as under the earlier definition, anyone who simply did not vote in favour regardless of whether they voted at all.
  • A new provision reported as Regulation 22(7)(b)(iv) gives dissenting unitholders an exit option specifically where a REIT or InvIT has more than one sponsor and one of them leaves, and a related proposal would require a trust to restore its minimum public unitholding within a year if an exit offer to dissenting unitholders pushes that holding below 25%.
  • Depository receipts can now be issued against InvIT units subject to compliance with the applicable regulations, a route reported as added by the same 2026 amendment package, which is relevant to a foreign investor who would otherwise have to hold units directly rather than through a depository receipt structure.

A Unit Is Not a Share: Why the Rights Had to Be Built Separately

A REIT and an InvIT are each structured as a trust, not a company, holding real estate or infrastructure assets and distributing the income those assets generate to the people who hold units in the trust. This structure, borrowed from similar vehicles abroad and adapted for the Indian regulatory framework under the SEBI (Real Estate Investment Trusts) Regulations, 2014 and the SEBI (Infrastructure Investment Trusts) Regulations, 2014, meant that a unitholder did not automatically get the bundle of rights a company law shareholder takes for granted, voting on director appointments, board representation tied to shareholding, a settled statutory definition of dissent. Those rights had to be built into the REIT and InvIT regulations specifically, provision by provision, rather than imported wholesale from the Companies Act, 2013.

This piece sets out how that building process has actually gone since the first Indian REIT listed in 2019, concentrating on three clusters of change: the 2023 reform giving a sizeable unitholder the right to nominate a director, the long-standing but periodically adjusted minimum public unitholding requirement, and a cluster of 2026 amendments that reworked how approval votes are counted, who counts as dissenting, and what happens when a sponsor exits a multi-sponsor structure. None of these changes was announced as a single consolidated reform; each arrived as its own amendment or circular, which is itself a reason an investor has to track the current state of the regulations rather than rely on an account of the framework as it stood when a particular trust first listed.

The market this framework governs has itself grown considerably since 2019, with reporting through 2026 describing several REITs and a larger number of InvITs now listed on Indian exchanges, spanning office and retail real estate on the REIT side and roads, power transmission and other infrastructure assets on the InvIT side. That growth is itself part of why SEBI's governance attention to these vehicles has intensified rather than settled once a basic framework was in place: a market with a handful of listed trusts and a narrow investor base raises different practical governance questions from one with a broader base of institutional, retail and increasingly foreign unitholders, each bringing different expectations about voting, board representation and exit that the original 2014 regulations were not written with fully in mind.

Board Nomination Rights: How a 10% Unitholder Actually Gets a Seat

SEBI's board approved introducing board nomination rights for unitholders at its meeting on 28 June 2023, and the implementing amendments and circulars that followed gave an 'eligible unitholder', a unitholder or group of unitholders together holding at least 10% of a REIT or InvIT's total outstanding units, the right to nominate one director to the board of the trust's manager, in a REIT's case, or investment manager, in an InvIT's case. The consultation that preceded this reform had floated a more generous formula, one additional director for every further 10% held, but the final framework did not carry that scaling mechanism forward, settling instead on a single nomination right once the 10% threshold is met, regardless of how far above it a unitholder or group actually holds.

Exercising this right is not a one-time formality. SEBI's implementing circulars require the manager or investment manager to verify, on an ongoing basis, that an eligible unitholder who has nominated a director continues to hold the required 10%, and a unitholder whose holding falls below that threshold after the nomination can expect the manager to treat the nomination right as no longer available on the facts that originally supported it. A trust's own trust deed and investment management agreement also have to be amended to build in the mechanics of this right, which means a trust that listed before 2023 did not automatically carry this provision and had to specifically amend its constitutional documents to give effect to it.

For a unitholder considering building a stake specifically to secure this right, the 10% threshold should be read as a floor that has to be maintained, not a target that, once crossed, can be let slip without consequence. A group of unitholders acting collectively to meet the threshold should also be clear, before relying on the right, about how collective action of this kind is treated under the trust's own documents and under the Stewardship Code discussed next, since coordinated action among otherwise unconnected unitholders raises its own disclosure and conduct questions.

Two or more unitholders combining to reach the 10% threshold should also settle, in writing between themselves before approaching the manager, what happens if one of the group later sells down its holding. The regulations' own test is whether the eligible unitholder or group collectively holds the required percentage at the relevant time, and a group that has not agreed in advance how a partial exit by one member affects the group's continued eligibility, or who actually controls the nomination once the group's composition changes, risks a dispute among themselves at exactly the point the manager is separately verifying whether the nomination right still stands.

The Stewardship Code That Comes With the Seat

SEBI did not give eligible unitholders a board seat without attaching conditions to how that seat is used. A Stewardship Code was inserted into the REIT and InvIT regulations alongside the nomination right, applicable to a unitholder holding the 10% threshold, setting expectations for how that unitholder engages with the trust's governance, monitoring the manager's performance, engaging with other unitholders where appropriate, and exercising voting rights in a considered rather than purely passive way, rather than treating the nominated director as a seat held for its own sake.

A unitholder nominating a director under this framework should treat the Stewardship Code as a genuine, ongoing compliance obligation rather than a one-time acknowledgment made at the time of nomination. An institutional investor already familiar with stewardship-style obligations from the equity markets, where SEBI and other regulators have imposed broadly similar expectations on large shareholders and asset managers, will find the underlying logic familiar, but the specific obligations under the REIT and InvIT stewardship framework are distinct provisions in their own right and should be read on their own terms rather than assumed to mirror an equity-market stewardship code precisely.

The Minimum Public Unitholding Rule: Why 25% Matters

Separately from board representation, SEBI's regulations impose a minimum public unitholding requirement on listed REITs and InvITs, broadly mirroring the logic of the equivalent requirement for listed companies. Regulation 14(1A) of the InvIT Regulations and Regulation 14(2A) of the REIT Regulations require a listed trust whose public unitholding sits below 25% to bring that figure up to at least 25% within three years of listing, and a trust is expected to maintain at least that level on a post-issue basis once it has been reached.

SEBI's 2023 circulars on this point set out the specific routes a trust can use to get there: a public offer of units under an offer document, sales by the sponsor of up to 2% of total paid-up unit capital, or five times the average monthly trading volume, whichever is relevant, in a given financial year until the deadline, an offer for sale, rights issues, bonus issues, and, for InvITs specifically, a capped route of transferring units to an exchange-traded fund managed by a SEBI-registered mutual fund. Stock exchanges monitor which of these routes a trust actually uses and are required to report any shortfall to SEBI on a quarterly basis, which gives the requirement a genuine ongoing monitoring mechanism rather than leaving compliance to be checked only once, at the three-year deadline itself.

An investor evaluating a trust that listed with lower public unitholding, structured this way deliberately to let a sponsor retain a larger initial stake, should factor the three-year glide path into its own liquidity expectations, since a trust working through sponsor sales within the annual caps described above will increase public float gradually rather than all at once, and the pace of that increase is itself a data point about how actively the sponsor is managing toward the deadline rather than leaving compliance to the final year.

A trust approaching its three-year deadline without having made meaningful progress toward 25% presents a different, more pointed question for an existing unitholder than one that is visibly on track: since stock exchanges report shortfalls to SEBI every quarter, a unitholder can, in principle, track a trust's own progress against the deadline through the same periodic disclosures exchanges rely on, and a pattern of repeated quarterly shortfall reports without a credible plan to close the gap is the kind of fact a unitholder, and particularly one weighing whether to add to an existing position, should treat as a genuine governance signal rather than a purely administrative footnote.

The 75% Approval Threshold: From Unitholding Value to Votes Cast

A cluster of significant decisions under the REIT and InvIT regulations, certain related-party transactions and other matters the regulations list, require approval by unitholders holding not less than 75% of the value of unitholding of unitholders, a formulation that measures approval against the entire base of outstanding units rather than against however many units actually participate in a given vote. SEBI's own August 2026 consultation paper on ease-of-business measures for REITs and InvITs identified a practical problem with this formulation directly: because a meaningful share of unitholders do not vote at all, despite remote e-voting being available, hitting 75% of total unitholding value has become difficult even for resolutions that face no real opposition, simply because participation itself falls short of the threshold.

The reform SEBI proposed, and which the InvIT (Second Amendment) Regulations, 2026, reported as notified in October 2026, appear to have carried into force, moves the relevant thresholds, reported as touching Regulations 22(5A), 22(5C) and 22(7)(a) of the InvIT Regulations, from a share of total unitholding value to a share of votes actually cast, with approval now requiring votes cast in favour to constitute at least 75% of the total votes cast, excluding votes cast by parties related to the transaction under consideration. This is the same basic logic the Companies Act, 2013 uses for an ordinary or special resolution, measuring approval against votes cast rather than against the entire shareholder or unitholder base, and bringing the InvIT framework into closer alignment with that more familiar company law standard.

A trust's investment manager has to update its own vote-counting procedures to reflect this changed basis, and a unitholder relying on past experience of how close a particular resolution came to passing should recalculate that experience against the new votes-cast standard rather than assume the margin would have looked the same under the old total-unitholding-value test, since a resolution that struggled to reach 75% of the entire unitholder base can clear 75% of actual votes cast considerably more easily where participation is modest. Commentary on the reform reports that, of seventeen stakeholder responses SEBI received on the August 2026 consultation paper, fourteen agreed with the proposed change, one partially agreed, and two disagreed, which gives some sense of how contested, or uncontested, this specific reform was among market participants before it was finalised.

Redefining the 'Dissenting Unitholder'

Closely related to the votes-cast reform is a change to who actually counts as a dissenting unitholder, a status that matters because dissenting unitholders are the group entitled to specific exit rights under the regulations in certain circumstances, discussed further below. Under the regulatory position that applied before the 2026 amendments, a dissenting unitholder was defined broadly enough to include anyone who had not voted in favour of a resolution, whether or not that person actually cast a vote at all, meaning a unitholder who simply abstained, or never voted, could technically be classified as dissenting alongside someone who had actively voted against the resolution.

The reform reported in the 2026 amendments narrows this definition to a person who has expressly voted against the relevant resolution, removing the earlier position's effective equation of silence with active dissent. This is a meaningful change for how exit rights tied to dissent actually get triggered and sized: a narrower class of genuinely dissenting unitholders means a smaller, more precisely identified group entitled to whatever exit mechanism the specific provision in question provides, rather than a potentially large group that includes unitholders who simply did not engage with the vote either way.

Multi-Sponsor Structures: an Exit When a Sponsor Leaves

A REIT or InvIT with more than one sponsor presents a specific governance question that a single-sponsor trust does not: what happens to the unitholders' position when one sponsor exits while another continues. SEBI's 2026 amendments reportedly address this through a new provision, cited in commentary as Regulation 22(7)(b)(iv) of the InvIT Regulations, giving dissenting unitholders an exit option specifically in this scenario, where the outgoing sponsor, or its sponsor group entities, or the continuing sponsor, or its sponsor group entities, is required to provide the exit.

This provision works together with the narrowed dissenting-unitholder definition discussed above: a unitholder who wants to rely on this exit right in a multi-sponsor sponsor-change scenario has to have expressly voted against the relevant resolution to qualify as dissenting in the first place, rather than simply having abstained or stayed away from the vote. An investor in a multi-sponsor trust who is specifically concerned about a sponsor exit scenario should understand this interaction between the two provisions clearly, since failing to cast an active dissenting vote at the relevant stage could mean losing access to the exit right even where that investor's underlying concern about the sponsor change was genuine.

Restoring Minimum Public Unitholding After an Exit Offer

An exit offer made to dissenting unitholders under the sponsor-exit mechanism, or under any other provision triggering a similar exit right, can itself push a trust's public unitholding below the 25% minimum discussed earlier, since units bought back or transferred out of public hands through such an offer reduce the base the 25% figure is measured against. SEBI's reform package addresses this interaction directly: where an exit offer causes public unitholding to fall below the prescribed minimum, the trust is required to restore that minimum within one year, a materially shorter window than the three years a newly listed trust gets to reach 25% in the first place.

This shorter restoration window reflects a different regulatory concern from the original three-year glide path: a trust that has already been trading publicly and has let its public float slip below the minimum through its own corporate action, an exit offer it structured and executed, is treated as having caused the shortfall itself, and is accordingly held to a tighter timeline for fixing it than a newly listed trust working from a lower starting base agreed at the time of listing.

A trust's investment manager planning a sponsor-exit transaction of any real size should model the public unitholding impact of the resulting exit offer before executing it, rather than discovering after the fact that the offer has pushed public unitholding below 25% and triggered the one-year restoration clock. Building this modelling into the transaction's own planning, alongside the pricing and funding questions a sponsor exit already raises, avoids a scenario where the trust is simultaneously managing a sponsor transition and a compressed regulatory deadline it did not anticipate.

Depository Receipts: a New Route for a Foreign Investor

Among the changes reported as part of the same 2026 amendment package, SEBI's InvIT regulations now permit depository receipts to be issued on InvIT units, subject to compliance with the applicable regulations, a structural option that did not exist, on the sources available, before this amendment. A depository receipt structure lets an investor, including a foreign investor who might otherwise face friction holding Indian trust units directly, hold an instrument representing those units through a depositary rather than holding the underlying units themselves, a route long familiar from the equity markets through instruments such as GDRs and ADRs but newly extended into the InvIT space specifically through this amendment.

A foreign investor interested in this route should treat it as a genuinely new option to evaluate against direct unit ownership, rather than assume the depository receipt structure simply replicates direct ownership's rights on a one-to-one basis; the specific voting, nomination and exit rights discussed throughout this piece attach to units and unitholders as the regulations define them, and the precise way those rights pass through a depository receipt structure to the receipt holder is a question that should be checked against the specific terms of the depository receipt programme and the regulations governing it, rather than assumed to be automatic.

The equity-market experience with GDRs and ADRs is a useful reference point for what to check specifically, since those instruments have long raised similar pass-through questions, whether the depository votes the underlying shares as the receipt holder instructs, how dividends and other distributions are converted and remitted, and what happens to the receipt holder's position if the underlying instrument is subject to a corporate action the receipt programme's documentation did not anticipate. A foreign investor new to the InvIT depository receipt route specifically should expect its own counsel and the depository bank administering the programme to work through each of these points against the InvIT regulations' own text, rather than assume the equity-market answer to each question necessarily carries across unchanged.

How a REIT Manager and an InvIT Investment Manager Actually Differ From a Company Board

An investor used to thinking about board representation in company law terms should be careful not to carry every assumption from that world directly into a REIT or InvIT structure. A REIT's manager and an InvIT's investment manager are themselves typically companies, with their own boards, but the trust whose assets they manage is a separate legal structure with its own unitholders, and the nominated director a 10% unitholder secures a seat for sits on the manager's or investment manager's board, not on a board of the trust itself, because the trust, being a trust rather than a company, has no board in the company law sense to begin with.

This structural distinction matters in practice because the manager or investment manager typically also owes duties to, and is itself often controlled by, the sponsor or sponsor group, so a nominated unitholder director is joining a board that already has its own existing dynamics and reporting lines running back to the sponsor, rather than joining a blank-slate board elected entirely by unitholders the way a company's board would be. A unitholder relying on the nomination right to meaningfully influence the trust's direction should go into the role with this structure clearly understood, rather than expecting the nominated director's practical influence to mirror what a 10% shareholder's nominee would carry on an ordinary listed company's board.

The manager or investment manager's own constitutional documents, its articles of association as a company, sit alongside the trust deed and investment management agreement discussed earlier as the full set of documents that actually govern how the nominated director's role works in practice, including matters such as quorum requirements, specific reserved matters requiring unanimous or enhanced board approval, and any information rights the nominated director does or does not automatically receive. A unitholder preparing to exercise the nomination right should review all three documents, beyond the REIT or InvIT regulations themselves, before assuming it understands the full scope of what the seat actually comes with.

Related-Party Transactions: Where the 75% Threshold Does Most of Its Work

The votes-cast reform discussed above matters most in practice for related-party transactions, since these are among the categories of decision the REIT and InvIT regulations specifically route through unitholder approval at the 75% threshold, reflecting a structural reality of these trusts: a sponsor who set up the trust, and whose own group companies the trust frequently transacts with for property management, construction, operation and maintenance services, and similar functions, has an obvious interest in how those transactions are priced and structured, and unitholder approval is the regulations' primary check on that interest.

The exclusion of related-party votes from the votes-cast calculation is therefore not a minor technical detail; it is the provision doing most of the actual work in protecting minority unitholders, because without it, a sponsor holding a large unitholding could, in principle, vote its own related-party transaction through using its own units, defeating the purpose of requiring unitholder approval for exactly this category of transaction in the first place. An investor assessing a specific related-party transaction up for approval should look closely at how the trust's investment manager has identified and excluded related-party votes in its own calculation, since an error or an aggressive interpretation of who counts as related at this stage changes the actual voting arithmetic in a way that can be decisive on a closely contested resolution.

A minority unitholder with genuine concerns about a related-party transaction's pricing should also recognise that casting a vote against the resolution, now that dissent requires an express negative vote rather than mere non-participation, is the specific act that both registers formal opposition and, where the resolution is a type that carries an associated exit right, preserves that unitholder's position to actually use it. A unitholder who raises concerns in correspondence with the manager, or at an investor call, but does not translate that concern into an actual vote against the resolution when the meeting is held, risks finding that its documented objection carries less formal weight than a vote would have.

What This Means for an Investor Weighing a Stake Today

An investor, domestic or foreign, sizing up a REIT or InvIT stake with influence in mind, rather than purely as a yield-generating holding, should treat the 10% threshold for board nomination rights as the first real planning number, building toward it deliberately if board representation is part of the investment thesis, and should separately track whether the specific trust in question has already amended its trust deed and investment management agreement to give effect to the nomination mechanism, since not every trust that listed before 2023 has necessarily completed that documentary step on the same timeline.

An investor concerned about a trust's governance in a contested scenario, a related-party transaction a sponsor is pushing through, or a sponsor exit in a multi-sponsor structure, should understand that the practical strength of a dissenting position now depends on actually casting an express vote against the relevant resolution, given the narrowed dissenting-unitholder definition, and should not assume that simply abstaining, or raising concerns informally outside the voting process, preserves whatever exit or other right the regulations attach specifically to dissent.

An investor comparing a REIT or InvIT stake against a comparable listed equity holding should also factor in that the governance toolkit available to a unitholder, while now closer to company law's than it was in 2019, is still not identical to it. There is no exact InvIT or REIT equivalent, on the sources reviewed for this piece, of the full range of minority shareholder remedies available under the Companies Act, 2013, including the oppression and mismanagement jurisdiction of the National Company Law Tribunal, and an investor whose primary concern is a minority-protection toolkit as extensive as company law's should weigh that structural difference specifically, rather than assume the REIT and InvIT frameworks, having adopted several company-law-style mechanisms, have become a complete substitute for company law's own remedial structure.

Finally, an investor should build periodic review of the current regulatory text into its own monitoring of an existing REIT or InvIT holding, rather than treating the regulatory framework as fixed once understood at the time of investment. The pace of change in this specific area, a significant reform in 2023, a further consultation and amendment cycle completing in 2026, suggests this framework is still being actively adjusted by the regulator in response to how the market actually uses it, and an investor who last reviewed the applicable rules years ago should assume at least one of the points discussed in this piece has moved since.

Where a Unitholder's Grievance Actually Goes

A unitholder with a specific grievance against a REIT or InvIT's manager or sponsor, a disputed vote count, a denied nomination right, an exit offer priced in a way the unitholder considers unfair, has several available avenues rather than a single obvious forum, and choosing among them depends on the nature of the complaint. SEBI's own investor grievance mechanism, SCORES, is the first port of call for a complaint about a listed trust's compliance with the regulations themselves, and SEBI's own enforcement powers, including the power to direct compliance or impose penalties, sit behind that complaint mechanism for matters within SEBI's regulatory remit.

Where a unitholder is challenging a specific SEBI order or direction made in response to a complaint, an appeal lies to the Securities Appellate Tribunal, the specialised forum for exactly this category of dispute, rather than to an ordinary civil court. A dispute over the underlying commercial terms of a transaction, a related-party transaction's pricing, the valuation underlying an exit offer, may separately raise questions that fall to be examined through the trust's own contractual and documentary framework, including the trust deed and investment management agreement discussed earlier in this piece, and a unitholder should expect that resolving a genuinely contested valuation or pricing dispute is likely to involve more than a SEBI complaint alone.

A unitholder weighing whether a specific grievance is best pursued through SCORES, through the trust's own documents, or through both in parallel should start by identifying precisely which rule the grievance actually concerns. A complaint that the manager miscounted votes under the new votes-cast standard, or wrongly excluded a unitholder from the nominated-director verification process, sits squarely within SEBI's own regulatory framework and is well suited to a SEBI complaint. A complaint that an exit offer's price undervalues the trust's assets is, by contrast, substantially a valuation dispute, and while SEBI's framework sets procedural requirements around how such an offer has to be made, the substantive adequacy of the price is more likely to require the kind of detailed factual and expert evidence that a SEBI complaint alone is not designed to resolve, making the trust's own documents and, where they provide for it, a reference to arbitration or the civil courts, the more realistic route for that specific kind of dispute, with a SEBI complaint on the procedural aspects of the same transaction pursued alongside it rather than instead of it where both genuinely apply.

What Remains Unsettled

Several pieces of this framework are better described as recently settled, and still being tested in practice, than as long-established law. The exact gazette date of the InvIT (Second Amendment) Regulations, 2026 is reported inconsistently across the sources available for this piece, some giving 12 October 2026 and others 14 or 15 October 2026, and whether a parallel REIT amendment carrying the same votes-cast and dissenting-unitholder changes into the REIT Regulations has also been notified is not confirmed on the sources reviewed here, a gap worth checking directly against SEBI's current regulations before relying on parity between the two regimes.

The practical operation of the new votes-cast threshold and the narrowed dissenting-unitholder definition, in an actual contested vote or sponsor-exit scenario, has not yet, on the available record, been tested through a reported dispute or SEBI enforcement action, which means an investor relying on this piece's account of how these provisions are meant to work should treat that account as the regulatory text's stated design, not as a position confirmed by how a real dispute under the new framework has actually been decided, and should revisit this assumption once a reported dispute or SEBI order applying these specific 2026 provisions becomes available to review against, treating the current state of the text as the best available guide rather than as a settled, litigated interpretation.

REITs and InvITs in India have moved, over roughly three years, from a structure where a unitholder's practical influence depended almost entirely on the sponsor's and manager's own conduct, to one where a sufficiently large unitholder can nominate a director, where a key approval threshold is measured against actual participation rather than the entire unitholder base, and where a dissenting unitholder in specific, defined scenarios has a real exit right rather than simply a vote that, if lost, leaves nothing further to do. None of this changes the fundamental economics of holding units in one of these trusts, but it changes meaningfully what an investor with a large enough stake can actually do if the trust's governance goes somewhere that investor disagrees with.

An investor acting on any of the rights this piece has described should confirm the current, specifically notified text of the relevant provision before relying on it, given how recently several of these changes were made and how much of the sourcing for the most recent of them rests on commentary describing a notification rather than the notification's own text directly reviewed, and should treat that confirmation step as part of exercising the right, not as an optional precaution to skip when time is short.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much of a REIT or InvIT do I need to hold to nominate a director?

At least 10% of the trust's total outstanding units, held individually or together with other unitholders acting collectively, under the board nomination right SEBI introduced in 2023. The right gives the eligible unitholder or group one nomination, not one for every additional 10% held.

What is the Stewardship Code and does it apply to me as a unitholder?

It applies specifically to an eligible unitholder exercising the board nomination right, setting expectations for how that unitholder engages with the trust's governance rather than simply holding a board seat passively. A unitholder below the 10% nomination threshold is not subject to this specific code.

Why do REITs and InvITs have to maintain 25% public unitholding?

Regulation 14(1A) of the InvIT Regulations and Regulation 14(2A) of the REIT Regulations require a listed trust to bring public unitholding up to at least 25% within three years of listing and maintain that level afterward, mirroring the public float logic applied to listed companies generally.

Has the way unitholder approval is calculated actually changed?

Yes, reported amendments in 2026 moved the headline 75% approval threshold for certain matters from a share of the trust's total unitholding value to a share of votes actually cast, excluding votes cast by related parties to the transaction, aligning it more closely with the Companies Act, 2013 standard.

Does abstaining from a vote count as dissent?

Under the earlier position, a unitholder who did not vote in favour, including one who simply abstained, could be treated as dissenting. The 2026 amendments are reported to have narrowed this to a unitholder who has expressly voted against the resolution, which matters because specific exit rights attach to dissenting unitholder status.

What happens to unitholders if one sponsor leaves a multi-sponsor InvIT?

A new provision, reported as Regulation 22(7)(b)(iv) of the InvIT Regulations, gives dissenting unitholders an exit option in this specific scenario, with the outgoing or continuing sponsor, or their sponsor group entities, required to provide the exit.

Can a foreign investor hold REIT or InvIT units through a depository receipt instead of directly?

The 2026 amendments are reported to permit depository receipts to be issued on InvIT units, subject to the applicable regulations, a new structural option. The specific voting, nomination and exit rights discussed in this piece attach to units and unitholders as the regulations define them, and how they pass through to a depository receipt holder should be checked against that specific programme's terms.
