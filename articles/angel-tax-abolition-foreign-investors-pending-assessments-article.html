Angel Tax Is Gone, But the Old Files Are Not: Section 56(2)(viib) After the 2024 Abolition

The provision that once taxed a foreign investor's own share premium as the Indian startup's income has been removed from the statute book, but every round priced before 1 April 2024 can still be reopened and argued over on exactly the old rules.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Adv. Anupam, Senior Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 22 November 2026 · Law stated as on 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign venture fund or strategic investor that priced a funding round into an Indian startup anytime before April 2024 is not necessarily done with angel tax, even though the provision itself no longer applies to anything priced today. Section 56(2)(viib) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 taxed the excess of what an investor paid for unquoted shares over their fair market value as the issuing company's own income, and for a short but consequential period, that net was cast wide enough to catch non-resident investors as well as resident ones. The provision has since been removed from the statute altogether, but removal is prospective, and every round priced under the old rule remains open to a reassessment notice for as long as the ordinary limitation period allows. The practical question for a foreign investor or an Indian startup with a pre-2024 round on its books is no longer whether angel tax applies going forward; it is whether an old file still needs defending.

Key points

  • Section 56(2)(viib) taxed the excess of share consideration over fair market value as the issuing company's income; the Finance Act, 2023 removed the word 'resident' from the investor description with effect from assessment year 2024-25, bringing non-resident investors into the provision's scope for the first time
  • The Central Board of Direct Taxes responded to that extension with Notification No. 81 of 2023 dated 25 September 2023, which added further valuation methods under Rule 11UA for shares issued to non-resident investors and a 10% safe harbour tolerance between the issue price and the fair market value
  • A start-up recognised by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade could still claim exemption from Section 56(2)(viib) for a pre-abolition round by filing a declaration, provided its aggregate paid-up capital and share premium after the proposed issue did not exceed Rs 25 crore, a ceiling that excluded consideration received from non-resident investors, venture capital funds and venture capital companies
  • The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 inserted a proviso into Section 56(2)(viib) providing that the clause does not apply from assessment year 2025-26, which abolished angel tax for every class of investor, resident and non-resident alike, without any carve-out
  • The abolition is not retrospective: a round priced in financial year 2022-23 or 2023-24, assessment years 2023-24 and 2024-25, remains governed by the old provision and can still be the subject of a scrutiny assessment or a reassessment notice today
  • Reassessment notices under Section 148 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 are now time-barred after three years and three months from the end of the relevant assessment year in ordinary cases, extending to five years and three months only where the escaped income is Rs 50 lakh or more, a window Parliament cut down sharply with effect from 1 September 2024
  • The Income-tax Act, 2025 came into force on 1 April 2026 and replaced the 1961 Act, but assessments, reassessments, appeals and penalty proceedings that were already governed by the 1961 Act for an earlier assessment year continue under that Act's own rules rather than being transferred to the new one
  • A foreign investor or startup sitting on an unresolved pre-2024 valuation question should treat the file as live until the applicable limitation period has actually run out, not as settled merely because the underlying provision has since been deleted

A Short History of a Long-Disputed Provision

Section 56(2)(viib) entered the Income-tax Act, 1961 through the Finance Act, 2012, aimed originally at a narrow mischief: a closely held Indian company issuing shares to a resident investor at a price well above what the company was actually worth, with the excess functioning as a route for unaccounted money to enter the company's books dressed up as a legitimate share premium. The mechanism the provision used was blunt but effective for that purpose. Where the consideration received for unquoted shares exceeded their fair market value as computed under Rule 11UA of the Income-tax Rules, 1962, the excess was taxed as the issuing company's own income under the head 'income from other sources', at the maximum marginal rate, regardless of who the investor actually was or why the premium was paid.

For roughly a decade, the provision stayed confined to resident investors by its own wording, since the clause as originally drafted referred specifically to consideration received from a person 'being a resident'. A foreign investor paying a premium that an Indian promoter would have struggled to justify on paper triggered no exposure under this clause at all, because the clause simply did not reach that transaction. Startups raising a priced round from an overseas venture fund, a strategic corporate investor abroad, or an angel investor based outside India operated, for this specific purpose, outside the provision's reach.

That changed with the Finance Act, 2023, which deleted the residency qualifier from the clause and, with effect from assessment year 2024-25, brought consideration received from any investor, resident or not, within the provision's scope. The amendment did not come with a stated rationale beyond the government's general concern about valuation practices in unlisted companies, and it landed at a time when Indian startups were already raising a large share of their funding from foreign venture capital, which meant the change reached a far larger share of real transactions than the original 2012 provision ever had.

The 2023 extension drew sustained criticism from the venture capital industry and from tax practitioners advising foreign investors, largely on the basis that fair market value for an early-stage company is inherently a matter of commercial judgment rather than arithmetic certainty, and that taxing a genuine arm's length premium as income merely because a tax officer later disagreed with the valuation methodology risked deterring exactly the foreign capital Indian startups needed. Whatever view one takes of that criticism, it is the immediate backdrop against which the Central Board of Direct Taxes moved, within months, to soften the provision's practical bite for non-resident investors specifically, which the next section addresses.

The CBDT's 2023 Response: More Valuation Room for Foreign Investors

Rule 11UA, before the 2023 changes, gave an Indian company two recognised ways to establish the fair market value of its unquoted equity shares for Section 56(2)(viib) purposes: the net asset value method and the discounted cash flow method, the latter certified by a merchant banker. Both methods leave real room for disagreement, particularly for an early-stage company with little in the way of assets or a demonstrated cash flow history, which is precisely the kind of company that attracts the bulk of venture investment.

Notification No. 81 of 2023, dated 25 September 2023, amended Rule 11UA specifically for shares or compulsorily convertible preference shares issued to non-resident investors, adding several further valuation methods to the two already available to residents and permitting a merchant banker's valuation report to be relied on if it was issued up to ninety days before the date of the actual share issue. The amendment also introduced a safe harbour: where the actual issue price did not exceed the fair market value, as determined under the rule, by more than ten per cent, the issue price itself would not be questioned on valuation grounds, a tolerance aimed squarely at the kind of minor, good-faith variance between a negotiated commercial price and a strict valuation-model output that had been generating disputes.

This amendment did two things that mattered for a foreign investor negotiating a round in late 2023 or through 2024. It gave the Indian company issuing shares a somewhat wider menu of defensible valuation methodologies to choose from when a foreign investor's agreed price needed to be reconciled with Rule 11UA, and it gave both the company and the investor a ten per cent cushion that removed the need to match the negotiated price to the valuation model with mathematical precision. Neither change removed the underlying exposure the 2023 extension had created; both simply made that exposure more manageable for a transaction that was, on its commercial facts, a genuine arm's length investment rather than an attempt to disguise unaccounted money as share premium.

A foreign investor who priced a round in this window, after the 2023 extension but before the provision was abolished altogether, should treat the valuation report and supporting documentation from that period as something worth retaining carefully rather than archiving and forgetting, for reasons the later sections on reassessment make clear.

It is also worth being precise about what the safe harbour actually protected and what it did not. The ten per cent tolerance addressed the gap between the issue price and the computed fair market value; it did not relieve the company of the obligation to actually carry out a proper valuation under one of the recognised methods in the first place, and it did not apply at all where the company had instead relied on the separate, more favourable valuation route available to a venture capital undertaking receiving money from a registered venture capital fund, since that route already operated outside the ordinary fair-value comparison altogether. A foreign fund structured as, or investing alongside, a registered venture capital fund in the relevant period should check which of these two routes its own investment actually travelled under, since the two carry materially different documentation requirements if the year is ever reopened.

The DPIIT Exemption Route for Recognised Startups

Separately from the valuation rules, a company recognised as a startup by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade has, since a 2019 notification, been able to claim exemption from Section 56(2)(viib) altogether by filing a declaration in the prescribed form with the department, which forwards it to the Central Board of Direct Taxes. The exemption is conditional: the aggregate of the company's paid-up share capital and share premium, after the proposed share issue, could not exceed Rs 25 crore, and this ceiling specifically excluded consideration received from non-resident investors, from a venture capital fund, and from a venture capital company, meaning that money raised from those sources did not count against the cap even though it still needed to be within a fairly recognised startup's overall structure to benefit.

The exemption route gave a genuine, DPIIT-recognised early-stage company a way to sidestep the valuation dispute entirely for a pre-abolition round, provided the company met the recognition and filing requirements and stayed under the cap. It was, and for pending matters still is, of no help to a company that either was not DPIIT-recognised at the relevant time, or that had already crossed the Rs 25 crore threshold on a basis that included the premium in question, which describes a meaningful share of the later-stage, larger rounds that foreign venture and private equity investors are more likely to have participated in.

For a foreign investor or an Indian startup now dealing with a reassessment notice or a pending appeal concerning a round priced before April 2024, whether the DPIIT exemption was actually claimed, and whether the declaration was properly filed and forwarded at the time, remains a live and sometimes decisive question, since a startup that qualified for the exemption but never filed the declaration cannot simply assert the exemption for the first time in the course of a reassessment without first establishing that the underlying conditions were genuinely met.

A point that trips up more startups than the exemption's substantive conditions is the Rs 25 crore ceiling's own arithmetic. The figure looks at the company's aggregate paid-up capital and premium after the proposed issue, across every round the company has ever raised from investors whose contribution counts toward the cap, not merely the size of the round currently in question, and a company that raised earlier domestic rounds before later bringing in foreign capital needs to add all of that history together correctly before concluding the exemption was available at all. A startup that assumed the exemption applied because the specific foreign round looked small in isolation, without checking the company's cumulative position, is in a weaker position to defend that assumption now than one that actually ran the calculation and kept a record of it at the time.

The 2024 Abolition: What Changed and From When

The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024, which received presidential assent on 16 August 2024, inserted a further proviso into Section 56(2)(viib) providing that the clause would not apply from assessment year 2025-26 onward. Assessment year 2025-26 corresponds to income earned in financial year 2024-25, so the practical effect is that consideration received for shares issued on or after 1 April 2024 falls outside the provision entirely, for every class of investor, with no distinction retained between resident and non-resident money and no carve-out preserved for any particular category of transaction.

This was not a narrowing of the provision or a further liberalisation of the valuation rules; the Memorandum explaining the Finance Bill's provisions described the change as a decision to sunset the clause altogether, and the amendment's drafting reflects that, adding a blanket non-application proviso rather than amending the substantive test the clause had applied for over a decade. Commentators have generally read this as the government concluding that the compliance and litigation burden the provision generated, weighed against the relatively narrow category of genuinely abusive transactions it was still catching by 2024, no longer justified keeping it on the books in any form, resident or non-resident.

What the abolition did not do is erase the provision's application to anything that came before it. A share issue priced and completed in financial year 2023-24, assessment year 2024-25, is assessed and can be reassessed entirely on the law as it stood for that assessment year, meaning Section 56(2)(viib) in its post-2023, pre-abolition form, complete with the residency extension and the valuation rules the CBDT had layered on top of it. The deletion of a taxing provision from the statute does not, by itself, close out a year to which the provision already applied; only the ordinary rules on assessment and reassessment limitation do that, and those rules are the subject of the next two sections.

The abolition also did nothing to settle disputes that were already in litigation when it was enacted. A startup that had an addition under Section 56(2)(viib) confirmed in an assessment order for, say, financial year 2021-22, and that carried the dispute on appeal to the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) or to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, continues to argue that appeal on the substantive merits of the valuation dispute as it stood for that year, since the 2024 amendment supplies no ground for abatement or automatic relief for a year the provision still governed in full. Some commentary has floated the question of whether taxpayers assessed under the provision between 2012 and 2024 have any retrospective refund claim now that it has been abolished; nothing in the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 itself creates such a right, and a taxpayer pursuing that argument would be doing so on general principles rather than on any express statutory basis, which makes it a considerably weaker position than simply defending the original valuation on its own facts.

Why the Old Files Are Not Closed: Open Assessment Years

A startup or foreign investor that priced a round in financial year 2022-23 (assessment year 2023-24) or financial year 2023-24 (assessment year 2024-25) should assume, as a starting position, that the year in question remains capable of being scrutinised or reopened until the applicable limitation period for that specific assessment year has actually expired, a calculation that depends on when the original assessment was completed and, separately, on how much escaped income a reassessment notice would need to allege. A company that has already received a scrutiny notice, a show-cause notice under Section 148A, or a reassessment notice under Section 148 for one of these years is dealing with precisely this exposure in active form rather than a theoretical one.

The practical pattern that has emerged in tax practice since the 2024 abolition is that assessing officers continue to raise Section 56(2)(viib) additions for pre-abolition years in the ordinary course of scrutiny, and continue to issue reassessment notices for such years where information suggesting an undervalued or questionable premium has come to light after the original assessment, because nothing in the 2024 amendment instructs them to treat those years differently merely because the provision no longer applies going forward. An officer examining assessment year 2024-25 is applying the law as it stood for that year, which still includes Section 56(2)(viib) in full, non-resident investors and all.

For a foreign investor specifically, the practical consequence is that a round priced in late 2023 under the CBDT's expanded Rule 11UA methods and the 10% safe harbour is not automatically beyond question just because those were, at the time, the most favourable rules available; an assessing officer can still dispute whether the valuation methodology chosen was properly applied, whether the merchant banker's report met the ninety-day currency requirement, or whether the safe harbour tolerance was correctly calculated against the right base figure. The 2023 relaxations narrowed the space for dispute considerably compared to the bare NAV and DCF methods alone; they did not eliminate it.

The underlying premise an assessing officer relies on in these cases has not changed since 2012: a valuation is, at bottom, a projection, and a projection made in good faith at the time can still look wrong with the benefit of hindsight, particularly where an early-stage company's actual performance after the round fell well short of what the valuation model assumed. An officer reviewing a 2023 round in 2026 or 2027, with several years of the company's actual financial results now available, is naturally inclined to treat a gap between the projection and the outcome as evidence the original valuation was inflated, even where the methodology used was entirely orthodox and properly documented at the time. A foreign investor defending such a round needs the file to show the assumptions were reasonable when made, not merely that they turned out, in retrospect, to be optimistic.

How Far Back a Reassessment Notice Can Reach Today

Parliament substantially tightened the limitation rules governing how far back the tax department can reopen a completed assessment, with effect from 1 September 2024. Under the amended Section 149 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, a reassessment notice under Section 148 cannot ordinarily be issued once three years and three months have elapsed from the end of the relevant assessment year. A longer window, five years and three months from the end of the relevant assessment year, applies only where the escaped income the department alleges is Rs 50 lakh or more, a materially shorter period than the ten years permitted for high-value escapements under the law as it stood before this amendment.

Applying these limits to a Section 56(2)(viib) dispute concerning a pre-abolition round requires care about exactly which assessment year is in question and exactly when the relevant time limit expires, since the figures run from the end of the assessment year rather than from the date the share issue actually took place, and since a Section 56(2)(viib) addition, by its nature, is frequently large enough on its own to cross the Rs 50 lakh threshold for a round of any real size, which pushes many of these disputes into the longer five-year-and-three-month window rather than the shorter general one.

A separate question, which case law on the 2024 reassessment amendments is still working through, is how the new limitation regime applies to notices issued, or proceedings already underway, in the period around the 1 September 2024 transition itself, where the pre-amendment and post-amendment regimes can point to different outcomes for the same assessment year depending on exactly when a notice was issued and under which version of the provision. A foreign investor or startup facing a notice issued close to that transition date should have the notice's timing checked specifically against both the old and the amended Section 149, rather than assuming the current, shorter limitation period automatically governs a notice that may have been issued, or whose foundation was laid, before the amendment took effect.

The practical arithmetic is worth setting out concretely. For assessment year 2023-24, the end of the relevant assessment year is 31 March 2024, so the general three-years-and-three-month window runs out around the end of June 2027, and the extended five-years-and-three-month window, where it applies, runs out around the end of June 2029. For assessment year 2024-25, the equivalent dates move forward by one year. A foreign investor or startup working out whether a particular year is still exposed should calculate the specific date rather than relying on a general sense that 'it has been a few years', since the gap between the general and the extended window, roughly two years, is often exactly the gap that determines whether a given notice is time-barred or not.

Which Act Actually Governs a Pending Dispute Now

Parliament passed the Income-tax Act, 2025 in August 2025; it received presidential assent shortly afterward and came into force on 1 April 2026, replacing the Income-tax Act, 1961 for income earned from that date. The new Act restructures the statute considerably, including replacing the previous-year and assessment-year terminology with a single 'tax year' concept, but it does not reach back and reopen or re-govern assessments, reassessments, appeals or penalty proceedings that relate to an assessment year governed by the 1961 Act.

For anyone with a pre-abolition Section 56(2)(viib) matter still open today, in November 2026, the practical consequence is that the dispute continues to be assessed, reassessed and litigated entirely under the Income-tax Act, 1961, including its Section 56(2)(viib), its valuation rules under Rule 11UA as they stood for the relevant year, and its reassessment limitation rules under Section 149, notwithstanding that the 1961 Act has itself been replaced for current income. The new Act chose not to carry forward any direct equivalent of Section 56(2)(viib) into its own architecture, which closes off any possibility of the dispute being reframed under a corresponding 2025 Act provision; there simply is none to reframe it under.

This means a foreign investor's advisers handling a live pre-2024 dispute need to keep working from the 1961 Act's own text, its CBDT notifications on Rule 11UA as they stood at the relevant time, and the pre-amendment and post-amendment versions of Section 149 depending on timing, rather than assuming the transition to the new Act in any way simplifies or resets the position. The dispute is, in every sense that matters procedurally, still a 1961 Act dispute.

The forums hearing these disputes have not changed either. An appeal from an assessment or reassessment order confirming a Section 56(2)(viib) addition still runs through the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals), or the faceless appeal mechanism that has largely replaced it for most taxpayers, and from there to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal and, on a substantial question of law, to the jurisdictional High Court. None of that procedural chain has been affected by either the 2024 abolition or the 2025 Act's commencement, so a foreign investor already partway through an appeal on a pre-2024 round should expect the appeal to run its ordinary course rather than anticipate any change in forum or procedure arising from either development.

What a Foreign Investor or Startup Should Do With an Old File

Any foreign fund, strategic investor or Indian startup with a share issue priced before 1 April 2024 should begin by working out precisely which assessment year that issue falls into, and from there, whether the ordinary or the extended reassessment window under the current Section 149 has already run out for that year, bearing in mind the transition questions the previous section flagged for notices issued close to September 2024. A round that is genuinely time-barred no longer needs active management from a risk perspective, though the underlying valuation file is still worth retaining in case the question resurfaces in a different form, such as a dispute over the same shares' cost basis on a later sale.

Where the year remains open, the valuation documentation from the time of the issue, the Rule 11UA computation actually used, any merchant banker's report and its date relative to the share issue, and, where relevant, the DPIIT declaration and its filing date, should be retrieved and reviewed now rather than only once a notice actually arrives, since reconstructing this material years after the event, once key personnel have moved on or records have been archived less carefully, is considerably harder than confirming it is already in order while the transaction is still reasonably recent.

A startup that believed it qualified for the DPIIT exemption on a pre-abolition round but cannot now confirm the declaration was actually filed and forwarded to the Board should treat that gap as a real exposure rather than a formality, since the exemption is conditional on the declaration having been made, not simply on the underlying facts having supported one. Where the gap cannot be cured retrospectively, the fallback position has to rest on the valuation itself withstanding scrutiny under Rule 11UA on its own terms, which makes the quality of the original valuation work considerably more important than it may have seemed at the time the round was priced.

Foreign investors negotiating fresh rounds today, by contrast, no longer need to build Section 56(2)(viib) into their term sheet or valuation diligence at all, since any share issue from 1 April 2024 onward falls outside the provision regardless of investor residency. The practical planning work has shifted entirely from the live transaction to the legacy file, which is the inversion this article has tried to make clear: the law that mattered for structuring a deal in 2023 is now, in November 2026, a question only for the deals that were actually done under it.

Does the Shares' Cost Basis Carry Any Lingering Exposure?

A question that arises less often but still surfaces, particularly where a foreign investor's pre-2024 shares have since been sold in a secondary transaction or on an exit, is whether the earlier Section 56(2)(viib) addition at the company level has any bearing on the investor's own cost of acquisition for capital gains purposes. The two computations are, under the 1961 Act's scheme, kept separate: Section 56(2)(viib) taxes the issuing company on the excess of consideration over fair market value, while the investor's cost of acquisition for its own later capital gains computation is ordinarily the price actually paid for the shares, not a figure reduced by whatever the company was taxed on.

This separation matters because a foreign investor who contributed to a round where the company suffered a Section 56(2)(viib) addition should not assume that addition in any way reduces the investor's own taxable gain on a later sale, nor should the investor assume the company's tax exposure on the round is somehow the investor's own liability to indemnify against as a matter of law; any indemnity on that point exists only if the investment agreement itself created one. A foreign fund negotiating indemnities in a fresh round, or renegotiating terms around an existing one, should treat the allocation of this specific risk, the company's own Section 56(2)(viib) exposure for a pre-2024 issue, as a point worth addressing expressly in the transaction documents rather than assuming the general representations and warranties package already covers it adequately.

Where a pre-2024 round is now the subject of a live dispute and the company's shares have since changed hands, whether through a follow-on round, a secondary sale, or an acquisition of the company itself, the practical burden of responding to a reassessment notice typically falls on whoever now controls the company's books and record-keeping, which is not always the same entity that negotiated the original round. A foreign investor that has since exited the company entirely, but retains contractual indemnity rights from the original transaction, should check those rights are still capable of being enforced against the right counterparty before assuming the exposure is someone else's problem to manage.

None of this changes the substantive tax position; it is a reminder that a company-level tax dispute over a pre-2024 round can still have practical consequences for parties who are no longer directly involved in running the company, and that those consequences are worth mapping now, while the people who actually negotiated the original transaction are more likely to still be reachable, rather than only once a notice has already landed on a successor's desk.

A Short Checklist Before Treating a Pre-2024 Round as Settled

Given how many moving parts this article has covered, a foreign investor or an Indian startup is better served working through a short, concrete checklist for any round priced before 1 April 2024 than relying on a general sense that the matter is probably fine. First, identify the exact assessment year the share issue falls into, and calculate the specific date on which the general and, separately, the extended reassessment window for that year actually expires under the current Section 149, rather than estimating it.

Second, confirm which valuation route was actually used at the time, the ordinary Rule 11UA methods, the non-resident-specific methods and safe harbour introduced in September 2023, or the separate venture capital undertaking route, and retrieve the supporting valuation report, merchant banker certificate and underlying financial projections rather than assuming they can be reconstructed later if needed. Third, where a DPIIT exemption was intended to apply, confirm the declaration was actually filed, that it was forwarded to the Board, and that the Rs 25 crore ceiling calculation included every round the company had raised up to that point, not merely the round in question.

Fourth, where any part of this picture cannot be confirmed with confidence, treat the round as an open exposure requiring active management rather than a closed matter, and have the specific limitation calculation and documentation gap reviewed with a tax adviser now, while records and institutional memory are still reasonably fresh, rather than waiting for a notice to arrive before beginning that review.

Section 56(2)(viib) spent roughly a decade as a provision aimed at resident promoters inflating their own company's books, briefly widened to catch foreign investors at exactly the point when Indian startups were raising the largest share of their capital from abroad, and was then removed from the statute entirely within about eighteen months of that widening. For anything priced from April 2024 onward, that history is now academic. For a round priced before then, it is not, because the assessment years the old provision governed remain open for as long as the current, shortened reassessment limitation period allows, and nothing about the provision's abolition changes how those specific years are assessed.

The sensible position for a foreign investor or an Indian startup with a pre-2024 round on its books is to treat the file as open until a specific limitation calculation says otherwise, to have the original valuation and any DPIIT declaration checked now rather than when a notice arrives, and to remember that the dispute, however old it eventually becomes, is being fought entirely under the Income-tax Act, 1961 and its own rules, not under the Act that has since replaced it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is angel tax still applicable to foreign investors in Indian startups?

No, not for any share issue from 1 April 2024 onward. The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 inserted a proviso to Section 56(2)(viib) providing that the clause does not apply from assessment year 2025-26, and the abolition covers resident and non-resident investors alike with no carve-out.

Can a foreign investor's pre-2024 share subscription still be reopened for angel tax today?

Yes, if the relevant assessment year is still within the reassessment limitation period. Section 149, as amended with effect from 1 September 2024, allows a reassessment notice within three years and three months of the end of the relevant assessment year generally, or five years and three months where the escaped income alleged is Rs 50 lakh or more.

When exactly did angel tax apply to non-resident investors?

The Finance Act, 2023 removed the residency qualifier from Section 56(2)(viib) with effect from assessment year 2024-25, bringing consideration received from non-resident investors within the provision for share issues from financial year 2023-24 onward, until the provision's abolition from assessment year 2025-26.

What valuation relief did the CBDT give for shares issued to non-resident investors before the abolition?

Notification No. 81 of 2023, dated 25 September 2023, added further Rule 11UA valuation methods specifically for non-resident investors, allowed a merchant banker's valuation report issued up to ninety days before the share issue, and introduced a 10% safe harbour tolerance between the issue price and the computed fair market value.

Does the DPIIT startup exemption from angel tax still matter now that the provision is abolished?

It matters only for rounds priced before the abolition. A DPIIT-recognised startup that filed the prescribed declaration could claim exemption for a pre-2024 round if its aggregate paid-up capital and premium, excluding amounts from non-resident investors and venture capital funds, stayed within Rs 25 crore. That exemption remains relevant to any pending dispute over such a round.

Which law governs an angel tax dispute now that the Income-tax Act, 2025 is in force?

A dispute concerning an assessment year before the new Act's commencement continues to be governed by the Income-tax Act, 1961, including Section 56(2)(viib) as it stood for that year. The Income-tax Act, 2025, in force from 1 April 2026, does not carry forward an equivalent provision and does not reopen or reframe pending 1961 Act matters.

Should a startup still keep its old angel tax valuation records now that the provision has been abolished?

Yes, for any share issue made before 1 April 2024, until the applicable reassessment limitation period for that specific assessment year has run out. The valuation report, the Rule 11UA computation used, and any DPIIT declaration remain the primary defence if the year is reopened.
