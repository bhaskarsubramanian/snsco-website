Exporting Agricultural Produce From India: APEDA, FSSAI and Resolving Disputes With a Foreign Buyer

Getting a consignment of rice, spices or processed food out of an Indian port is a compliance exercise with at least three regulators attached to it, and the contract signed to sell it abroad often puts the actual dispute somewhere a domestic lawyer does not expect.

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Deba Prasad Guha, Food Safety & Regulatory Expert, Advisory Panel, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 20 November 2026 · Law stated as on 18 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An Indian exporter of rice, spices, processed food or other scheduled agricultural products deals with at least three separate regulatory touchpoints before a container leaves an Indian port: registration with the Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority for the product category concerned, an FSSAI licence that, after a March 2026 overhaul, now treats a wholly export-oriented manufacturer differently from a domestic one, and whatever restriction or permission the Directorate General of Foreign Trade currently has in force for that specific commodity, a position that for staples such as wheat and non-basmati rice has changed more than once within 2026 alone. None of this compliance work, however, determines what happens when the foreign buyer disputes the shipment, a question that is usually answered by a trade association contract written under English law, naming an English arbitral forum, that many Indian exporters sign without appreciating how completely it displaces the domestic law they assumed would apply.

Key points

  • An exporter of a product listed in the First Schedule to the Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority Act, 1985 needs a Registration-Cum-Membership Certificate from APEDA, obtained online as an e-RCMC after first securing an Import Export Code from the DGFT, and a lapsed, unrenewed RCMC stops that exporter from lawfully exporting the scheduled product or claiming APEDA's own export benefits until it is renewed.
  • A March 2026 order from the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, effective 1 April 2026, reworked FSSAI's licensing categories so that a 100% export-oriented food manufacturing unit needs a Central Licence regardless of its turnover, a departure from the turnover-based licensing that otherwise determines whether a food business needs a State or Central FSSAI licence.
  • Basmati rice sits in the Second Schedule to the APEDA Act as a 'special product' rather than the First Schedule's ordinary scheduled products, which matters because APEDA's own developmental and regulatory functions, and the specific compliance steps an exporter has to complete, differ between the two schedules.
  • Government control over agricultural exports is exercised separately from APEDA and FSSAI, through the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992 and DGFT notifications under the ITC (HS) Export Policy, a power that has been used on short notice against staples including wheat and non-basmati rice more than once within 2026, so an exporter has to check the current DGFT notification for the specific commodity before assuming last month's position still holds.
  • A large share of international agricultural commodity trade, including trade an Indian exporter enters into, runs on standard form contracts published by trade associations such as GAFTA and FOSFA, which specify English law and, almost always, arbitration in London under that association's own rules, so an Indian exporter who signs one of these contracts has, as a rule, already agreed to resolve any dispute under a legal system and in a forum entirely outside India.
  • The Supreme Court's 2020 decision in National Agricultural Cooperative Marketing Federation of India v Alimenta S.A., refusing enforcement in India of a London FOSFA award against an Indian canalising agency, is the clearest illustration of how narrowly Indian courts, and the English-law arbitral tribunals these contracts actually use, read a force majeure defence built around a government export restriction the exporter itself was positioned to avoid or anticipate.
  • An Indian exporter's practical protection against a foreign buyer's non-payment is shaped primarily by the payment mechanism negotiated into the contract, typically a letter of credit, rather than by Indian litigation against a buyer who may hold no assets in India at all, which makes getting the payment mechanism right at the negotiation stage considerably more valuable than planning to litigate a dispute after the fact.

Three Regulators, One Consignment

A business exporting agricultural produce out of India, whether rice, spices, processed foods, fresh fruit or any of the wider range of products the law treats as agricultural or processed food exports, answers to at least three separate regulatory regimes before a shipment legally leaves the country, and each operates on its own statute, its own registration process, and its own timeline for change. The Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority Act, 1985 governs registration and, for the products it covers, quality standards and inspection. The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, administered by FSSAI, governs the licensing of the food business itself, a requirement that applies regardless of whether the business sells domestically, exports, or both. The Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992, administered through the Directorate General of Foreign Trade, governs whether the specific commodity can be exported at all at a given time, a question that sits entirely separately from either of the other two and can change on short notice.

This piece works through each of these three regimes in turn, then turns to a question that has nothing to do with Indian regulation at all: what happens when the foreign buyer on the other end of the contract disputes the shipment, a question usually answered by the commercial contract itself rather than by Indian law, and frequently answered in a way the Indian exporter did not fully appreciate when the contract was signed.

APEDA and the First Schedule: Who Actually Needs an RCMC

The Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority Act, 1985 (Act 2 of 1986) creates APEDA and gives it functions that include setting standards and specifications for scheduled products meant for export, registering exporters of those products, and carrying out inspection of certain categories, meat and meat products among them, at processing plants, storage premises and other points along the supply chain. 'Scheduled products' under the Act means the products listed in its First Schedule, a list the Central Government can add to or remove items from by notification, and which APEDA's own published amendments show has been updated repeatedly since the Act was passed, adding items such as de-oiled rice bran, green pepper in brine, and cashew nuts and their products at various points.

An exporter of a First Schedule product needs a Registration-Cum-Membership Certificate from APEDA, commonly called an APEDA RCMC, as a mandatory registration before exporting that product, obtained since 17 July 2023 as an e-RCMC through APEDA's online system, and conditioned on the exporter first having secured an Import Export Code from the DGFT, since the RCMC process builds on that code rather than operating independently of it. The registration is not tied to a specific destination country and covers exports of the relevant scheduled product to any market once granted, and APEDA's own guidance indicates a new applicant does not need an established export track record to obtain it in the first place.

The RCMC has to be kept current rather than treated as a one-time formality. An exporter whose RCMC has lapsed without renewal loses the ability to lawfully export the scheduled product, and loses access to whatever APEDA benefits or schemes attach to registered exporters, until the registration is renewed, and customs and port authorities are reported to check RCMC status before clearing a First Schedule product for export, which means a lapsed registration is not a paperwork problem that surfaces later; it is capable of stopping a shipment at the point of clearance.

Basmati Rice and the Second Schedule: Why the Distinction Matters

The APEDA Act does not treat every product it covers identically. Alongside the First Schedule's scheduled products, the Act maintains a Second Schedule of 'special products', and rice, including Basmati rice specifically, sits in this second category rather than the first. This distinction matters practically because APEDA's functions and the compliance architecture built around each schedule are not identical, and an exporter dealing in Basmati rice should not assume that guidance written for First Schedule scheduled products generally, registration mechanics, inspection requirements, applicable schemes, necessarily transfers across unchanged to a Second Schedule special product.

Basmati rice exporters face an additional layer that has nothing to do with the First Schedule and Second Schedule distinction as such: Basmati's geographical indication status and the specific varietal and quality notifications that attach to it, administered in part through APEDA and in part through other bodies concerned with geographical indications, create a compliance layer of their own that a general scheduled-products exporter does not face. An exporter moving between ordinary First Schedule products and Basmati rice within the same export business should treat the compliance obligations for each as genuinely separate workstreams rather than variations on a single theme.

FSSAI's Side of Export: the March 2026 Licensing Overhaul

Separately from APEDA's product-specific registration, every food business operating in India, including one whose entire output is exported and none of it sold domestically, needs a licence or registration under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, the general food safety statute FSSAI administers. The ordinary rule under this framework has long been that whether a food business needs a Central Licence, a State Licence, or merely a basic registration turns primarily on the scale of its turnover and the nature of its activity, with larger manufacturers and certain higher-risk categories falling into the Central Licence bracket and smaller operations into State licensing or registration.

A Ministry of Health and Family Welfare order dated 13 March 2026, effective from 1 April 2026 and reported as the most significant change to FSSAI's licensing structure in over a decade, altered this picture for a specific category directly relevant to agricultural exporters: a 100% export-oriented food manufacturing unit now requires a Central Licence regardless of its turnover, alongside certain other categories, health supplements and proprietary food manufacturing among them, that the same order pulled into mandatory Central licensing irrespective of scale. A smaller agricultural processing business that exports its entire output, and that might previously have qualified for State-level licensing on turnover grounds alone, should check this specific change rather than assume its existing State licence remains the correct one after 1 April 2026.

An export-oriented manufacturer has historically also benefited from a specific exemption reported under FSSAI's older guidance: a manufacturer whose production is 100% for export is not required to submit the certificate of conformity to Indian food product standards that a domestic manufacturer otherwise has to produce, substituting an undertaking instead, on the reasoning that a product never sold in the Indian market need not be certified against standards written for the Indian market. Whether this specific exemption survives unchanged alongside the March 2026 licensing overhaul is not confirmed on the sources available for this piece, and an exporter relying on it should check FSSAI's current guidance directly rather than assume an older exemption automatically continues to apply after a licensing framework it was built around has itself changed.

Export consignments also frequently need an export health certificate specific to food products, a document distinct from the FSSAI licence itself, declaring that the consignment is fit for human consumption and meets the importing country's own requirements, with the precise form and necessity of this certificate depending heavily on the destination country and the specific product, information an exporter should confirm against the destination country's own import requirements, available in part through APEDA's AgriExchange portal, rather than assume a single standard certificate suffices for every market.

DGFT and the Export Policy That Can Change Overnight

Registration with APEDA and licensing under FSSAI answer the question of whether a business is entitled to export a given product in principle. They do not answer a separate, and for several major Indian agricultural commodities genuinely live, question: whether the Government currently permits that specific commodity to be exported at all, a question governed by the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992 and the export policy the Directorate General of Foreign Trade notifies under the ITC (HS) Classification, Schedule 2 (Export Policy).

This is not a theoretical risk. Within 2026 alone, reported DGFT action moved wheat export policy more than once, from a February 2026 notification permitting a capped quantity of 25 lakh metric tonnes subject to specific conditions, to a reported August 2026 notification freeing wheat and wheat flour exports with immediate effect, while non-basmati rice has continued through 2026 to carry its own distinct conditions, including a requirement that export contracts be registered with APEDA before shipment, alongside separately reported government-to-government allocations to specific countries on request. The exact sequence and scope of these changes is, on the sources available for this piece, not fully consistent across reporting, which is itself the point worth taking from this section: an exporter dealing in a politically sensitive staple commodity should check the current DGFT notification for that specific commodity immediately before committing to a shipment, rather than rely on a position understood to be current even a few months earlier.

An export contract for a commodity of this kind should, as a matter of drafting rather than hope, address what happens if a DGFT restriction intervenes between the contract's signature and the shipment date, since, as the next sections discuss, the standard trade association contracts commonly used for agricultural commodity exports have their own, often narrowly construed, force majeure or prohibition provisions for exactly this scenario, and an Indian exporter should understand precisely how that specific contractual provision, rather than Indian law generally, will treat a government export restriction before relying on it as an excuse for non-performance.

The Contract Itself: Why a Standard Trade Association Form Changes Everything

A meaningful share of international trade in agricultural commodities, grain, oilseeds, pulses and related products among them, is conducted under standard form contracts published by international trade associations, most prominently GAFTA, the Grain and Feed Trade Association, and FOSFA, the Federation of Oils, Seeds and Fats Associations. These are not neutral templates an Indian exporter can freely amend to apply Indian law and Indian jurisdiction; they are drafted around English law and an arbitration clause referring disputes to that association's own arbitration rules, with English law, the Sale of Goods Act 1979 and the Arbitration Act 1996 among the statutes actually governing the contract, applying as the contract's own chosen framework regardless of where either party to the trade is actually based.

An Indian exporter signing one of these standard forms, often because the foreign buyer insists on it as the only basis on which it trades, should understand from the outset that doing so is a deliberate choice to resolve any dispute under English law, in an English-administered arbitration, rather than before an Indian court applying Indian contract law. This is not a technicality buried in boilerplate; it is the entire dispute resolution architecture for the transaction, and an exporter who assumes Indian courts will have the final word on a dispute arising from a GAFTA or FOSFA contract has misunderstood the basic structure of what was signed.

Both associations' standard forms typically provide for a two-tier arbitration structure, a first-tier arbitration followed by a right of appeal to a further tribunal under the same association's rules, a structure genuinely different from the single-tier arbitration more familiar under Indian domestic practice, and an exporter should factor the cost and time of potentially running through both tiers into its assessment of how a dispute under one of these contracts is actually likely to play out, rather than assuming a first-tier award is necessarily the end of the process.

The GAFTA Default Clause and What It Actually Does

A large share of GAFTA contracts, reported as present with only minor variation across most of the roughly seventy to eighty standard forms the association maintains, include what is commonly called the default clause, a provision that governs how damages are calculated where one party fails to perform, typically by reference to the market price for the relevant commodity on the date of default rather than requiring an injured party to prove its actual loss through more conventional means. This clause has been the subject of detailed English court and tribunal consideration over the years, including on how a tribunal should treat an injured party's duty to mitigate its loss once default has occurred, and an Indian exporter on either side of a default scenario under a GAFTA contract should expect the default clause's own specific mechanics, not general Indian contract law principles on damages, to govern how any resulting liability is actually calculated.

An exporter should also be alert to how differently a default clause of this kind can operate from the more familiar liquidated damages or penalty clause analysis Indian courts apply under Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 to a domestic contract. Because the GAFTA and FOSFA contracts choose English law specifically, Section 74 and the Indian case law built up around it has no application to a dispute under one of these contracts at all, a point worth understanding clearly rather than discovering only once a dispute is already underway and an exporter's own counsel starts from the wrong body of law.

Force Majeure and the Lesson of NAFED v Alimenta

The leading Indian authority on how a government export restriction interacts with a force majeure or prohibition clause in one of these contracts arose from a dispute that ran for decades before it was finally resolved. In National Agricultural Cooperative Marketing Federation of India v Alimenta S.A., decided by the Supreme Court on 22 April 2020 and reported at (2020) 9 SCC 866, the underlying contract, for the supply of Indian groundnut (HPS groundnut) to a Swiss buyer under a FOSFA standard form, could not be performed in full because of a shortfall in the groundnut crop during 1979-80, and the Indian seller, NAFED, a canalising agency through which this category of export had to be routed, found itself unable to obtain the government permission needed to complete the contracted shipment.

Alimenta commenced arbitration in London under the FOSFA contract, and NAFED spent years litigating in the Delhi High Court and the Supreme Court trying to restrain that arbitration from proceeding at all, litigation that ran from the early 1980s into the late 1980s without stopping the arbitration, which eventually produced an award against NAFED. When Alimenta sought to enforce that foreign award in India decades later, the Supreme Court, on the weight of the sources reviewed for this piece, declined enforcement, treating the award's enforcement as contrary to the fundamental policy of Indian law given the specific circumstances in which NAFED's performance had become impossible, including that NAFED, as the entity actually required to seek the Government's export permission, was positioned to know, and arguably ought to have known, that the permission required to complete the contract might not be forthcoming.

The lesson an Indian exporter should take from this history is not that force majeure defences built around a government export restriction never succeed; it is that an arbitral tribunal applying English law, and an Indian court later asked to enforce or set aside the resulting award, will scrutinise closely whether the restriction was a genuine, unforeseeable impediment or one the exporter itself was in a position to anticipate or avoid, including through the specific structure the exporter was operating under, a canalising agency's own dependence on government permission being treated in NAFED's case as working against, rather than in support of, the force majeure argument. An exporter in a comparable position today, dependent on a government licence, quota or permission to complete an export contract, should treat the risk of that permission being delayed or refused as a risk to address specifically in the contract's own force majeure drafting, rather than assume general hardship principles will excuse non-performance after the fact. The decades the NAFED dispute actually took to resolve, from the original 1979-80 shortfall to the Supreme Court's 2020 ruling on enforcement, are themselves a caution worth taking seriously: an exporter should not assume a force majeure dispute of this kind, once it escalates into arbitration and cross-border enforcement litigation, will be resolved on any timeline a commercial business can comfortably plan around.

Where an Indian Exporter Can Actually Go Wrong on Permission and Prohibition

The NAFED history also illustrates a specific drafting point worth making explicit. A force majeure or prohibition clause in a standard trade contract typically distinguishes between an external event that makes performance genuinely impossible and a party's own failure to secure something it was always required to obtain, and an exporter relying on a DGFT export restriction as an excuse for non-performance should expect the tribunal to ask pointedly whether that restriction was a sudden, unforeseeable change, or one the exporter should have priced into its planning given how frequently, as the earlier section on DGFT policy describes, India's export policy for sensitive agricultural commodities has moved within a single year.

An exporter operating in a product category with a recent history of export restriction, wheat and non-basmati rice both qualifying on the facts described earlier in this piece, should treat the live possibility of a restriction being imposed, suspended or modified between contract signature and shipment as a known, rather than unforeseeable, risk, and should negotiate the contract's force majeure or prohibition language with that specific history in mind, rather than relying on a generic clause and hoping a tribunal applying English law will read it generously in the exporter's favour if the restriction does materialise.

Payment Risk: Why the Letter of Credit Matters More Than the Lawsuit

An Indian exporter's realistic protection against a foreign buyer who disputes payment, underpays against a claimed quality shortfall, or simply does not pay, is shaped far more by the payment mechanism negotiated at the contract stage than by any litigation or arbitration pursued after shipment. A letter of credit, issued by the buyer's bank in the exporter's favour and payable against the exporter's presentation of the shipping and quality documents the credit specifies, gives the exporter a payment obligation running against a bank rather than against the buyer directly, and, subject to the credit's own terms being met precisely, one that is largely independent of any separate dispute the buyer may raise about the underlying sale contract, a principle long recognised in Indian law as the autonomy of a bank's payment obligation under a letter of credit.

An exporter negotiating payment terms should treat the precision of the documents a letter of credit actually requires, the exact description of goods, the exact quality certificate, the exact shipping documents, as a commercial priority in its own right, since banks examine a letter of credit presentation strictly against its own terms, and a presentation that does not match those terms precisely can be refused regardless of whether the underlying goods were, in commercial reality, entirely in order. An exporter new to a particular buyer or a particular destination market, where a letter of credit may not be the buyer's preferred mechanism, should weigh open account or documents-against-payment terms against the real credit risk those mechanisms carry, rather than default to whatever payment term a buyer proposes without separately assessing that buyer's own payment history and the exporter's actual exposure if payment is delayed or refused.

An exporter should also keep in mind that a letter of credit's autonomy from the underlying sale contract is not absolute; Indian courts have recognised a narrow fraud exception that lets a bank, or a court, decline to honour a credit where the beneficiary's presentation is tainted by established fraud, as distinct from a mere quality or commercial dispute between buyer and seller. This exception is applied narrowly precisely to preserve the certainty a letter of credit is meant to provide, and an exporter whose presentation is genuinely in order should not expect a buyer's commercial unhappiness with the goods, however genuine, to succeed in blocking payment through this route.

Export credit insurance and the facilities available through India's export-import financing institutions offer a further layer of protection against a buyer's default risk specifically, distinct from the quality or contractual disputes discussed elsewhere in this piece, and an exporter dealing in politically sensitive commodities, or exporting to buyers in markets with their own currency or payment control risk, should weigh this cover against its cost at the time terms are negotiated rather than treat it as an afterthought available only once a specific shipment has already run into a payment problem.

Quality Disputes: Certificates, Surveyors and Who Gets the Final Word

A meaningful share of disputes under agricultural export contracts are not about non-delivery at all but about whether the goods actually delivered matched the quality the contract specified, moisture content, foreign matter, oil content, grade, and a long list of similarly technical specifications that vary by commodity. Standard trade association contracts typically address this by naming a specific certifying body or survey process, often a superintendence or inspection agency's certificate, as final and binding between the parties on quality at the point of shipment, which means an Indian exporter's own internal quality testing, however rigorous, is not what actually decides a quality dispute under one of these contracts; the named certifying process is.

An exporter should treat the choice of certifying agency and the precise sampling and testing method the contract specifies as a negotiation point worth real attention, not a detail to leave to the buyer's preference, since a contract that names a certifying process the exporter has no practical access to or confidence in effectively hands the buyer a one-sided advantage on every quality dispute that arises. Where the contract allows a secondary or appeal survey in the event the first certificate is disputed, an exporter should understand exactly how that secondary process is triggered and funded before a dispute actually arises, rather than discovering the mechanism only once a shipment already sits in dispute at the discharge port.

Indian exporters new to a particular commodity or a particular trade association's standard forms should also be alert to how differently these contracts treat a quality shortfall from how an Indian buyer-seller dispute might be approached domestically: rather than a claim for damages assessed after the fact through evidence of actual loss, many of these contracts build in a specific price allowance or rejection mechanism tied directly to the certifying body's findings, which can produce a faster, more mechanical resolution than domestic litigation would, but only where the exporter has understood and planned for that mechanism in advance.

Shipping and Insurance: Where Another Set of Standard Terms Takes Over

Once a quality and price dispute is resolved, or avoided altogether, an agricultural export shipment still passes through a further layer of standardised international terms that an Indian exporter should understand rather than leave entirely to a freight forwarder. The INCOTERMS rules, published and periodically revised by the International Chamber of Commerce, allocate which party bears the cost and risk of loss at each stage of a shipment's journey, and the specific INCOTERM a contract selects, FOB, CIF and CFR being among the terms most commonly used in agricultural commodity trade, determines not just cost allocation but the precise point at which risk in the goods passes from seller to buyer, a point that can matter considerably if a cargo is damaged or lost in transit.

Marine insurance, taken out by whichever party the selected INCOTERM and the contract's own terms make responsible for it, is the practical mechanism that actually responds to a cargo loss or damage claim once risk has passed, and an exporter should confirm, rather than assume, which party is responsible for arranging this cover under the specific INCOTERM used, and should keep its own documentation, bills of lading, loading certificates, survey reports taken at the point of loading, in a state that would actually support a claim under that cover if a loss occurs during transit, since a cargo claim investigated months after loading, with incomplete contemporaneous documentation, is considerably harder to make good than one supported by records kept as a matter of course at the time of shipment. A business exporting the same commodity repeatedly to the same or similar routes should build this documentation discipline into its standard shipping procedure rather than treat each consignment's paperwork as a one-off task, since the value of contemporaneous records only becomes apparent once a specific shipment has actually run into a loss or damage claim, by which point the opportunity to create better records has already passed.

A Practical Sequencing Checklist

An exporter setting up a new agricultural export business, or adding a new scheduled product to an existing one, is generally better served working through these requirements in a specific order rather than all at once. Securing the Import Export Code from the DGFT comes first, since both the APEDA e-RCMC process and much of the rest of the compliance architecture discussed in this piece depend on that code already being in place. Confirming the correct FSSAI licence category for the business, in light of the March 2026 changes discussed earlier, and specifically whether the business's export-only status now requires a Central Licence it may not previously have needed, should happen alongside or immediately after securing the IEC, since manufacturing cannot lawfully proceed without the correct FSSAI licence regardless of export status.

Confirming whether the specific product sits in the APEDA Act's First Schedule or Second Schedule, and completing the e-RCMC registration or the Second Schedule's own specific compliance steps accordingly, is the next step, followed immediately by checking the current DGFT export policy notification for that specific product, since a business that has completed every registration step correctly can still find itself unable to ship if the commodity is, at that particular time, subject to a restriction none of the registration steps address. A new exporter should also confirm, at this same stage, which destination-country certificate or health certificate requirements actually apply to the intended market, rather than leaving that question for the shipment itself to surface, since a destination's own requirements can add weeks to a timeline an exporter had otherwise planned around the domestic compliance steps alone.

Only once these regulatory steps are in hand does the commercial contract itself deserve the attention this piece's later sections describe, reviewing whether a standard trade association form is being proposed, understanding precisely what law and forum that form actually selects, and negotiating the force majeure, prohibition and payment terms with India's own specific export-policy volatility and the exporter's own actual banking and credit position in mind, rather than treating the contract as a formality to sign once the regulatory groundwork is complete.

What Remains Unsettled

Several points in this area are better understood as live and moving rather than settled. The precise current scope of FSSAI's older certificate-of-conformity exemption for 100% export manufacturers, following the March 2026 licensing overhaul, is not confirmed on the sources available for this piece, and an exporter should check FSSAI's current guidance directly rather than rely on this piece's account of the older position. The current DGFT export policy for non-basmati rice and wheat, as this piece has described at length, has moved more than once within 2026 alone and should be checked immediately before any shipment rather than assumed from this piece's account of the position as it stood when this piece was researched.

More broadly, the interaction between India's own, periodically shifting export-control policy and the force majeure provisions of internationally standard trade contracts remains an area where the outcome in a specific case depends heavily on the precise facts, how foreseeable the particular restriction was, how the specific exporter was positioned relative to it, rather than on a single, generally applicable rule an exporter can rely on across every scenario. NAFED v Alimenta is instructive precisely because of how fact-specific its reasoning was, and an exporter facing a comparable situation today should expect to need the same close factual analysis rather than a simple, transferable answer.

Exporting agricultural produce from India is, on the regulatory side, a matter of getting three separate registrations and permissions right at the same time, an APEDA registration tied to the specific schedule the product falls under, an FSSAI licence whose category has just been reworked for export-only manufacturers, and a DGFT export policy position that for several major commodities has shifted more than once within a single year. None of that work, however, determines how a dispute with the foreign buyer actually gets resolved, a question the contract itself answers, often by selecting English law and London arbitration through a standard trade association form the Indian exporter may not have fully appreciated it was agreeing to.

An exporter who treats the contract's dispute resolution architecture with the same seriousness given to the regulatory compliance work, understanding precisely what law and forum govern, how a force majeure defence built around an Indian government restriction is actually likely to be read, and how payment is actually protected through the mechanism chosen rather than through litigation after the fact, is in a materially stronger position than one that completes every domestic registration correctly and only discovers the contract's real terms once a dispute has already arisen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need APEDA registration to export any agricultural product from India?

Only for products listed in the First Schedule (or the Second Schedule's special products, such as Basmati rice) to the APEDA Act, 1985. For those products, a Registration-Cum-Membership Certificate, obtained online as an e-RCMC after securing an Import Export Code from the DGFT, is mandatory before exporting.

What happens if my APEDA RCMC lapses?

An exporter with a lapsed, unrenewed RCMC cannot lawfully export the scheduled product, and loses access to APEDA's own export-related benefits and schemes, until the registration is renewed. Customs and port authorities are reported to check RCMC status before clearing scheduled products for export.

Does an export-only food manufacturer need the same FSSAI licence as a domestic one?

Not after a Ministry of Health and Family Welfare order dated 13 March 2026, effective 1 April 2026, which requires a 100% export-oriented food manufacturing unit to hold a Central Licence regardless of turnover, a departure from the turnover-based licensing that otherwise applies.

Can the government stop me exporting a product I am otherwise fully registered and licensed to export?

Yes. Separately from APEDA and FSSAI compliance, the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992 lets the Government restrict or prohibit export of specific commodities through DGFT notifications, a power used more than once against wheat and non-basmati rice within 2026 alone, so the current notification for the specific product should be checked immediately before shipment.

If my contract is a standard GAFTA or FOSFA form, does Indian law apply to my export dispute?

Generally not. These standard forms are drafted around English law and refer disputes to that association's own arbitration rules, typically in London, so an Indian exporter who signs one has, as a rule, already agreed that English law and an English-administered arbitration, not Indian courts, will resolve any dispute under the contract.

Can I use a government export restriction as a force majeure defence if I fail to deliver?

It depends heavily on the facts. In National Agricultural Cooperative Marketing Federation of India v Alimenta S.A., (2020) 9 SCC 866, the Supreme Court's history with the underlying award turned substantially on whether the Indian exporter was itself positioned to anticipate the government permission problem, and a tribunal is likely to scrutinise closely whether the restriction was genuinely unforeseeable or one the exporter should have planned around.

What is my best protection if a foreign buyer refuses to pay?

The payment mechanism negotiated into the contract, typically a letter of credit, matters far more in practice than litigation after the fact, since a letter of credit gives the exporter a payment claim against a bank, largely independent of the buyer's own separate disputes, provided the exporter's documents match the credit's terms precisely.
