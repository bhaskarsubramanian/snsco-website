Civil and Commercial Litigation in Delhi: The Forum Map, Pecuniary Jurisdiction and Pre-Institution Mediation

Where a suit belongs in Delhi today, who hears it once it is commercial, and what has to happen before it is even filed

By Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 12 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A business dispute in Delhi can end up in front of a civil judge in a district court, a District Judge sitting as a Commercial Court, or the Delhi High Court's Commercial Division, and choosing wrong can mean a return of the plaint, a transfer, or worse, a rejection under Order VII Rule 11 for skipping a mandatory step before filing. This piece sets out the current forum map, the pecuniary thresholds that decide it, the pre-institution mediation requirement under Section 12A, and the procedural routes, Order XXXVII summary suits and execution, that businesses litigating in Delhi rely on most.

Key points

  • Delhi's district courts hear ordinary civil suits up to a pecuniary value of Rs 2 crore, a limit raised from Rs 20 lakh in 2015; suits above that value go to the Delhi High Court's original side, and the Delhi High Court's Full Court has since recommended raising the district ceiling to Rs 10 crore, a change not yet notified.
  • For commercial disputes specifically, Delhi has notified District Judges as Commercial Courts at the statutory floor of Rs 3 lakh specified value, so a commercial dispute between Rs 3 lakh and Rs 2 crore goes to the district Commercial Court, not to the High Court.
  • A commercial dispute of Rs 2 crore specified value or more is heard by the Commercial Division of the Delhi High Court on the original side, not by an ordinary civil bench.
  • Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 makes pre-institution mediation compulsory for a commercial suit that does not contemplate urgent interim relief; the Supreme Court has held this mandatory, with the obligation applying prospectively from 20 August 2022.
  • Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure gives a faster summary procedure for suits on negotiable instruments and certain written contracts, where the defendant needs the court's leave to even contest the claim.
  • A decree passed in Delhi can be transferred for execution to a court elsewhere in India with jurisdiction over the judgment debtor's assets, under Section 39 and Order 21 of the CPC, and the transferring court loses authority over execution once the transfer is made.
  • A money claim must be valued and stamped on an ad valorem basis matching the amount actually claimed, under Section 7(i) of the Court Fees Act read with Section 8 of the Suit Valuation Act; undervaluing for court fee while overvaluing for jurisdiction invites a direction to pay up or face rejection.
  • An appeal from a Commercial Court or the Commercial Division goes to the Commercial Appellate Division under Section 13 of the Commercial Courts Act within a strict sixty-day period that Delhi High Court benches have generally been reluctant to condone beyond exceptional cases.

Why the forum question comes first in Delhi

A business preparing to sue a defaulting customer, a landlord, or a former partner in Delhi faces a forum question before it faces almost anything else, because Delhi, unlike many smaller cities, runs a layered court system in which the same type of dispute can land before a civil judge, a District Judge sitting specifically as a Commercial Court, or the Commercial Division of the Delhi High Court, depending on value and on whether the dispute qualifies as commercial at all. Getting this wrong is not a minor inconvenience. A suit filed in the wrong forum invites an objection to jurisdiction, can be returned for presentation to the correct court under Order VII Rule 10, and in the commercial context can be rejected outright under Order VII Rule 11 if a mandatory pre-filing step, pre-institution mediation under Section 12A, was skipped altogether.

This piece works through the forum map as it stands today: the general civil threshold that separates the district courts from the Delhi High Court's original side, the lower commercial-specific threshold that puts most business disputes before a District Judge rather than a civil judge or the High Court, the compulsory mediation step that applies before many commercial suits can even be filed, and the procedural tools, principally Order XXXVII summary suits and the machinery for executing a decree once obtained, that businesses in Delhi use most often once the forum question is settled.

The general civil threshold: Rs 2 crore and the Delhi High Court's original side

The Delhi High Court has what is called ordinary original civil jurisdiction, meaning it can hear certain civil suits directly, as a trial court, rather than only in appeal. Section 5(2) of the Delhi High Court Act, 1966, as amended by the Delhi High Court (Amendment) Act, 2015, fixes the threshold for that jurisdiction at suits valued above Rs 2 crore, a figure raised from the earlier Rs 20 lakh, which had itself been fixed back in 2003. The amendment took effect on 26 October 2015, and from that date the Delhi High Court stopped accepting fresh suits valued at or below the new threshold, with pending Original Side suits below the revised cut-off transferred out to the subordinate courts under a separate transfer order, except where judgment had already been reserved.

The practical effect is straightforward: an ordinary civil suit, one that does not qualify as a commercial dispute under the Commercial Courts Act, valued at Rs 2 crore or less is filed before a civil judge or District Judge in the district court system, most commonly at the relevant district complex such as Tis Hazari, Patiala House, Karkardooma, Saket, Dwarka or Rohini depending on where the cause of action or the defendant's residence or business lies, while a suit above that value goes to the Delhi High Court's original side. This pressure has since moved from discussion to a formal recommendation: the five-judge committee set up by the December 2025 circular reported in mid-2026, and the Delhi High Court's Full Court recommended raising the ceiling to Rs 10 crore, short of the Rs 20 crore some Bar Associations continue to press for. The Delhi High Court Bar Association's challenge to the committee process did not stop it, and advocates struck work for two days in July 2026 over the recommendation. As on the date of this article, Rs 2 crore remains the notified, legally operative figure, since the recommendation still needs a further notification step to take effect, but anyone filing a suit near the threshold should treat a change as likely and confirm the current notified limit before relying on it.

The lower commercial threshold: why most business disputes never reach that Rs 2 crore line

The general civil threshold described above is not the number that actually governs most business litigation in Delhi, because the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 operates on its own, lower pecuniary floor for disputes that qualify as commercial in nature, a broad category covering disputes arising out of ordinary transactions of merchants, bankers, traders and the like, including contracts for sale of goods and services, construction and infrastructure contracts, franchising, distribution and licensing agreements, and several other categories listed in Section 2(1)(c) of the Act. The 2018 amendment to the Commercial Courts Act lowered the specified value threshold for a dispute to qualify for the commercial courts regime from Rs 1 crore to Rs 3 lakh, with effect from 3 May 2018, and this lower figure, not Rs 1 crore, has governed since.

Delhi has notified Commercial Courts at the level of its District Courts, with pecuniary jurisdiction matching the statutory floor of Rs 3 lakh specified value, meaning a commercial dispute valued between Rs 3 lakh and Rs 2 crore is heard by a District Judge specifically designated as a Commercial Court, applying the streamlined procedure, including the summary judgment provisions under Order XIII-A and the disclosure obligations under Order XI, that the Commercial Courts Act writes into the ordinary CPC for such suits. A commercial dispute of Rs 2 crore specified value or above instead goes to the Commercial Division of the Delhi High Court on the original side, which applies the same Commercial Courts Act procedure but sits within the High Court rather than the district system. The Delhi High Court has separately clarified, in rulings on the 2018 amendment, that the lower Rs 3 lakh threshold operates prospectively from the amendment's commencement and does not reopen suits that were correctly filed above Rs 1 crore under the earlier regime before the amendment took effect.

The consequence for a business considering litigation in Delhi is that the commercial character of the dispute, not merely its value, decides which track applies. A straightforward unpaid-rent dispute between two individuals who are not in trade is an ordinary civil suit governed by the Rs 2 crore general threshold; a dispute over an unpaid invoice between two companies for supply of goods is a commercial dispute governed by the Rs 3 lakh commercial threshold, even though the amount in question might be far below Rs 2 crore, and is heard before a specially designated Commercial Court rather than an ordinary civil bench, with a correspondingly different and generally faster procedure.

Section 12A: mediation before filing, not after

Before a commercial suit that does not contemplate any urgent interim relief can even be filed in Delhi, Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 requires the plaintiff to first attempt pre-institution mediation through the mechanism the Act and the accompanying rules set up for that purpose. The Supreme Court settled, in Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. & Ors. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd., decided on 17 August 2022 and reported at (2022) 10 SCC 1, that this requirement is mandatory, not merely directory, and that a commercial suit instituted without first exhausting pre-institution mediation is liable to be rejected under Order VII Rule 11 of the CPC for disclosing no cause of action that can be entertained without that step.

The mandatory character of Section 12A applies prospectively, from 20 August 2022, the date the provision was given effect, and does not disturb commercial suits that were filed before that date without pre-institution mediation; those earlier suits are not retrospectively liable to rejection on this ground. For a plaintiff deciding whether mediation can be skipped, the narrow exception that remains is a suit that contemplates urgent interim relief, which the courts have read as requiring the plaintiff to show a genuine, demonstrable urgency on the facts, rather than a routine prayer for interim relief included as a matter of drafting convenience; a plaintiff who includes an interim relief prayer purely to sidestep mediation, without a real urgency to back it, risks the court finding the urgency claim unconvincing and treating the suit as one that should have gone through mediation first.

In practice, this means a business in Delhi planning to sue a commercial counterparty should build the mediation step into its litigation timeline from the outset rather than treating it as paperwork to be completed after drafting the plaint. The mediation process itself, run through the mediation centres set up for this purpose, typically has its own notice period and a window within which it must conclude or be certified as unsuccessful before the suit can be filed, and factoring that period into any limitation-sensitive timeline is essential, since limitation continues to run during the mediation period under the Act's own provisions for that purpose, though the exact computation can matter in a case close to the limitation deadline and deserves specific advice rather than assumption.

Order XXXVII: the summary suit route for written contracts and negotiable instruments

Where a claim is based on a bill of exchange, hundi, promissory note, or certain written contracts for a fixed or ascertained sum, Order XXXVII of the CPC offers a summary procedure designed to secure expeditious adjudication, rather than putting every such claim through the full ordinary trial process. Under this procedure, a defendant served with summons cannot simply file a defence as of right; the defendant must apply for, and be granted, leave to defend, and the court can refuse leave altogether if no triable issue is disclosed, or grant leave conditionally, for instance on deposit of all or part of the admitted or claimed sum, where the defence raises some issue but not one the court considers substantial enough to warrant unconditional leave.

Delhi courts, including the Delhi High Court, have repeatedly applied the governing Supreme Court standard that where the defence raises a bona fide, clear triable issue, going beyond a sham or illusory denial, unconditional leave to defend should ordinarily be granted, since the summary procedure is meant to filter out defences that exist only to delay payment, not to deny a genuine defendant a fair hearing on a real dispute. Rule 3(5) and the related provisions allow the court to impose conditions on leave, including requiring a deposit, where the balance between expedition and fairness tips toward some, but not complete, protection for the defendant. For suppliers, lenders, and businesses holding dishonoured cheques or unpaid invoices backed by clear written acknowledgment, Order XXXVII remains one of the fastest routes to a decree in the Delhi court system, provided the underlying claim fits squarely within the categories the Order covers; a claim resting on an oral understanding, or on a written contract that does not evidence an ascertained sum, does not qualify and must proceed as an ordinary suit.

Execution: getting from decree to recovery, including outside Delhi

A decree is only as useful as the recovery it produces, and a judgment debtor with no assets within the jurisdiction of the Delhi court that passed the decree is a common problem for businesses that have otherwise won their case. Section 39 of the CPC allows the court which passed the decree, on the decree-holder's application, to send it for execution to another court of competent jurisdiction, typically the court within whose territorial limits the judgment debtor resides or holds assets, and Order 21 Rule 8 governs how the transferee court, or a subordinate court under it, then takes up execution. Once a decree is transferred in this way, the transferring Delhi court loses authority to execute it itself; execution control passes entirely to the transferee court, and a decree-holder who wants to execute simultaneously in more than one place needs to apply for transfer to each relevant court separately, since a single transfer order does not automatically authorise parallel execution elsewhere.

A decree passed by a Commercial Court or Commercial Division retains its commercial character for certain execution purposes, and the Delhi High Court has held that it can execute a decree even where the amount now sought to be recovered through execution falls below the pecuniary threshold that would have governed the original suit, so long as the original suit itself was correctly instituted within the court's pecuniary jurisdiction at the time of filing; a later reduction in the amount actually recoverable, for instance after partial satisfaction, does not strip the executing court of jurisdiction it validly had over the suit itself. For a foreign money decree being executed in Delhi, Section 44A of the CPC provides a separate route, letting a decree from a court in a reciprocating territory be executed as if passed by the Delhi court itself, subject to the pecuniary and procedural conditions the section and the relevant notifications impose, a route distinct from execution of a decree Delhi's own courts have passed.

Territorial jurisdiction within Delhi: which court, not just which level

Pecuniary value decides the level of court; Sections 16 to 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure decide which particular court at that level actually has territorial jurisdiction. For suits relating to immovable property, Section 16 requires the suit to be filed where the property is situated, subject to limited exceptions, which matters within Delhi itself because the district court complexes are organised on a territorial basis, so a property dispute over a flat in Dwarka ordinarily belongs before the Dwarka courts, not at Tis Hazari or Saket, regardless of where the parties happen to live or work. For suits on contracts and other disputes not tied to immovable property, Section 20 allows filing where the defendant resides or carries on business, or where the cause of action, wholly or in part, arose, giving a plaintiff some choice among genuinely connected forums but no licence to file wherever happens to be convenient.

A contractual clause naming Delhi, or a specific Delhi court, as the agreed forum is given effect by Indian courts, but only within this framework: where more than one court would otherwise have had jurisdiction under Sections 16 to 20, the parties can validly agree to confine themselves to one of those courts, and Delhi courts will enforce such a clause. What a clause cannot do is manufacture jurisdiction in Delhi where none of the ordinary connecting factors, residence, business, cause of action or property location, has any real link to Delhi at all; a purely cosmetic Delhi jurisdiction clause in a contract performed entirely in Gurugram between parties based in Gurugram is vulnerable to a jurisdiction challenge notwithstanding what the contract says. For a commercial suit specifically, the same territorial principles apply to deciding which Commercial Court within Delhi's district system, or whether the Commercial Division of the High Court, has territorial jurisdiction, layered on top of the pecuniary question already discussed.

Court fees: ad valorem cost as a filing decision, not an afterthought

Filing a civil or commercial suit in Delhi requires payment of court fees under the Court Fees Act, 1870 as applicable in Delhi, and for a money claim, which covers most commercial disputes over unpaid amounts, Section 7(i) of the Act requires ad valorem fees calculated on the sum actually claimed, not on a nominal or tentative figure chosen for convenience. Delhi High Court rulings have been consistently strict on this point: where a plaint is substantially a claim for a specific sum of money, the plaintiff cannot value it nominally for court fee purposes while valuing it at a much higher figure for jurisdiction, since Section 8 of the Suit Valuation Act requires the value for court fees and the value for jurisdiction to match, and a mismatch between the two can result in the court directing payment of the correct ad valorem fee within a fixed period, failing which the suit is liable to rejection.

This has a direct practical consequence for how a claim should be drafted and budgeted from the outset. A claim for mesne profits or ongoing damages, where part of the amount is already quantified and part remains to accrue, still attracts ad valorem fees on the quantified portion as a money claim, and a plaintiff cannot avoid paying fees on a known sum by bundling it into a notionally unliquidated prayer. By contrast, a suit seeking only a declaration, for instance that a particular deed is void, without any consequential relief such as possession or recovery, generally attracts a fixed fee rather than an ad valorem one, though adding a consequential relief such as possession or cancellation to the same suit can convert the fee calculation for that part of the claim to an ad valorem basis. Because the ad valorem fee on a large commercial claim can itself run into a substantial sum, payable upfront before the suit is even registered, this cost should be budgeted and, where relevant, discussed with the client as part of the litigation decision, not treated as a procedural detail to be resolved after the decision to sue has already been made.

Appeals from a Commercial Court or the Commercial Division

A judgment or specified order of a Commercial Court or the Commercial Division of the Delhi High Court is not appealed through the ordinary civil appeal route; Section 13 of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 creates a dedicated appeal to the Commercial Appellate Division of the same High Court, and Section 13(1-A) fixes a strict sixty-day period for filing that appeal from the date of the judgment or order. Delhi High Court benches have repeatedly treated this sixty-day period as tight, declining to condone delays running into several months in more than one reported matter, on the basis that condonation under Section 13 is to be allowed only exceptionally rather than routinely, which is a materially shorter and less forgiving timeline than the ninety-day period generally available for an ordinary first appeal under the Limitation Act.

There is an unresolved tension worth flagging rather than glossing over. The Supreme Court, in the different context of an appeal under Section 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 against an order arising from a commercial arbitration matter, has held that the sixty-day period under Section 13 does not exclude the general power to condone delay under Section 5 of the Limitation Act, which sits in some tension with the stricter line some Delhi High Court benches have taken in ordinary commercial appeals not arising from arbitration. There is also a live question over precisely which interlocutory orders of a Commercial Court can be appealed at all under Section 13(1-A), with some Delhi High Court authority reading the right of appeal as confined to the specific categories of order listed in Order XLIII of the CPC, and other, more recent decisions taking a broader view. Given this, a party considering an appeal from a Commercial Court or Commercial Division order should treat the sixty-day period as the operative deadline to meet rather than assume a longer period or a forgiving condonation standard will be available, while separately taking specific advice on whether the particular order being challenged is even appealable under Section 13 at all.

Delhi NCR complications: Noida, Gurugram, Faridabad and overlapping hubs

Delhi does not sit in isolation from the rest of the National Capital Region, and a significant share of commercial disputes involving Delhi-based businesses in fact have some connection to Noida, Greater Noida, Gurugram or Faridabad, whether because a counterparty is based there, because goods were delivered there, or because the contract itself names one of those locations as the seat of arbitration or the agreed jurisdiction. Each of these is a separate state or sub-state jurisdiction with its own forum structure: Noida and Greater Noida fall under Uttar Pradesh's court system and the Allahabad High Court, with their own commercial court notifications and their own pecuniary thresholds, distinct from Delhi's; Gurugram and Faridabad fall under Haryana's district court system and the Punjab and Haryana High Court. A contract that simply recites Delhi jurisdiction without more care can still face a genuine territorial jurisdiction challenge if the cause of action, the defendant's residence, or the subject property in fact lies substantially outside Delhi, since a jurisdiction clause chosen by the parties is generally only effective where more than one court would otherwise have had jurisdiction under ordinary CPC principles; it cannot by itself confer jurisdiction on a Delhi court that would have none at all on the underlying facts.

This makes early, careful analysis of where the cause of action actually arose, not merely where the parties would prefer to litigate, an essential first step for any Delhi-based business entering a cross-border NCR contract, and a jurisdiction clause drafted without that analysis can end up being challenged and overturned at exactly the point, often after considerable expense, where it matters most.

The Delhi forum map for civil and commercial disputes turns on two separate pecuniary lines, not one: the general Rs 2 crore threshold that separates the district courts from the Delhi High Court's original side, and the much lower Rs 3 lakh commercial threshold that puts most business disputes before a specially designated District Judge rather than an ordinary civil bench or the High Court. Layered on top of both is the compulsory pre-institution mediation step under Section 12A, which can get an otherwise properly filed commercial suit rejected if skipped without a genuine claim to urgent interim relief.

None of this is static. The pecuniary thresholds have moved before, in 2015 and again in 2018, and the Delhi High Court's Full Court has already recommended raising the district courts' general civil ceiling to Rs 10 crore, a mid-2026 recommendation not yet implemented. A business or its counsel filing in Delhi should treat the current figures as a starting point to be verified against the latest notification, not as settled numbers that will hold indefinitely.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the pecuniary threshold for the Delhi High Court's original civil jurisdiction?

Rs 2 crore, as fixed by the Delhi High Court (Amendment) Act, 2015, effective 26 October 2015. Suits valued at or below that figure are filed in the district courts; suits above it are filed directly before the Delhi High Court.

Is the threshold the same for commercial disputes?

No. Commercial disputes are governed by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, which fixes a much lower specified-value floor of Rs 3 lakh for a dispute to qualify as commercial. In Delhi, commercial disputes between Rs 3 lakh and Rs 2 crore go to a District Judge designated as a Commercial Court; Rs 2 crore and above go to the Commercial Division of the Delhi High Court.

Do I have to attempt mediation before filing a commercial suit in Delhi?

Yes, for most commercial suits. Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act makes pre-institution mediation mandatory unless the suit contemplates genuine, demonstrable urgent interim relief, and the Supreme Court has held that a suit filed without it can be rejected under Order VII Rule 11. A prayer for interim relief added only to avoid mediation, without real urgency behind it, risks being found unconvincing. This applies prospectively from 20 August 2022.

What kind of claims can use the Order XXXVII summary suit procedure in Delhi?

Claims based on a bill of exchange, hundi, promissory note, or a written contract for a fixed or ascertained sum, including many unpaid invoice and loan recovery claims. The defendant needs the court's leave to contest the suit, and leave can be refused or granted conditionally if no genuine triable issue is shown.

Can a Delhi decree be executed against a defendant's assets in another state?

Yes. Under Section 39 and Order 21 Rule 8 of the CPC, the Delhi court that passed the decree can, on the decree-holder's application, transfer it for execution to a court with jurisdiction over the judgment debtor's assets elsewhere, and execution control then passes to that transferee court.

Does a jurisdiction clause naming Delhi always guarantee a Delhi court will hear the dispute?

Not automatically. A jurisdiction clause is generally effective only to choose between courts that would otherwise have had jurisdiction under ordinary CPC principles; it cannot by itself confer jurisdiction on a Delhi court if the cause of action and the parties have no real territorial connection to Delhi at all.

How is court fee calculated on a money recovery suit in Delhi?

Ad valorem, on the actual sum claimed, under Section 7(i) of the Court Fees Act, 1870. The value used for court fees must match the value used for jurisdiction under Section 8 of the Suit Valuation Act; a mismatch can result in a direction to pay the correct fee or face rejection of the suit.

How long do I have to appeal a Commercial Court or Commercial Division judgment in Delhi?

Sixty days from the date of the judgment or order, under Section 13(1-A) of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015. Delhi High Court benches have generally treated this period strictly and have declined to condone lengthy delays, so it should not be treated as a flexible deadline.
