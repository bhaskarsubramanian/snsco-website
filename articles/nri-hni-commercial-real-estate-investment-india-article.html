NRI and HNI Investment in Indian Commercial Real Estate: FEMA, RERA, GST and Repatriation

What an NRI or OCI can buy without RBI permission, why a commercial purchase does not run on the same funding and GST rules as a residential one, and how much of the rent and sale proceeds can actually be sent abroad

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Souvik Bose, Associate, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 24 November 2026 · Law stated as on 23 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Most of what an NRI or a high-net-worth family abroad reads about buying property in India is written for a residential buyer: a flat for the family to return to, or a second home to rent out casually. An investor buying an office floor, a retail shop or a warehouse for pure yield runs into a materially different set of rules the moment GST, RERA's commercial projects, and the mechanics of repatriating rent rather than a single sale price enter the picture, and conflating the two sets of rules is where most of the costly mistakes in this kind of investment actually originate.

Key points

  • An NRI or OCI can buy any immovable property in India, residential or commercial, without prior RBI approval, subject to the same exclusion that applies to every other category of property: agricultural land, plantation property and farmhouses remain off-limits for purchase, though they can still be acquired by inheritance.
  • The purchase consideration must be paid through normal banking channels, by inward remittance from abroad or from the buyer's own NRE, NRO or FCNR(B) account, and a commercial purchase intended purely for investment and rental yield typically cannot rely on the specific home-loan financing route built around residential purchase, so funding needs separate planning from the outset.
  • The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 applies to a commercial project in the same way it applies to a residential one, based on the project's land area or number of units rather than its use, so a registered commercial project gives a buyer the same escrow, carpet-area and defect-liability protections a residential buyer gets.
  • An under-construction commercial unit, an office or a shop, typically attracts GST at 12 percent with the developer entitled to claim input tax credit on construction costs, a materially different rate and treatment from the 5 percent, no-input-tax-credit regime that applies to most residential under-construction sales; a ready, completed commercial unit attracts no GST on the sale itself.
  • Renting out a commercial property is itself a taxable supply under GST, generally at 18 percent, payable by the landlord once registration is required, which is a recurring compliance obligation a purely residential NRI investor typically never encounters and needs to plan for separately from the one-time purchase transaction.
  • Rental income and sale proceeds held in an NRO account can be repatriated up to USD 1 million per financial year on the strength of a chartered accountant's certificate and the remitter's own declaration, a route that applies to commercial property without the two-property lifetime cap that the RBI's own rules specifically impose on repatriating the full sale proceeds of residential property.
  • TDS obligations run differently depending on which side of the transaction the NRI sits on: a resident buyer purchasing from an NRI seller must withhold tax on the full sale consideration under what is now Section 393 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (which replaced Section 195 of the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026) unless the seller has obtained a lower or nil deduction certificate, while an NRI buying from a resident seller has the ordinary one percent withholding obligation, now under Section 393(1) of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (previously Section 194-IA), that applies to any buyer once the consideration crosses the statutory threshold.

Why a commercial purchase needs its own analysis, not an adaptation of the residential one

Most published guidance aimed at an NRI investor assumes the property in question is residential: a flat bought for eventual return to India, for a child studying or working there, or as a straightforward buy-to-let. An NRI or HNI family buying an office floor, a retail unit, a warehouse, or a shop purely for rental yield and capital appreciation is making a different kind of investment, and several of the rules that matter most for that investment, GST on the purchase, GST on the ongoing rental income, and which RERA protections actually apply, either work differently for commercial property or barely feature in residential-focused guidance at all.

This article addresses that investor specifically: an NRI, OCI or HNI family based abroad considering a commercial property purchase in India as an investment rather than as a home, and it assumes the reader already has a broad sense of the FEMA framework for NRI property ownership from more general reading. Where the underlying FEMA eligibility rule is genuinely identical for residential and commercial property, this article states it briefly and moves to what is actually different; the separate article on this site addressing an NRI's ordinary residential purchase and title due diligence remains the better starting point for that different transaction.

It is worth being specific, too, about what kind of commercial property this article has in mind, because the category covers a genuinely wide range of assets with different practical dynamics: a single retail shop in a mixed-use building, an office floor in a commercial tower let to one corporate tenant on a long lease, a small warehouse or light-industrial unit, and a share in a larger, professionally managed commercial development are all 'commercial property' in the sense this article uses the term, but they differ considerably in lease structure, tenant concentration risk, and the ease of eventually exiting the investment. The legal framework discussed in this article, FEMA, RERA, GST and repatriation, applies across all of these variants in broadly the same way, but the commercial judgement of which variant actually suits a given investor's risk appetite and liquidity needs sits outside what this article addresses and deserves separate advice from a property or investment professional rather than a legal one.

FEMA eligibility: the same permissive rule, the same one exclusion

The starting position is genuinely the same for residential and commercial property. Under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, which consolidated and replaced the earlier 2018 regulations on this subject, an NRI or OCI can acquire any immovable property in India other than agricultural land, plantation property, or a farmhouse, and needs no prior approval from the Reserve Bank of India to do so. This permissive position applies with equal force to an office, a shop, a warehouse, or an industrial unit as it does to a residential flat; there is no separate, more restrictive FEMA category for commercial property specifically, and no RBI filing or reporting requirement triggered purely by the property being commercial rather than residential.

The excluded categories remain excluded regardless of the buyer's intended use. An NRI cannot purchase agricultural land, a plantation, or a farmhouse even if the stated intention is a commercial agricultural operation or an agri-business investment; that restriction turns on the nature of the land itself under the applicable state land laws and the FEMA rules, not on whether the buyer frames the proposed use as residential, commercial or agricultural investment. Where such property has already passed to an NRI by inheritance, the acquisition itself is permitted, but this is a narrow exception for inheritance specifically and does not open a route for an NRI to purchase agricultural land framed as a commercial acquisition.

A related point worth addressing because investors frequently ask about it is whether the FEMA position changes once the Indian subsidiary, LLP or holding vehicle through which an NRI structures the investment, rather than the NRI personally, is the party on the title deed. The underlying immovable property rules discussed in this section govern an NRI's or OCI's own direct acquisition; where the acquiring entity is instead an Indian company or LLP, the acquisition of the property by that entity is governed by ordinary Indian company and property law rather than by the NRI-specific FEMA property rules, while the NRI's own investment of capital into that entity is governed by a separate layer of FEMA rules dealing with foreign investment into Indian companies and LLPs. The practical result is that a corporate or LLP holding structure does not evade or simplify FEMA; it substitutes one FEMA framework (governing the NRI's capital contribution to the entity) for another (governing the NRI's own property acquisition), and the two frameworks need to be assessed together rather than assumed to cancel each other out.

Funding the purchase, and why the residential home-loan route usually will not fit

Payment for any property acquisition by an NRI or OCI has to come through approved banking channels, either by inward remittance from abroad or by debit to the buyer's own NRE, NRO or FCNR(B) account, and this requirement is identical whether the property is residential or commercial. What differs in practice is the financing route available to fund that payment. Indian banks and housing finance companies have built specific, FEMA-compliant home-loan products for an NRI buying residential property, typically for the buyer's own use or for a family member's use, with structured disbursement and repayment mechanics designed around that residential purchase.

A commercial property purchased purely for investment and rental yield does not fit neatly into that residential home-loan product category, and an NRI investor should expect to fund a commercial acquisition predominantly from the buyer's own NRE, FCNR or NRO balances, from a fresh inward remittance, or through a commercial loan arrangement that the lending bank structures and documents specifically for that purpose, rather than assuming the same loan product used by NRI buyers of residential flats will be available on the same terms for a commercial purchase. An investor should raise financing specifically and early with an authorised dealer bank rather than assume a residential lending relationship will simply extend to the commercial purchase.

Even where a bank is willing to extend commercial lending to an NRI investor, the practical underwriting the bank applies to a commercial purchase tends to differ from residential underwriting in ways that affect timing and documentation. A lender financing a commercial purchase will typically want to see the project's RERA registration and the specific unit's lease or expected rental yield before agreeing terms, in a way a straightforward residential home loan to an NRI, assessed mainly against the buyer's own income and the property's value, generally does not require to the same degree. An NRI investor who has arranged residential home-loan financing in the past, and expects a comparably quick process for a commercial purchase, should build extra time into the transaction timeline for this additional underwriting, particularly where the commercial unit is still under construction and the lease or tenant has not yet been finalised.

Where the NRI investor chooses not to borrow at all, funding the purchase entirely from existing NRE, FCNR or NRO balances or a fresh inward remittance, the transaction becomes considerably simpler from a FEMA compliance perspective, since there is no lender to coordinate with and no loan-specific FEMA conditions to track alongside the property acquisition rules themselves. For a sizeable commercial purchase, this all-equity route also removes the loan-to-value and debt-servicing constraints a lender would otherwise impose, though it naturally requires the investor to have, or to be willing to remit, the full purchase price up front rather than spreading the cost over a loan term.

RERA applies to a commercial project the same way it applies to a residential one

The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 does not distinguish between residential and commercial projects in setting its own registration threshold. Section 3(2)(a) exempts a project from registration where the land proposed to be developed does not exceed 500 square metres, or where the number of units proposed does not exceed eight, counting all phases of the project together, and the appropriate state government can lower either threshold for projects within that state. Most regulatory practice and practitioner guidance reads this as registration being required once either limit is crossed, though the precise 'either, or both' reading of the provision's wording has been the subject of some litigation, and a buyer should not assume registration status purely from a developer's own representation without checking the relevant state RERA authority's own project register.

For a commercial project that crosses the applicable threshold and is duly registered, the buyer of a unit in that project gets the same statutory protections a residential buyer in a registered project gets: the developer's obligation to deposit at least seventy percent of amounts realised from buyers in a separate escrow account used only for that project's construction and land costs, a declared timeline for possession with the authority's power to act on delay, a defect liability period running for five years from possession during which the developer remains responsible for structural defects, and the ongoing obligation to update the project's status with the authority. An NRI or HNI investor evaluating a commercial unit should verify the project's actual RERA registration and its quarterly progress filings in exactly the way a residential buyer would, rather than assume commercial buyers are somehow outside this protective framework.

One practical consequence of RERA's land-area and unit-count threshold, rather than a use-based threshold, is that a single mixed-use development combining residential towers with a retail or office podium is typically assessed as one project against the combined threshold, rather than the residential and commercial components being treated as two separate projects each measured against its own threshold. An NRI investor buying a shop or office unit within a larger mixed-use development should check the RERA registration covering the project as the authority has actually registered it, including which specific units and phases that registration covers, rather than assume the commercial component automatically carries its own separate registration or, conversely, automatically falls outside registration because the commercial portion alone might not cross the threshold if assessed in isolation.

A further point specific to commercial units concerns what RERA's carpet-area disclosure rules actually protect against in a commercial context. RERA requires a developer to sell on the basis of carpet area, the actual usable floor area, rather than the more inflated super built-up area measure that was common practice before RERA, and to specify that carpet area, along with the method used to calculate any attached balcony or terrace area, in the agreement for sale. For a commercial unit, where the difference between carpet area and the common corridor, lobby and shared-facility loading can be substantial in a large commercial tower, this disclosure matters as much, if not more, than it does for a residential flat, and an investor should check the carpet-area figure quoted in marketing material against the figure actually stated in the registered project's own RERA filing and the draft agreement for sale before relying on either.

GST at purchase: 12 percent with input tax credit for an under-construction unit, none for a completed one

GST treatment is one of the clearest points of divergence between residential and commercial property purchase. An under-construction commercial unit, an office space or a retail shop bought directly from a developer before completion, is reported consistently in current practitioner commentary as attracting GST at 12 percent, with the developer entitled to claim input tax credit on construction-related costs, a materially different rate and credit treatment from the 5 percent (for most residential projects) or 1 percent (for affordable housing) rate that applies without input tax credit to most residential under-construction purchases following the 2019 restructuring of real estate GST rates. An investor comparing the headline price of a commercial unit against a residential one should build this GST difference into the comparison rather than assume the same percentage applies across both.

A completed commercial unit, one for which the developer has already obtained the completion or occupancy certificate before the sale, is not subject to GST on the sale itself, since the transaction is treated as a sale of immovable property rather than a supply of a service or works contract for GST purposes; the transaction instead attracts stamp duty and registration charges under the relevant state law, the same as any completed-property transaction. A buyer who understands this distinction can, in some cases, meaningfully affect the all-in cost of acquisition by choosing a completed unit over an under-construction one purely on this basis, though that comparison should sit alongside the ordinary commercial considerations, price, location and rental demand, rather than be decided on the GST point alone.

A related complication specific to an investor who is themselves not a GST-registered business buying the unit for leasing out is that GST paid on an under-construction commercial purchase is generally not available as input tax credit to a buyer purchasing purely for personal, non-business use, in the same way a residential buyer cannot claim credit on GST paid for a flat bought to live in. An NRI investor intending to lease the commercial unit out, rather than occupy it for the investor's own business, sits in a different position, discussed further below, because leasing the unit out is itself a taxable business activity that can support an input tax credit claim, but only once the investor has actually registered for GST in connection with that leasing business; an investor who delays GST registration until after possession, intending to sort out the credit question once the unit is actually let out, risks losing the ability to claim credit on GST paid during the construction period if the registration and credit-claim timing rules are not carefully managed from the point of purchase, a sequencing question worth raising with a GST advisor before the purchase agreement is signed rather than after.

GST on rental income: the recurring obligation a residential-only investor rarely meets

Letting out a commercial property is itself a taxable supply of service under GST, and the rent charged on commercial premises generally attracts GST at 18 percent, a liability that falls on the landlord once the landlord's aggregate turnover from taxable supplies, including this rental income, crosses the ordinary GST registration threshold. This is a materially different position from residential renting for use as a dwelling, which is generally exempt from GST regardless of the landlord's turnover or the amount of rent charged, and it is a compliance obligation an NRI investor moving from residential property to commercial property investment for the first time needs to build into the investment's own operating model from the very outset, not discover once the first year's rent has already been collected without GST having been charged or accounted for.

An NRI landlord who crosses the registration threshold on commercial rental income will need a GST registration in India, periodic GST returns filed on a recurring schedule, and, in practice, a local tax representative or accountant actually managing that ongoing compliance on the landlord's behalf, since routine GST filing is not something an NRI landlord abroad typically manages directly. This operating cost should be budgeted into the investment's expected net yield from the outset rather than treated as a detail to be handled once the property is already let out and generating income.

The registration threshold itself deserves specific attention because an NRI investor with a single, modestly rented commercial unit may genuinely sit below it. The ordinary GST registration threshold for a supplier of services, which letting out commercial property is treated as, is commonly stated at Rs 20 lakh of aggregate annual turnover for most states and Rs 10 lakh for certain special category states, calculated across all of the landlord's taxable supplies under the same PAN rather than unit by unit. An investor with one small commercial unit generating modest rent may fall below this threshold and therefore not need to register or charge GST on that rent at all, while an investor with several units, or one high-value unit let at a substantial rent, is likely to cross it; the threshold should be checked against the investor's actual, aggregate position across all taxable supplies under the same PAN, not assessed unit by unit as though each property were a separate taxpayer.

Where GST registration is required, the compliance obligation is genuinely recurring rather than a one-time registration exercise: periodic GST returns have to be filed whether or not the tenant has paid on time that particular period, and the landlord's GST liability on rent due is generally not contingent on the landlord having actually collected the rent from the tenant. An NRI landlord should factor this into the lease's own payment terms and into the working-capital buffer kept available for GST remittance, since a tenant's late payment does not, by itself, excuse or defer the landlord's own GST filing and payment obligations to the government.

TDS: the buyer's obligation and the seller's obligation are not the same rule

Two distinct TDS obligations can apply to the same commercial property transaction depending on which side of it the NRI occupies, and conflating them is a common and costly error. Where an NRI is the seller, the resident buyer must withhold tax under what is now Section 393 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (replacing Section 195 of the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026) on the entire sale consideration, not merely the capital gain, at the rate applicable to a non-resident seller, unless the NRI seller has obtained a lower or nil deduction certificate from the tax authority under the new Act's equivalent of the former Section 197, a process this article does not repeat in detail since a separate guide on this site addresses it specifically for an NRI seller.

Where an NRI is instead the buyer purchasing from a resident seller, a different and generally simpler provision applies: Section 393(1) of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (the provision that replaced Section 194-IA from 1 April 2026) requires any buyer, resident or non-resident, to withhold tax at one percent of the sale consideration where that consideration is Rs 50 lakh or more, and to deposit that amount with the government and report the transaction, regardless of the buyer's own residency status. An NRI investor buying a commercial unit from an Indian resident developer or seller should expect to comply with this ordinary obligation in the buyer's own capacity, a routine compliance step rather than a residency-specific complication, and should not confuse it with the considerably more involved process that applies only when the NRI is on the selling side of a transaction.

This distinction matters in practice because an NRI investor assembling a small commercial portfolio over time is likely, eventually, to sit on both sides of this question across different transactions: a buyer under Section 194-IA when acquiring a new unit from a resident developer or seller, and potentially a seller under Section 195 if a tax authority or counterparty characterises the investor as non-resident when the investor eventually sells a unit the investor has held for some years. An investor who has only ever experienced the buyer-side 194-IA obligation, a comparatively light, one percent, flat-rate withholding the buyer handles rather than the investor personally, should not assume the eventual sale will be administratively similar or equally light; the seller-side Section 195 position, where the investor is the NRI seller and a resident buyer is withholding on the investor's own sale proceeds, generally requires considerably more active engagement from the investor, including applying in advance for the Section 197 lower or nil deduction certificate if the investor wants to avoid tax being withheld on the full sale price rather than only on the actual capital gain.

Repatriation: the USD 1 million NRO route, and the residential cap that likely does not reach a commercial sale

Rental income and sale proceeds credited to an NRO account can generally be repatriated abroad up to USD 1 million per financial year, running from April to March, on the strength of the remitter's own declaration in the prescribed form and a chartered accountant's certificate confirming that applicable tax has been accounted for on the funds being remitted. This route applies to commercial property rental income and sale proceeds in the same way it applies to any other NRO-account balance, and is the practical mechanism most NRI commercial-property investors will use to bring both the rent and, eventually, the sale proceeds back out of India.

A separate and more restrictive rule, worth flagging specifically because it is easy to misapply, limits the free repatriation of the full sale proceeds of residential property, as distinct from the general USD 1 million NRO scheme, to no more than two residential properties over an NRI's lifetime, with proceeds from a third or later residential property sale falling back to the general USD 1 million annual scheme rather than qualifying for immediate, uncapped repatriation of the full amount. The sources reviewed for this article describe this specific two-property cap as applying to residential property, and this research did not find an equivalent cap stated for commercial property; an NRI investor should nonetheless have their own authorised dealer bank confirm the applicable position for a specific commercial transaction before relying on the absence of such a cap, since bank practice on a point like this can be more conservative than the strict legal position, and the authorised dealer bank is, in practice, the gatekeeper for any repatriation regardless of what the underlying rule technically allows.

The chartered accountant's certificate required for this repatriation is not a purely administrative formality and deserves attention well before the investor actually wants to move funds abroad. The certificate has to confirm that applicable tax on the funds being remitted has been accounted for, which means the investor's Indian tax position, including GST compliance on rental income where that applies, income tax on the rent itself, and capital gains tax on any sale, needs to be current and properly documented before a chartered accountant can issue the certificate the authorised dealer bank will insist on seeing. An NRI investor who has let GST or income tax compliance slip, intending to tidy it up before the next repatriation, risks the certificate itself being delayed or qualified in a way that holds up the remittance precisely when the investor wants to move funds, which is a strong practical reason to keep the investment's tax compliance current on an ongoing basis rather than treating it as something to catch up on only when a repatriation is actually being arranged.

Structuring the investment: direct personal ownership versus a corporate or LLP vehicle

An NRI or HNI investor buying a single commercial unit for rental yield often defaults to direct personal ownership, in the same way a residential purchase is usually held directly, without separately considering whether holding the property through an Indian company or LLP in which the NRI holds shares or a partnership interest would serve the investment better. A corporate or LLP holding structure can simplify succession planning where multiple family members will eventually share in the investment, can centralise GST registration and compliance for a portfolio of several commercial units rather than repeating that compliance personally for each one, and can, depending on the specific facts, affect how gains on an eventual sale are taxed as against a direct personal sale.

This structuring decision interacts with FEMA in its own right, since an NRI's investment into an Indian company or LLP is itself governed by India's foreign investment framework rather than purely by the immovable property rules discussed in this article, and the two frameworks need to be read together rather than treated as alternatives. An investor seriously considering a portfolio of several commercial properties, as against a single unit bought opportunistically, should have this structuring question assessed before the first purchase rather than after several properties have already been acquired directly in the investor's own name, since restructuring an existing direct holding into a corporate or LLP vehicle later is a separate transaction in its own right, with its own stamp duty, capital gains and transfer considerations, rather than a simple administrative reorganisation.

A further consideration for a family weighing a corporate or LLP structure is how the eventual exit, whether a sale of the property itself or a sale of the investor's own shareholding or partnership interest, is likely to be taxed and documented. Selling the shares of the holding company or the partnership interest in the LLP, rather than the underlying property directly, can in some circumstances be a cleaner exit for a buyer who wants the asset without the stamp duty cost of a fresh property transfer, but it shifts the buyer's due diligence onto the holding entity's own history, its compliance record, any liabilities it may carry, and the terms on which the NRI investor's own stake was originally structured. A family building toward this kind of structure should have the eventual exit mechanics considered at the time the structure is first set up, rather than discovering only at the point of sale that the structure chosen years earlier does not, in fact, support the exit route the family now wants to use, a mismatch that is often expensive and sometimes impossible to correct once the structure has been in place for several years.

A practical checklist before signing

Before committing to a commercial property purchase, an NRI or HNI investor should confirm the project's actual RERA registration status directly with the state authority rather than relying on the developer's own representation, obtain written confirmation from the funding bank of exactly which account and remittance route will be used for payment and whether that route is itself FEMA-compliant for the specific transaction, and get a clear, written answer on the applicable GST treatment, 12 percent with credit for an under-construction unit or no GST for a completed one, built into the price negotiation rather than treated as a detail to be resolved after signing.

The investor should also confirm, before signing, who is responsible for the Section 393(1) withholding (the provision that replaced Section 194-IA from 1 April 2026) at the point of payment, since the buyer's own obligation does not depend on the seller reminding the buyer to comply, and should separately plan, from the outset, for the GST registration and periodic compliance the investment will require once it starts generating rental income, rather than treating that as a problem to solve once the first tenant has already moved in. None of these steps is unusually burdensome taken individually, but taken together they are precisely the points at which a commercial property investment, approached with a residential buyer's checklist, most commonly goes wrong.

Two further items belong on this list specifically because they are easy to overlook until a dispute or an audit forces the question. The investor should confirm, in writing with the authorised dealer bank, that funds being remitted into India for the purchase and funds expected to be repatriated later from rent and eventual sale will flow through accounts and documentation the bank itself will recognise for FEMA compliance purposes, rather than assume any ordinary NRE or NRO account automatically satisfies every requirement for a transaction of this specific kind. The investor should also keep a single, organised file from the outset covering the FEMA remittance documentation, the RERA registration and agreement for sale, the GST registration and periodic filings once the property is let out, and every TDS certificate generated on either side of the transaction, since reconstructing this record years later, whether for a tax assessment, a repatriation request, or an eventual sale, is considerably harder than maintaining it contemporaneously as each document is generated, and a well-organised file is often the single most useful thing an advisor can be handed when a question does eventually arise.

FEMA's basic permission for an NRI or OCI to buy Indian property, residential or commercial, without RBI approval is genuinely as simple as it is often described. Everything downstream of that basic permission, funding, GST on the purchase and separately on the rent, RERA's buyer protections, TDS depending on which side of the deal the NRI sits on, and the actual mechanics of sending money back out, runs on rules that diverge meaningfully between a residential flat and a commercial unit bought for yield, and an investor who carries over residential assumptions wholesale into a commercial purchase is the investor most likely to be surprised by a GST bill, a withholding obligation, or a repatriation delay that a residential-focused checklist never would have flagged.

None of these differences make commercial property a materially harder or riskier investment for an NRI or HNI family than residential property; they simply require a separate, specifically commercial analysis rather than an adapted residential one. An investor who maps the FEMA eligibility, confirms the project's RERA status directly, understands the GST position on both the purchase and the ongoing rent, and agrees the funding and repatriation route with the authorised dealer bank before signing, rather than after, is working from the same basic legal permission every NRI property buyer has, applied correctly to the kind of asset actually being bought.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an NRI or OCI buy a commercial shop or office in India without RBI approval?

Yes. Under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, an NRI or OCI can acquire any immovable property in India, including commercial property such as an office, shop, or warehouse, without needing prior approval from the Reserve Bank of India. The only properties excluded from this general permission are agricultural land, plantation property and farmhouses, and that exclusion applies regardless of whether the buyer frames the intended use as residential, commercial or agricultural.

Does RERA apply to a commercial real estate project in the same way it applies to a residential one?

Yes. The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 sets its registration threshold under Section 3(2)(a) by reference to the project's land area (over 500 square metres) or number of units (more than eight), not by reference to whether the project is residential or commercial. A registered commercial project gives a buyer the same escrow, timeline and five-year defect-liability protections a residential buyer in a registered project receives, and a buyer should verify a commercial project's registration directly with the state RERA authority rather than rely solely on the developer's representation.

What GST applies when buying a commercial office or shop that is still under construction?

An under-construction commercial unit is reported in current practitioner commentary as generally attracting GST at 12 percent, with the developer able to claim input tax credit on construction costs, a different rate and treatment from the 5 percent, no-input-tax-credit regime that applies to most residential under-construction purchases. A completed commercial unit, sold after the developer has obtained the completion or occupancy certificate, attracts no GST on the sale itself, only stamp duty and registration charges under the applicable state law.

Does an NRI buying a commercial property from a resident seller have to deduct TDS?

Yes, under the ordinary rule that applies to any buyer. Section 393(1) of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (which replaced Section 194-IA from 1 April 2026) requires a buyer, whether resident or non-resident, to withhold tax at one percent of the sale consideration where that consideration is Rs 50 lakh or more, and to deposit it with the government. This is a separate and generally simpler obligation from the withholding a resident buyer must undertake when purchasing from an NRI seller (now under the 2025 Act's equivalent of the former Section 195), which is based on the seller's non-resident status rather than the buyer's.

How much rental income can an NRI repatriate abroad from a commercial property investment each year?

Rental income credited to an NRO account can generally be repatriated up to USD 1 million per financial year, on the strength of the remitter's declaration and a chartered accountant's certificate confirming applicable tax has been accounted for, under the same general scheme that applies to other NRO account balances. There is no separate, lower cap specific to commercial rental income under this scheme.

Does the rule limiting free repatriation to two residential properties also apply to commercial property sales?

The specific two-property lifetime cap on freely repatriating the full sale proceeds is described in the RBI's own guidance as applying to residential property; this research did not find an equivalent cap stated for commercial property, and commercial property sale proceeds are generally expected to fall under the general USD 1 million per financial year NRO scheme instead. An investor should still have the authorised dealer bank confirm the applicable position for a specific transaction, since banks can apply their own, more conservative practice.

Can an NRI landlord claim GST input tax credit on a commercial property bought to lease out?

Generally, a landlord renting out commercial property and charging GST on the rent, once registered, can claim input tax credit on GST paid on inputs and services used for that taxable rental business, including, depending on the specific facts and current rules, GST paid on the property's own construction where it was bought under construction. This is distinct from an ordinary buyer purchasing property for personal use, who cannot claim input tax credit on an under-construction purchase; an NRI investor intending to lease the property out should have this position confirmed specifically with a GST advisor before relying on it for cash-flow planning.
