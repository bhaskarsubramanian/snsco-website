Where to Sue in Delhi NCR: Territorial Jurisdiction Across Delhi, Noida, Ghaziabad, Gurugram and Faridabad

Why the right court is not always the nearest one, and how a jurisdiction clause, an arbitration seat or a cheque case can each point somewhere different

By Bhaskar Subramanian, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors and Subhradip Roy, Partner, S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors

Published 13 November 2026 · Law stated as on 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A supplier in Noida, a buyer in Gurugram and a contract signed in a Delhi office is not an unusual fact pattern in the National Capital Region, and it raises a question that is logically separate from which level of court should hear the dispute: which of several plausible courts actually has territorial jurisdiction over it. Sections 16 to 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 answer that question for an ordinary suit; a contractual jurisdiction clause, an arbitration seat, and the 2015 amendment on cheque cases each answer it differently again. This piece works through where to sue across Delhi, Noida and Greater Noida, Ghaziabad, Gurugram and Faridabad once the pecuniary and subject-matter questions already covered elsewhere have been settled.

Key points

  • Sections 16 to 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 fix territorial jurisdiction by the nature of the suit: a suit over immovable property ordinarily goes where the property lies, under Section 16, while a suit for compensation for a wrong to a person or to movable property, and the residuary category of suits not covered elsewhere, go where the defendant resides, carries on business, or where the cause of action wholly or partly arises, under Sections 19 and 20.
  • A contract clause naming one of two or more courts that could otherwise hear the dispute is valid once the words show an intention to exclude the others; neither Hakam Singh v. Gammon India nor Swastik Gases v. Indian Oil requires the word "exclusive" or "alone" to achieve that, though using one of those words remains the safer drafting choice.
  • Once an arbitration agreement fixes a seat, the courts at that seat get exclusive supervisory jurisdiction under Section 42 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, following BALCO, Indus Mobile Distribution and BGS SGS Soma JV; a clause naming a "venue" is read as fixing the seat unless the agreement shows a contrary intention.
  • A complaint for a dishonoured cheque under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 is filed where the payee maintains the account through which the cheque was presented for collection, under the amended Section 142(2), reversing the Supreme Court's 2014 ruling in Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod that had confined jurisdiction to the drawee bank's branch.
  • The proviso to Section 16 of the CPC lets a plaintiff sue where the defendant resides even in a suit touching immovable property, but only where the relief claimed can be had entirely through the defendant's personal compliance, a line the Supreme Court restated in Rohit Kochhar v. Vipul Infrastructure Developers in late 2024.
  • The territorial question in Delhi NCR sits on top of, and should be read together with, the pecuniary and subject-matter forum maps already set out for Delhi's own courts and for Noida and Greater Noida's commercial courts; getting the level of court right does not answer which specific court within that level has jurisdiction.
  • Once a defendant has filed a written statement without raising an objection to territorial jurisdiction, courts are reluctant to entertain the objection later unless it goes to the very competence of the court to try the suit at all, which makes raising the point early, not as an afterthought, the safer practice on both sides.

A different question from "which court level": why NCR disputes need both answers

Our earlier piece on civil and commercial litigation in Delhi set out which level of court hears a given dispute there, the district Commercial Court, the Delhi High Court's Commercial Division, or an ordinary civil bench, depending on pecuniary value. Our piece on Noida and Greater Noida did the same for the Gautam Buddh Nagar Commercial Court and the Allahabad High Court. Neither answers the separate question this piece takes up: once you know a dispute belongs at the district level, or at a High Court's original side, which district, and which High Court, actually has territorial jurisdiction when the parties, the property or the events span Delhi, Noida, Ghaziabad, Gurugram and Faridabad.

The two questions get confused because getting either one wrong produces the same symptom, a plaint returned or an objection raised at trial, but the fixes are different. A pecuniary or subject-matter error is usually fixed by filing in the correct court within the same place; a territorial error means refiling in a different city altogether, with its own limitation and cost consequences. Sorting the two questions separately, rather than treating "which court" as a single decision, avoids that second, more expensive mistake.

Section 16: suits about immovable property go where the property is

Section 16 sends a suit for the recovery, partition, sale, redemption or determination of rights in immovable property, and a suit for compensation for a wrong to such property, to the court within whose local limits the property is situate. A dispute over a plot in Sector 62, Noida, or a flat in Dwarka, is heard in Gautam Buddh Nagar or in the relevant Delhi district court respectively, regardless of where the defendant lives or where the sale agreement happened to be signed.

The proviso to Section 16 is the real exception worth knowing. Where the relief sought against a defendant who holds property, or is alleged to have wronged it, can be obtained entirely through that defendant's personal compliance, a money decree being the clearest example, the plaintiff may instead sue where the defendant actually and voluntarily resides, carries on business, or works for gain. The Supreme Court in Rohit Kochhar v. Vipul Infrastructure Developers, deciding a dispute over commercial space in a Gurugram complex, confirmed in November 2024 that the proviso only reaches that far: it does not apply where executing the eventual decree would require the defendant to act on the property itself, handing over possession being the obvious case the proviso does not cover.

Sections 17 and 18: property that crosses a jurisdictional line

Section 17 covers immovable property that falls within the local limits of more than one court's jurisdiction, a farmhouse straddling a boundary between two tehsils being the stock example, and lets the suit be filed in any one of those courts for the whole of the property, so long as the entire claim falls within the pecuniary jurisdiction of the court chosen. Section 18 deals with genuine uncertainty about which of two courts a property actually falls within, and lets either court record a statement to that effect and proceed, a decree from that court then binding as if the property had always been within its limits.

Neither provision comes up often in Delhi NCR litigation in its pure form, since municipal and revenue boundaries are, in practice, well documented. Where it does arise is in peripheral development areas, along the Delhi-Noida or Noida-Ghaziabad boundary in particular, where a large integrated township or industrial estate can straddle two revenue jurisdictions, and a plaintiff should check the relevant khasra or municipal record before assuming Section 16's single-court rule applies cleanly.

Section 19 and the residuary Section 20: everything that is not about land

Section 19 covers suits for compensation for a wrong done to a person, or to movable property, where the wrong was done within one court's jurisdiction but the defendant resides or carries on business within another; the plaintiff can choose either court. Section 20, the residuary provision, catches everything else, a straightforward breach of contract or recovery suit being the usual case, and allows the suit where the defendant resides or carries on business, or where the cause of action wholly or partly arises.

The Explanation to Section 20 deals specifically with a corporation or company as defendant, deeming it to carry on business at its principal office, and, where the cause of action arises at the place of a subordinate office, at that subordinate office as well, for that specific cause of action. A Gurugram-registered company with a Noida branch that actually negotiated and breached the contract in question can, on this reading, be sued in Gautam Buddh Nagar rather than only at its Gurugram head office, so long as the cause of action genuinely arose at the branch rather than merely being administered there after the fact.

What a jurisdiction clause can and cannot do: Hakam Singh to Swastik Gases

Where more than one court would otherwise have jurisdiction under Sections 16 to 20, parties regularly try to fix the question in advance by contract. The Supreme Court upheld exactly this in Hakam Singh v. Gammon (India) Ltd. in 1971, reported at AIR 1971 SC 740, holding that a clause confining disputes to one of several competent courts does not offend Section 28 of the Contract Act's bar on agreements restricting a party's right to enforce its rights through the ordinary tribunals, because the clause only chooses among courts that already had jurisdiction, rather than ousting the jurisdiction of courts altogether.

A.B.C. Laminart Pvt. Ltd. v. A.P. Agencies, decided in 1989, added the requirement that the intention to exclude other competent courts has to actually appear from the clause's words, considered as a whole, rather than being assumed from the mere mention of one court. Swastik Gases Pvt. Ltd. v. Indian Oil Corporation Ltd., a three-judge decision from 2013, then clarified that the absence of words such as "alone," "only" or "exclusive" is not decisive either way; what matters is whether the clause, read naturally, shows the parties meant to confine themselves to the named court. Drafting with one of those express words remains the more reliable approach precisely because the question is otherwise left to construction, case by case.

For an NCR contract, this means a clause reading "courts at Noida shall have jurisdiction" is likely to be read as excluding Delhi or Gurugram courts that might otherwise have had concurrent jurisdiction under Section 20, provided Noida genuinely was one of the courts that could have heard the dispute in the first place. A clause naming a court that has no connection at all to the parties, the property or the cause of action does something different, and raises a separate question of whether the parties can confer jurisdiction on a court that would otherwise have none, which Hakam Singh and Swastik Gases do not address, since both dealt with a choice among courts already competent.

Arbitration seat versus venue: why BALCO and BGS SGS Soma matter for NCR contracts

Where the contract provides for arbitration rather than litigation, the jurisdiction question shifts from Sections 16 to 20 of the CPC to Section 42 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, read with the Supreme Court's seat jurisprudence. Bharat Aluminium Co. v. Kaiser Aluminium Technical Services, the 2012 decision usually called BALCO, established that choosing a seat is, in substance, choosing the exclusive supervisory jurisdiction of the courts at that seat, independent of where the cause of action arose or where the parties are based.

Indus Mobile Distribution Pvt. Ltd. v. DataWind Innovations Pvt. Ltd. applied that principle in 2017 to hold that, once a seat is fixed, courts elsewhere that might otherwise have had jurisdiction under the CPC are ousted, and BGS SGS Soma JV v. NHPC Ltd., a three-judge bench decision from 2019, confirmed this is the settled position and added that a clause naming a city as the arbitration "venue," without more, is ordinarily read as fixing that city as the seat, not merely as a convenient location for hearings, unless the agreement's other terms point the other way.

For an NCR arbitration clause naming, say, Gurugram as the venue for arbitration, with no separate seat clause, the practical consequence is that Gurugram courts alone would ordinarily have jurisdiction over Section 9 interim relief, Section 11 appointment applications and Section 34 challenges, even if the underlying contract was performed in Noida and the defendant resides in Delhi. Section 42 reinforces this once one court has actually been approached: that court's jurisdiction over all subsequent applications in the same arbitration becomes exclusive, which is why filing the first application in the seat court, rather than in whichever NCR court happens to be most convenient that week, matters from the outset.

Interim relief under Section 9: which NCR court actually hears it

A party needing urgent interim protection, an injunction against alienating property, an order preserving goods or funds, pending or before an arbitration, applies under Section 9 of the Arbitration Act to the court that would have jurisdiction over the arbitration's subject matter. Once BALCO and BGS SGS Soma are applied, that ordinarily means the seat court, not any NCR court where the applicant finds it convenient to file, and a Section 9 application filed in the wrong NCR city risks being returned for want of jurisdiction at exactly the moment speed matters most.

Where the arbitration clause is silent on seat and only names a venue, or is silent on both, the question of which court counts as the seat court can itself become contested, and Section 42's rule that the court first approached acquires exclusive jurisdiction over later applications becomes the operative principle rather than a fallback. Filing the first Section 9 application carefully, in a court that has a genuine connection to the seat or, absent a fixed seat, to the cause of action, therefore does double duty: it secures the interim relief and it fixes the forum for everything that follows in that arbitration.

Interim relief in an ordinary suit: Order XXXIX and the limits of reach across State lines

Outside arbitration, an ad-interim injunction under Order XXXIX of the CPC is granted by the court actually seised of the suit, which means the territorial analysis above decides where urgent interim protection has to be sought, every bit as much as it decides where the final decree will eventually come from. A plaintiff who sues in the wrong NCR court does not get to treat that court's interim order as valid simply because the order was granted quickly and the error is discovered later; an injunction from a court that never had territorial jurisdiction is vulnerable to being set aside on that ground alone, independent of its merits.

An injunction validly granted by, say, a Gautam Buddh Nagar court does bind the defendant personally wherever the defendant is found, since an injunction operates against the person rather than against a particular State's territory, and disobedience anywhere can be pursued as contempt before the court that granted it. What the order does not do on its own is compel a bank branch, a land registry or a third party in Delhi or Haryana to act; enforcing an interim order against assets or records located outside the granting court's own State routinely needs a separate step, serving a certified copy on the third party and, where the third party resists, approaching the court where that asset or record actually sits. Parties who assume an NCR interim order self-executes across State lines without any further step are usually the ones who discover the gap only when a bank or registrar in another State simply does not comply.

Cheque cases across NCR: Section 138 and 142 after the 2015 amendment

Cheque bounce litigation has its own, statute-specific territorial rule, and it changed significantly in 2015. The Supreme Court held in Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v. State of Maharashtra, decided in 2014, that jurisdiction under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 lay exclusively with the court within whose limits the drawee bank, the bank on which the cheque was drawn, was situated. That ruling caused real difficulty for payees who had presented cheques for collection through their own bank in a different city from the drawee bank.

Parliament responded with the Negotiable Instruments (Amendment) Act, 2015, inserting Section 142(2), which fixes jurisdiction instead by reference to the payee's own account. Where the cheque is delivered for collection through an account, the court having jurisdiction is the one within whose limits the branch of the bank at which the payee or holder in due course maintains that account is situated, and the Explanation to the clause treats a cheque deposited at any other branch of the same bank as delivered to the account-holding branch. Where the cheque is instead presented for payment otherwise than through an account, jurisdiction lies with the court within whose limits the branch of the drawee bank at which the drawer maintains the account is situated. Section 142A was added as a transitional provision requiring pending multiple prosecutions on the same cheque to be transferred to the court that would have jurisdiction under the amended rule. For an NCR cheque dispute, the practical upshot is that a payee who maintains the collecting account at a Noida branch can prosecute in Gautam Buddh Nagar even though the drawer and the drawee bank are both in Gurugram, reversing what Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod would have required.

A civil recovery suit on the same dishonoured cheque, as distinct from the Section 138 criminal complaint, is not governed by Section 142 at all and instead follows the ordinary Section 20 CPC rule, where the defendant resides or carries on business, or where the cause of action, the dishonour itself, arose. The two proceedings can therefore end up in different cities even though they arise from the same cheque, and a complainant running both should check each forum separately rather than assume the criminal court's jurisdiction carries over to the civil suit.

Reading this alongside Delhi's forum map and Noida's commercial courts

This territorial layer sits on top of the pecuniary and subject-matter answers already worked out elsewhere. A commercial dispute of specified value above Delhi's Commercial Division threshold, arising wholly from events and parties in Delhi, goes to the Delhi High Court's Commercial Division under the forum map already described; the same value of dispute, if the cause of action and the defendant's business are genuinely in Noida or Greater Noida, goes instead to the Gautam Buddh Nagar Commercial Court or, above its ceiling, to the Allahabad High Court, following the separate forum map set out for that district.

What changes none of that is a plaintiff's own convenience or preference. A Delhi-based plaintiff cannot sue a Noida-based defendant over a wholly Noida transaction in a Delhi court merely because filing there is easier, and the correct response to a plaint that gets this wrong is usually an application for return of the plaint under Order VII Rule 10 of the CPC for presentation to the proper court, not a fight over the merits in a court that was never the right one to begin with.

Gurugram and Faridabad add a further wrinkle that the Delhi and Noida pieces did not need to address: both fall under Haryana, with their own Commercial Courts constituted under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 and their own High Court, the Punjab and Haryana High Court at Chandigarh, rather than the Delhi High Court or the Allahabad High Court. A dispute that would be heard at the Delhi High Court's Commercial Division had it arisen wholly in Delhi does not simply move to the Delhi High Court because the parties are used to litigating there; if the defendant's business and the cause of action are genuinely in Gurugram, the Punjab and Haryana High Court's appellate and supervisory jurisdiction over the Gurugram Commercial Court is what actually applies.

Working through three common NCR fact patterns

Take a supply contract negotiated by email, signed by the Noida-based buyer at its Noida office, with goods delivered to a Gurugram warehouse and the seller's registered office in Delhi, and no jurisdiction clause. Section 20 gives the plaintiff a genuine choice here: Delhi, where the defendant seller carries on business through its registered office, or Noida or Gurugram, wherever the cause of action, the breach itself, actually arose, which usually means the place of delivery or the place payment was due, depending on how the contract defines performance.

Add an arbitration clause naming "Delhi" as the seat, and the picture changes entirely: Section 9, Section 11 and Section 34 applications now belong exclusively to Delhi courts under BALCO and Section 42, regardless of where delivery happened or where the defendant is based, because the parties chose to take that question out of the CPC's default rules altogether by agreeing on a seat.

Now take a cheque issued by the Gurugram buyer, dishonoured, and presented by the Noida seller at its own bank. The Section 138 complaint goes to Gautam Buddh Nagar under the amended Section 142(2), following where the cheque was presented for collection; a parallel civil suit for the underlying debt, if the seller chooses to file one instead of or alongside the criminal complaint, follows Section 20 and can equally be filed in Gurugram, where the buyer carries on business, or in Noida, if the cause of action is read as having arisen there. The same set of facts, three different proceedings, three potentially different answers to where each one belongs.

Finally, suppose the Noida seller, worried the Gurugram buyer is about to move stock out of a Faridabad warehouse, needs an urgent injunction. Filing that application in the court actually seised of the main proceeding, Gautam Buddh Nagar on the facts above, gets a valid order, but collecting it is a second step: a certified copy served on the Faridabad warehouse operator, and, if the operator does not comply, a further application to the Haryana court that can actually reach that warehouse, rather than assuming the Uttar Pradesh court's order reaches across the State line on its own.

Raising, and losing, the territorial objection

Territorial jurisdiction, unlike the inherent competence of a court to try a particular kind of suit at all, is treated as a matter the defendant can waive by not raising it in time. A defendant who files a written statement contesting the suit on the merits without objecting to the court's territorial jurisdiction runs a real risk of losing the point later, since courts have consistently held that an objection to the place of suing has to be taken at the earliest opportunity, ordinarily in the written statement itself, and supported by a showing of prejudice, not raised for the first time once the trial has moved on.

The safer course on both sides of an NCR dispute is to treat the territorial question as a threshold issue decided before, or at the latest alongside, the first substantive hearing, rather than as a card to be played later if the case is going badly on the merits. A plaintiff who has genuinely checked the Section 16 to 20 position, any jurisdiction clause, and any arbitration seat before filing avoids the far more expensive outcome of a plaint returned, or a suit dismissed for want of jurisdiction, after years of litigation on the merits.

None of the rules in this piece displace the pecuniary and subject-matter forum maps already worked out for Delhi and for Noida and Greater Noida; they sit above those maps and answer a different question, which city's courts get to apply them in the first place. Getting that question right at the outset, before a plaint is drafted rather than after it is returned, is the difference between one filing and two.

A contract that spans Delhi, Noida, Ghaziabad, Gurugram and Faridabad, as most commercial relationships in the region eventually do, is worth a specific jurisdiction clause and, where arbitration is contemplated, a specific seat clause, rather than leaving Sections 16 to 20 of the CPC and Section 42 of the Arbitration Act to sort out the answer after a dispute has already arisen and positions have already hardened.

The State line running through the region is easy to forget in day-to-day business dealings and expensive to rediscover in litigation. Delhi is a Union Territory with its own High Court; Noida and Greater Noida sit in Uttar Pradesh under the Allahabad High Court; Gurugram and Faridabad sit in Haryana under the Punjab and Haryana High Court. A forum choice that looks like a matter of convenience when a deal is signed can turn into the first contested issue once it is not.

Frequently Asked Questions

My contract has no jurisdiction clause. Can I sue in Delhi simply because my office is there?

Only if Delhi is actually one of the places Section 20 of the CPC recognises for this suit, meaning the defendant resides or carries on business there, or the cause of action wholly or partly arose there. Your own location as plaintiff is not on that list; a suit filed in Delhi on that basis alone is liable to be returned for presentation to the correct court.

Does a jurisdiction clause need the word "exclusive" or "alone" to actually work?

Not as a strict rule; Swastik Gases v. Indian Oil held that the absence of such words is not decisive, so long as the clause's language, read as a whole, shows an intention to confine disputes to the named court. That said, using an express word such as "exclusive" removes an argument the other side could otherwise raise, and is the safer drafting practice regardless of what the case law allows on its own.

My arbitration clause names Delhi as the "venue." Does that rule out Noida or Gurugram courts?

Under BGS SGS Soma, a venue named without a separate seat clause is ordinarily read as the seat itself, which would give Delhi courts exclusive supervisory jurisdiction over Section 9, Section 11 and Section 34 applications under Section 42 of the Arbitration Act. Check the rest of the clause for any wording that points the other way before assuming this is settled for your specific agreement.

I presented a dishonoured cheque at my bank in Noida, but the drawer's bank is in Gurugram. Where do I file the Section 138 complaint?

At the court within whose jurisdiction the branch of your own bank at which you maintain the collecting account is situated, under the amended Section 142(2) of the Negotiable Instruments Act, since the Explanation to that clause treats a cheque deposited at any other branch of the same bank as delivered to the account-holding branch. The 2015 amendment moved jurisdiction away from the drawee bank's location, which is where the Supreme Court's earlier ruling in Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod had placed it.

The defendant lives in Ghaziabad but the disputed property is in Noida. Which court hears the suit?

Ordinarily the court where the property is situated, Gautam Buddh Nagar in this example, under Section 16 of the CPC, since a suit concerning rights in immovable property is tied to the property's location rather than the defendant's residence. The proviso to Section 16 could shift this to Ghaziabad only if the relief sought can be obtained entirely through the defendant's personal compliance, such as a pure money claim, and does not require dealing with the property itself.

Can I challenge territorial jurisdiction after I have already filed my written statement?

It becomes considerably harder. Courts treat an objection to the place of suing as one that has to be raised at the earliest opportunity and supported by a showing of actual prejudice, and a defendant who contests the suit on the merits first, raising jurisdiction only later, risks being held to have waived the objection.

Does a company's registered office in Delhi mean every dispute with its suppliers must be filed in Delhi?

No. Under the Explanation to Section 20, a company is deemed to carry on business at its principal office, and equally at a subordinate office for a cause of action that genuinely arose there. A supplier whose dealings were entirely with the company's Noida or Gurugram branch, and whose cause of action arose there, can generally sue at that branch location rather than being forced to the Delhi head office.

An arbitration award was passed with its seat in Delhi. Can I enforce it against assets in Gurugram without going back to the Delhi court?

Enforcement of an award under Section 36 of the Arbitration Act is treated as execution of a decree, and execution can generally be pursued, or transferred, to a court with jurisdiction over the assets to be attached, even where that court is outside the seat. The seat court's exclusive jurisdiction under Section 42 governs applications concerning the arbitration itself, challenge, appointment, interim relief before an award, rather than the mechanics of chasing a judgment debtor's assets once an enforceable award or decree already exists.
