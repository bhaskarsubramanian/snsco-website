Unfastened Doors and No Security Escort in a Reserved Coach Amount to Deficiency in Service: NCDRC

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Law Trend and ksandk.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A robbery on a moving train more than two decades ago has finally produced a final word from the country's top consumer forum on a question that outlived the original complaint by years: can a passenger sue the railways in a consumer forum for a crime committed by someone else on board. Surender Pal Singh and his daughter Preeti were travelling in a reserved sleeper coach, S-8, of the Awadh-Assam Express on the night of 28 August 2004, on a journey from Hapur to Guwahati. Somewhere between Barpeta and Rangiya, six or seven armed men entered the coach and robbed the passengers.

The complainants said the robbery happened because nobody was watching the coach. There was no attendant on duty, no security escort assigned to the reserved coach, and the doors were not fastened for the night, which the complainants argued gave the robbers an easy way in and an easy way out. They filed a consumer complaint seeking compensation for their loss, treating the railways' failure to secure the coach as a deficiency in service rather than pursuing only the statutory compensation route under the Railways Act for what the law calls an untoward incident.

That framing was itself contested. The railways' position, consistent with how such cases are often defended, was that an armed robbery on a train is an untoward incident covered by a specific statutory compensation scheme under the Railways Act, and that a separate consumer complaint over the same event should not be entertained alongside that remedy. The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission rejected that argument. The bench of President Justice A.P. Sahi and Member Bharatkumar Pandya held that a consumer complaint grounded in negligent security arrangements is maintainable before the consumer fora as an additional statutory remedy, even where the underlying event also qualifies as an untoward incident under railway law.

Having cleared that threshold question, the Commission turned to whether the railways had actually failed in its duty. It found that passengers holding reserved berths carry a legitimate expectation that the railways will provide reasonable security arrangements for their safety during the journey. The absence of any attendant or escort in the coach, combined with the fact that the robbers were able to remain in the coach for a sustained period and then leave without being stopped, pointed squarely to a security lapse rather than an unavoidable criminal act the railways could not have prevented.

On the question of how much to award, the Commission was considerably less generous to the complainants than their claim asked for. Surender Pal Singh and his daughter had sought compensation of Rs 59,725 for the property taken in the robbery, but the Commission found the proof of that figure weak and declined to accept it at face value. It settled on a consolidated compensation of Rs 20,000 for the two complainants together, awarded with interest, rather than the amount claimed or the sum a lower consumer forum had previously granted in the same proceedings.

The ruling is a reminder that winning on liability and winning on quantum are two different battles in consumer litigation, and a complainant can succeed completely on the first while still coming away with far less than expected on the second. The Commission did not doubt that the railways had failed to secure the coach adequately. It simply was not satisfied that the complainants had proven the value of what was stolen to the extent they claimed, and compensation followed the proof rather than the demand.

For railway passengers and their lawyers, the case confirms that a security lapse in a reserved coach, whether it is an unfastened door or the absence of an escort, can ground a successful deficiency in service claim in a consumer forum, independent of whatever compensation scheme applies under railway-specific legislation for the criminal act itself. For the Railways, the lesson running through more than two decades of litigation on this single incident is that documentation of security measures actually deployed on a given journey matters, because the absence of that documentation, more than anything else, is what tips these cases toward a finding of deficiency.

Why It Matters

Passengers robbed or injured in a reserved railway coach because of inadequate security, such as missing escorts or unfastened doors, can pursue a consumer complaint for deficiency in service independently of any statutory compensation available under the Railways Act for an untoward incident. The ruling also signals that consumer fora will scrutinise the proof behind a compensation claim closely, so complainants should be prepared to substantiate the value of any loss with more than a bare assertion.
