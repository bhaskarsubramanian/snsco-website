A Signed Term Sheet Marked 'Non-Binding' Can Still Create an Enforceable Exclusivity Obligation: High Court

Source: As reported by startup and venture-finance trade press in October 2026, on a High Court ruling arising from a collapsed funding round. Party names below are fictitious.

A High Court has held that a venture capital term sheet signed between fictitious startup NovaStack Ventures Pvt. Ltd. and an investor, though expressly stating that the commercial and valuation terms were 'non-binding and subject to definitive documentation,' contained a separately worded exclusivity clause that remained independently binding on the company.

The founders argued that the entire term sheet, having been labelled non-binding, could not give rise to any enforceable obligation, including the exclusivity clause under which the company had agreed not to solicit or negotiate with competing investors for 45 days. The Court disagreed, holding that a term sheet is not an all-or-nothing document: parties are free to designate certain clauses, such as exclusivity, confidentiality, cost allocation and governing law, as intended to take immediate binding effect even while the commercial terms of the investment itself remain subject to definitive agreements and closing conditions.

On the facts, the term sheet's exclusivity clause used unambiguous language distinguishing it from the surrounding commercial terms, which the Court found sufficient to establish the parties' intention that it bind the company independently of whether the funding round ultimately closed.

Why It Matters

Startups and investors negotiating term sheets should read every clause on its own terms rather than assuming a general 'non-binding' label at the top of the document protects them from all its provisions: exclusivity, confidentiality and cost-allocation clauses are routinely drafted, and enforced, as immediately binding regardless of what happens to the underlying deal.
