CCI Clears Advent International's Rs 3,150-Crore Stake Buy in Noida's Yatharth Hospitals

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, digitalhealthnews.com, omnicuris.com and digitalhealthnews.com (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A client asking whether an offshore private equity fund can simply write a cheque into an Indian hospital company without clearing a regulatory hurdle first usually gets the same answer: not if the numbers cross the thresholds under the Competition Act, 2002. The Competition Commission of India confirmed as much again on 10 November 2026, when it approved the acquisition of a stake in Yatharth Hospital & Trauma Care Services Limited by Rasmalai Limited, an investment-holding vehicle incorporated in Cyprus and controlled indirectly by funds managed or advised by Advent International, the Boston-headquartered private equity firm.

Yatharth runs a chain of multi-speciality hospitals concentrated in Noida, Greater Noida and the wider Delhi-NCR belt, with a listed holding company, Yatharth Hospital & Trauma Care Services Limited, at the top of the structure. Under the definitive agreement the company announced on 17 September 2026, Advent International is to put in roughly Rs 3,150 crore of primary capital in exchange for a reported 24.9 per cent stake, with the founding Tyagi family staying on as the largest shareholder once the transaction closes. A primary infusion of that kind does not buy out the promoters; it hands the company fresh capital to expand bed capacity and build out newer facilities, while the family keeps operational control.

The CCI's role here has nothing to do with whether the deal is good for Yatharth's patients or its minority shareholders. Section 6 of the Competition Act requires any party to a combination that crosses the asset or turnover thresholds in Section 5 to notify the Commission before giving effect to it, and Section 31 sets out the three outcomes available: approval, approval subject to modifications, or an order that the combination is likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition. For a private equity fund buying a minority stake in one hospital group with no overlapping Indian healthcare assets of its own, the assessment tends to be a formality in substance, but it is not a formality the parties can skip. A deal that proceeds to closing before the Commission has signed off exposes both sides to penalties under Section 43A, calculated on turnover or assets, regardless of how uncontroversial the underlying transaction might be.

Structuring the investment through a Cyprus holding company is itself unremarkable and follows a familiar route for funds investing into India, usually chosen for treaty access and for the ease of layering further co-investors above the Indian operating company without disturbing the Indian capital structure each time. What a foreign investor cannot avoid, whatever jurisdiction the holding vehicle sits in, is that the target is an Indian company and the transaction has to clear both the Foreign Exchange Management Act's reporting requirements for inbound investment and, once the financial thresholds are met, the CCI's merger control process. Hospitals sit on the automatic route for foreign direct investment, which removes one layer of prior government approval that would otherwise apply in more sensitive sectors, but it does not remove the competition clearance.

For promoter families weighing a primary capital deal with an overseas fund, the Yatharth structure is a reasonably common template worth understanding before the term sheet stage. Capital comes in against freshly issued shares rather than against the family's own shareholding, dilution is calibrated so the family keeps the largest single block and, by extension, the boardroom, and the fund gets its economic upside through growth in the company's value rather than through an immediate control premium. None of that changes what the fund has to disclose to the CCI or how long the clearance can take to come through, and deal timetables that assume a quick signing-to-closing gap without building in time for that clearance tend to run into trouble later.

The practical lesson for a foreign fund or an Indian family business looking at a similar deal is to treat the competition filing as a work-stream that starts the day term sheets are signed, not a box to tick once the shareholders' agreement is final. Getting the combination notice drafted, the relevant market defined correctly and the thresholds calculated on the right financial year's figures takes real time, and a hospital chain operating across several states can throw up classification questions on market definition that a purely single-city target would not. Advisers on both sides of a cross-border healthcare deal would do well to build that lead time in from the outset rather than discover it once the lawyers start talking to each other.

Why It Matters

A foreign private equity fund investing fresh capital into an Indian hospital group, even through an offshore holding vehicle and even where the promoter family keeps control, still has to clear the Competition Commission before the deal can close if the Section 5 thresholds are met. Families negotiating a primary capital round with an overseas fund, and funds structuring the investment vehicle, should treat CCI timelines as part of the deal calendar from the term sheet stage rather than a late-stage formality.
