NCLAT Keeps Company Assets Frozen Pending Share Valuation in Thakkar Family's Rukshmani Syntex Dispute

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and TaxGuru. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Two brothers who each hold an equal stake in a family company found themselves in front of the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal not to settle whether one had wronged the other, but to decide what happens to the company's assets while that question waits to be answered. Mehul Narayan Thakkar holds 45,993 equity shares in Rukshmani Syntex Private Limited, amounting to 37.09 per cent of its paid-up capital. His elder brother Vikram holds an identical 37.09 per cent stake, and their father holds a negligible remaining shareholding.

Mehul's petition alleged oppression and mismanagement under the Companies Act, built around claims that he had been excluded from management, removed as whole-time director, and that money and company assets had been extracted in ways that hurt his interests as a shareholder. His prayer gave the tribunal two routes: restore joint management so that he and his brother ran the company together again, or, failing that, order a buyback of his shares at a fair value fixed by an independent valuer.

The NCLAT's New Delhi bench had already stepped in once before, granting a status quo order on 27 March 2026 to freeze the position while the dispute played out. By the time the matter came back before the bench on 18 August 2026, something had shifted the terrain: Mehul himself had offered a buyback of his shares to the company, effectively narrowing the live dispute from a full-blown oppression fight to a question of valuation and exit.

That offer changed what the tribunal needed to decide. Because Mehul had put a buyback on the table himself, the bench found that the underlying merits, the detailed allegations of exclusion, removal and asset extraction, did not need to be resolved on this round. The order records that, owing to the appellant having offered a buyback of his shares, much of the controversy the respondents might otherwise have contested was not left to be decided on merits. The tribunal kept the status quo over the company's assets in place and directed the NCLT Mumbai bench to appoint a valuer and obtain a valuation report without delay.

What the order does not do is pick a side on the oppression allegations themselves. Mehul's claims about being pushed out of management and about asset extraction remain exactly where they were, neither accepted nor rejected, because the buyback offer gave the tribunal a narrower, more practical problem to solve first: what are the shares actually worth, and how quickly can that number be established so the brothers can go their separate ways commercially.

This is a common pattern in family company disputes once relationships have broken down beyond repair. Rather than litigate every disputed fact about who did what to whom, a buyback offer lets the tribunal sidestep the harder merits questions and focus the proceeding on valuation, which is a more tractable problem for a court-appointed expert to solve than years of accumulated grievance between brothers who built a business together and then fell out over how to run it.

For minority shareholders in similarly deadlocked family companies, the case shows a workable route: offering a buyback can move a stalled oppression petition toward a concrete exit without requiring the tribunal to adjudicate every contested allegation first. For majority shareholders and the companies themselves, the lesson is that keeping the status quo on assets frozen while valuation proceeds is likely to be the default order in a case like this, so advance planning for liquidity to fund an eventual buyback is worth doing before the valuer's report lands rather than after.

The order also leaves open a practical question that will matter once the valuer reports back: what happens if either side disputes the valuation figure. A family dispute framed around a buyback offer can still produce a second round of litigation if the number the valuer settles on strikes one brother as too low and the other as too high, which is a common outcome in contested share valuations where there is no active market price to anchor the figure. The tribunal's direction to the NCLT Mumbai bench to move quickly on appointing a valuer suggests an awareness that delay here carries its own cost, since a frozen company cannot easily raise fresh capital, enter new contracts with confidence, or plan beyond the short term while its ownership structure remains unsettled. Family businesses watching this dispute from the outside should also note how little time it took for a full-blown oppression petition to narrow to a single practical question once one side offered an exit, though that compression depends heavily on whether the remaining shareholders can actually fund a buyback once the valuation lands.

Why It Matters

A minority shareholder locked in a family company dispute can shift the proceeding away from contested oppression allegations and toward a faster valuation-and-exit track simply by offering a buyback of their own shares, as this appellant did. Companies and majority shareholders facing such a petition should expect the tribunal to freeze assets pending valuation rather than rule on the merits once a buyback offer is on the table, and should plan liquidity for a potential buyout accordingly.
