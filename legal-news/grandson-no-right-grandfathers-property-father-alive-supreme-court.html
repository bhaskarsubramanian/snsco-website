Supreme Court: Grandson Has No Birthright In Property While Father Is Alive

Source: Originally reported by Verdictum, businesstoday.in, outlookmoney.com and LiveLaw. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A grandson cannot claim a share in his grandfather's property simply because he was born into the family, once that property has already passed to his father as an individual heir under the Hindu Succession Act. The Supreme Court has confirmed this in dismissing a grandson's long-running claim to half of land that his grandfather's estate had allotted to his father well before the grandson was even born.

The dispute traced back to 1993, when the appellant's aunt, one of the grandfather's daughters, filed a suit for partition of the family's land. A compromise decree in 1994 allotted the disputed plot to the appellant's father as his share. The appellant himself was born later that year, and in 1997 his father sold the land to two purchasers. Years afterward, the appellant, acting as a minor through his mother and natural guardian, sued claiming half of that land, arguing it had remained coparcenary property in his father's hands and that he, as a grandson, held a birthright in it from the moment of his birth.

A trial court initially agreed and decreed partition in the appellant's favour. The first appellate court reversed that decree, and the Madras High Court upheld the reversal. The matter reached the Supreme Court on a further appeal, with the appellant leaning on Section 29A of the Hindu Succession (Tamil Nadu Amendment) Act, 1989, arguing that his father continued to hold the land as karta of a joint family rather than as its absolute owner, so that the grandson's interest had never been extinguished.

Justices Augustine George Masih and Sunita Agarwal rejected that argument. The Bench held that once the grandfather's estate devolved under Section 8 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, it passed to his Class I heirs, including the appellant's father, as individual, exclusive property rather than as property held in a continuing coparcenary. A grandson is not a Class I heir while his own father is alive, so he could not step directly into a share of the grandfather's estate, and the 1994 partition made the father's allotted share his own property to deal with as he wished, sale included.

The ruling closes off an argument that recurs often in Indian family property disputes: that a male descendant acquires an automatic, birth-based interest in ancestral land regardless of how many generations and partitions have already passed. Where a grandfather's property has already devolved under Section 8 and been formally partitioned among his statutory heirs, a grandson born afterward inherits nothing directly from the grandfather; whatever he eventually receives comes through his own father, on his father's terms and at his father's time, not as of right from birth.

This question comes up constantly for families with members settled abroad, where a grandchild living overseas assumes that a share in the grandparents' house or land is automatically theirs because the property has stayed in the family for generations. The Supreme Court's reasoning applies regardless of where the grandchild lives: what decides the claim is whether the grandfather's estate was still undivided coparcenary property at the relevant time, or had already devolved and been partitioned under Section 8, converting it into the father's individual property. An NRI or OCI grandchild checking into a family property dispute needs to establish which of those two states applied, rather than assume lineage alone creates a right.

For families negotiating a partition or drafting a family settlement, the lesson is to be precise about whether a partition has actually occurred and when. A compromise decree, like the one in 1994 here, fixes each heir's share as individual property from that point forward, and later-born descendants of that heir cannot unwind it by claiming the pre-partition character survived. Anyone advising on succession in a multi-generation Hindu family, whether the family is based entirely in India or spread across several countries, should treat the date and legal effect of any earlier partition as the first fact to establish in a dispute, not an afterthought raised once litigation is already underway.

Why It Matters

This ruling matters for any Hindu family, including those with members living abroad, where more than one generation has an interest in ancestral property. It confirms that once a grandfather's estate has devolved under Section 8 of the Hindu Succession Act and been partitioned, a grandson acquires no independent birthright in it; his only claim runs through his own father. NRI and OCI family members who assume a share in grandparental property is automatic because of lineage need to first establish whether an earlier partition already converted that property into their parent's individual, freely disposable property.
