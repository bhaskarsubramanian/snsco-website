NOIDA Cannot Add a New Document Requirement After the Game Has Started: Allahabad HC

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Law Trend and supremetoday.ai. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A residential plot application NOIDA received in 2006 has finally produced a ruling, two decades and several rounds of litigation later, with the Allahabad High Court's Lucknow Bench ordering the authority to allot the plot at the rates that applied when the application was filed. The case is a long-running dispute over whether NOIDA could reject an old application for want of a document the scheme's own brochure never asked for.

Nalini Khandelwal, a shareholder and whole-time director of M/s Charu Apparels Manufacturing Company Pvt. Ltd., applied on 16 June 2006 for a residential plot in NOIDA's Sector-100, under Plot Category-3, covering plots between 250 and 300 square metres, as part of the Residential Plots Scheme, 2006. NOIDA later rejected her application on the ground that she had not filed Form-32, the document that evidences a person's appointment as a company director. Khandelwal's position was that the scheme brochure never made Form-32 a condition of eligibility at the time she applied, and that she had, in any event, already furnished proof of her directorship.

The litigation has a long back-story. An interim stay granted on 22 November 2006 had restrained NOIDA from making allotments in the reserved category while the dispute over eligibility played out. A later order, passed on 26 November 2011, had already quashed one of NOIDA's earlier rejections, finding that the brochure contained no cut-off date of 31 March 2006 of the kind NOIDA was relying on to deny her claim. Despite that 2011 finding, NOIDA continued to resist the allotment, eventually falling back on the missing Form-32 as a fresh ground for refusal.

Justice Irshad Ali rejected that fallback. The Court held that a statutory authority running a public allotment scheme cannot reject an application for failing to produce a document that the scheme's own terms never made mandatory at the time of filing. An authority may certainly scrutinise the documents an applicant has filed and ask for clarification, but that scrutiny power cannot be stretched into an authority to invent a new mandatory document once applications are already in and the scheme has moved past its opening stage. The Court summed up the principle in language borrowed from recruitment law but applied here to a public allotment scheme: the rules of the game cannot be changed once the game has already started.

On that basis, the Court directed NOIDA to allot Khandelwal the residential plot at the premium rates applicable in 2006, the rates in force when she first applied, adjusting for whatever registration amount she had already deposited. If the original plot is no longer available, the order contemplates an alternative plot, preferably within Sector-100 or at a comparable location, allotted strictly at the same 2006 rate. NOIDA's filing of an application alone does not create a vested right to a plot, the Court noted, but the delay in this case traced back to NOIDA's own successive and shifting grounds for rejection, not to any fault of the applicant.

The ruling gives homebuyers and applicants dealing with NOIDA, Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority and comparable bodies a concrete precedent for a familiar grievance: an authority sitting on an old application for years and then inventing a fresh documentary requirement when the original grounds for rejection no longer hold up. Applicants facing a similar late-stage demand for paperwork the original scheme terms never mentioned now have High Court authority for the proposition that the brochure in force at the time of application, not whatever requirement the authority prefers years later, governs the outcome.

What stands out about the twenty-year timeline is how many separate occasions NOIDA had to get the decision right and did not. The 2011 order had already told NOIDA, in terms, that the brochure carried no cut-off date of the kind it was relying on. Rather than allotting the plot at that point, the authority appears to have found a new ground, the missing Form-32, to keep the file open. Each fresh ground of rejection cost Khandelwal more years of litigation over a plot that, on the facts the Court eventually accepted, she was entitled to from the outset. The order directing an allotment at 2006 rates, rather than current rates, is in part the Court's way of ensuring that NOIDA's own delay does not end up working in its favour by making the applicant pay today's substantially higher premium for a benefit she was owed two decades ago.

The ruling also has a bearing on how development authorities handle the scrutiny stage of any public scheme more generally. Brochures for plot and flat allotment schemes typically list the documents an applicant must submit, and applicants rely on that list when deciding whether, and how, to apply. If an authority could add new mandatory documents at any later point simply by framing them as part of its scrutiny power, the brochure would stop functioning as a reliable statement of the rules and would become, in effect, whatever the authority decided it wanted after the fact. The Court's insistence that scrutiny cannot be used this way protects the basic reliability of these published schemes for every other applicant, beyond Khandelwal alone.

Why It Matters

Development authorities across the NCR region routinely sit on old plot and flat allotment applications for years, and applicants often face late-stage objections based on requirements that were never part of the original scheme. This ruling gives such applicants a clear basis to insist that eligibility be judged by the brochure terms in force when they applied, and that the rate payable be fixed accordingly rather than at whatever rate prevails when the dispute is finally resolved.
