Delhi High Court Restrains Srinagar Trader From Selling Electrical Goods Under 'GREAT MEDAL' Mark

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Goldmedal Electricals Pvt. Ltd., which has built its brand around the 'Goldmedal' name in the electrical goods trade since 1979, went to the Delhi High Court after it traced a Srinagar-based trader selling lamps, luminaires and wiring under a mark it considered dangerously close to its own. The suit, CS(COMM) 1199/2026, named Bashir Ahmed Bhat, the sole proprietor of M/s R & B Electricals in Khonmoh, Srinagar, as defendant, and sought an injunction against his use of the mark 'GREAT MEDAL' on LED lamps, LED luminaires, heating apparatus, ceiling fans and wires.

According to the plaint, Bhat was not running a small neighbourhood shop invisible to the rest of the country. He was advertising and selling the goods through IndiaMART, Facebook and YouTube, and the company's case was that he had procured orders from dealers as far away as Delhi. On 5 November, Justice Tejas Karia granted an ad-interim injunction restraining Bhat from dealing in electrical goods under the GREAT MEDAL mark, or any mark deceptively similar to Goldmedal's registered marks, until the next hearing.

The reach of online marketplaces did more than establish the scale of the alleged infringement; it also answered the jurisdictional question of why a Srinagar trader could be sued in Delhi at all. Indian courts have increasingly held that where a seller's goods are accessible and orders can be placed by buyers in a particular city through e-commerce platforms and social media, that is enough to found territorial jurisdiction there, regardless of where the seller is physically based. Goldmedal's claim that Bhat procured orders from Delhi dealers through these platforms fit squarely within that line of reasoning.

On the merits, the order records the court's view that Goldmedal had demonstrated long and continuous use of its marks since 1979, along with the goodwill and reputation that such use builds over four and a half decades. Against that backdrop, a newer trader adopting a mark built around the same idea, a 'medal' name on electrical goods, carried an obvious risk of consumers assuming some connection between the two brands, whether as licensee, authorised dealer or sister concern. The court's claims recorded in the suit include passing off and copyright infringement, along with the usual reliefs of delivery up of infringing stock and unfair competition, though the November order itself was confined to the ad-interim injunction rather than a final adjudication of the suit.

The suit also carries a cancellation angle: Goldmedal has filed a separate petition, C.O.(COMM.IPD-TM) 163/2026, before the same court seeking cancellation of a trademark registration connected to this dispute, which suggests the defendant's side may hold some registered protection for a similar mark that Goldmedal wants removed from the register entirely. Running the infringement suit and the cancellation petition together is a common strategy where a defendant has managed to register a mark that the original owner considers too close for comfort; a bare injunction stops the immediate selling, but it is the cancellation proceeding that strips away any registered right the defendant might later rely on.

This is not Goldmedal's only trademark fight running through the Delhi High Court around the same period. The company has separately gone after plywood and board sellers using its name to ride on its reputation in an entirely different product category, underlining how a well-known mark attracts imitators across unrelated trades, not just from direct competitors in the same line of business.

For brand owners, the case is a reminder that an ad-interim injunction against a small, geographically distant trader is well within reach once online sales and marketplace listings put that trader's goods in front of buyers anywhere in the country. For sellers using marketplace platforms to sell goods under a name that echoes an established brand, the ruling is a caution that a modest online footprint does not provide shelter from a suit filed hundreds of kilometres away, and that reach which helps sell more goods is the same reach that can be used to establish jurisdiction against them.

Why It Matters

E-commerce and social-media selling have made it routine for brand owners to pursue small, geographically distant infringers in whichever city their reputation and customer base happen to sit, rather than having to chase a defendant to its home town. This order confirms that courts will treat marketplace accessibility and dealer orders from a city as enough to found jurisdiction there, which matters for any business building an online sales presence under a mark that echoes an established brand.
