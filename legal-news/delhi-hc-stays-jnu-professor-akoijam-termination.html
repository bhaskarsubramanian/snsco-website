Delhi HC Stays JNU's Termination of Professor Over Sexual Harassment Findings, Cites No Notice

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, theprint.in and republicworld.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Jawaharlal Nehru University dismissed one of its own professors on 9 October 2026 over findings arising from a sexual harassment complaint. Three weeks later, the Delhi High Court put that dismissal on hold, not because it had ruled on whether the complaint was made out, but because the professor says the university never gave him a hearing before signing off on his termination.

The professor is Angomcha Bimol Akoijam, a Congress Member of Parliament who also holds a teaching position at JNU. After the university's internal mechanism returned adverse findings against him, the administration passed an order terminating his service. Akoijam approached the High Court arguing that no notice was issued to him before the decision to dismiss was finalised, meaning he was denied an opportunity to respond at the one stage that mattered most, the point at which the university actually decided his fate.

Justice Anup Jairam Bhambhani heard the plea and stayed the termination order on 2 November 2026, directing JNU to file its response. The stay is interim. The Court has not examined whether the internal committee's findings were sound or whether the harassment allegations are made out; it has only paused the consequence of dismissal while it works out whether the university followed fair procedure before imposing that consequence. The matter is listed for further hearing in January 2027.

The ruling sits alongside, but separately from, a criminal investigation. Days after his termination, Delhi Police registered a first information report against Akoijam on charges connected to sexual harassment and outraging a woman's modesty, and he has approached the Rouse Avenue Court seeking anticipatory bail in that matter. The High Court's stay concerns only JNU's service action against him; it leaves the criminal proceeding, and any eventual finding on the underlying allegations, to run on its own track before a different forum.

Akoijam has denied the allegations and has called his dismissal politically motivated, a characterisation JNU disputes. None of that features in what the High Court actually decided. The order turns entirely on procedure, specifically whether an employee facing termination over disciplinary findings is entitled to notice and a chance to be heard before the employer signs the final order, not merely during the fact-finding stage that precedes it.

That distinction between the fact-finding stage and the decision stage runs through a line of Indian service law that predates this case by decades. Courts have long drawn a line between the inquiry that establishes the facts, where an employee's right to be heard is usually accepted without much argument, and the separate decision that follows, where an employer weighs the findings and settles on a punishment. Natural justice can require a hearing at both points, not just the first, particularly where the punishment itself, such as outright termination rather than a lesser penalty, was never put to the employee for a response before it was imposed. Akoijam's case rests on exactly that second, often overlooked, stage.

JNU, for its part, has not yet placed its version of events before the Court. Its written response, due under the Court's 2 November order, will presumably set out what notice, if any, was given and at what point in the process, and may well dispute Akoijam's account of how the final decision was reached. Until that reply is filed and considered, the stay simply preserves the status quo, leaving Akoijam in his teaching position rather than deciding the underlying question either way.

For institutions handling internal complaints under the Posh framework and allied service rules, the order is a reminder that the procedural safeguards owed to an employee do not end once an internal committee submits its findings. A university, or any employer, that moves straight from an adverse internal finding to a termination order without giving the employee notice of that specific, final step risks having the termination stayed on procedural grounds alone, regardless of how strong the underlying findings may be. Employers who want a termination to survive judicial scrutiny need to build in that last opportunity to respond, even where the facts already appear settled by an internal inquiry.

Delhi's higher educational institutions, several of them central universities answering to service rules with similar structures, will be watching how this plays out heading into the January 2027 hearing. A ruling that treats notice before the final termination order as a separate and independent safeguard, rather than something subsumed within the earlier inquiry, would affect how every such institution drafts and executes a dismissal once its internal committee has reported.

Why It Matters

The order is an interim stay, not a ruling on the harassment allegations, but it confirms that an employer cannot move from an internal committee's adverse findings straight to a final termination order without giving the employee a chance to respond to that specific decision. Institutions in Delhi NCR handling Posh complaints and disciplinary terminations should treat this procedural step as a distinct requirement, separate from the fairness of the inquiry itself.
