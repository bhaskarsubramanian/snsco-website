Supreme Court Upholds Allahabad HC Order Restoring UP Roadside Land to Original Tenure Holders

Source: Originally reported by RawLaw, Law Trend, supremecourtcases.com and intolegalworld.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

The Supreme Court has refused to disturb an Allahabad High Court order that took a valuable roadside plot in a Uttar Pradesh village away from strangers who had been allotted it during land consolidation and gave it back to the families who held it originally. The appeal in Rama Pandey and Others v. Ram Lal (Dead), Through LRs and Others was dismissed on 5 November 2026, with no order as to costs, bringing to an end a dispute over consolidation allotment that had run for decades before reaching the apex court.

Consolidation proceedings under the Uttar Pradesh Consolidation of Holdings Act, 1953 are meant to rearrange scattered agricultural holdings into more workable, contiguous plots. In practice, that rearrangement sometimes swept up land that was never meant to be pooled in the ordinary way, including roadside plots whose value came less from their agricultural yield than from their frontage and location. During consolidation of the village in question, such a roadside plot was allotted away from its original tenure holders to other villagers who had no prior right in it.

The original holders challenged that allotment, relying on departmental circulars issued in 1981 and 1988 which directed that roadside land of commercial value should be kept out of the general consolidation exercise altogether, and that if such land had already been brought into consolidation, it should be allotted back to its original tenure holders rather than to third parties. The Allahabad High Court accepted that argument and restored the plot to the original holders, setting aside the allotment made in favour of the other villagers.

On appeal, the Supreme Court examined whether that allotment, and the valuation and displacement of the original right holders that came with it, had a rational and lawful basis. It found none sufficient to overturn the High Court's view. The Bench did not go so far as to hold that roadside land can never be brought into consolidation; the ruling is narrower than that, confined to whether this particular allotment, which displaced tenure holders who held prior rights in favour of villagers who did not, could stand.

The decision leans on the two circulars as policy guidance that consolidation authorities are expected to follow, rather than treating consolidation officers as having unfettered discretion to redistribute roadside plots as they see fit once such land enters the consolidation process. Where a departmental circular points one way and a consolidation authority allots the other way, without any rational reason shown for departing from the circular, the allotment is vulnerable to challenge on exactly the ground raised here.

For families whose agricultural or roadside land was absorbed into consolidation decades ago, the ruling is a reminder that an original holder's claim to that land does not automatically disappear once consolidation proceedings have concluded and land has been allotted elsewhere. Where the allotment departs from the kind of policy safeguard the 1981 and 1988 circulars represent, and where no rational basis for that departure is shown, courts have been willing to unwind even long-settled allotments and restore the land to the family that originally held it.

The dismissal of the appeal, with no order as to costs, closes this particular dispute without adding new law beyond affirming the High Court's reasoning. Its practical value for other litigants lies less in any fresh legal principle than in the Supreme Court's willingness to let a High Court ruling favouring original tenure holders stand against a renewed challenge from the parties who had benefited from the consolidation allotment, even after the matter had already been contested for a long period.

Consolidation disputes of this kind rarely move quickly through the system. A village-level allotment made decades ago can sit unchallenged for years before an affected family finds the resources or the occasion to contest it, and by the time the matter reaches the Supreme Court, the land itself may have changed hands more than once or acquired a commercial value its original agricultural classification never anticipated. That lag is part of why the circulars on roadside land matter so much in practice: they give consolidation authorities, and later the courts reviewing their decisions, a fixed policy anchor to apply even when decades have passed since the original allotment and the surrounding facts have otherwise grown complicated.

Why It Matters

Consolidation of agricultural holdings under state tenancy laws can sometimes allot away roadside or commercially valuable land to parties with no prior claim to it, even decades after the original family lost effective control. This ruling confirms that where departmental policy required such land to be kept out of consolidation or returned to its original holders, courts will set aside an allotment that departed from that policy without rational justification, which matters to any family whose land was absorbed into a consolidation scheme on terms it never agreed to.
