Bombay High Court: Framing Issues in a Section 44A Foreign Decree Execution Is the Exception, Not the Default

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Verdictum, law.asia and drishtijudiciary.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Elis Jane Quinlan and her co-decree-holders held a money decree from the Fujairah Civil Court in the United Arab Emirates against Naveen Kumar Seth, a director of Candica Industries. The UAE has been notified as a reciprocating territory under Section 44A of the Code of Civil Procedure, which lets a decree from such a territory be executed in India as if an Indian court had passed it, without a fresh suit. The decree-holders accordingly sought execution before the District Judge, Pune. Seth resisted, raising doubts about natural justice and alleging fraud in how the foreign proceedings had been conducted. On 3 November 2022, the District Judge allowed his application and framed issues for trial, directing that evidence be led on them before the decree could be executed.

Quinlan and her co-petitioners went to the Bombay High Court by way of writ petition, arguing that this turned a summary execution proceeding into a full civil trial and defeated the point of Section 44A, which exists precisely so that a foreign money decree does not have to be re-litigated from scratch in India. Their position was that an executing court's inquiry under Section 13 of the Code, into whether the foreign decree falls within one of the narrow exceptions to its conclusiveness, is meant to be conducted on affidavits and the record, not through a full evidentiary trial with issues framed as in an ordinary suit.

Justice Sandeep V. Marne agreed with the general principle but not with the outcome the decree-holders wanted. The judgment holds that framing issues and directing a full evidentiary exercise is not the ordinary course in Section 44A execution, and that an executing court should resolve objections to a foreign decree through a summary inquiry in the normal run of cases. Treating every execution petition as an occasion for a mini-trial would defeat the reciprocal arrangement that Section 44A is built on, since a judgment-debtor could then force the decree-holder to prove the underlying case all over again by making bare allegations.

This ordinary rule, the Court held, gives way where exceptional circumstances are shown, and it found that Seth's objections, going to natural justice and fraud in the Fujairah proceedings, were capable of falling within the Section 13 exceptions and could not fairly be decided on affidavits alone. On that narrower finding, the writ petition was dismissed and the District Judge's order framing issues was left standing, so Seth gets to lead evidence on the specific issues already framed rather than have them struck off. Justice Marne did ask the District Judge to decide those framed issues without delay, directing a decision preferably within three months, so that the case does not simply drift into another prolonged trial under a different name.

The decree-holders did not get the summary disposal they were asking for, but the judgment is still useful to parties seeking to execute a foreign decree in India, because it fixes the default position they can invoke in the next case. An executing court is not meant to frame issues and take evidence as a matter of course whenever a judgment-debtor raises an objection. It may do so only where the objection genuinely falls within the Section 13 exceptions, such as fraud or a denial of natural justice at the foreign court, and even then the inquiry is expected to move quickly rather than mimic a fresh trial on the merits of the original dispute.

This ruling also confirms that Indian courts continue to treat Section 44A as a mechanism for recognising a foreign decree's conclusiveness rather than reopening it, consistent with how the provision has been read in other reciprocating-territory cases this year. A judgment-debtor who wants to slow down execution will need more than a general complaint about the foreign proceedings; the objection has to be specific enough to fit one of the recognised exceptions before a court will let it convert a summary execution into anything resembling a trial.

Why It Matters

Decree-holders from the Gulf and other reciprocating territories often find execution in India held up by objections that effectively ask the Indian court to retry the foreign case. This ruling confirms that such objections can be entertained only within the narrow Section 13 exceptions, and that framing issues and taking evidence remains an exceptional step rather than the default, while also showing that a specific fraud or natural-justice objection can still earn a judgment-debtor that exceptional treatment.
