Karnataka HC: A Fertiliser Dealer Selling Sealed Stock Cannot Be Prosecuted for Substandard Quality

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, siasat.com and newskarnataka.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A fertiliser shop owner in Bagalkot has got a criminal case against him thrown out at the threshold, with the Dharwad Bench of the Karnataka High Court holding that a dealer who sells fertiliser exactly as it arrives in sealed bags cannot be made to answer for defects a laboratory finds in the contents later. Justice T. Venkatesh Naik quashed proceedings pending before the Principal Civil Judge and JMFC Court at Bagalkot against Allahbaksh M. Makandar, who ran the shop out of which the samples were lifted.

The case began the way most fertiliser prosecutions in India do. An inspecting officer drew samples from stock lying at Makandar's shop and sent them for testing. The lab report came back finding the samples substandard and not conforming to the norms prescribed for that grade of fertiliser. On the strength of that report, the authorities registered a case against two companies said to be connected with the product and against Makandar himself, invoking Sections 3 and 7 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, along with Rules 19 and 21 of the Fertiliser (Control) Order, 1985.

Makandar's counsel argued that his client had done nothing more than stock and sell bags that came to him sealed from the manufacturer. There was no allegation anywhere in the complaint that he had opened those bags, altered the contents, or had any means of verifying what was inside before the lab report arrived. If the fertiliser fell short of the prescribed standard, counsel submitted, that failure belonged to whoever manufactured it and controlled its composition, not to a retailer who merely passed on a sealed product.

The High Court agreed with that reading of the complaint. It noted that nowhere did the prosecution allege that Makandar manufactured the fertiliser or exercised any control over its quality. A dealer selling a sealed, pre-packed product cannot be fastened with criminal liability for a quality defect simply because a later test throws one up, the court held, unless the complaint itself ties him to the act of manufacture or to some specific lapse within his control. Continuing a prosecution against a dealer on that footing, without any such allegation, amounted to an abuse of the process of the court.

Justice Naik anchored this conclusion in a line of authority the Karnataka High Court has followed before, in particular the 2016 ruling in Sanjay Gowda v. State of Karnataka, which read the Fertiliser (Control) Order as fixing responsibility for compliance on the specific officer a company appoints for that purpose, not on every person in the chain of sale. The logic extends naturally to an unconnected retailer: the Control Order's enforcement machinery is built to catch the person who actually answers for quality, and stretching it to catch a dealer with no hand in that process serves no purpose the order was designed to serve.

For dealers who stock agricultural inputs on a retail basis, this is a welcome marker of where the line is drawn under a regulatory regime that otherwise moves quickly from a lab report to a criminal complaint. The ruling does not immunise every retailer from every fertiliser prosecution; a dealer who repacks, dilutes or otherwise handles the product before sale, or whom the complaint specifically implicates in a quality lapse, remains exposed. What the order protects is the dealer who does nothing but sell what reaches him sealed and unopened, and against whom the complaint says no more than that a later test found a problem.

The ruling also carries a lesson for enforcement agencies drafting these complaints. A bare lab report showing a sample failed the prescribed standard is not, by itself, enough to drag every name in the retail chain into a criminal case. Investigators and the prosecution need to show, even at the stage of filing the complaint, some specific act or omission on the part of the person they are proceeding against, whether that is manufacture, blending, storage in conditions that could affect quality, or a role in quality control that the Fertiliser (Control) Order actually assigns to that person. Absent that link, the case risks being quashed exactly as this one was, at the cost of years of pointless litigation for a shopkeeper who had no part in creating the defect.

Why It Matters

Fertiliser and agri-input dealers are routinely named in Essential Commodities Act prosecutions whenever a lab report flags a sample as substandard, regardless of whether they had any role in manufacturing or quality control. This ruling confirms that liability under the Fertiliser (Control) Order tracks the person actually responsible for quality, not every retailer who happens to stock the product, giving dealers a concrete basis to seek quashing where the complaint does not allege manufacture or a specific quality lapse on their part.
