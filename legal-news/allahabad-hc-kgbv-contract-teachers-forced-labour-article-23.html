Keeping KGBV Teachers on Contract Pay for Years Is Forced Labour: Allahabad High Court

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Law Trend, Law Chakra and drishtijudiciary.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Archana Boodh has been teaching at a Kasturba Gandhi Balika Vidyalaya in Amethi since 16 January 2010. For all those years she has remained a contractual appointee, renewed year to year, paid not a salary but what the state's own paperwork calls an honorarium, a figure that stayed far below what a regularly appointed teacher doing the same job would draw. She went to the Allahabad High Court's Lucknow Bench asking the state to finally create proper teaching posts at these residential schools for girls and to regularise teachers like her who had spent a decade and more holding the institution together on temporary terms.

Kasturba Gandhi Balika Vidyalayas were set up as residential schools for girls from disadvantaged backgrounds, and the state has staffed many of them for years through contractual hiring rather than through the regular teacher recruitment process used elsewhere in the school system. The practical effect, as the petition described it, was a workforce doing the full range of a teacher's duties, classroom teaching, hostel supervision, examinations, on pay pitched as a token honorarium rather than wages tied to the work performed or reviewed against inflation and the cost of living over the years the arrangement continued.

Justice Pankaj Bhatia did not treat this as a routine service-law dispute about pay parity. He held that keeping teachers engaged year after year on renewable contracts, with the state taking no real step either to convert the posts into regular ones or to pay the incumbents anything close to a fair wage for the work, amounted to a form of forced labour of the kind Article 23 of the Constitution prohibits. The reasoning tied the pay structure to the compulsion built into it: a teacher with a decade of service and no realistic alternative employment has little choice but to keep accepting a contract renewal on the state's terms, year after year, because the alternative is no income at all. That dependence, the court reasoned, is what turns continued low-paid engagement into something closer to compelled service than to a freely negotiated contract of employment. The judgment also drew in Article 14, linking the fairness of how these teachers are treated to the quality of education the children in their care actually receive.

The directions that followed were specific rather than aspirational. The state was given four months to undertake an exercise to create regular teaching posts at KGBVs and to consider the regularisation claims of teachers who qualify for it. Until that exercise is completed, every KGBV teacher is to be paid at a minimum of Rs 17,000 a month, a figure the court set as an interim floor rather than a final settlement of what these teachers are owed. The state was also directed to frame proper service conditions for KGBV teachers, bringing them in line with regularly appointed teachers up to the intermediate level, and the Central government, which funds part of the KGBV scheme, was directed to release its agreed share of the expenditure so the state is not left arguing it cannot afford to comply.

None of this converts every contractual teacher into a permanent employee overnight. The court left the actual regularisation exercise, and the question of who qualifies for it, to the state to carry out within the four-month window it set, rather than ordering regularisation outright for every KGBV teacher across Uttar Pradesh. What it decided was narrower and, for that reason, harder for the state to simply absorb and move past: that the honorarium model itself, sustained over years without any plan to end it, cannot continue as it has been run, and that calling the payment an honorarium does not change what it functions as in practice.

The ruling sits inside a wider pattern of litigation in Uttar Pradesh over long-serving contractual staff in government-run welfare and education schemes, where the state has repeatedly relied on temporary engagement to staff posts that, on the ground, operate as permanent ones. Framing that practice as a constitutional question under Article 23, rather than purely as a service-law grievance about equal pay for equal work, raises the stakes considerably for departments that have built entire cadres on contractual or honorarium terms with no fixed end date. A finding that such an arrangement amounts to forced labour gives contractual staff in comparable schemes, beyond just KGBV teachers, a constitutional argument to point to the next time a department tries to treat a decade of renewed contracts as something other than permanent employment in substance.

Why It Matters

The ruling treats chronic reliance on renewable contracts and token honoraria, rather than regular pay and posts, as a constitutional problem under Article 23, not merely a service-law grievance. State departments and agencies that staff long-term functions through contractual or honorarium arrangements, in education or elsewhere, now face a real risk that courts will read sustained low-paid renewal as compelled labour rather than a valid employment choice, especially where the worker has no realistic alternative but to keep renewing.
