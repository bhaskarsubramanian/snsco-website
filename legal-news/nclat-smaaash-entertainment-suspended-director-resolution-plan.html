NCLAT: Once a Resolution Plan Is Implemented, a Suspended Director Cannot Reopen It

Source: Originally reported by Taxscan, TaxGuru and nclat.nic.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A suspended director who waited until two days after a resolution plan was approved to ask for it to be thrown out has lost his appeal before the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal. The Tribunal's Principal Bench in New Delhi dismissed Shripal Sevantilal Morakhia's challenge to the rejection of his objections to the resolution plan for Smaaash Entertainment Pvt. Ltd., holding that a plan already carried into effect leaves very little room for a suspended director to come back and reopen it.

The timeline mattered more than the grounds of objection. Smaaash Entertainment's Committee of Creditors had approved the resolution plan on 7 May 2025. Morakhia withdrew one objection and then, within two days of the approval, filed an application asking the adjudicating authority to reject the very plan that had just been cleared. By the time his challenge reached the Tribunal, the plan had moved well past the approval stage and into actual implementation, reportedly completed by 6 June 2025. That gap between objection and implementation became the crux of the Tribunal's reasoning.

Taxscan's report of the ruling records that the NCLAT leaned on the Supreme Court's decision in Torrent Power Ltd. v. Ashish Arjunkumar Rathi to frame how limited judicial intervention becomes once a resolution plan has actually been carried out. The logic runs through the structure of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code itself: the Code is built to give a resolution plan finality once creditors have approved it and the successful applicant has acted on that approval, because reopening an implemented plan unsettles every step the resolution applicant has already taken in reliance on it, from fresh investment to altered management and restructured operations.

Morakhia had also tried to rely on an earlier NCLAT order, dated 23 March 2026 in a separate appeal, arguing that it had kept his right to challenge the plan's approval alive. The Tribunal rejected that reading, finding nothing in the earlier order that preserved a fresh route to contest an approval that had, by the time of the present appeal, already been implemented. A suspended director does not get a second opportunity to attack a resolution plan simply by pointing to an unrelated procedural order from a different proceeding and arguing it left the door open.

This is not the first time Smaaash Entertainment's insolvency has produced a reported ruling involving the company's founder. The brand itself was the subject of an earlier, separate dispute in which the NCLAT had earlier upheld an NCLT order setting aside what it called a fraudulent transfer of the Smaaash brand away from the corporate debtor before the insolvency process began. The latest ruling, by contrast, concerns the resolution plan that eventually took the company out of insolvency, and confirms that once such a plan is implemented, a suspended director's scope to contest it narrows sharply, however the objection is framed.

The decision fits a broader pattern the NCLAT and the Supreme Court have both reinforced through 2026: a resolution plan's approval by the Committee of Creditors, once acted upon, is treated as commercial wisdom that courts and tribunals will not reopen except on the narrowest grounds, and implementation of the plan closes that window further still. For promoters and suspended directors weighing whether to contest an approved plan, the practical message is that timing and follow-through matter as much as the substance of the objection. An application filed within days of approval, without securing a stay before the resolution applicant acts on the plan, risks being overtaken by events long before the Tribunal gets to decide its merits.

For resolution professionals and successful resolution applicants, the ruling offers reassurance that completing implementation promptly, once a plan clears the Committee of Creditors, builds a real legal shield against later challenges from suspended directors or promoters who did not secure interim protection in time. It also signals to appellate counsel that recycling arguments from an earlier, unrelated order will not revive a challenge that the underlying facts, principally full implementation, have already overtaken.

Why It Matters

Suspended directors and promoters frequently use objections and appeals to slow down or reopen resolution plans after a Committee of Creditors has approved them. This ruling confirms that full implementation of an approved plan sharply narrows the scope for later challenge, giving resolution professionals and successful resolution applicants a clearer basis to proceed with implementation once approval has been secured, rather than waiting out further litigation.
