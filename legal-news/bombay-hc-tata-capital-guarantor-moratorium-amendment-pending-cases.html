Bombay HC Holds New IBC Rule Stripping Guarantors of Interim Moratorium Applies to Pending Cases Too

Source: Originally reported by Taxscan, IBC Laws, RawLaw and LiveLaw Biz. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Tata Capital Financial Services, now merged into Tata Capital Limited, had extended credit to Neel Motors LLP under a Channel Finance Agreement, with four individuals standing as personal guarantors. When the loan soured, Tata Capital moved a Commercial Arbitration Petition, numbered 620 of 2021, seeking to proceed against the guarantors. The guarantors resisted on a familiar ground in recent personal insolvency litigation, pointing to Section 95 applications they had filed before the NCLT, which under the unamended Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code triggered an interim moratorium shielding a personal guarantor from exactly this kind of proceeding the moment such an application is filed.

That shield changed shape in 2026. Parliament amended Sections 96 and 124 of the Code to insert a new sub-section excluding personal guarantors of corporate debtors from the interim moratorium altogether, with the change taking effect from 26 May 2026. The practical question before Justice Somasekhar Sundaresan was not whether the amendment existed, both sides agreed it did, but whether it reached insolvency applications the guarantors had filed before that date, applications that were already pending when the new sub-section came into force.

The guarantors argued for a narrow reading confined to future filings. Their position was that the amendment should only apply to Section 95 applications filed on or after 26 May 2026, leaving their own pending applications, filed earlier, governed by the old regime and its interim moratorium. On that reading, Tata Capital's arbitration against them would remain stayed notwithstanding the amendment, simply because the guarantors had gotten their applications in before the cut-off date.

That reading did not survive scrutiny. The Court held that the amendment operates on an ongoing legal relationship and status, the moratorium's continued existence, rather than on a closed transaction frozen at the date of filing, and described this as the amendment applying retroactively rather than retrospectively, meaning it reaches forward to alter the legal consequence of pending applications from the date it came into force without reopening anything that had already concluded. On that footing, the interim moratorium these particular guarantors had been relying on ceased to operate from 26 May 2026 regardless of when their Section 95 applications happened to have been filed.

With the moratorium out of the way, the Court allowed Tata Capital's arbitration petition to proceed against the guarantors, rejecting the argument that a pending personal insolvency application could continue to function as a shield against the bank's enforcement action. The ruling sits alongside a near-identical position taken by the Delhi High Court around the same period, and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India subsequently issued a circular in September confirming, for the benefit of adjudicating authorities and practitioners generally, that the interim moratorium for personal guarantors ceased from 26 May 2026 even in applications filed earlier and still pending.

For lenders chasing personal guarantors who have filed, or who file, a Section 95 insolvency application purely to freeze recovery action, this ruling removes a tactic that depended on reading the 2026 amendment as forward-looking only in the narrowest sense. A pending application no longer buys a guarantor protection from arbitration, suit or other recovery proceedings once the amendment's effective date has passed, whatever the filing date of that application happened to be.

Guarantors and their counsel face the opposite lesson. Filing a Section 95 application is no longer a reliable way to pause a lender's enforcement action, and any defence built on the interim moratorium needs to be tested against this reading of the amendment's temporal scope before it is relied upon in ongoing proceedings elsewhere. The Bombay High Court's reasoning, now echoed by the IBBI's own circular, gives lenders a strong basis to resist any renewed attempt to invoke the pre-amendment moratorium in a pending matter.

The ruling also matters beyond the specific facility in dispute because channel finance and dealer funding arrangements of the kind Tata Capital extended to Neel Motors typically carry several personal guarantors standing behind a single corporate borrower, and lenders in that space have faced a wave of Section 95 filings timed to coincide with recovery action once a facility turns bad. A court-tested answer on whether the 2026 amendment reaches those already-pending filings removes a major source of delay that lenders across the NBFC and bank sectors had been contending with in parallel matters.

Why It Matters

The ruling closes off a tactic some personal guarantors used, filing a Section 95 insolvency application to trigger an interim moratorium and stall a lender's arbitration or recovery proceedings, by confirming that the 2026 amendment removing that moratorium for guarantors of corporate debtors applies equally to applications already pending on its effective date, besides fresh filings made afterward, a reading the IBBI has since echoed in its own circular.
