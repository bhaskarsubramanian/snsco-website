SEBI Scraps Agri Versus Non-Agri Margin Gap For Commodity Derivatives From December 2026

Source: Originally reported by TaxGuru, jmfinancialservices.in, newkerala.com, outlookmoney.com and corpseed.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

For years, a trader holding a position in an agricultural commodity future, say castor seed or chana, had to put up a higher minimum margin than a trader holding an equivalent position in a non-agricultural commodity such as crude oil or copper, even within the same volatility bracket. SEBI has now closed that gap. A circular dated 17 November 2026 revises the minimum initial margin and the minimum margin period of risk that clearing corporations must apply across the commodity derivatives segment, and does away with the separate, higher thresholds that had applied to agricultural commodities.

The circular amends paragraph 9.1.3.I.a(iv) of Chapter 9 of SEBI's Master Circular for Stock Exchanges and Clearing Corporations, originally issued on 4 August 2023, which lays down the margin framework recognised clearing corporations must follow for commodity derivatives. Under the framework being replaced, commodities were grouped by volatility category, and within each category agricultural commodities carried a higher minimum initial margin and a longer minimum margin period of risk than non-agricultural commodities. The new circular equalises the two columns, so a commodity's volatility category now determines its minimum margin and risk period regardless of whether it is an agricultural or a non-agricultural product.

Margin period of risk is the regulatory estimate of how long it could take a clearing corporation to close out a defaulting member's position in stressed market conditions, and it feeds directly into how much margin a broker must collect from a client holding a given position. A longer minimum period, as agricultural commodities previously carried, generally means a higher margin requirement for the same position, since the exchange has to provision for a longer window of adverse price movement before it can liquidate the position. Removing that distinction means agricultural commodity traders should see their minimum margin obligations converge with those for comparable non-agricultural commodities in the same volatility bracket.

SEBI has built in both an effective date and a sunset clause. The revised norms take effect from 1 December 2026, giving clearing corporations and exchanges a short runway to update their risk systems, and the circular says the changes will apply for one year from the date of issuance, with SEBI reserving the right to extend that period through a separate circular if it decides the new framework should continue. That structure suggests the regulator is treating the parity between agricultural and non-agricultural margins as something to be reviewed against market experience, rather than a permanent settlement.

The change sits within a broader set of commodity derivatives reforms SEBI has rolled out through the year, including an earlier September 2026 circular on client-level position limits and penalty caps for breaches. Read together, the direction is toward a framework that treats commodity derivatives risk by volatility and market behaviour rather than by whether the underlying commodity happens to be grown or mined, a distinction that market participants had long argued added cost to agricultural hedging without a corresponding risk justification.

Parity also matters to institutional and foreign participants who trade Indian commodity derivatives through registered intermediaries rather than taking physical delivery. A foreign portfolio investor or a trading desk running a diversified commodity book across agricultural and non-agricultural contracts has, until now, had to model two different minimum margin tables for positions that could sit in the same risk bucket on every other measure. A single table by volatility category simplifies that modelling and removes one source of friction for anyone comparing the cost of holding an agricultural position in India against a comparable position in an overseas market.

For commodity brokers, clearing members and the agricultural trade bodies that use futures and options to hedge price risk, the practical effect from 1 December is a recalculated, generally lower, margin requirement on agricultural commodity positions that had been carrying the older, higher thresholds. Clearing corporations will need to republish their margin tables ahead of the effective date, and brokers advising clients on hedging strategies in commodities such as grains, oilseeds, spices or cotton should expect their margin calls on those positions to move closer to what a trader in a metal or energy contract of similar volatility already pays.

Why It Matters

Margin requirements directly determine how much capital a trader or hedger must park with a broker to hold a commodity derivatives position, and agricultural commodities had long carried a higher minimum margin than non-agricultural commodities in the same volatility band. Removing that gap from 1 December 2026 should lower the cost of hedging for agricultural traders, processors and exporters who use futures and options to manage price risk, while giving clearing corporations a one-year window before SEBI decides whether to make the change permanent.
