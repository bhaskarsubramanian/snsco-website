NCLT Mumbai Sanctions Tata Steel's Merger With Wholly Owned Subsidiary Rujuvalika Investments

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, psuconnect.in and indiaipo.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Rujuvalika Investments Limited was, for Tata Steel's purposes, a wholly owned subsidiary holding shares of the parent company, a fairly common structural arrangement inside large corporate groups where an investment vehicle sits between the listed parent and some part of its own shareholding. The scheme sanctioned by the NCLT's Mumbai Bench on 1 October 2026 folds Rujuvalika into Tata Steel, dissolving the subsidiary without a formal winding up and vesting its liabilities directly in the parent.

The mechanics of the amalgamation reflect its intra-group, wholly owned character. Tata Steel's own shares that were held by Rujuvalika stand cancelled on the scheme taking effect, and no new shares or cash consideration are issued by Tata Steel in exchange, since there is no outside shareholder in Rujuvalika whose interest needs to be bought out or converted. The appointed date for the scheme, the date from which the merger is treated as effective for accounting purposes, was fixed at the opening of business on 1 April 2023, well over three years before the tribunal's order, underscoring how long these intra-group restructuring exercises can take to work through the statutory process even where there is no third-party opposition.

That process itself followed the standard two-motion route under Sections 230 to 232 of the Companies Act, 2013. The tribunal records that the first motion application was allowed on 25 March 2026, clearing the way for the companies to convene the necessary meetings or seek dispensation from them, followed by a second motion order on 5 June 2026 admitting the scheme for final hearing. The final sanction came roughly four months later.

Two statutory voices feature in the order, as they do in most Section 230-232 sanctions involving a listed company's subsidiary. The Regional Director for the Western Region raised observations touching on compliance matters, which the companies addressed through clarifications and undertakings recorded before the tribunal, a routine but necessary step that gives the Regional Director's office a documented basis for not objecting to the scheme going through. The Official Liquidator, separately tasked with reporting on whether a transferor company's affairs have been conducted in a manner prejudicial to its members or to the public interest, reported that Rujuvalika's affairs disclosed no such prejudice.

On that basis, the Mumbai Bench recorded its own satisfaction that the scheme was fair and reasonable and did not violate any provision of law or run contrary to public policy or public interest, the formulation tribunals typically use to sanction a scheme once the statutory gatekeepers, the Regional Director and the Official Liquidator, have cleared it. No objector appears to have come forward, consistent with the scheme's nature as a wholly owned subsidiary merger with no minority shareholders in Rujuvalika to protect.

For corporate groups running similar investment-holding subsidiaries, the order is a useful, recent template for how long and through which statutory checkpoints an uncontested intra-group merger travels even at the scale of a company as large as Tata Steel. The roughly seven months between the first motion order in March 2026 and final sanction in October 2026, itself coming more than three years after the chosen appointed date, is a realistic benchmark for groups planning to consolidate similar holding structures, and a reminder that fixing an appointed date well in the past does not shorten the tribunal timeline that follows.

The choice of an appointed date three and a half years before the sanction order is itself relevant for tax and accounting purposes. Groups structuring a subsidiary merger often prefer an appointed date that aligns with the start of a particular financial year, so that the amalgamation's accounting effect, the consolidation of the subsidiary's books into the parent, can be given retrospective effect cleanly from that year's opening balance sheet rather than from some date mid-year that complicates the accounting treatment. Tata Steel's choice of 1 April 2023 as the appointed date follows exactly that logic, even though the tribunal did not sanction the scheme until well over three years later.

The absence of any recorded objection from a third party is also consistent with how these schemes are typically structured to minimise contestability. Because Rujuvalika was wholly owned, there was no minority shareholder in the transferor company whose approval or consent needed to be separately secured, and because no cash or share consideration moved in either direction, there was no valuation dispute of the kind that often draws objections in mergers involving unrelated or only partly owned entities. The scheme's simplicity on this front likely explains why it proceeded from second motion to final sanction without any contested hearing surfacing in the available reports.

Why It Matters

Large corporate groups routinely use wholly owned subsidiaries to hold parent-company shares or manage specific asset pools, and periodically need to collapse those subsidiaries back into the parent for simplification or compliance reasons. This sanction confirms the standard sequence, timeline and statutory clearances, Regional Director observations and an Official Liquidator report, that such an uncontested intra-group merger must clear even when there are no minority shareholders or creditors likely to object, giving corporate counsel a current benchmark for planning similar restructurings.
