SEBI Cuts FPI Unhedged Currency Derivative Limit From $100 Million To $5 Million

Source: Originally reported by sebi.gov.in, TaxGuru, moneylife.in and corplawupdates.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A foreign portfolio investor that wants to take a position on the rupee's exchange rate without holding any actual Indian debt or equity to back it up now has far less room to do so. The Securities and Exchange Board of India issued a circular on 5 November 2026 cutting the ceiling on such uncovered, or unhedged, currency derivative positions from USD 100 million to USD 5 million of outstanding notional value at any point in time.

The change flows from the Reserve Bank of India's own A.P. (DIR Series) Circular No. 25, dated 10 October 2026, which revised the underlying-exposure threshold under its risk management and inter-bank dealings framework. SEBI's circular carries that change through to the securities market by amending Clause 5.3.3 of its Master Circular for Stock Exchanges and Clearing Corporations of 30 December 2024, and it directs exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories to put the new limit into effect for every client trading in exchange-traded currency derivatives.

For foreign portfolio investors, the practical effect is immediate. An FPI that wants to run a long or short position in a foreign currency-INR pair beyond USD 5 million, without actually holding the underlying Indian debt or equity that position is meant to hedge, can no longer do so under the exchange-traded framework. An FPI's aggregate short position across all exchanges in a given currency pair similarly cannot exceed the threshold unless it is backed by genuine underlying exposure. Positions taken before the new threshold took effect are not automatically grandfathered under the reporting seen in this run, so an FPI carrying an older, larger position needs to check with its custodian how the transition itself is being handled. Domestic clients are treated differently: they may still exceed the threshold, but only subject to conditions the Reserve Bank of India separately prescribes.

Regulators tightened this corner of the forex derivatives market through much of 2026. The RBI's October circular also introduced a parallel requirement for authorised dealers around a foreign exchange risk reserve on specified rupee-linked contracts, and separately revised rules on cancelling and rebooking FX derivative contracts and on documenting the exposures those contracts are meant to hedge. Taken together, the direction is toward requiring a demonstrable underlying transaction before a large currency bet is allowed, rather than letting market participants, foreign or domestic, run large speculative positions on the rupee through the derivatives route alone.

For an FPI or its Indian custodian, the numbers that mattered under the old ceiling will often no longer work. A fund that routinely carried unhedged exposure of around USD 40 million against an anticipated flow has gone from being comfortably inside the old USD 100 million limit to being eight times over the new one, and will have to either trim the position, document genuine underlying exposure in Indian securities, or route the balance through a different hedging structure that RBI and SEBI still permit.

Custodians and designated depository participants, who monitor FPI positions against regulatory limits, will need to recalibrate their systems and flag existing positions that now breach the new threshold well before exchange-level enforcement catches up with them. Funds that had structured strategies around the earlier USD 100 million headroom should review open positions against the new figure rather than wait for a margin call or a compliance notice from their custodian to force the point. Compliance teams at India-facing funds would also do well to update their internal position-limit dashboards and investor disclosures to reflect the USD 5 million figure, since continuing to quote the superseded USD 100 million ceiling in client-facing material could itself become a compliance gap.

SEBI's circular does not carry a separate effective-date clause of its own; it amends the Master Circular directly and directs market infrastructure institutions to implement the change, which secondary reporting has treated as taking hold from the date of issue. Funds and custodians uncertain about the exact operative date for their own exposures would do well to confirm the position with their exchange or clearing corporation rather than assume the older USD 100 million figure still applies to a trade placed after 5 November 2026.

Why It Matters

Foreign portfolio investors who ran unhedged currency derivative positions close to the old USD 100 million ceiling are now sharply restricted, since the new SEBI-enforced limit is USD 5 million unless the position is backed by real underlying exposure in Indian debt or equity. Funds, custodians and designated depository participants need to recheck open positions against the new threshold immediately, because continuing to carry a large unhedged bet on the rupee that was lawful before 5 November 2026 may no longer be. Domestic clients retain more flexibility, since they can exceed the threshold subject to separate Reserve Bank of India conditions.
