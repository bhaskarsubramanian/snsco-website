Supreme Court Limits Homebuyer's Refund From Lodha to His Own Payments, Not the Bank's Unpaid Loan

Source: Originally reported by Verdictum, Law Trend and advocatekhoj.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A homebuyer who finances part of his purchase through a bank loan, and then has to walk away from the project because the builder never delivers, usually assumes the builder owes him a full refund of whatever money changed hands in his name, bank portion included. The Supreme Court's judgment of 15 October in Madhu Kudapa's case says otherwise, and the reasoning is worth following closely because triangular arrangements of exactly this kind, buyer, builder and financing bank, are the norm rather than the exception in Indian real estate.

Kudapa, then known as K. Madhusudhan Rao and a non-resident Indian at the time, booked an apartment in the Lodha Bellezza project in Hyderabad in 2009 for roughly Rs 2.30 crore. He put in Rs 51.11 lakh of his own money. The balance was to come from a loan HDFC sanctioned for Rs 2 crore, under a tripartite arrangement by which the bank paid the builder directly in instalments rather than handing the money to Kudapa first. By July 2011, HDFC had released Rs 1.68 crore to the builder across thirteen tranches. Possession never followed, and the buyer eventually sought his money back.

The NCDRC, ruling in July 2022, ordered the builder to refund the entire amount paid towards the flat, bank disbursement included, with 9% interest. It also found HDFC deficient in service, on the footing that a bank releasing construction-linked tranches is expected to verify physical progress at each stage, and there was no evidence it had done so here; the Commission directed the bank to pay Rs 2 lakh on that count. Both the builder and the bank appealed.

On appeal, the Supreme Court agreed with the deficiency finding against HDFC and left the Rs 2 lakh compensation undisturbed, but it took a different view of what the builder owed the buyer directly. The Court's reasoning rests on a fairly simple proposition about whose money is whose. The Rs 1.68 crore released by HDFC was the bank's money, advanced to the builder on the strength of a loan contract between the bank and Kudapa; it was never Kudapa's own money merely because the loan was taken in his name, and the loan obligation to repay that amount to the bank was unaffected by the builder's default. Ordering the builder to pay that sum to Kudapa, on top of the bank's separate right to recover it as a loan, would hand the buyer money he never actually spent and expose the builder to a liability measured by someone else's advance rather than his own.

The Court accordingly restricted Kudapa's refund entitlement to Rs 1,27,34,726, representing what he had actually paid from his own funds across the transaction, with 12% simple interest running from the respective dates of each payment rather than the 9% the NCDRC had fixed. On the separate question of rent, the Court held that a buyer who already receives interest calculated to compensate for being kept out of the use of his money cannot also claim a separate rent award duplicating that same loss; interest at an appropriate rate already serves that compensatory purpose. In place of a separate rent claim, the Court awarded Kudapa a consolidated Rs 15 lakh covering rent, mental agony, harassment and litigation costs as a single, one-time figure, rather than letting those heads be claimed piecemeal on top of the interest-bearing refund.

Its reasoning draws on the Court's earlier ruling in DLF Homes Panchkula v. D.S. Dhanda, repeating that consumer fora cannot fix interest or compensation by a rule of thumb applied uniformly across cases, and must instead calibrate the figure to the actual loss shown on the facts before them. Here that meant separating the buyer's own money from the bank's, and separating interest meant to offset delay from a further head of compensation meant to address a different kind of loss.

For homebuyers who financed part of a purchase through a loan and are now looking at a builder default, the case narrows what can realistically be claimed from the builder to the amount actually paid out of pocket, leaving the bank-disbursed portion to be sorted out as a matter between the buyer and the lender under their own loan contract. For banks, the deficiency finding is the sharper warning: releasing construction-linked tranches without verifying physical progress at each stage remains a finding of deficient service against the lender, independent of whatever the builder is separately made to pay.

Why It Matters

Tripartite buyer-builder-bank financing is routine in Indian housing, and this ruling draws a clear line on what a defaulting builder actually owes when a deal collapses: only the buyer's own money, not the bank's disbursed loan, and interest that already compensates for delay without a separate award of rent on top. Banks releasing construction-linked tranches without verifying physical progress remain exposed to deficiency findings regardless of the builder's separate liability.
