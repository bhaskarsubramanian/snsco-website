State's Own Delay Means It Cannot Keep a Bidder's Deposit: Patna High Court in Mining Lease Dispute

Source: Originally reported by uniindia.com and Law Chakra. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A mining auction in Bihar that should have ended with a lease being signed instead ended with the state trying to keep the winning bidder's deposit after the lease fell through. M/s Balaji Enterprises had won the bid for Stone Block No. 27 at Mauza Hazratpur, Mandro Chandi, in Sheikhpura district, and was handed a letter of intent on 21 September 2017. What followed was years of delay, a lease that was never executed, and a government that chose to forfeit the firm's security deposit rather than own its part in the failure.

The lease did not go through because the District Survey Report, a document the government itself was supposed to prepare, did not carry the geo-coordinates the law requires. Without that information, the lease deed could not be finalised. Balaji Enterprises had done what an auction winner is expected to do: it paid its deposit and waited for the state to complete the paperwork on its end. When that paperwork never arrived in usable form, the department moved to forfeit the deposit instead of fixing its own records.

A single judge first intervened in the firm's favour and directed the authorities to complete execution of the lease within six weeks. The State of Bihar appealed that order through a Letters Patent Appeal, arguing that it should not be compelled to hand over a lease on a timeline the single judge had fixed. The Division Bench of Justice Bibek Chaudhuri and Justice Rana Vikram Singh allowed that appeal, so the state is not bound to execute the lease on the single judge's schedule. But the bench did not stop there, and the second half of its order is where the real damage to the state's position shows up.

On the deposit, the Division Bench sided squarely with the bidder. It held that the delay in executing the lease could not be pinned on Balaji Enterprises when the documentation the government itself was required to furnish was incomplete. A bidder cannot be penalised for a lease that never came together because the state's own District Survey Report was deficient. The bench set aside the forfeiture and ordered the Rs 2.90 crore deposit refunded to the firm.

The court went further than simply ordering a refund. It directed that the refund carry interest, and it did not stop at resolving this one dispute. The bench laid down timelines and guidelines for the state to follow in future mining tenders, aimed at preventing the same pattern repeating itself: a bidder paying up in good faith, the government sitting on its own paperwork, and the bidder being blamed for a stalled lease. A copy of the judgment was sent to the Chief Secretary, which signals that this was not meant to be treated as a one-off correction but as a standing instruction for how the mining department should run its tenders going forward.

The ruling draws a sharp line between the government's authority to decide whether and when to grant a lease, which the bench left largely intact by allowing the state's appeal on that point, and the government's duty not to profit from its own administrative failures. Those are different questions, and the Division Bench answered them differently. The state keeps its discretion over the lease itself, but it cannot also keep the bidder's money when the reason the lease fell apart traces back to an incomplete survey report that was never the bidder's responsibility to fix.

For companies bidding in state mineral auctions, the practical takeaway is that a security deposit is not an unconditional hostage to administrative delay on the government side. Where a lease stalls because the state has not completed its own prerequisite documentation, a bidder has a real basis to resist forfeiture and to seek a refund with interest rather than simply writing off the money and walking away. For state mining departments, the judgment is a reminder that forfeiting a deposit is not a costless way to paper over internal delay, particularly once a court is asked to look at whose failure actually caused the lease to collapse.

Why It Matters

Mining departments routinely forfeit bidders' security deposits when a lease does not go through, often without examining whether the delay was the department's own doing. This ruling tells state authorities in Bihar, and potentially elsewhere, that forfeiture cannot stand where the government's own incomplete paperwork, such as a deficient District Survey Report, is the real cause of the lease failing, and that a court-ordered refund with interest is a live remedy for affected bidders.
