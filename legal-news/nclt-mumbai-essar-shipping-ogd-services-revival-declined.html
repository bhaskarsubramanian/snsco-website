NCLT Declines To Revive Essar Group's OGD Services Even After Essar Shipping Settled All Creditor Dues

Source: Originally reported by moneylife.in, Bar and Bench and LiveLaw Biz. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

OGD Services Limited went into the corporate insolvency resolution process in February 2023 and, when no plan rescued it, was pushed into liquidation by an order dated 29 April 2024. More than two years later, its holding company and corporate guarantor, Essar Shipping Limited, came back to the National Company Law Tribunal's Mumbai Bench with an offer that, on paper, should have settled the matter: it had paid off every financial creditor, and most of the operational creditors too. The Tribunal still said no.

Essar Shipping's application, numbered I.A. 915 of 2026 within the original petition C.P. (IB) No. 17/MB/2019, asked for three things in the alternative: revive OGD Services and run it under Essar Shipping's own management, recall the April 2024 liquidation order, or at least allow withdrawal of the insolvency proceedings altogether. Behind the request sat a concrete settlement record. Punjab National Bank had issued a no-dues certificate in November 2025, and Union Bank of India had issued one earlier, in December 2024, confirming that both financial creditors had been paid in full.

That settlement did not stop at the financial creditors. Essar Shipping told the Tribunal it had settled or bought out eighty percent of the admitted operational creditor claims, amounting to about thirteen crore eighty-eight lakh rupees, and offered to clear the remaining operational dues and bear the costs of the insolvency and liquidation process itself if OGD Services were handed back to it as a going concern. On the numbers alone, every creditor who had a claim in the process stood to be paid in full, one way or another, if the Tribunal agreed.

None of that moved the Tribunal. Its reasoning turned on where OGD Services already stood in the process, not on how willing Essar Shipping now was to pay. Liquidation had commenced, and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code does not contain a provision letting a company walk back out of liquidation the way Section 12A allows withdrawal of a petition before admission, or the way a resolution plan can be approved before the resolution period lapses. Without that statutory hook, the Tribunal held it could not use its inherent powers to grant what amounted to the same relief by another route.

The Tribunal also rejected the argument that reviving OGD Services would preserve value, including its intangible assets, that liquidation would otherwise destroy. Value preservation, it held, is not by itself a ground for a tribunal to step outside the statutory scheme and revive a company once liquidation has begun. The order is reported to have treated this as a settled proposition rather than a close question on the facts of this case. Liquidators routinely argue the opposite in other cases, that a running business is worth more than its parts sold piecemeal, but that argument usually surfaces before liquidation starts, while a resolution plan is still possible, not after the tribunal has already found the company could not be rescued through the resolution process.

Essar Shipping's settlement, however complete, therefore bought it nothing in terms of getting OGD Services back. The liquidation process is reported to continue notwithstanding the payouts to creditors, and the liquidator remains in charge of winding the company down and distributing its assets according to the waterfall the Code sets out, rather than handing control back to the group that once owned it. No report traced in this search records whether Essar Shipping intends to appeal the order to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal.

For promoters and group holding companies watching a subsidiary slide toward liquidation, the order is a pointed warning about timing. Settling with creditors before a resolution plan is approved, or even before liquidation is ordered, keeps options open that the Code does not preserve once liquidation has actually started. Waiting until after the liquidator has taken over, then offering to pay everyone off, is not the same transaction in the Tribunal's eyes, however identical the cheques might look.

Why It Matters

Group holding companies and promoters often assume that paying off creditors in full will reopen the door to a company already pushed into the insolvency process. This order confirms that once liquidation has commenced, the absence of a specific statutory route to reverse it means a tribunal will not use its inherent powers to do so, even against a complete settlement, which changes how promoters of financially stressed group companies should think about the timing of any rescue attempt.
