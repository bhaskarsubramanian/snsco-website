Converting an IBC Appeal Into a Companies Act Appeal Does Not Rescue a Time-Barred Case, Rules NCLAT

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and supremetoday.ai. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

An appellant who files late under one statute sometimes tries to switch tracks to another, hoping a fresh provision buys a fresh limitation clock without losing the benefit of the time already spent litigating. The NCLAT's Principal Bench has shut that door, at least for a litigant who tried converting a time-barred Section 61 appeal under the IBC into a Section 421 appeal under the Companies Act.

The order under challenge was passed by the NCLT on 8 September 2025. An appeal under Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code was filed on 20 November 2025, which worked out to the 76th day after the order, well past the 30-day limitation period and the further 15-day maximum condonable extension that Section 61(2) allows. That delay alone put the Section 61 appeal in serious jeopardy from the outset.

Rather than let that appeal be dismissed outright, the appellant sought liberty from the Tribunal, which was granted on 29 January 2026. The Tribunal's order contemplated the appellant amending the existing Section 61 memorandum of appeal and converting it into one framed under Section 421 of the Companies Act, 2013, a provision governing appeals from NCLT orders generally. Instead of amending the existing appeal paper, the appellant filed what amounted to a fresh Section 421 appeal on 30 January 2026, a day after the liberty was granted.

That choice turned out to be decisive. The Bench, comprising Justice Sharad Kumar Sharma and Technical Member Arun Baroka, held that converting an appeal from one statutory provision to another is not a continuation of the earlier proceeding for limitation purposes. It described the change in memorable terms, calling it a rebirth with a fresh start from the date of institution under the different provision of law, rather than an unbroken carrying-forward of whatever time had already elapsed under the old one.

Measured on that basis, the Section 421 appeal's limitation had to be assessed from 30 January 2026, running back to the original 8 September 2025 order, a total gap the Tribunal calculated at 144 days. Section 421(3) of the Companies Act allows 45 days as of right, with a further 45 days condonable on sufficient cause shown, for a maximum reach of 90 days. Even granting the appellant the full benefit of that maximum condonable period, the appeal still fell short by 54 days, and the Tribunal held it could not invoke Section 14 of the Limitation Act to borrow back the time spent on the earlier Section 61 proceeding.

With limitation unresolved in the appellant's favour, the Tribunal rejected both the Company Appeal (AT) No. 39 of 2026 filed under Section 421 and the connected Company Appeal (AT)(Ins) No. 1874 of 2025 that had originated under Section 61, along with the delay condonation applications filed alongside each.

The lesson for insolvency practitioners sitting on a defective or late-filed Section 61 appeal is to treat any liberty to convert the appeal as an instruction to amend the existing filing, not an invitation to file something new under a different provision. Filing afresh, even where a tribunal's order seems to permit it, resets the limitation clock entirely and strips away whatever time had already been spent litigating the earlier appeal, a cost that, as this case shows, can prove fatal even where the tribunal has shown some sympathy for the appellant's underlying grievance. The order also exposes a drafting trap in how liberty orders of this kind get framed. The Tribunal's 29 January 2026 order spoke of amending the existing Section 61 memorandum, language that, read carelessly, might seem to permit either an amendment or a fresh filing achieving the same practical result. The Bench's insistence on the distinction shows that courts will read such liberty strictly, giving effect to the specific mechanism described in the order rather than to whatever route the appellant finds more convenient once liberty has been granted. There is a broader point here about how appellate forums treat statutory limitation generally. Both Section 61(2) of the IBC and Section 421(3) of the Companies Act set out their own outer limits, 45 days with a further 15 days condonable under the first, and 45 days with a further 45 days condonable under the second, and neither provision contains any mechanism for borrowing time spent under the other. An appellant cannot mix and match the more generous condonable period under Section 421(3) with time already spent pursuing a Section 61 appeal; each clock runs entirely on its own terms from its own date of institution. For corporate debtors, resolution professionals and creditors tracking appeal timelines, the safer practice going forward is to decide at the outset which provision genuinely governs the order being appealed, rather than filing under Section 61 as a default and hoping a later conversion to Section 421 will cure any defect in that choice. Getting the provision right the first time avoids the entire limitation trap this appellant fell into, and avoids staking a case's survival on a procedural choice a tribunal may read far more literally than the appellant expected.

Why It Matters

Where a tribunal grants liberty to convert a time-barred Section 61 IBC appeal into a Section 421 Companies Act appeal, counsel must amend the existing appeal memorandum rather than file a fresh appeal, since a fresh filing resets the limitation clock under Section 421(3) and forfeits any benefit of Section 14 of the Limitation Act for time spent on the earlier proceeding. Getting this procedural choice wrong can leave an otherwise meritorious appeal dismissed purely on limitation.
