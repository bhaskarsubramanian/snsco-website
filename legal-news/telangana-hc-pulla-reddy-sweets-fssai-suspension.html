Food Safety & FSSAI · 4 November 2026

Telangana HC Refuses Interim Relief to G. Pulla Reddy Sweets Over Food Licence Suspension

Source: Originally reported by telanganatoday.com, deccanchronicle.com, siasat.com and indtoday.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Days before Diwali, when sweet shops in Hyderabad do a large share of their yearly business, G. Pulla Reddy Sweets found its food safety licence suspended at its Sanathnagar unit. The company went to the Telangana High Court asking for interim relief against the suspension. On 4 November 2026, Justice N.V. Shravan Kumar refused to grant it, and instead directed the state's food safety authorities to carry out a fresh inspection of the premises and place their findings before the court at the next hearing, fixed for 6 November.

The suspension arose out of a citywide drive by TG SAFE, the state's food safety enforcement wing, which inspected several sweet manufacturing units ahead of the festive season. At the Pulla Reddy unit, inspectors reportedly found rotten vegetables, rodent droppings, expired and mislabelled products, unhygienic processing and washing areas, and food handlers without basic personal hygiene practices. The unit was also said to be operating under the wrong licence category for the scale and nature of its production. Officials seized food articles weighing roughly 3,133 kilograms, valued at about Rs 15.03 lakh, from the premises. The Food Safety Designated Officer for the Khairatabad Zone issued the suspension order in the closing days of October 2026.

Before the High Court, senior counsel V. Raghu argued for the company that the suspension was arbitrary and disproportionate, and that it fell outside what the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 actually permits. His case was that the deficiencies flagged during the inspection had since been fixed, and that the company had already filed a point-wise response to the authorities on 30 October addressing each of the alleged violations. On that basis, he sought an order letting the unit resume operations while the underlying challenge to the suspension was heard.

The state's counsel pushed back on timing as much as on substance. Lifting the suspension now, the government pleader argued, would blunt a crackdown that the authorities had launched specifically to protect consumers during the period of heaviest sweet consumption in the state's calendar. The court appears to have accepted that framing. Rather than weighing the point-wise rectification claims itself and ruling on whether they were adequate, it chose to send the factual question back to the people equipped to answer it: have the problems actually been fixed, verified not through the company's own written response but through another visit by the Food Safety Department.

That is a narrower outcome than either side was asking for. The company did not get its unit reopened, and the state did not get a ruling vindicating the suspension on the merits. What the order does is keep the suspension in place for at least two more days while an independent check determines whether the Sanathnagar premises now meet the standard the inspectors found wanting. If that report comes back clean, the pressure shifts back toward restoring the licence, since a suspension aimed at correcting unsafe conditions has less to stand on once those conditions are shown to be fixed.

For other food businesses facing suspension under the FSS Act during an enforcement drive, the message from this order is fairly direct. A court weighing whether to stay a suspension pending a facility's own claim of compliance is not inclined to take that claim at face value, particularly where the underlying inspection findings involve matters of basic hygiene rather than a paperwork lapse. A business in that position is better served preparing for, and inviting, a fresh verification inspection than relying solely on a written explanation of remedial steps taken, since the former is what a court appears readier to credit.

The timing also matters beyond this one company. TG SAFE's drive reportedly covered several sweet manufacturing units in Hyderabad around the same period, with licences suspended at multiple locations. How the Telangana High Court treats the Pulla Reddy matter, once the fresh inspection report is in, will likely shape how other affected businesses in the same drive frame their own challenges, whether they choose to contest the suspension order head-on or, as Pulla Reddy's counsel attempted, to argue that compliance has already overtaken the need for continued suspension.

Why It Matters

Food businesses hit with an FSSAI licence suspension during an enforcement drive often argue that the deficiencies have since been corrected, hoping a court will accept that claim and restore operations pending a full hearing. This order shows a court is more likely to order independent verification of that claim through a fresh inspection than to accept it on the strength of a written response alone, especially where the original findings concerned basic hygiene rather than documentation. Businesses facing suspension should prepare for, and welcome, re-inspection rather than resisting it.
