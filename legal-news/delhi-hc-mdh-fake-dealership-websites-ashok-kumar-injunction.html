Delhi High Court Restrains Fake Websites Selling Bogus MDH Dealerships

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A foreign company setting up its first Indian subsidiary often worries about a slow-moving dispute years down the line. The more immediate threat, as the spice maker Mahashian Di Hatti Private Limited found this month, is someone else building a fake website around your brand and selling dealerships that do not exist. The Delhi High Court's order of 5 November 2026 in MDH's suit against a set of unidentified website operators is a useful illustration of how Indian courts respond to that specific problem, and how fast.

MDH, which sells spices and spice mixes under a mark the company traces back to 1919, told the court that its own online monitoring in April 2026 turned up a cluster of websites, domain names and email addresses using the MDH name and its registered Kirti Nagar address to look like genuine company communications. The sites invited members of the public to pay for dealership, distributorship or franchise rights that MDH says it never granted. The company could not identify who was actually running the sites, so it sued the first four named defendants along with unknown operators described as Ashok Kumar and John Doe, a pleading device Indian courts have used for years to go after anonymous online infringers.

To get ahead of the harm those sites were causing, MDH applied under Order XXXIX Rules 1 and 2 of the Code of Civil Procedure for an ex parte ad-interim injunction, asking the court to grant interim relief before the other side had even been served or had a chance to respond. Courts grant that kind of relief sparingly, and only where delay itself would cause the harm the plaintiff is trying to prevent. Justice Vikas Mahajan accepted that case on the facts before him on 5 November 2026, finding that the balance of convenience favoured MDH and that it would suffer a loss that could not be adequately made good later if the fake sites kept operating while the suit proceeded.

The injunction restrains the first four defendants, and anyone acting on their behalf, from using or registering the MDH name or any identical or deceptively similar variant across goods, services, domain names, websites, email addresses and social media handles. It also restrains them from passing off their dealership or franchise offerings as connected with, authorised by, or originating from MDH. The order does not yet bind the unidentified John Doe defendants in the same way, since they have not been served or identified, but it gives MDH a legal basis to pursue domain registrars, hosting providers and payment intermediaries as the search for who is actually behind the sites continues.

This is not an isolated fact pattern. The Delhi High Court has granted broadly similar ex parte relief in disputes brought by other well-known brands over fake dealership and franchise websites in the past year, and the pattern in those cases, like this one, follows the same shape: a cluster of lookalike websites appears, offers dealerships or franchises for a brand that never authorised them, collects advance payments from hopeful applicants, and disappears once the fraud is noticed.

The suit is listed next on 20 January 2027 for completion of service, the filing of pleadings, admission and denial of documents, and the marking of exhibits, the ordinary next steps in a commercial suit once an ex parte order has been passed. Whether the named defendants appear and contest the injunction, and whether MDH can actually identify and serve the John Doe defendants in the meantime, will decide how the case develops from here. For now, the only operative outcome is the interim restraint itself, and it remains open to the defendants to apply to have it varied or vacated once they are heard.

For a foreign company licensing its brand into India, or an Indian subsidiary of a multinational building out a dealer or franchise network, the lesson here has little to do with spices. It is that the remedy most commonly used against brand impersonation of this kind in India is not a drawn-out trademark infringement trial but a targeted ex parte interim injunction application, provided the company can show the court a concrete pattern of misuse and move before the fraud causes further harm. Waiting to build a complete picture of who is behind the sites before approaching a court is usually the wrong sequence; MDH went to court while the identities of the operators were still unknown, and got relief the same way.

Why It Matters

This order shows how quickly an Indian court will grant interim relief against anonymous operators running fake dealership or franchise websites under a well-known mark, without waiting to identify who controls the sites. Any company licensing a brand into India, including a foreign company building a distributor network through an Indian subsidiary, faces the same exposure: lookalike websites that collect advance payments from prospective dealers before disappearing. The case confirms that a prompt ex parte Order XXXIX application, backed by monitoring evidence, remains the primary tool against this pattern, ahead of a full trademark infringement trial.
