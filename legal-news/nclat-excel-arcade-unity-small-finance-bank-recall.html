NCLAT: Recall Cannot Be Used to Reopen a Condonation-of-Delay Order Rejected on Merits

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and Taxscan. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

An appeal that misses its limitation window by even a day can die right there, and Excel Arcade Private Limited found out just how final that death is. The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal's Principal Bench in New Delhi, headed by Officiating Chairperson Justice Yogesh Khanna sitting with Technical Members Barun Mitra and Ajai Das Mehrotra, dismissed Excel Arcade's application to recall an earlier order that had rejected its plea for condonation of delay, in a ruling reported on 2 November 2026.

The story begins with Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 330 of 2026, which Excel Arcade filed against Unity Small Finance Bank Limited arising out of insolvency proceedings before the NCLT's Mumbai bench. Because the appeal reached the NCLAT outside the window the IBC allows, Excel Arcade first had to ask the Tribunal to condone the delay through I.A. No. 1245 of 2026. That application failed. On 22 May 2026, the Tribunal rejected the condonation plea, which meant the underlying appeal itself could go nowhere.

Rather than challenge that rejection before the Supreme Court, the only forum empowered to review an NCLAT order of this kind, Excel Arcade filed I.A. No. 7094 of 2026 within the same proceeding, asking the NCLAT itself to recall its own 22 May order. Its stated ground was that technical glitches in the Tribunal's e-filing system had caused the delay in the first place, a justification it argued the Tribunal had not properly weighed when it rejected condonation.

The Bench was unmoved, and its reasoning goes to the heart of what recall jurisdiction is for. Recall, under Rule 11 of the NCLAT Rules, 2016, lets the Tribunal correct an order where there has been a procedural lapse, such as a party not being heard or a fact not placed on record through no fault of its own. It is not a backdoor review of a decision already taken on the merits of the very point a party raised and lost on. Excel Arcade had argued the e-filing glitch point when the Tribunal first considered condonation, and the Tribunal had rejected it then. Asking for recall on the same ground, the Bench held, amounted to seeking a second hearing on a question already decided, dressed up as a recall application.

Budhia Swain v. Gopinath Deb, the Supreme Court's leading authority on a tribunal's inherent power to recall its own orders, supplied the yardstick the Bench applied here. That ruling draws a sharp line between recalling an order for a defect that goes to the Tribunal's jurisdiction to pass it in the first place, such as fraud, lack of notice, or an order passed without authority, and recalling an order because a party believes the Tribunal reached the wrong conclusion on a point it did consider. Only the first kind of defect opens the door to recall. The second kind is what a further appeal, in the NCLAT's case to the Supreme Court, exists to fix.

The Bench also noted that Excel Arcade had not carried the 22 May order any further up the chain, so it had become final by the time the recall application was filed. That point reaches beyond this one dispute. Once a litigant lets an adverse order attain finality by not appealing it within the time the law allows, a recall application to the same Tribunal cannot be used as a substitute route back into the same case.

For companies and lenders litigating insolvency appeals, the lesson is less about this particular bank dispute and more about the discipline the NCLAT expects from parties who miss a filing deadline. A technical glitch, a courier delay, a clerical mix-up, these are the kind of explanations condonation applications are built to test, and if the Tribunal rejects that explanation once, a recall application repeating the same explanation is not a second chance at persuasion. The only way to contest that rejection is to carry it to the Supreme Court within the time the law allows for a further appeal, not to ask the Tribunal that rejected it to think again.

Why It Matters

Personal guarantors, promoters and companies contesting insolvency proceedings often treat a missed filing deadline as something a later recall application can fix once the real explanation comes out. This ruling confirms that the NCLAT will not use its recall power to give a second hearing on a condonation plea it has already rejected, however sympathetic the stated reason. Litigants who miss limitation in an NCLAT appeal, including foreign creditors and resolution applicants working through Indian insolvency timelines, must challenge an adverse condonation order before the Supreme Court within the time allowed, not seek a rehearing from the same Bench.
