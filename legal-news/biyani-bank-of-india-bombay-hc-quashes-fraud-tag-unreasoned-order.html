Bombay High Court Quashes Bank of India's Fraud Tag on Kishore and Rakesh Biyani for Lack of Reasons

Source: Originally reported by Bar and Bench, LiveLaw Biz, freepressjournal.in and chambers.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Future Lifestyle Fashions Limited's bank account with Bank of India was classified as fraud, and the bank extended that classification to the company's promoters and managing director, including Kishore Biyani and Rakesh Biyani, under the framework the Reserve Bank of India lays down for identifying and reporting fraud in bank accounts. A classification of this kind is not a minor administrative label. Being named in the RBI's Central Fraud Registry carries consequences for future access to credit and banking facilities that follow an individual well beyond the account in question, which is why the procedure leading up to such a classification matters as much as the underlying allegation.

Kishore and Rakesh Biyani challenged the classification before the Bombay High Court, and their core complaint was procedural rather than a denial of the underlying facts. Bank of India's order, they argued, did no independent analysis at all. It reproduced portions of a forensic audit report that had examined the accounts, recorded its conclusions, and stopped there, without engaging with the detailed response the petitioners had filed to the bank's show cause notice.

A Division Bench of Justices B.P. Colabawalla and Firdosh P. Pooniwalla agreed. It described the bank's order as wholly unreasoned and held that it breached the RBI's 2024 Fraud Master Directions, which require a bank classifying an account as fraud to pass a reasoned order, one that actually grapples with the material the account holder has put forward in response to the show cause notice, rather than simply adopting a third party auditor's findings as its own conclusion without more.

The distinction the court drew is an important one for how banks are expected to use forensic audit reports in fraud classification proceedings. An audit report is an input into the bank's decision, a piece of evidence the bank is entitled to rely upon, but it cannot substitute for the bank's own reasoned decision on whether the statutory criteria for a fraud classification are met, especially once the account holder has filed a detailed response raising specific objections to the audit's findings. A bank that merely copies the audit's conclusions into its own order has not actually decided anything; it has delegated the decision to the auditor while keeping the legal responsibility for itself.

The relief granted reflects the limited, procedural nature of the court's finding. The Bench directed that the Biyanis' names should not appear in the RBI's Central Fraud Registry on the strength of the quashed order, but it expressly left Bank of India free to initiate fresh proceedings against them if it chooses to, this time with an order that actually addresses their submissions. The court made no observations on whether the underlying allegations against the petitioners have merit, confining itself entirely to the adequacy of the bank's reasoning.

The ruling sits alongside a cluster of similar Bombay High Court decisions on fraud classification procedure from around the same period, including rulings quashing a fraud tag for vague allegations and addressing how far courts can go in reviewing a bank's compliance with the RBI's Master Directions, suggesting banks' internal processes for fraud classification are drawing sustained judicial attention on the procedural side even as courts remain reluctant to second-guess the substance of a genuine fraud finding.

For promoters, directors and borrowers facing a fraud classification process, the order is a practical reminder to put real weight into the written response to a show cause notice, since a bank's failure to engage with that response in its final order is now a concrete and judicially recognised ground to have the classification quashed. For banks and their compliance teams, the message is equally direct: a forensic audit report can inform a fraud classification decision, but the bank's own order must show that it independently considered and answered the account holder's defence, not merely that it read the auditor's findings and agreed with them by silence.

The quashing of this one classification is unlikely to be the end of the matter for the parties involved, given the court's express liberty to the bank to start over. A bank that wants its next order to survive scrutiny will need to do the work the first order skipped, actually setting out why each specific objection raised in the petitioners' response does not hold up, rather than repeating the same forensic findings with a thin layer of additional text. Until that happens, the practical effect of this judgment is to buy the Biyanis time outside the Central Fraud Registry, not to resolve the underlying dispute over whether the account was in fact mishandled.

Why It Matters

Fraud classification under the RBI's Master Directions can permanently affect a borrower's or promoter's access to banking facilities through the Central Fraud Registry, making the procedural rigour of the classifying order critically important. This ruling confirms that a bank cannot discharge its obligation to pass a reasoned order simply by reproducing a forensic auditor's conclusions, giving account holders a concrete, high-profile precedent to challenge fraud classifications that fail to engage with their response to the show cause notice.
