Delhi HC Sets Aside Part Of MMTC-PAMP Mauritius Award Over Unaddressed Good-Faith Claim

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, delhihighcourt.nic.in and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

MMTC Limited, the public sector trading corporation, went to the Delhi High Court under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, to challenge an arbitral award that had gone almost entirely against it. The dispute traces back to MMTC's joint venture with Swiss refiners PAMP S.A. and MKS, routed through MMTC-PAMP India Private Limited, in which PAMP Holding Mauritius Limited holds the majority stake. On 3 November 2026, Justice Om Prakash Shukla gave MMTC a narrow but real win, setting aside the part of the award that concerned a specific allegation the arbitral tribunal had simply never decided.

The commercial dispute arose from payments of roughly seventy-one crore rupees that MMTC-PAMP India had made to PAMP and MKS in Switzerland. MMTC's claims touching those payments formed a batch that the tribunal rejected in the underlying award. Among the surviving claims MMTC pressed in that batch was an allegation that PAMP Holding Mauritius, as the joint venture's majority shareholder, had broken the good-faith obligations it owed MMTC under the shareholders' agreement governing the venture.

Before the High Court, MMTC did not ask the judge to re-examine the commercial merits of the underlying payments. Its case was narrower and, on the facts recorded, harder to answer: the tribunal had rejected the good-faith claim along with everything else, but nowhere in the award had it actually engaged with that specific allegation on its own terms. No reasoning addressed whether PAMP Holding Mauritius owed MMTC a duty of good faith as majority shareholder, or whether that duty had been broken.

Justice Shukla accepted that distinction. An arbitral tribunal is obliged under Section 31(3) of the Act to record reasons for its decision on every claim pressed before it, and a claim that is left without any independent finding is, for practical purposes, a claim that was never decided at all. The Court held that this omission caused MMTC material prejudice, since a party cannot test, let alone correct, reasoning that was never set out. That gap in the award, confined to the good-faith allegation, was enough to attract interference.

The Court was careful about how far its order went. It did not rule on whether PAMP could, as a matter of contract, charge MPIPL for services rendered under the joint venture arrangement, nor did it hold that the good-faith allegation was made out on the facts. Its finding was limited to the tribunal's failure to decide that one claim. What happens to the claim next, whether it goes back before an arbitrator for a reasoned finding or is pursued through some other route the parties choose, was left open by the order as reported.

For companies running joint ventures with offshore partners, the ruling is a reminder that a shareholders' agreement's good-faith or fair-dealing clause is not boilerplate a tribunal can pass over in silence. Once a party pleads a breach of that clause as a distinct claim, with its own factual basis, the tribunal has to deal with it on its own footing, separately from the larger commercial dispute around it. Treating it as subsumed in a broader rejection, without saying why, leaves the award vulnerable exactly as it did here.

The order also illustrates how narrow the ground for setting aside an award can be kept even when a petitioner succeeds. MMTC lost on most of what it originally claimed and won only on the discrete point that one allegation had gone unaddressed. Parties drafting arbitration clauses for cross-border joint ventures, and counsel framing claims before a tribunal, would do well to separately plead and separately press every distinct obligation in a shareholders' agreement, rather than let it ride on the coattails of a larger payment dispute.

Section 34 does not let a court sit in appeal over an arbitrator's view of the facts or the law, and the grounds on which an award can be touched are deliberately narrow: fraud, a breach of fundamental public policy, patent illegality on the face of the award, or a failure to deal with the dispute actually referred. MMTC's petition fit squarely into that last category rather than asking the judge to second-guess the tribunal's commercial judgment, which is likely why it succeeded on this one point while failing on everything else it originally sought to reopen. Petitions of this kind are also comparatively rare to succeed on, precisely because courts are wary of parties dressing up a disagreement with an arbitrator's reasoning as a complete absence of reasoning; what distinguished MMTC's claim, on the record as reported, was that the good-faith allegation against PAMP Holding Mauritius was pleaded as a free-standing breach of a specific clause, separate from the payment claims, which made the tribunal's silence on it easier to isolate and easier for the Court to test against the requirement in Section 31(3).

Why It Matters

Indian companies routinely enter joint ventures with foreign shareholders through layered holding structures, often incorporated in Mauritius or Singapore, with shareholders' agreements that carry good-faith or fair-dealing clauses. This ruling confirms that an arbitral tribunal must independently address a pleaded breach of such a clause, with its own reasoning, and that failing to do so can get that part of an award set aside under Section 34, even where the rest of the award survives untouched.
