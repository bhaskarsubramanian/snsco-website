NCLAT: Email Objections Sent Before a Demand Notice Can Be Enough to Block Insolvency

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and nsearchives.nseindia.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A corporate insolvency process that has already started, with an interim resolution professional in place and a moratorium running, can still be unwound if the company facing it can show the creditor's claim was genuinely disputed before the formal demand for payment went out. The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal's order against FTF Pharma Private Limited's insolvency makes that point with an unusually clear timeline of emails.

Immacule Lifesciences Private Limited had filed an application under Section 9 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, as an operational creditor, against FTF Pharma Private Limited. The National Company Law Tribunal's Ahmedabad Bench admitted the application on 9 September 2026. That order started the corporate insolvency resolution process against FTF Pharma, imposed the moratorium under Section 14 of the Code, and appointed Sunil Kumar Kedia as interim resolution professional, which in practice meant the company's board lost control of its own affairs from that date.

Abhay Sapre, a suspended director of FTF Pharma, appealed to the NCLAT's Principal Bench in New Delhi. His case rested on a set of email exchanges between the two companies, running from 21 July 2023 to 9 August 2024, all of which were sent before Immacule Lifesciences issued its demand notice on 22 October 2024. Those emails, he argued, already showed FTF Pharma disputing what it owed, well before the Section 9 process was even set in motion.

The tribunal, comprising Judicial Member Justice Mohd Faiz Alam Khan and Technical Member Naresh Salecha, agreed that this was enough. It held that a corporate debtor does not need to have filed a civil suit or started arbitration proceedings before a demand notice to show a dispute exists; correspondence raising genuine objections to the debt, sent before the notice, can do the job just as well. The relevant question for the tribunal at this stage is not whether the dispute will succeed on its merits, but only whether a plausible dispute existed before the insolvency process began.

That distinction matters because of how narrow the NCLT's own role is supposed to be at the admission stage. Once a tribunal is satisfied that a plausible pre-existing dispute exists between the parties, it has no business treating that dispute as too thin or too late to matter and admitting the application regardless. The NCLAT held exactly that here: the existence of the dispute, not its eventual merits, was the test the Ahmedabad Bench should have applied, and on the record before it, a genuine dispute was plainly there.

Having found that the admission should never have happened, the NCLAT set aside the Ahmedabad Bench's order of 9 September 2026 in full. The corporate insolvency resolution process against FTF Pharma was closed, the moratorium was terminated, the interim resolution professional was discharged, and the company's board got its powers back. A corporate disclosure filed with the stock exchanges by Shilpa Medicare, which holds an interest linked to FTF Pharma, recorded the same outcome on 5 November 2026, confirming that the CIRP had been annulled. By that point, Immacule Lifesciences itself had told the tribunal that the dispute between the two companies had been settled and did not oppose the appeal being allowed.

The practical lesson for any operational creditor chasing an unpaid bill through the IBC is to look closely at what the other side said before the demand notice went out, not just at what it says afterwards in response to the notice itself. A corporate debtor that raised real objections over email, well before any notice was served, has a workable defence to an insolvency application even after that application has been admitted and a resolution professional has stepped in. Equally, for any company on the receiving end of a demand notice with a genuine grievance about the underlying bill, this order is a reminder to keep a paper trail of those objections from the outset, because it is exactly that kind of correspondence that persuaded the appellate tribunal here.

For promoters and directors who find their company's board suspended by an admission order they consider unjustified, the case also confirms that an appeal to the NCLAT remains a real remedy even after a moratorium has taken hold and an interim resolution professional has been appointed. The process is not irreversible merely because it has started; what matters is whether the underlying claim was ever as undisputed as the creditor's petition made it out to be.

Why It Matters

Operational creditors often assume that once a demand notice goes unanswered or unpaid, admission into insolvency follows almost automatically, but this order confirms that a corporate debtor's pre-notice email objections can defeat that application even after admission, a moratorium and the appointment of a resolution professional. For company promoters facing a Section 9 petition, keeping and relying on a documented trail of objections raised before the demand notice can mean the difference between losing control of the board and having the whole process set aside on appeal.
