Delhi HC Revives 2017 Writ, Orders MCD and Police to Clear Chandni Chowk of Vendors Again

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon and LiveLaw. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Chandni Chowk's main stretch from the Red Fort to Fatehpuri, along with H.C. Sen Marg and Subhash Marg, has carried a no-vending and no-squatting tag since the Supreme Court's long-running Gainda Ram v. MCD litigation set those zones out years ago. The registered traders' body for the area, Chandni Chowk Sarv Vyapar Mandal, went to the Delhi High Court in 2017 to get those zones actually enforced on the ground, filing W.P.(C) 5002/2017. On 19 April 2022 the Court directed, in terms specific enough to leave little room for argument, that no vending occur on any of those three roads at all.

To make that direction stick, the Court set up a Special Task Force the following month, co-headed by the Deputy Commissioner of MCD's City SP Zone and the DCP of Delhi Police's North District, with a brief to stop illegal hawking and encroachments from creeping back. For a while it worked by the Court's own account of the record: between April 2022 and December 2023 the City SP Zone ran 167 removal drives, seized 3,236 articles from vendors, and recovered Rs 2,51,000 in penalties. On 20 December 2023, satisfied with the minutes the task force had recorded on 7 December that year, the Court disposed of the writ petition, holding the local SHOs and the MCD's Assistant Commissioner personally responsible for keeping up the compliance going forward.

That is where matters rested until the traders' body came back to court this November, saying the task force had gone quiet and the vendors had returned, this time in numbers it called alarming. It backed the claim with photographs and said repeated letters to the task force asking it to act had gone unanswered. The Division Bench of Justice Prathiba M. Singh and Justice Dinesh Bhatt, looking at the photographs placed before it, agreed that they showed, in the Court's own words, an alarming state of affairs on the ground.

The Bench was not interested in revisiting whether the original 2022 order was correctly made. That had already been settled, and the task force's own conduct between 2022 and 2023 was proof enough that compliance was achievable when the mechanism actually functioned. What it addressed instead was whether vendors with a Certificate of Vending stood on any different footing from those without one on these three roads, and it held plainly that they did not. Irrespective of whether the vendors hold such certificates, the Court said, they cannot be vending on the main road of Chandni Chowk, on H.C. Sen Marg, or on Subhash Marg. The task force, the Bench added, is under a continuing obligation to conduct regular visits and remains responsible for ensuring strict compliance with the very decisions it recorded in its own minutes back in 2023.

Rather than simply reprimand the authorities and leave it there, the Bench set a concrete next step. It directed the Assistant Commissioner of MCD's City SP Zone, the DCP North, the Additional DCP-I North, and the DCP Traffic North to hold an urgent meeting on 6 November 2026 at 11 a.m., with three representatives of the traders' body permitted to attend. The same officers were directed to remove all unauthorised and irregular vending from the three roads and to ensure that it does not simply resurface once the latest drive is done, and to file a status report before the matter is next taken up.

The application to revive the writ was allowed, the petition restored to its original number and position on the Court's docket rather than treated as a fresh filing, and the matter listed for a further hearing on 26 November 2026. Nothing in the order suggests the Bench regards this as a one-time exercise; the language of continuing obligation and personal responsibility for named officers points toward a case the Court intends to keep an eye on rather than close out again on the strength of minutes alone.

For traders, residents and anyone running a business along one of Delhi's oldest and busiest commercial stretches, the order is a reminder that a disposed writ petition enforcing a civic obligation is not necessarily the end of the matter if the enforcement mechanism it relied on stops functioning. A task force created by court order, and the officers named to run it, remain answerable to that order long after the petition that produced it has formally been closed, and a revival application supported by photographic evidence of non-compliance is enough to bring the file, and the officers, straight back before the Bench.

Why It Matters

Court-monitored enforcement mechanisms set up to police civic obligations, such as the joint MCD-Delhi Police task force here, do not survive on the strength of a disposal order alone; they need to keep functioning on the ground. This ruling confirms that a disposed writ can be revived years later if the mechanism lapses, that officers named to run such mechanisms carry a continuing personal responsibility, and that a vendor's certificate of vending does not entitle them to operate in a zone a court has already declared off limits.
