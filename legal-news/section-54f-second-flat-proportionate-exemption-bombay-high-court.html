Bombay High Court Bars Full Addition Of Second Flat's Cost Under Section 54F

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, thetaxcorp.in and TaxGuru. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A taxpayer who buys two flats out of the sale proceeds of a capital asset does not forfeit the whole of a Section 54F exemption merely because the tax department refuses to count one of those flats. The Bombay High Court has held that once an assessing officer disallows exemption for a particular flat, the law requires a proportionate recomputation under Section 54F(1)(b), not a blanket addition of that flat's entire purchase price back into the taxpayer's capital gains.

The petitioner, Nilesh Ramesh Modi, had sold a capital asset and invested the proceeds in two residential flats, claiming exemption under Section 54F for both. The assessing officer accepted the exemption for one flat but rejected it for the second, reasoning that owning more than one residential house at the time of transfer could disqualify the claim. Having denied the exemption on that flat, the officer then added its entire purchase cost of about Rs 4.78 crore back to Modi's income while computing long-term capital gains. Modi challenged this addition before the Bombay High Court in Writ Petition No. 4898 of 2026, arguing that the department had conflated two separate questions: whether the exemption was available at all, and how much of the gain remained taxable if it was not.

A Division Bench of Justices B.P. Colabawalla and Farhan P. Dubash agreed with him. The Bench held that Section 54F(1)(b) prescribes a formula tying the exemption to the proportion of net consideration invested in the qualifying asset, and that formula does not disappear merely because the department later decides a particular flat does not qualify. Disallowing exemption on one flat changes how much exemption is available; it does not convert the flat's purchase price into undisclosed or additional income. The Court set aside the addition and directed the assessing officer to recompute the gains proportionately instead of adding back the full cost.

Pending that fresh computation, the Court also eased the burden on Modi by cutting the amount he had to deposit for a stay of recovery, from Rs 38,75,018 to Rs 6,50,108, recognising that the department's own figure rested on the flawed full-addition approach. The Bench was careful to leave one question open: whether two flats bought together and used as a single residential unit, sometimes described as jodi flats, can themselves qualify as one house for Section 54F purposes. That issue was left for the appellate authority to decide on its own facts, and this ruling should not be read as having settled it.

High net worth individuals selling family property, business premises or listed shares often reinvest the proceeds across more than one residential unit, sometimes for genuine family reasons such as housing separate branches of the same household. This ruling tells them, and the officers assessing them, that a dispute over how many houses qualify under Section 54F should be fought on that narrow question rather than ballooning into a demand for tax on the entire disallowed property's cost. The Bombay High Court had taken a similar proportionate approach in Humayun Suleman Merchant v. CCIT in 2016, where it allowed exemption to the extent consideration was actually deposited or utilised, rather than denying it altogether for a technical shortfall.

For a taxpayer currently contesting a Section 54F claim, the practical lesson is to separate the two arguments at the assessment stage itself: first, whether the second property genuinely disqualifies the exemption, and second, if it does, what the correct proportionate exemption and resulting tax liability actually are. Tax officers who skip the second step and simply tax the whole disputed investment risk an order that collapses on exactly the ground Modi succeeded on. Return filings and appeals that rely on Section 54F should keep a clear computation sheet showing how the proportion was arrived at, since that working is what a reviewing court will look for.

None of this decides the recurring jodi-flat question, so anyone relying on two adjoining or jointly used flats for a single Section 54F claim is still litigating in uncertain territory until an appellate authority or a later judgment addresses it directly. What the Bombay High Court has settled, for assessees within its jurisdiction, is narrower but still significant: a disallowance under Section 54F is a computation exercise under the statute's own formula, not a licence to tax an entire disputed asset's cost as if the exemption claim had never been made.

Why It Matters

This ruling protects high net worth taxpayers who reinvest sale proceeds into more than one residential property from an inflated tax demand when only one of those properties is later found ineligible for exemption. Instead of losing exemption on one flat and simultaneously having its entire cost added back as income, taxpayers get a proportionate recomputation under Section 54F(1)(b), which usually results in a far smaller addition. The ruling leaves open whether two flats used as a single dwelling can themselves qualify, so anyone structuring a reinvestment across multiple units should still get that specific question examined before relying on this decision alone.
