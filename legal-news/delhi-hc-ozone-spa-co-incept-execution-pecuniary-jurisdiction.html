A Lower Decretal Sum Does Not Strip a Court of Power to Execute Its Own Decree: Delhi High Court

Source: Originally reported by Bar and Bench, LiveLaw Biz and courtbook.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A franchise dispute that settled years ago came back to the Delhi High Court not because anyone wanted to reopen the merits, but because the losing side spotted an opening in the numbers. Ozone SPA had filed a commercial suit in 2018 against Co-Incept seeking recovery of Rs 2,53,66,750, comfortably above the Delhi High Court's pecuniary threshold for commercial suits. The parties settled on 5 July 2018, and the court passed a consent decree for a lower sum, Rs 1,19,43,000, on 19 July 2018.

Years later, when Ozone SPA moved to execute that decree, the judgment debtor objected. Its argument was straightforward on its face: the decretal amount was now below the Rs 2 crore threshold that marks the boundary of the Delhi High Court's commercial jurisdiction, so the execution petition could not proceed there. The debtor also argued that Sections 38 and 39 of the Code of Civil Procedure, which govern which court can execute a decree, simply did not apply to decrees arising out of commercial suits, and it leaned on a Division Bench ruling in Asian Patent Attorneys Association to support that position.

Justice Subramonium Prasad was not persuaded that a settlement reducing the final figure could strip the court of a power it had when the suit was filed. The judge reasoned that a court's authority to execute a decree it has passed does not evaporate merely because the eventual decretal sum, after a settlement or otherwise, comes in below its monetary threshold. A change in the value of what is finally awarded does not retroactively undo the jurisdiction the court had over the suit itself.

The judgment draws the line clearly: this court has the jurisdiction to entertain suits above Rs 2 crore, and only this court can execute a decree where the suit was filed here for a value above Rs 2 crore, even if the decretal amount later comes out below that figure. On the specific objection, the court held that Sections 37 and 38 of the CPC do apply to decrees passed in commercial suits, rejecting the debtor's attempt to carve commercial decrees out of the ordinary civil execution framework altogether.

The ruling matters because settlements in commercial litigation routinely produce a final figure lower than what was originally claimed, often substantially lower once both sides compromise to avoid a trial. If every such settlement risked knocking the resulting decree out of the court that passed it, decree-holders would face a fresh jurisdictional fight at the execution stage almost as a matter of course, even in cases where nobody disputes the decree's validity. This judgment closes that door by anchoring jurisdiction to the suit as filed, not to the number that happens to emerge from a later compromise.

For judgment debtors looking for ways to delay execution, the case narrows one avenue that might otherwise have looked promising: arguing that a reduced settlement figure pushes the matter out of the forum that decided it. That argument failed here, and the reasoning does not leave much room for a different outcome on similar facts. For decree-holders, the practical lesson is that where the original suit was correctly filed above the pecuniary threshold, a settlement reducing the amount payable should not by itself create a jurisdictional obstacle at the execution stage.

The decision also reaffirms a broader point about how courts read changes to their own monetary limits over time. A court does not cease to have authority over matters within its original jurisdiction simply because its pecuniary limits are later revised, or because the specific sum finally due drops below whatever that limit happens to be. Litigants and their counsel should treat the forum question as settled once the suit is properly instituted, rather than reopening it every time the final figure changes.

The order itself, delivered on 6 October 2026, is available as a primary source alongside the secondary reporting, which makes this one of the more easily verifiable recent rulings on commercial court jurisdiction. That combination, a clear holding plus an accessible copy of the judgment, is likely to make the case a frequently cited reference point in future execution petitions where a debtor tries the same jurisdictional objection.

Why It Matters

Commercial litigants who settle a suit for less than originally claimed, or whose decretal amount otherwise ends up below the Delhi High Court's pecuniary threshold, can now rely on this ruling to resist a jurisdictional challenge at the execution stage. The decision confirms that a court's power to execute its own decree tracks the suit as filed, not the final figure awarded, closing off a delay tactic judgment debtors had been trying in commercial execution petitions.
