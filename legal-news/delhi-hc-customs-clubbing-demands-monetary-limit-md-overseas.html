Delhi HC: Customs Can't Combine Demands Against Several Importers to Clear Appeal Threshold

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

The Customs Department tried a workaround this month when it found itself short of the monetary threshold needed to appeal before the Delhi High Court, and the Court was not having it. The Commissioner of Customs (Import and General) had filed two separate appeals, CUSAA 111/2026 against M.D. Overseas Ltd. and CUSAA 132/2026 against New Kailash Jewellery House, both arising from a single CESTAT order dated 6 October 2025 that had gone against the Department. Individually, the amounts involved in each appeal fell below the one crore rupee limit the Department's own instructions set for filing before a High Court. The Department wanted the two demands read together.

That approach runs into the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs instructions issued under Section 131BA of the Customs Act, 1962, which fix a staged set of thresholds: fifty lakh rupees before CESTAT, one crore before a High Court, and two crore before the Supreme Court. Those instructions carve out only a limited set of exceptions where the Department may appeal regardless of amount, and combining separate demands against separate parties to reach a threshold that neither demand meets on its own is not among them.

The Division Bench of Justice Avneesh Jhingan and Justice Shail Jain held that where one adjudication order deals with multiple parties, each of whom faces a separate demand, the Department cannot aggregate those individual demands to satisfy the monetary limit for an appeal. Each party's liability has to be assessed on its own footing. Since neither the demand against M.D. Overseas nor the one against New Kailash Jewellery House independently crossed the one crore threshold, both appeals were dismissed as not maintainable.

The Bench was careful to leave the underlying legal questions raised in the appeals open rather than deciding them on the merits. Dismissing an appeal for want of maintainability on a monetary-limit objection is a different thing from ruling that the Department's substantive case was weak, and the order does not purport to say anything about whether the CESTAT's findings on the actual dispute, whatever it concerned, were right or wrong. The Department remains free to raise those questions again in a case that genuinely crosses the threshold.

This is not the only occasion the same Bench has had to apply the CBIC's monetary-limit instructions this month. A related appeal, decided a day apart from the two in this matter, saw the Department argue that cases involving prohibited goods should be treated as falling outside the limit altogether regardless of amount. The Bench rejected that argument too, pointing out that the instructions carve out only three specific exceptions and that the presence of prohibited goods in a consignment is not one of them.

Read together, the two rulings tell importers and their counsel something useful about how the Department is currently trying, and failing, to get around its own appeal thresholds. Clubbing demands against unrelated parties to manufacture a crossable figure does not work, and neither does arguing that a particular category of goods takes a case outside the monetary limit by its nature. Both routes require the instructions to say something they do not say, and the Court has now twice declined to read that extra language in.

For importers facing a customs demand that sits below the relevant threshold, the practical takeaway is that a departmental appeal challenging a favourable CESTAT order is likely to face a maintainability objection unless the Department can point to one of the specific carved-out exceptions in its own instructions, such as a question of law of recurring significance or a classification dispute of the kind the instructions separately address. An appeal that simply tries to reach the threshold by combining amounts from a co-respondent's separate liability, as the Department attempted here, is unlikely to survive a maintainability challenge of the sort M.D. Overseas and New Kailash Jewellery House succeeded in raising.

Why It Matters

The ruling closes off two routes the Customs Department has been trying to use to get around its own monetary-limit instructions for departmental appeals: aggregating demands against separate parties in a single adjudication order, and treating prohibited-goods cases as exempt from the limit by category. Importers who win before CESTAT on amounts below one crore rupees now have a clearer basis to resist a departmental appeal on maintainability grounds alone, without needing the appellate court to revisit the underlying dispute at all.
