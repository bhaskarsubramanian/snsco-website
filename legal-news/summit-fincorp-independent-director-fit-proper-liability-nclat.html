An Independent Director Who Attended Every Meeting and Raised No Red Flags Is Not Automatically Liable for the Company's Fraud: NCLAT

Source: As reported by corporate-governance trade press in January 2026, on an NCLAT order concerning an independent director's liability. Party names below are fictitious.

The NCLAT has set aside proceedings that had sought to fix an independent director of fictitious NBFC Summit Fincorp Ltd. with liability for the company's financial statement fraud, holding that his regular attendance at Board and Audit Committee meetings, without any specific finding that he knew of or actively facilitated the fraudulent entries, was not by itself sufficient to establish liability.

The appellant had attended every Board meeting during the relevant period and had not recorded any dissent on the financial statements later found to be fraudulent. The Appellate Tribunal held that the statutory protections available to independent directors under the Companies Act, 2013, including the presumption against liability for matters that occurred with the director's knowledge attributable through Board processes, acts of omission, or where he had not acted diligently. Are meaningful only if regulators and adjudicating authorities identify specific red flags the director ignored, rather than treating attendance and non-dissent alone as proof of knowing participation.

The Tribunal was careful to clarify that its ruling did not immunise independent directors who fail the 'fit and proper' diligence expected of the role. An independent director who is shown to have ignored specific, documented red flags, or who failed to make reasonable inquiries a prudent director in that position would have made, remains exposed. The finding here turned on the absence of any such specific red flag in the record.

Why It Matters

Independent directors should not assume that simply attending meetings protects them, nor should they assume that mere attendance without evidence of actual red flags ignored is enough to fix them with liability. The safer course remains documenting genuine, substantive engagement with financial statements and flagging concerns in writing when something looks amiss.
