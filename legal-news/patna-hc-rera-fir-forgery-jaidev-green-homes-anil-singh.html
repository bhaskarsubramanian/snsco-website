Patna HC: RERA Must First Probe Forgery Claims Before Any FIR Against a Builder

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A homebuyer's attempt to get the police to register an FIR against his builder, bypassing the Real Estate Regulatory Authority altogether, ran into a wall at the Patna High Court this month. Anil Singh had accused Jaidev Green Homes Pvt. Ltd. and its former director of filing fabricated documents and false affidavits before the Bihar RERA, and he wanted the state to be directed to register a criminal case over it. Justice Sourendra Pandey was not persuaded that a writ court was the place to start.

The order records a telling admission from Singh's own counsel: he had not approached either the police or the RERA bench itself with a request to look into the alleged forgery before heading straight to the High Court. That sequencing mattered to the outcome. The Court held that where the fraud is said to have been committed in proceedings before a specific forum, in this case RERA, it is that forum which must first examine the documents and record a finding, rather than a writ court being asked to short-circuit the process by ordering an FIR on the strength of the complainant's own say-so.

Justice Pandey's reasoning rested on a fairly simple point of sequence rather than any view on whether the documents were in fact forged. Seeking registration of an FIR solely on the basis of one's own allegations, without first approaching the RERA bench for an inquiry into the claim, was impermissible in law, the Court held. Nothing in the order suggests the forgery allegation was weak or ill-founded; the dismissal turned entirely on Singh having picked the wrong first step, not on the merits of what he was alleging.

The Court also made a point of recording that this was not a case of a complainant without recourse. It noted that the RERA order in the underlying dispute had gone against Singh, and that an order adversely affecting him left him free to pursue remedies before RERA on his own, rather than needing the state to intervene by way of a criminal complaint before any inquiry into the forgery claim had even begun. Put another way, there was an available and more appropriate channel sitting unused when the writ was filed.

This is not the first time a court has drawn that line between a regulator's fact-finding role and a writ petitioner's urge to get straight to a police complaint. RERA benches across states routinely deal with allegations that a developer altered allotment letters, backdated payment receipts, or filed affidavits that do not match the project's own records with the authority. Courts have generally insisted that such allegations be tested where they arose, before the same forum that received the disputed document, rather than treated as a shortcut into the criminal process.

For homebuyers dealing with builders they suspect of doctoring paperwork, the practical lesson from this order is about sequence rather than substance. A complainant who believes a developer has submitted forged documents to RERA should raise that specifically before the RERA bench hearing the matter, asking it to examine the document and record its own finding, before any parallel request for a criminal complaint is made. Filing a writ that asks a High Court to leapfrog straight to an FIR, without having first given the regulator the chance to look into its own record, risks exactly the outcome Singh got here: a dismissal on a threshold objection, with the substantive forgery claim left completely unexamined by anyone.

The ruling also serves as a reminder to developers and their counsel that a RERA bench's own findings on the authenticity of documents filed before it can carry real weight if a criminal complaint does eventually follow. A clean finding from the regulator, or conversely an adverse one, will shape whatever comes next far more than an unaddressed allegation sitting in a writ petition that never reached the forgery question at all.

Why It Matters

The order draws a clear sequencing rule for homebuyers who suspect a builder of forging documents before RERA: the regulator's own bench must be asked to inquire into the allegation first, before any request for an FIR or a writ seeking one. A complaint filed out of that order risks dismissal on a threshold point, leaving the underlying forgery allegation unexamined. Builders and purchasers litigating before state RERAs elsewhere can expect courts to take the same view of where such allegations belong.
