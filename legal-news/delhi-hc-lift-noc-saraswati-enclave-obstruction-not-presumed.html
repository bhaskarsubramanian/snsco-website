Delhi HC: Claim That a Lift Blocks a Neighbour's Balcony and Light Must Be Proved, Not Presumed

Source: Originally reported by delhihighcourt.nic.in and advocategandhi.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A dispute between neighbours in a cooperative group housing society reached the Delhi High Court this November over something that comes up often in Delhi's older residential colonies: one flat owner's plan to install a lift, and another's objection that the lift would come at the cost of his balcony, his light and his air.

The Municipal Corporation of Delhi had granted a no-objection certificate on 24 March 2026, clearing the way for private respondents to install an individual lift in their building at Saraswati Enclave Group Housing Society. Krishan Kumar Sharma, whose first-floor flat sat beside the proposed structure, challenged that NOC before the High Court, arguing the lift's location would block his main balcony and cut off the light and fresh air his flat currently received.

Sharma's petition sought quashing of the NOC on the footing that MCD had cleared the lift without properly accounting for its effect on his flat. He pointed to the DDA's 2015 lift policy, which governs how such installations are to be sited in group housing societies, as the framework MCD was bound to apply before issuing any clearance.

MCD's answer was that the matter had already been looked at once before, under an earlier direction of the High Court itself, and that its officials had re-examined the proposed site and found it viable within the terms of the 2015 policy. On that basis, the Corporation maintained the NOC had been properly issued and that due process had been followed in reaching that conclusion, including the site inspection the earlier court direction had called for.

Justice Saurabh Banerjee's judgment turns on where the burden sits in a dispute of this kind. The Court held that obstruction to a neighbour's balcony, light or air is not something a petitioner can simply assert and expect the Court to accept; it has to be established through relevant material placed on record, such as measurements, site plans or some other concrete basis showing the actual extent of the interference. Sharma's petition, the Court found, did not carry that kind of supporting material. The allegations of blocked balcony access and lost light and air remained at the level of assertion, unsupported by the evidence needed to displace MCD's own site assessment.

Because MCD had already carried out a site examination pursuant to the earlier court direction and had come back with a finding that the location was viable, the Court was not persuaded to go behind that assessment on the strength of unproven claims alone. The writ petition challenging the NOC was accordingly dismissed, leaving the 24 March 2026 clearance, and the lift project it permits, in place.

The ruling does not hold that a lift installed under an NOC can never be challenged on obstruction grounds. It holds that such a challenge needs an evidentiary foundation, not a bare description of inconvenience, particularly where the municipal authority has already carried out its own site inspection and arrived at a reasoned view that the policy's siting requirements are met. Disputes of this kind have become a recurring feature of litigation around Delhi's older residential colonies, many of them built decades before lifts were a standard feature and now populated by ageing residents for whom a lift is less a convenience than a necessity. The DDA's 2015 policy exists precisely because retrofitting a lift into a building never designed for one almost always means encroaching on some shared or adjoining space, whether a stairwell landing, a side setback or, as here, the area abutting a neighbouring flat's balcony. The policy tries to set out how municipal authorities should weigh that trade-off before clearing a lift, which is why MCD's own site inspection, carried out under the earlier court direction, carried real weight once Sharma's petition reached the Court. That framing of the burden of proof mattered as much as the ultimate outcome: if a bare allegation of blocked light or a blocked balcony were enough to stall an NOC, nearly every lift project in a densely built cooperative society could be held up indefinitely by an objecting neighbour, regardless of whether the municipal authority's own technical assessment found the siting acceptable. By insisting on actual supporting material, such as measurements or site plans showing the real extent of any encroachment, the Court kept the inquiry anchored to the specific site rather than to the general friction that lift installations tend to generate between neighbours.

For residents of Delhi's group housing societies, many of which are now adding lifts to ageing low-rise blocks under the 2015 policy framework, the judgment sets a practical marker: an objector who wants an NOC quashed on the ground that a lift blocks a balcony or cuts off light needs to come to court with the material to prove it, since a site assessment already carried out by the municipal body will not be displaced by assertion alone.

Why It Matters

Delhi's group housing societies are increasingly retrofitting lifts into older low-rise blocks, and neighbours frequently object on light, air or balcony-access grounds once an NOC is granted. This ruling tells such objectors that a court will not quash a municipal NOC on bare allegations of obstruction; they need plans, measurements or other concrete material, especially once the authority has already conducted its own site inspection under the governing lift policy.
