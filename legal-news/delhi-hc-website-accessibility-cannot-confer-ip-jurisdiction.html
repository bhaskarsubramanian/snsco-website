Website Being Visible in Delhi Is Not Enough for an IP Suit There: Delhi HC

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, LiveLaw Biz, Bar and Bench, mlex.com and Indian Kanoon. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A Delhi High Court bench has sent back seventeen commercial suits, most of them trademark infringement and passing-off actions, telling the plaintiffs to go and file where the dispute actually belongs. Justice Purushaindra Kumar Kaurav passed a common order on 3 November 2026 in a batch headed by ABD Maestro Private Limited v. Great Galleon Ventures Ltd., returning each plaint under Order VII Rule 10 of the Code of Civil Procedure for want of territorial jurisdiction, with liberty to the plaintiffs to seek appropriate directions under Order VII Rule 10A before refiling.

ABD Maestro, a Mumbai company, had sued Great Galleon, based in Indore, claiming the Delhi High Court had jurisdiction because the defendant's website and social media pages could be opened from Delhi. The product photograph in the record itself carried the words 'FOR SALE IN MADHYA PRADESH ONLY'. ABD Maestro had not pleaded that Great Galleon held any excise licence to sell in Delhi, and the court found nothing to show the goods were actually sold there. The defendant's GST registration showed a Delhi address, but the court held that this fact carries a specific meaning under GST law and does not by itself answer the separate question of where a civil suit can be filed under the CPC.

The other suits in the batch raised the same pattern. Plaintiffs, many represented by the same counsel, had placed test orders through company representatives or investigators, had the goods delivered to a Delhi address, and then relied on that single transaction to anchor the entire suit in the capital. The court was unimpressed by this. It said a transaction engineered solely to create a jurisdictional fact cannot be treated the same as a genuine sale sought out by an ordinary customer, and that accepting such purchases as proof of jurisdiction would let a plaintiff sue almost anywhere in the country simply by placing an order and asking for delivery to that city.

The judgment deals at length with when a website crosses the line from being merely accessible to being something the defendant used to target customers in a particular place. A passive website that can be viewed from Delhi is not enough. What matters, the court said, is whether the defendant transacted business with someone in Delhi, promoted the goods specifically to buyers there, or otherwise purposefully directed its commercial activity at the city, rather than simply being reachable online the way every website on the internet is reachable from everywhere.

Justice Kaurav also wrote about what the order calls the risk of the Delhi High Court becoming the default forum for intellectual property disputes with only a slender connection to the city. He used the image of a Banyan Tree whose wide canopy, if left unchecked, keeps younger trees underneath it from growing, suggesting that Delhi's heavy share of IP litigation may be crowding out the development of IP jurisprudence in other High Courts. He acknowledged that after years of entertaining suits on a near pan-India basis, it is not easy for a court to draw what he called a Lakshman Rekha and hold itself back within its own limits, but said that restraint is exactly what the law on territorial jurisdiction demands.

The order leans on the doctrine of forum non conveniens alongside the plain requirements of the CPC, holding that even where a technical argument for jurisdiction can be constructed, a court should decline to proceed if another forum is clearly the more appropriate one for the parties and the dispute. Among the suits returned were matters involving well-known names such as Beiersdorf's Nivea business and Amazon's Indian marketplace entity, which shows the ruling was not confined to small or obscure litigants but cut across the kind of brand-protection suits that are filed in large numbers in Delhi.

None of this settles the question for good. Lawyers have since flagged several findings in the order before a larger bench of the same High Court, which had earlier in the year been asked to resolve conflicting Division Bench views on when website accessibility can support jurisdiction in online infringement cases. Until that reference is decided, litigants filing IP suits in Delhi on the strength of an online listing or a single arranged purchase now face a bench willing to send the case back if the connection to the city does not go beyond that.

For businesses facing look-alike products or marks sold through e-commerce platforms, the immediate lesson is practical. A plaint that wants to stay in Delhi needs to plead more than a website that opens on a Delhi screen. It needs facts showing the defendant actually did business there, such as real sales to Delhi customers, advertising aimed at the city, or a course of dealing that targeted it, rather than a one-off purchase arranged for the purpose of litigation.

Why It Matters

Brand owners who route every India-wide enforcement suit through Delhi on the strength of a website or a single test purchase now face a real risk of the plaint being returned at the threshold. Suits need to plead and be ready to show actual targeting of Delhi customers or genuine transactions there, not just that the defendant's online presence can be viewed from the city, before filing in the Delhi High Court.
