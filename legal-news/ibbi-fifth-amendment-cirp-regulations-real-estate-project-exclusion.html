Real Estate & RERA · 30 October 2026

IBBI Lets Creditors Carve Out Stalled Real Estate Projects From Insolvency, Mandates Project-Wise Accounts

Source: Originally reported by TaxGuru, IBC Laws, Business Standard and LiveLaw Biz. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A real estate developer that goes into insolvency rarely has just one project running. More often it has several, spread across different sites and at different stages of construction, funded out of a common pool of company accounts where money collected from buyers of one tower can end up paying contractors working on another. When the company lands in a CIRP, that commingling has made it unusually hard for a resolution professional to work out which homebuyers are actually owed what, and which of the developer's projects are worth salvaging as going concerns versus which ones are better excluded from the resolution process entirely. The IBBI's Fifth Amendment to the CIRP Regulations, notified on 30 October 2026, is aimed squarely at that problem.

The core change substitutes Regulation 4D of the Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons Regulations, 2016, and its first requirement is structural: a real estate corporate debtor's resolution professional must now maintain a separate bank account for each project rather than running receipts and payments for every site through the company's general accounts. Alongside that, the amendment requires periodic project-wise statements of receipts, payments and other financial information to be placed before the committee of creditors, so that creditors assessing a resolution plan can see how each individual project is actually funded rather than reading the company's finances as a single undifferentiated pool.

The amendment also gives the committee of creditors a power it did not clearly have before: to recommend excluding a single, individually stalled project from the ongoing CIRP, provided at least 66 percent of the voting share of the committee supports that recommendation and records its reasons for it. The resolution professional then takes that recommendation to the adjudicating authority for approval. The point of carving a project out this way is to stop one badly stuck or legally encumbered site from holding back the resolution of a developer's other, viable projects, where homebuyers are waiting on possession and a resolution plan for the company as a whole might otherwise be delayed indefinitely by the one project nobody can resolve.

Resolution plans submitted under the amended framework also have to do more than they did before. A plan must now set out project-wise timelines for completion and delivery of possession where that is what is being proposed, provide for periodic progress reports to a monitoring committee once the plan is approved, and spell out how the plan treats homebuyers who never filed a claim in the CIRP at all, a category that earlier resolution plans had often left to one side. Reporting from the same package of changes describes the resolution professional, with the committee's approval, now being expected to bring in independent professionals to assess the real, on-ground state of each project, its construction progress, its outstanding regulatory approvals, unsold inventory and the realistic cost and time needed to finish it, rather than relying on the developer's own figures going into the resolution process.

Taken together, the changes push the CIRP framework for real estate companies toward treating each project almost as its own accounting and reporting unit within a single corporate insolvency, even while keeping the company itself as the entity actually undergoing resolution. That is a meaningful shift from treating a real estate CIRP as a single undifferentiated pot of assets and liabilities, and it follows IBBI's Fourth Amendment to the same regulations earlier in 2026, part of a continuing effort to adapt the general CIRP framework, built with manufacturing and trading companies in mind, to the particular mess that a stalled, multi-project real estate developer's insolvency tends to be.

For resolution professionals already handling real estate CIRPs, and for the committees of creditors overseeing them, the amendment is not merely a reporting formality. Separate project accounts and project-wise reporting will make it considerably harder for funds collected against one project to be diverted, deliberately or otherwise, toward finishing or propping up another, which has been a recurring complaint from homebuyers' associations in stalled housing projects for years. The 66 percent threshold for excluding a single project is high enough that it should not become a routine escape hatch for abandoning difficult sites, but it does give a committee a formal route to stop one irretrievable project from being the reason an entire developer's resolution collapses. Promoters and resolution applicants bidding for distressed real estate companies going forward should expect resolution plans to be scrutinised project by project rather than approved as one lump figure for the company as a whole.

Why It Matters

Real estate insolvencies have long been complicated by developers running several projects through commingled company accounts, leaving resolution professionals unable to tell which homebuyers' money funded which site. The Fifth Amendment forces project-level accounting and reporting, and gives creditors a formal, high-threshold route to excise a single unsalvageable project rather than let it stall the resolution of an entire company. Resolution professionals, committees of creditors and homebuyers' associations in ongoing real estate CIRPs should expect fresh compliance obligations and more granular scrutiny of resolution plans under the amended Regulation 4D.
