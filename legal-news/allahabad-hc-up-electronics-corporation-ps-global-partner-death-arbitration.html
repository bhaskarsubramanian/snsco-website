Allahabad HC: A Partner's Death Before a Claim Reaches the Arbitrator Does Not Void the Firm's Case

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and TaxGuru. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

U.P. Electronics Corporation Ltd. tried three separate routes to escape an arbitral award of ₹8,99,38,275, plus interest at 8 per cent a year, that had gone against it in favour of P.S. Global, a partnership firm. One of those routes turned on a fact that had nothing to do with the merits of the underlying dispute: the partner who had signed the firm's statement of claim died before that claim actually reached the arbitrator. The Corporation argued this made the claim non-est, filed in the name of someone no longer alive to answer for it. The Allahabad High Court's Lucknow Bench, hearing the Corporation's appeal under Section 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, rejected the argument along with the other two.

On the partner's death, the Court worked from the actual sequence of dates rather than treating the Corporation's objection as self-evidently fatal. The partner had been alive when he signed the statement of claim on behalf of the firm, and had died only afterward, before that claim was placed before the arbitrator. Because the claimant was a partnership firm and its other partners remained alive and able to carry on the proceedings, the Court held, by analogy to Order XXX of the Code of Civil Procedure, that the firm's claim did not abate merely because one partner who had signed it was no longer alive by the time of a later procedural step. The firm had disclosed the death at the first opportunity available to it, in its replication, which the Court treated as consistent with good faith rather than concealment.

A second objection concerned non-joinder: the Corporation argued that the State Government ought to have been impleaded as a necessary party, since the tender, the screening of bids, the negotiations, the work order and the eventual termination letter had all, on the Corporation's own account, involved governmental participation at some stage. The Court was not persuaded that this made the State Government a necessary party to an arbitration arising out of a contract to which the Corporation, not the State, was the actual signatory and counterparty.

The third and more substantive objection concerned allegations that the bidding process behind the underlying contract had been affected by cartel-like conduct among bidders. The arbitrator had already considered this allegation during the reference and found it unproved on the material placed before him. The High Court declined to reopen that finding on appeal, holding that an arbitral tribunal's assessment of disputed factual allegations, reached after considering the evidence actually led, is not something a court exercising Section 37 jurisdiction will re-examine absent patent illegality or a jurisdictional defect, neither of which the Corporation had shown.

This appeal itself originated in a Section 34 petition that Commercial Court No. 2, Lucknow had dismissed on 15 December 2025, upholding the arbitrator's award of 4 February 2022. The Corporation's Section 37 appeal to the High Court was, in substance, a second attempt at the same three objections that had already failed once before the Commercial Court, and the High Court found no illegality in either the award or the Commercial Court's reasoning that would justify interference.

For parties dealing with partnership firms in arbitration, the ruling is a reminder that a partner's death during the pendency of a claim is a procedural event to be managed, through disclosure and continuation by the surviving partners, rather than a ground that automatically defeats the claim. For government corporations and public sector undertakings contesting arbitral awards, it is also a reminder that reviving a factual allegation the arbitrator has already examined and rejected rarely succeeds on appeal, particularly where the appellant offers no fresh material beyond what the tribunal already considered and found wanting.

Indian partnership law has long treated a firm's identity as distinct from any single partner who happens to sign a particular document on its behalf, and the Court's reliance on Order XXX of the Code of Civil Procedure by analogy fits squarely within that approach. A suit or claim brought in the name of a firm is, in substance, brought by all its partners collectively, so the death of one does not extinguish the claim as long as others remain to carry it forward and the fact of the death is placed on record rather than concealed. Corporations contesting awards on this kind of technical ground will need to show something more specific than the bare fact of a signatory's death, such as a genuine dispute over who had authority to pursue the claim after that death, before a court will treat the point as a serious challenge to the award rather than a last throw of the dice after the arbitrator has already ruled against them.

Why It Matters

Partnership firms are common claimants in commercial arbitration, and a partner's death during a long-running reference is not unusual given how many years these disputes can take to resolve. This ruling confirms that such a death does not void the firm's claim if disclosed promptly and the firm continues through its surviving partners, while also showing how narrow an appellate court's review of an arbitrator's factual findings on disputed allegations like bid-rigging remains under Section 37.
