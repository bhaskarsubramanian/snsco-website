Police Cannot Decide If a Neighbour's Dairy Farm Is Illegal or a Health Hazard: Calcutta HC

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, supremetoday.ai and dairynews.today. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Angurbala Mondal had a complaint familiar to anyone who has ever lived next to someone else's livestock operation: she said two neighbouring dairy units were running without proper authorisation and that their effluent, waste and general operation posed a serious health hazard to her and the rest of the area. She wanted the local police to step in and shut the units down, and when that did not happen to her satisfaction, she took the matter to the Calcutta High Court by way of a writ petition.

The State's response raised a point that recurs often across nuisance and pollution complaints against small agricultural and livestock units: the police are not equipped, and were never given the legal mandate, to measure whether a dairy operation's waste disposal, effluent discharge or general hygiene standards cross a regulatory line. That is fundamentally a technical and statutory determination, not a simple law-and-order one that a beat constable can resolve on the spot. The State also pointed out, not without some force, that Mondal herself ran a dairy farm of her own, a fact the Court noted without treating it as decisive either way on the underlying complaint.

Justice Saugata Bhattacharyya accepted the State's framing of the problem in full. Police officers, he held, have neither the technical expertise nor the statutory authority to adjudicate whether a dairy unit's day-to-day operations comply with environmental or health regulations, or whether they amount to a hazard serious enough to justify closure. Those are questions for a specialised regulatory body equipped to test effluent, assess waste management practices and apply the specific standards that govern livestock and dairy operations, not for a police station working off a neighbour's complaint and a site visit.

The Court accordingly directed Mondal to take her grievance instead to the West Bengal Pollution Control Board, the authority actually empowered under the relevant environmental statutes to investigate and act on claims that a dairy unit's operations violate environmental norms or pose a genuine health hazard. The writ petition was disposed of on that basis, with the Court granting her liberty to pursue her complaint before that Board rather than leaving her without a remedy altogether; it simply redirected her to the forum equipped to actually assess the technical merits of what she was alleging.

This ruling is not an isolated one-off. A separate Calcutta High Court order around the same period, in a case brought by a different petitioner against livestock operations, reached a materially similar conclusion: police lack the specialist capacity or statutory footing under the Code of Criminal Procedure to measure effluent discharge, assess solid-waste compliance or determine whether a livestock operation's activities amount to pollution. That determination, the court held, belongs with the Pollution Control Board and the relevant municipal or civic authority, echoing exactly the division of labour Justice Bhattacharyya drew in Mondal's case.

Read together, the two rulings mark out a fairly clear boundary for residents, dairy and poultry operators, and local police across West Bengal dealing with neighbourhood friction over livestock units. A complaint that a dairy or similar agricultural operation is causing a nuisance, discharging effluent improperly, or posing a health risk is, at bottom, a regulatory compliance question that the Pollution Control Board and municipal health authorities are built to answer. Police intervention premised on the same facts, without that regulatory body first weighing in, risks being set aside as an exercise of power the police were never given.

For dairy and poultry operators who find themselves on the receiving end of a neighbour's complaint, the practical upshot cuts both ways. They are protected from summary police action taken purely on a complainant's say-so, but they remain fully exposed to an actual Pollution Control Board inquiry once a complaint is properly lodged there, and that inquiry, unlike a hurried police visit, will test their effluent management and waste disposal practices in detail against the regulatory standards that actually govern their operations.

Why It Matters

Complaints against dairy and poultry units over nuisance, effluent or health hazards are common, and operators and complainants alike often look first to the police for a quick resolution; this ruling confirms that route is legally unavailable and that only the State Pollution Control Board can determine whether such an operation's practices actually violate environmental or health standards. Agricultural and dairy businesses facing police pressure over such complaints now have a clear basis to insist the matter go through the proper regulatory channel instead.
