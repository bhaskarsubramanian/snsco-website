A Medical Representative Is Not a 'Workman' Under the UP Industrial Disputes Act: Allahabad High Court

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw on 20 July 2026. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes — it is not a reproduction of the original report. Readers are encouraged to consult the original source and the underlying judgment or order directly.

The Allahabad High Court has applied the well-established duties-based test for 'workman' status under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 to hold that a medical representative — the pharmaceutical sales role responsible for promoting drugs to doctors and pharmacies — does not qualify as a workman under the Uttar Pradesh-specific application of the Act, even where the individual's job involved a degree of reporting and coordination work.

The reasoning follows the Supreme Court's long-standing precedent in H.R. Adyanthaya v. Sandoz (India) Ltd. — where the predominant character of a role is sales and canvassing rather than manual, skilled, technical or clerical work, incidental paperwork does not convert it into a workman's role. The Allahabad High Court's application of this test to the specific facts before it confirms that medical representatives, as a class, continue to fall outside the ID Act's protective umbrella in the ordinary case, notwithstanding periodic attempts to argue otherwise based on the reporting and data-entry components of the job.

This particular question has generated a surprising volume of litigation over the decades, precisely because medical representative roles sit in an awkward middle ground — they are neither purely managerial nor purely manual, and the job genuinely does involve some clerical-adjacent tasks such as call reports, expense documentation, and stock reconciliation alongside the core canvassing function. What the Adyanthaya line of cases, and this ruling's application of it, ultimately turns on is which function is predominant: where canvassing and sales promotion clearly dominate the role's actual time and purpose, the incidental reporting obligations do not tip the balance toward workman status, however administratively burdensome those obligations might feel to the individual employee.

For pharmaceutical companies and their HR teams, the ruling reinforces that termination and disciplinary matters involving medical representatives can generally be handled under the terms of the individual employment contract and applicable state Shops and Establishments law, rather than under the more protective statutory machinery reserved for workmen — though, as always, the specific duties actually performed by a given individual should be checked against the test before assuming the classification applies uniformly across a sales team. Companies restructuring or terminating medical representative roles should nonetheless maintain clear documentation of the role's actual day-to-day duties, since an individual whose actual responsibilities have drifted meaningfully toward operational or clerical work over time could present a genuinely different fact pattern from the one this ruling addressed.

Why It Matters

Medical representative roles generally sit outside 'workman' protection under the Industrial Disputes Act — but always verify the actual predominant duties of the specific role before relying on this as a blanket classification.
