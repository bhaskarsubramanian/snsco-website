Calcutta High Court: An Unregistered Project Joint Venture Cannot Sue to Recover Contract Dues

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Two contractors came together under a joint venture agreement to execute a railway project for South East Central Railway, the kind of arrangement that is routine in Indian infrastructure work, where two firms pool their technical and financial capacity to bid for and execute a single large contract. When payment disputes arose and the joint venture, Rahee-GPT (JV), sued the Railway to recover amounts it said were due under the contract, it won at first instance. A learned single judge of the Calcutta High Court passed a judgment and decree in the joint venture's favour on 10 March 2026.

South East Central Railway appealed, and its central argument was not about the merits of the payment claim at all. It argued that Rahee-GPT (JV) was, in substance, a partnership between the two contractor entities, and that this partnership had never been registered under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. Section 69(2) of that Act bars an unregistered firm from instituting a suit to enforce a right arising from a contract, a bar that exists regardless of how strong the underlying claim on the merits might be. If the joint venture was a partnership in the eyes of the law, the Railway argued, the suit should never have been entertained in the first place.

The Commercial Appellate Division, comprising Justices Debangsu Basak and Aryak Dutt, agreed with the Railway. The court treated the project-specific joint venture arrangement between the two contractors as falling within the definition of a partnership under the Act, reasoning that an arrangement where two or more persons agree to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them, acting for the others, meets that definition regardless of whether the parties chose to label their arrangement a joint venture rather than a partnership.

Importantly, the bench noted that the respondents themselves had admitted the firm was not registered, which left little room for the joint venture to argue its way out of the Section 69(2) bar through a factual dispute. With that admission on record, the appellate court set aside the single judge's decree and dismissed the suit outright, rather than remitting it for the question of registration to be tried separately. A right cannot be enforced through a suit the law never permitted to be filed, and curing the registration defect after the suit has already run its course and reached decree stage was not treated as an option.

This is a recurring trap for infrastructure contractors structuring project-specific joint ventures in India. Two companies forming a JV to bid for and execute a single contract often think of the arrangement as closer to a company or a consortium than to a partnership firm, particularly where each party's scope of work is defined and invoiced separately in the underlying contract. Courts applying Section 69(2) have repeatedly looked past that label to the substance of the arrangement, and where the substance amounts to persons agreeing to share the profits of a joint business, the partnership registration requirement follows whether or not the parties intended it to.

The consequence of getting this wrong is not a procedural inconvenience that can be fixed with an amendment. Section 69(2) operates as an absolute bar on the suit itself, so a joint venture that discovers only after years of litigation, and in this case after already winning at first instance, that it was never entitled to sue in the first place loses the entire claim regardless of its underlying merits. The registration itself is a simple, low-cost filing, but it must be done before the cause of action is sued upon, not after a dispute has already crystallised.

For any joint venture formed to bid on a single Indian infrastructure or government contract, particularly where foreign and domestic partners come together for a project without setting up a separate corporate vehicle, the practical lesson from this ruling is to register the arrangement as a partnership firm at the outset, or structure it clearly as something else entirely, such as an unincorporated joint venture governed purely by contract with no shared-profit arrangement that could later be characterised as a partnership. Waiting to see whether a dispute arises before sorting out that question is exactly what cost Rahee-GPT (JV) its decree in this case.

Why It Matters

The ruling confirms that courts look past the label a project joint venture gives itself and apply Section 69(2)'s bar on unregistered partnerships suing to enforce contract rights, which means contractors, including foreign partners entering Indian infrastructure joint ventures, can lose an otherwise winning claim entirely if the venture was never registered as a partnership before the suit was filed.
