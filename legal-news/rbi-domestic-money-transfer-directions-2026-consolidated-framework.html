RBI Consolidates Domestic Money Transfer Rules Into One Set Of 2026 Directions

Source: Originally reported by psuwatch.com, corplawupdates.in and dailypioneer.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Every year, lakhs of migrant workers in Delhi and the industrial belt around Noida and Greater Noida send money home through a bank agent or a kirana-store correspondent rather than a branch counter, using what the Reserve Bank calls the Domestic Money Transfer channel. The channel exists precisely because many remitters and beneficiaries at this end of the market do not hold, or do not wish to open, a full savings account, so the transfer happens through an agent acting on behalf of a bank rather than at a teller's window. On 11 November 2026, the RBI replaced the rules governing that channel with a single consolidated set of directions, folding together instructions that had sat scattered across a 2011 circular and a 2024 review into one document.

The Reserve Bank of India (Domestic Money Transfer) Directions, 2026 carry the reference RBI/DPSS/2026-27/476 and took effect the day they were issued. They expressly repeal the earlier 5 October 2011 circular on domestic money transfer and the 24 July 2024 circular that had reviewed the framework, so banks, white-label ATM operators, payment aggregators and business correspondents handling these transfers now work off a single text rather than two layered ones.

On the money itself, the Directions carry forward limits the sector already knew: a remitter paying in cash through an agent or an ATM can send up to Rs. 5,000 in a single transaction and Rs. 25,000 in a month, cash pay-outs to a beneficiary are capped at Rs. 10,000 a transaction, and a walk-in customer using NEFT at a bank branch can remit up to Rs. 50,000. Coverage of the notification describes this less as a tightening and more as a reaffirmation, since the figures match what was already in force under the 2024 review; what has changed is the structure, with the older circulars now folded into one consolidated set of directions rather than read together.

The Directions keep the onboarding requirement that has applied to this channel for some time: a remitting bank or its business correspondent must register a first-time sender against a verified mobile number and a self-certified Officially Valid Document before any transfer can be initiated in that person's name, and every transaction a registered remitter carries out afterwards must clear an Additional Factor of Authentication. For a channel built around walk-in customers who often do not hold a full bank account, that registration step is the main safeguard against a transfer being pushed through on a stolen or borrowed phone number.

Complaints about a domestic money transfer gone wrong, whether a payment misdirected to the wrong beneficiary or a cash pay-out denied at the agent's counter, continue to fall within the Reserve Bank's ombudsman mechanism, and banks and non-bank entities running this business may charge remitters a reasonable fee for the service, a point the Directions leave largely where the 2024 review had left it.

None of this changes what a Delhi, Noida or Kolkata resident sending Rs. 20,000 to family in another state can actually move through an agent in a month; the Rs. 25,000 cap was already there. What changes is that a bank's compliance team, or a business correspondent's operations desk, no longer has to read a 2011 circular against a 2024 amendment to work out which clause currently governs a given transaction. Having one consolidated document cuts the chance of an agent or a smaller payments company applying a withdrawn limit by mistake, a risk that grows the longer two circulars are meant to be read together rather than as one.

For banks, payment aggregators, white-label ATM operators and business correspondents running this channel, the practical task is to confirm their internal compliance manuals now cite the 2026 Directions rather than the superseded 2011 and 2024 circulars, since an audit or an RBI inspection from this point on will test compliance against the consolidated text, not its predecessors.

Smaller correspondent networks and newer payment companies, the ones most likely to have been working off whichever of the two older circulars their compliance vendor flagged first, stand to gain the most clarity from the consolidation. A single reference document also makes it easier for a bank's internal auditor, or an RBI examiner during a branch or agent inspection, to check a transaction against one set of numbers rather than cross-referencing an amendment against the circular it modified.

Why It Matters

Domestic money transfer agents and the banks and payment companies that run them need to update their compliance references to the 2026 Directions, since the earlier 2011 and 2024 circulars no longer apply even though the underlying limits are unchanged. For remitters, including migrant workers and small businesses who move money through agents rather than bank branches, the practical caps, Rs. 5,000 per cash transaction, Rs. 25,000 a month, and Rs. 50,000 for a walk-in NEFT transfer, stay as they were. The change is about which document now governs, not about new restrictions on sending money.
