Karnataka High Court Stays Circulars That Deferred Revised Minimum Wages for Contract Workers

Source: Originally reported by Bar and Bench, LiveLaw and legalsuvidha.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Three trade unions have managed to stop the Karnataka government's own departments from sitting on a wage hike the government itself announced. The Karnataka High Court has stayed a set of circulars that told local bodies and audit departments to hold back a revised minimum wage notification, in a dispute that pits the state's left hand against its right.

The State notified revised minimum wage rates for 81 scheduled employments on 22 May 2026. Rather than roll out the hike, three arms of the government, the Karnataka State Audit and Accounts Department, the Directorate of Municipal Administration, and the Rural Development and Panchayat Raj Department, issued circulars on 29 July, 10 August and 28 August directing local authorities to defer or suspend implementation for outsourced and contract staff. No court had stayed the May notification at that point, and none has since.

Three unions, the All India Trade Union Congress, the Karnataka Rajya Sarkari Khayametharara Noukarara Okkuta and the All India Central Council of Trade Unions, moved the High Court against these circulars. Their case was straightforward: a statutory wage notification cannot be suspended by executive circular, and departments lacked any legal authority to override a notification that stood unchallenged and unstayed. Justice H.T. Narendra Prasad accepted that logic for the purposes of interim relief and stayed the circulars, leaving the May notification operative while the matter proceeds to full hearing.

Running in parallel, and cutting the other way, is a separate challenge brought by employers. The Karnataka Employers' Association and others filed their own writ petition, this one attacking the May notification itself rather than the circulars that deferred it. Their argument rests on a point of statutory interpretation: that the Code on Wages, 2019 has displaced the Minimum Wages Act, 1948 as the governing framework, and that the May notification, issued under the older Act, cannot stand. Section 69 of the 2019 Code contains a savings clause whose scope is the crux of that argument.

Justice Narendra Prasad declined to grant the employers interim protection. Instead, he directed that their petitions be tagged with Writ Appeals already pending before a Division Bench, numbered 1684 and 1685 of 2026, since those appeals raise the identical question about how Section 69's savings clause interacts with the 1948 Act. Rather than have a single judge pre-empt an issue already before two judges, the court chose to let the Division Bench settle the point once, for both sets of litigation together.

The net effect, for now, is that contract and outsourced workers across Karnataka keep the benefit of the May wage hike, and the circulars that tried to put it on hold stay frozen. Employers get no parallel relief suspending the notification itself; their fate rides on how the Division Bench eventually reads the Code on Wages against the older Act. Departments that issue circulars overriding a government's own wage notification should expect short shrift from the courts unless they can point to a specific statutory power to defer implementation, something none of the three departments here were able to show.

This ruling is a reminder that administrative convenience is not law. A circular, however urgent the fiscal pressure behind it, cannot quietly undo a notification that carries the force of a statutory instrument. Local bodies and audit departments who were banking on the deferral circulars to manage payroll costs now have to budget for the revised rates with immediate effect, pending the Division Bench's eventual decision on the notification's validity. There is also a lesson here for how litigants should sequence related challenges. Rather than let two single judges reach potentially conflicting views on the same statutory question, the Court consolidated the employers' interim prayer with appeals already pending before two judges, a sensible use of judicial resources when the identical point of law, how Section 69 of the Code on Wages, 2019 interacts with the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, was going to be argued twice over otherwise. Practitioners bringing a fresh single-judge petition that overlaps with a pending Division Bench appeal on the same point should expect a similar tagging order rather than an independent ruling on the merits. The practical tension for employers in the interim is unmistakable. They must now pay the revised rates to contract and outsourced staff with no interim shield of their own, while carrying the risk that the Division Bench could later uphold their argument that the Code on Wages has displaced the older Act altogether. Until that happens, though, the May notification stands, the deferral circulars stay frozen, and workers keep the benefit of a hike the government's own departments tried, and failed, to put on hold.

Why It Matters

Government departments cannot use internal circulars to defer or suspend a statutory minimum wage notification that has not been stayed by any court. Employers, both private and public-body contractors in Karnataka, must now pay the revised May 2026 rates to outsourced and contract staff while the deeper question, whether the 2019 Code on Wages has overridden the 1948 Act, awaits a Division Bench ruling in connected appeals.
