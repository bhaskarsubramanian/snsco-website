NCLAT: Appeal Against Rejected Resolution Plan Is Non-Est Where Certified Copy Was Not Sought Within Limitation

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Technofab Engineering Limited has been under insolvency proceedings since its corporate insolvency resolution process commenced on 18 November 2022, with Atul Mittal acting as resolution professional. On 17 July 2026, the NCLT's New Delhi Bench rejected an application seeking approval of a resolution plan for the company. Anil Khandelwal, acting together with NOIDA Holdings Pvt. Ltd. as part of a consortium, appealed that rejection to the NCLAT's Principal Bench in New Delhi.

The appeal ran into a procedural obstacle before the Tribunal ever reached the merits of why the plan had been rejected. Under Rule 22(2) of the NCLAT Rules, 2016, every appeal must be accompanied by a certified copy of the order under challenge, and Section 61(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code sets a thirty-day limitation period for filing such an appeal, extendable by up to fifteen days on sufficient cause. The appellants did not apply for a certified copy of the 17 July order within that window. According to the record before the Tribunal, the application for a certified copy was made only on the sixty-sixth day after the order, well outside even the extended forty-five-day outer limit, and the appeal itself was filed on the forty-fourth day without a certified copy in hand.

The Tribunal treated this as more than a curable filing defect. It held that failing to apply for a certified copy within the limitation period renders the appeal incompetent, not merely irregular, because compliance with Rule 22(2) goes to the root of whether an appeal has been properly instituted at all. An appeal that does not meet this threshold requirement is non-est in law, meaning it is treated as if it was never filed, rather than as a defective appeal capable of being cured by a later condonation order.

That approach follows the line the Supreme Court had already set earlier in 2026 in Angelwoods Apartment Allottees Association v. M. Lalitha, which held that an appeal filed before the NCLAT without a certified copy of the NCLT's order is an incompetent appeal liable to be rejected at the threshold. The NCLAT in the Technofab matter applied that same reasoning to the consortium's appeal, dismissing the applications seeking condonation of delay and exemption from filing a certified copy, and closing the pending interlocutory applications without any order as to costs.

The practical consequence is that the Tribunal never examined whether the NCLT was right to reject the consortium's resolution plan for Technofab Engineering. The appeal was shut out on the certified-copy point before that question could be reached, leaving the NCLT's 17 July 2026 order rejecting the plan undisturbed.

For resolution applicants and other parties who intend to appeal an NCLT order under the Code, the ruling is a reminder that the thirty-day limitation clock under Section 61(2), and the fifteen-day condonable extension that follows it, run regardless of how quickly or slowly a certified copy can be obtained from the registry. An appellant who waits past that combined forty-five-day window to even apply for the certified copy risks having the entire appeal treated as never filed, with no plan-rejection merits ever reaching the Tribunal for consideration.

The ruling also reinforces that the NCLAT will not treat the certified-copy requirement as a mere formality that can be regularised once the appeal is otherwise ready for hearing. Where the Supreme Court has already described the consequence of missing it as rendering an appeal incompetent and liable to rejection at the threshold, appellate practice before the NCLAT now has a consistent answer: the clock on applying for the certified copy starts with the impugned order, not with the appellant's own convenience in preparing the appeal.

The distinction the Tribunal drew between an incompetent appeal and a merely defective one is not a technical nicety confined to this case. A defective appeal, one filed with some curable shortcoming such as a missing annexure or an unsigned affidavit, can ordinarily be set right and carried forward once the defect is removed. An incompetent appeal, on the Tribunal's reasoning, never came into existence as a valid appeal in the first place, so there is nothing left to cure once the limitation period for seeking the certified copy has run out. That difference is why the consortium's applications for condonation of delay and for exemption from filing a certified copy could not rescue the appeal: condonation presumes there is a validly instituted appeal to which further time can be granted, and the Tribunal held that no such appeal existed here to begin with.

Why It Matters

Resolution applicants and other parties appealing NCLT orders under the IBC face a hard procedural gate: the certified copy of the impugned order must be applied for within the limitation period itself, not merely before the appeal is filed. Missing that step, as the consortium did here by waiting until the sixty-sixth day, now risks the entire appeal being treated as non-est, with no chance for the Tribunal to even consider the underlying merits of a rejected resolution plan.
