CCPA Fines Flipkart Rs 10 Lakh for Listing an Unregistered 'Cyclosinone' Herbicide, Rejects Marketplace Defence

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Business Standard, Law Chakra and eng.ruralvoice.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A weed killer sold online as a 'Farmer and Gardener Approved Weed Killer' has cost Flipkart a Rs 10 lakh penalty, after the Central Consumer Protection Authority found that the chemical behind the label, Cyclosinone, does not exist in the Schedule to the Insecticides Act, 1968 at all. The Crop Care Federation of India had written to the Department of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare on 22 January 2026 flagging the listings, and the Department forwarded the complaint to the CCPA on 18 February 2026. By September the Authority had traced the same product across three marketplaces and penalised each of them separately.

The Authority's order against Flipkart Internet Private Limited records that the platform had listed, advertised and facilitated sale of Cyclosinone Herbicide to buyers who had no way of knowing that no insecticide or herbicide by that name is registered anywhere under Indian law. An unregistered agro-chemical is not a labelling technicality. The Insecticides Act exists precisely to keep untested chemical formulations away from crops and the people who eat what those crops produce, and a product that has never cleared that registration process carries no assurance about what it actually contains or does to soil, produce or the farmer handling it.

Flipkart's defence followed a pattern familiar to anyone who has watched e-commerce platforms answer for third-party listings: it said it was merely an intermediary, that the seller had uploaded the product and its claims, and that responsibility for an unregistered chemical being sold under a false approval claim belonged to that seller and not to the marketplace carrying the listing. The Authority was not persuaded. It held that Flipkart's own role in listing, advertising and facilitating the sale carried statutory due diligence obligations under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, obligations that do not evaporate merely because the seller, not the platform, physically fulfilled the order.

The Authority also leaned on Rule 4(3) of the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020, which bars e-commerce entities from engaging in unfair trade practices, whether as principal seller or as marketplace. Advertising Cyclosinone as approved by farmers and gardeners, when the Agriculture Ministry itself confirmed no such registered chemical exists, fell squarely within that bar. A marketplace that allows a seller to make an approval claim it cannot verify, and that turns out to be false, has itself made a misleading representation to the buyer who read the listing and trusted it.

Beyond the Rs 10 lakh fine, the Authority directed Flipkart to stop selling and advertising the product immediately and to carry out a self-audit of its listings to catch comparable claims before a regulator has to. Flipkart told reporters it had already removed the listings and tightened its keyword-based blocking in response. Amazon received an identical Rs 10 lakh penalty on the same facts, while JioMart was fined Rs 5 lakh, the difference apparently reflecting the scale or duration of each platform's listings, though the Authority's orders on Amazon and JioMart were issued separately from the one against Flipkart.

What makes the order worth watching beyond its facts is the message it sends to every marketplace carrying agricultural inputs. Pesticides and herbicides sit in the smaller set of consumer products where a false claim does not just mislead a buyer about value for money, it can damage a season's crop or expose a farmer and farmhands to a chemical nobody has tested for safety. The CCPA treated the marketplace's listing and advertising function as inseparable from the seller's underlying fraud, rather than as a neutral pipe through which somebody else's claim passed unexamined.

For agro-chemical sellers and the platforms that carry them, the order leaves little room to argue that a marketplace's obligations end at taking down a listing once a complaint lands. The CCPA expects verification before the claim goes live, not clean-up after a trade body complains. Any seller advertising a pesticide, herbicide or fungicide online would do well to check the Schedule to the Insecticides Act before using words like 'approved' in a product title, since a regulator is now reading that word literally and fining the platform that let it stand.

Why It Matters

The order confirms that an e-commerce marketplace cannot treat a seller's product claims as someone else's problem once a trade body complains; the CCPA will treat listing and advertising an unregistered agro-chemical as the platform's own unfair trade practice under the Consumer Protection Act, exposing marketplaces to direct penalties for third-party agricultural product claims they never independently verified before publishing them to buyers.
