Pending Maintenance Case Does Not Shift a Domestic Violence Complaint to Family Court: P&H HC

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw and Verdictum. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A husband facing both a domestic violence complaint and a maintenance claim from his wife in Gurugram tried a familiar tactic: move one proceeding into the forum already hearing the other, hoping the Family Court dealing with maintenance would absorb the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act case as well. The Punjab and Haryana High Court has shut that door, at least on the ground he argued.

The wife had filed a complaint under Section 12 of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 before the Judicial Magistrate First Class, Gurugram, seeking protection and other reliefs available under that Act. Separately, a maintenance petition between the same two parties was already pending before the Family Court, Gurugram. The husband applied to have the Section 12 complaint moved out of the Magistrate's court and clubbed with the maintenance case before the Family Court, arguing that since both proceedings involved the same parties and overlapping facts, one forum should hear both.

Justice Mandeep Pannu rejected that application. The order holds that the mere pendency of a maintenance petition before a Family Court does not, by itself, confer jurisdiction on that Family Court to adjudicate a separate application under Section 12 of the Domestic Violence Act. The two proceedings, even when they arise between the same spouses and touch overlapping facts, remain distinct creatures of the statutes that create them, and a party cannot engineer a transfer simply by pointing to the other case's forum.

The Court relied on the settled position that a Family Court's jurisdiction is defined by the Family Courts Act, 1984 and does not automatically extend to every proceeding a married couple happens to be litigating against each other. A maintenance petition under that framework is one thing; an independent application under Section 12 of the Domestic Violence Act, which carries its own scheme of reliefs including protection orders, residence orders and compensation, is another, created by a different statute with its own forum and its own procedure under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act Rules. The order notes that the Supreme Court has already clarified that a Family Court's jurisdiction over maintenance proceedings does not, without more, extend to adjudicating a standalone Section 12 application, so pendency of one cannot be read as implicitly widening the other court's remit.

Dismissing the husband's plea, the Court left the domestic violence proceeding where the wife had filed it, before the Judicial Magistrate First Class, Gurugram, to continue on its own track regardless of how the maintenance case before the Family Court proceeds. The two matters will now run in parallel, as the wife had originally structured them, rather than being merged at the husband's request.

The ruling matters for how family disputes actually get litigated in Gurugram and across the wider National Capital Region, where it is increasingly common for a wife to file a Domestic Violence Act complaint before a Magistrate while also pursuing maintenance before the Family Court, often alongside a parallel matrimonial case. Respondents in that position frequently try to consolidate everything into a single forum, calculating that one court handling all the overlapping facts works to their advantage, whether on timing, evidence, or simply the convenience of fighting one battle instead of three. This order confirms that such consolidation is not available merely because the proceedings share parties and facts; a party seeking to move a Domestic Violence Act case out of the Magistrate's court needs a ground rooted in that Act's own transfer provisions, not the existence of a related maintenance case elsewhere.

For practitioners advising either side in Gurugram's family courts, the order is a useful marker on a point that recurs often in practice: pendency of a connected proceeding before a different forum is not, without more, a jurisdictional hook. A spouse defending a Section 12 complaint will need to meet it in the Magistrate's court on its own merits, even while a connected maintenance dispute continues before the Family Court down the corridor.

Why It Matters

Spouses litigating in Gurugram and across the National Capital Region frequently run a Domestic Violence Act complaint before a Magistrate alongside a maintenance petition before the Family Court, and one side often tries to merge the two into a single forum. This ruling confirms that pendency of a maintenance case before the Family Court does not, by itself, give that court jurisdiction over a separate Section 12 application. Litigants and counsel on both sides now have clear authority that each proceeding must be fought on its own statutory footing rather than consolidated by a transfer application.
