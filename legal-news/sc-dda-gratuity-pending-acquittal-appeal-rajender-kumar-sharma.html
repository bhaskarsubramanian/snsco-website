Pending Appeal Against Acquittal Cannot Hold Back an Employee's Gratuity: Supreme Court

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Law Trend and courtbook.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Two employees of the Delhi Development Authority spent years waiting for a terminal benefit that the DDA would not release, and on 16 November the Supreme Court told the DDA why it had no business holding onto the money. Rajender Kumar Sharma had joined the DDA in 1984 as a Lower Division Clerk and later worked as an Assistant Field Investigator. He and a colleague were tried in a corruption case and acquitted. The State chose to appeal that acquittal. No court stayed it. The DDA treated the pending appeal as reason enough to keep the gratuity in its account.

The employees went to the Central Administrative Tribunal, which ordered the gratuity released. The DDA took the fight to the Delhi High Court, which upheld the Tribunal's order on 25 March 2026. Still unwilling to pay, the DDA appealed to the Supreme Court. The Bench of Justices Prashant Kumar Mishra and Shree Chandrashekhar dismissed the DDA's appeals and left the release order undisturbed, with gratuity to be paid along with interest.

The DDA's argument rested on a simple proposition: an acquittal that the State is still contesting on appeal is not a final acquittal, so the employer should be allowed to wait and see. The Court rejected that logic. An acquittal is an acquittal until a superior court actually reverses it. A pending appeal, without an order staying the acquittal, does not revive a disciplinary bar that the acquittal itself has already lifted. If the law worked the other way, an employer could withhold a retiree's dues indefinitely simply by keeping an appeal alive on paper, regardless of how weak it is or how long it drags on.

The Court leaned on its earlier decision in Kishan Lal Taneja, which had taken the same position on withheld retirement benefits after an acquittal. The DDA had argued that Kishan Lal Taneja was wrongly decided and should not be followed, calling it per incuriam. The Bench did not accept that submission. The DDA also tried to draw support from the Court's decision in Bikram Chand Rana, 2026 INSC 326, but the Bench found that ruling dealt with a different situation and did not help the DDA's case here.

The release the Court ordered is not unconditional. The employees must give an undertaking that if the pending criminal proceedings eventually go against them and their acquittal is overturned, they will repay the gratuity with interest. That condition is the Court's way of balancing two things that often pull against each other in service law: an employee's statutory right to be paid what is due on retirement, and an employer's legitimate worry about paying out money to someone who might later be found guilty. The undertaking lets the money move now while keeping a safety net in place for the remote possibility that the appellate court reverses the acquittal.

For employers, government and private, this ruling draws a clear line around how far a pending appeal can be used as a shield. Gratuity under the Payment of Gratuity Act, and under equivalent service rules for government staff, is not a discretionary reward that management can sit on while litigation continues elsewhere. It becomes due on retirement, superannuation or termination, subject to specific statutory grounds for forfeiture, and a disciplinary or criminal case that has already ended in acquittal is not one of those grounds merely because the State has chosen to appeal. An employer that wants to keep withholding gratuity after an acquittal needs an actual stay of that acquittal, not just a pending appeal sitting on a cause list.

The practical lesson for HR and legal teams handling retiral dues is to track the acquittal itself, not the status of any appeal against it. Once an employee is acquitted and there is no order staying that acquittal, the clock for paying gratuity starts running, and resisting payment on the ground that an appeal is pending is likely to end the way it did for the DDA here, in a dismissed appeal and an order to pay with interest.

Why It Matters

Government bodies and large employers often hold back retiral dues from employees facing or acquitted in criminal cases, citing pending appeals as cover. This ruling confirms that an acquittal takes effect immediately unless a court actually stays it, and a pending appeal against that acquittal, by itself, is not a lawful ground to withhold gratuity. Employers across sectors should review any gratuity files held back on this basis, since continuing to withhold payment without an actual stay now carries a real risk of being ordered to pay with interest.
