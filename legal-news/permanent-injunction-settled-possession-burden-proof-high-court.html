A Plaintiff Seeking a Permanent Injunction Must First Establish Settled, Lawful Possession, Not Merely Assert It: High Court

Source: As reported in civil-law commentary and case digests through 2026. This article has been independently researched and written by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes, summarising the general legal position rather than reproducing any single report. Readers should verify current developments against the underlying judgment and primary sources.

A suit for permanent injunction, seeking to restrain another party from interfering with the plaintiff's possession of immovable property, is one of the most commonly filed civil suits involving property in India, frequently used in boundary disputes, disputes between family members over a shared property, and disputes with a neighbour or an encroaching third party. Its apparent simplicity, however, conceals a threshold requirement that is often the actual battleground of such suits.

A High Court has reiterated the settled principle that a plaintiff seeking a permanent injunction to protect possession must first affirmatively establish that they were in settled, lawful possession of the property as of the date the suit was filed. An injunction is a remedy to protect existing possession, not a means of asserting or recovering a right to possession. A plaintiff who is not actually in possession, or whose possession is itself of a disputed, precarious, or wrongful character, is not entitled to an injunction and must instead pursue a suit for possession, and establish their title, rather than simply their occupation.

The Court observed that this distinction is frequently blurred in practice, particularly in family and co-ownership disputes where possession has shifted informally between family members over time, and where a party who has recently been excluded from a property files for an injunction rather than the more demanding suit for possession that their actual circumstances require. Filing the wrong relief can result in the suit being dismissed even where the underlying grievance may otherwise have merit through a differently framed suit.

The Court also reiterated the standard three-fold test applicable at the interim stage (prima facie case, balance of convenience and irreparable injury), and cautioned that courts should be circumspect about granting an ex-parte interim injunction disturbing a settled state of affairs without first hearing the party against whom the injunction is sought, except in genuinely urgent circumstances.

Why It Matters

A party considering an injunction suit to protect a property should first assess candidly whether they are actually in settled possession. If possession is genuinely disputed or has recently changed hands, a suit for possession, rather than an injunction alone, is likely to be the more appropriate and effective relief.
