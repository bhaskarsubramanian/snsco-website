Agreement to Sell Without a Registered Deed Gives No Title, Rules Supreme Court

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon, Verdictum, advocatekhoj.com and supremecourtcases.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Plot No. 8 in Delhi's Friends Colony measures a little over 4,132 square yards, the sort of prime holding that keeps litigation alive across generations. The Supreme Court finally closed one such fight on October 16, 2026, dismissing a decades-old suit for possession because the people suing for the plot had only ever held an agreement to sell, never a registered sale deed. A bench of Justice Aravind Kumar and Justice Vipul M. Pancholi rejected the appeal filed by Rano Singh and the other legal heirs of the original claimant, confirming that their claim had failed both on title and on limitation.

The appellants' case before the trial court was straightforward on its face. They said their predecessor had entered into an agreement to sell for the plot, had been put in possession under that agreement, and could therefore resist being thrown out by invoking Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, the provision that protects a transferee in possession against the transferor's own breach of contract. What complicated matters was that the respondents held a registered sale deed to the same property, a document the appellants had never sought to challenge, set aside, or have declared void. They simply asked the court for a decree of possession, as though the question of who actually owned the plot was not really in dispute.

The trial court took a mixed view. It held the suit was maintainable and filed within time, but still dismissed it, finding that the appellants were estopped from pressing their claim. The matter went up to the Delhi High Court, which affirmed the dismissal but on a different footing. The High Court held the suit was barred by limitation and separately rejected the plea of part-performance under Section 53A, leaving the appellants with nothing to show for either ground.

Before the Supreme Court, the central question was whether a suit confined to possession could survive when a registered sale deed cast a serious and specific cloud over the plaintiffs' claim of title. The Court held that it could not. Once the defendants produced a registered conveyance in respect of the very plot the plaintiffs claimed to possess under an agreement to sell, the plaintiffs could no longer treat their suit as one of possession simpliciter. A plaintiff who faces that kind of challenge to title has to ask for a declaration, or for cancellation of the instrument standing in the way, rather than skip past it and seek only possession. Filing the narrower suit and hoping the title question would never be examined was not open to the appellants on these facts.

On the substance of the title claim itself, the Court reiterated settled law that bears repeating because litigants keep testing it anyway: an agreement to sell, by itself, transfers no interest in immovable property. It creates a right to obtain a sale deed and, where possession has followed, a shield against dispossession under Section 53A in narrow circumstances, but it does not make the agreement holder the owner. Only a registered sale deed executed and registered under the Registration Act, 1908 passes title. The appellants' entire case rested on treating the agreement as though it already amounted to ownership, and the Court declined to indulge that reading.

The limitation finding closed off whatever room remained. The Court examined the suit against Articles 54, 59 and 64 of the Limitation Act, 1963, covering suits for specific performance, for cancellation of an instrument, and for possession based on a prior possessory title respectively, and held that the claim was barred under each of them. Even taking the appellants' own version of when they learned of the adverse sale deed, too much time had passed before they came to court. There was no fresh starting point the appellants could point to that would bring their suit within time.

The appeal was accordingly dismissed, with no order as to costs, leaving the respondents' registered title and possession undisturbed. For a dispute that had already run through a trial court and a High Court before reaching the Supreme Court, the outcome turns on two points litigators meet constantly in property work: a possession suit cannot be used to dodge a live title dispute, and an agreement to sell is a promise to convey, not a conveyance. Buyers who pay under an agreement and sit on it for years without pursuing a registered deed or a suit for specific performance are the ones who end up losing on limitation, as these appellants did.

The ruling also reads as a caution against drafting possession suits too narrowly to avoid confronting an opposing title document. Where the record already contains a registered deed that contradicts the plaintiff's claim, courts will treat the dispute as one over title whether or not the plaint says so, and will expect the plaintiff to have sought the appropriate declaratory relief within time.

Why It Matters

The judgment reaffirms two basics that recur in Indian property litigation: an unregistered agreement to sell never substitutes for a registered conveyance, and a possession suit cannot survive by ignoring a registered deed that clouds the plaintiff's title. Purchasers under long-pending agreements to sell should treat this as a reminder to pursue specific performance or a declaratory suit promptly, rather than wait and later seek bare possession once a competing registered deed surfaces.
