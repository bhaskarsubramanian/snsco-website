Delhi HC Fines Delhi Government Rs 1 Lakh for Stalling Company's Industrial Plot Claim Since 2009

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Verdictum and courtbook.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A shoe manufacturer that has been chasing an alternative industrial plot from the Delhi government since 1996 has finally secured something concrete from the Delhi High Court: a costs order against the government itself, with directions that the amount be recovered from the salaries of the officers responsible for the delay. Justice Amit Mahajan, deciding a writ petition filed by Central Footwear Co., called the authorities' conduct deplorable and imposed Rs 1 lakh in costs on the Government of NCT of Delhi, payable to the Delhi High Court Legal Services Committee within six weeks.

The company's grievance goes back three decades. Its unit stood at Plot No. 19, Swarn Park, Mundka, an area the government later found to be non-conforming for industrial use. Under the Relocation Scheme of 1996, units in non-conforming areas became eligible for an alternative plot elsewhere, and Central Footwear applied accordingly. Its initial claim was rejected on the footing that the unit was actually situated in a conforming area, a finding the company disputed from the outset.

That rejection did not survive scrutiny. In March 2009, the High Court quashed the order rejecting the company's claim and directed the authorities to decide the application afresh and expeditiously. Seventeen years on, that direction had still not been honoured in any final sense, prompting the company to return to court yet again. The petition before Justice Mahajan was, by the court's own description, part of a fourth round of litigation over the same unresolved claim.

The judgment does not dwell on fresh legal questions. There was no dispute left to adjudicate about whether the company was entitled to consideration under the Relocation Scheme; that had been settled back in 2009. What the court confronted instead was a government machinery that had simply not acted on its own earlier order, leaving a commercial claimant to make repeated trips to the High Court to get what had already been decided in its favour.

Justice Mahajan's order reflects that frustration in its structure as much as its language. Costs against a government department are not unusual in Delhi High Court writ practice, but directing that the amount be recovered from the salaries of the officers who allowed the matter to drift is a sharper remedy, aimed at the individuals responsible rather than letting the burden fall on the public exchequer in the ordinary way. The matter has been listed again for February 2027, keeping the file open rather than closing it on the costs order alone.

What makes the delay notable is that none of the usual excuses for slow government action applied. There was no pending appeal against the 2009 order, no stay, and no fresh legal objection raised by the authorities that might explain fifteen years of inaction. The file had simply not moved. Each time the company approached the court again, it was not asking for a new right to be recognised; it was asking the court to make the authorities do what they had already been told to do, which is a different and in some ways more frustrating kind of litigation to have to run. That pattern is familiar to anyone who has dealt with industrial relocation claims against Delhi's municipal and NCT authorities, where applicants can spend years moving between departments without a final decision, only to end up back in the High Court asking it to enforce its own earlier directions.

For businesses dealing with Delhi's industrial relocation and allotment framework, the case illustrates a familiar problem: a favourable order from a court does not always translate into compliance, and an applicant may need to litigate the same entitlement more than once before an authority actually acts. The repeated rounds here, stretching from the original 1996 application through a 2009 quashing order to a fourth writ petition in 2024, show how long such claims can remain open even after the legal question is no longer contested.

The order also signals how Delhi courts are increasingly willing to make non-compliance personally costly for the officials involved, rather than treating repeated government delay as a routine cost of doing business with the state. Whether that translates into faster resolution of the underlying plot claim will depend on what happens before the next listed date, but the immediate effect is to put a price, however modest, on official inertia.

Why It Matters

Businesses operating in Delhi's industrial areas that have been found non-conforming often wait years for relocation under government schemes, even after a court has already ruled in their favour once. This order shows that courts are willing to penalise the officials responsible for inaction personally, through salary recovery, rather than treating repeated non-compliance with a prior court order as a cost-free option for a government department.
