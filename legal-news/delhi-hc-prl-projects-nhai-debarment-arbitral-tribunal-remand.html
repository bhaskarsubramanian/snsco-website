Delhi HC Sends Contractor Back To Arbitral Tribunal Over NHAI Debarment's Reach On Other Authorities

Source: Originally reported by delhihighcourt.nic.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A contractor fighting a debarment order on two fronts at once, before an arbitral tribunal and before the courts supervising it, found the Delhi High Court unwilling to short-circuit either process. PRL Projects and Infrastructure Ltd. had approached the High Court under Section 37(2)(b) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, challenging an arbitral tribunal order of 19 September 2026 that had given it only partial interim protection against a debarment order dated 6 May 2026.

The debarment order's effect, as the arbitral tribunal's order described it, reached the company's standing before three authorities: the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, the National Highways Authority of India, and the National Highways and Infrastructure Development Corporation Limited. The tribunal had stayed the debarment's operation only in respect of authorities other than these three, leaving the bar intact for MoRTH, NHAI and NHIDCL projects while it waited to see how a connected appeal played out. That connected matter, FAO(OS)(COMM) 171/2026, titled NCC Limited v. Airports Authority of India, raised an overlapping question about the scope of interim relief against a debarment order, and the tribunal had expressly kept the door open for either side to seek a variation of its order once that appeal was decided.

PRL Projects took the tribunal's partial order itself to the Delhi High Court rather than waiting. Justice Om Prakash Shukla, hearing the appeal on 17 November 2026 (the matter having been listed a day after the scheduled 16 November date, which a Delhi High Court notification of 11 November had declared a holiday), noted that the NCC Limited appeal the tribunal had been waiting on was disposed of on 28 October 2026. That meant the very development the tribunal's order had been built around had already occurred by the time the High Court came to hear PRL Projects' challenge.

Rather than deciding the merits of the debarment's scope itself, the Court held that the appellant should first use the liberty the tribunal's own order had already given it, to apply for modification or variation of the partial relief now that the NCC Limited appeal had concluded. The Court disposed of the Section 37 appeal along with the pending applications, directing that PRL Projects file its application before the arbitral tribunal within one week, and asking the tribunal to decide that application within four weeks, with a further instruction to both sides against taking unnecessary adjournments.

The order is narrow by design. It does not rule on whether the original debarment of 6 May 2026 was justified, nor on how far interim protection against such a debarment should extend; it simply declines to let a High Court appeal leapfrog a tribunal process that still has an unresolved application sitting within its own four corners, now that the obstacle the tribunal cited for not deciding it, the pending NCC Limited appeal, has fallen away.

For contractors facing debarment from government infrastructure authorities while an arbitration over the underlying contract or the debarment itself is live, the order is a reminder that an interim order granting only partial relief, with liberty reserved to revisit it on a changed circumstance, is ordinarily meant to be revisited before that same tribunal rather than appealed afresh once the changed circumstance arrives. A Section 37 appeal against an arbitral tribunal's interim order succeeds in disturbing that order only where the tribunal itself has failed to account for the change; here, since the tribunal had expressly anticipated it and reserved liberty to deal with it, the High Court found nothing left for it to do but send the parties back.

The practical clock now runs on the four-week timeline the High Court has set for the tribunal, with PRL Projects required to file its fresh application within a week of the 17 November order. How the tribunal rules on the application, and specifically whether it extends interim protection to cover MoRTH, NHAI and NHIDCL projects as well, will determine whether the debarment continues to bar the contractor from highway and infrastructure tenders run by those three authorities while the underlying arbitration proceeds.

Why It Matters

Debarment from bidding on National Highways Authority of India and Ministry of Road Transport and Highways projects can shut a contractor out of a large share of India's infrastructure pipeline while the underlying dispute is still being arbitrated. This order shows that a tribunal's partial interim relief, reserved for revisiting once a named contingency resolves, will usually have to be revisited at the tribunal first, not reopened by a fresh Section 37 appeal, which matters for how infrastructure contractors time their challenges to debarment orders during live arbitrations.
