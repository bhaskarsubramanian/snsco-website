Allahabad HC Quashes GST Notice Sent to RedBus After Its Merger Into MakeMyTrip

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon, Taxscan, TaxGuru and jurishour.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

RedBus India Private Limited stopped existing as a separate company on 1 February 2026, when it merged into MakeMyTrip (India) Private Limited. Four months later, on 12 June 2026, MakeMyTrip told the GST department about the merger in its reply to a show cause notice the department had issued under Section 73 of the CGST Act for the financial year 2021-22. The department went ahead anyway. On 3 August 2026, it issued a fresh notice in Form GST DRC-01, raising the same financial-year demand once more, and addressed it to RedBus India Private Limited, a company that by then had been absorbed into MakeMyTrip for a year and a half.

MakeMyTrip filed a writ petition before the Lucknow Bench of the Allahabad High Court under Article 226, asking the court to quash the notice and declare the proceedings void from the start. Its argument was straightforward: once a company merges into another with the sanction of the tribunal, it stops being a legal person capable of being served notice or made liable to answer a demand. Any notice for that period has to go to the entity that survives the merger, in this case MakeMyTrip, not to the name on the old GST registration.

A bench of Justice Shekhar B. Saraf and Justice Amitabh Kumar Rai agreed. The court recorded that the Department did not dispute the merger or the date it took effect, and that MakeMyTrip had flagged the merger to the Department months before the fresh notice went out. Given that, the bench said it was at 'consensus ad idem' with the petitioner's submission that the notice should have named the amalgamated company. The show cause notice dated 3 August 2026 was quashed and set aside, and the writ petition was disposed of on that basis.

The court did not shut the door on the Department's underlying demand for FY 2021-22. It gave the Department liberty to proceed in accordance with law, which in practice means issuing a fresh notice, this time correctly addressed to MakeMyTrip India Private Limited as RedBus's successor. The quashing is procedural, not a verdict on whether tax is actually owed for that year. What it decides is who the Department has to proceed against, and that a notice sent to a company that has already merged away cannot be cured after the fact by treating the surviving company as if it had somehow been served.

This is not a novel proposition. Courts have said much the same thing before in both income tax and GST matters: a notice issued to an entity that has ceased to exist by amalgamation is issued to a non-person, and a non-person cannot be made to answer it, however closely connected it once was to the business the Department is chasing. Tax officers deal with thousands of registrations and often work off the name on file rather than checking whether that name still has a legal existence. Mergers, especially of subsidiaries into their parent, do not always get flagged quickly enough across every department database, and GST registrations in particular can lag behind a merger that has already been approved by the tribunal and recorded with the Registrar of Companies.

For a corporate group running an amalgamation, the practical lesson is to put the change in the Department's hands in writing, on the record, and early, rather than assuming internal reporting will catch up with the GST portal on its own. MakeMyTrip had done exactly that in June, months before the fresh notice landed in August, and that paper trail is what let the court find the point in its favour without much difficulty. A group that cannot show it told the Department, or that only raises the amalgamation defence for the first time at the hearing, may find a court less willing to treat the notice as a nullity rather than send it back for correction on narrower grounds.

The order also leaves open what happens once a corrected notice does reach MakeMyTrip. The company still has to answer the substance of the FY 2021-22 demand; it has only won the right to be sued, so to speak, under its own name rather than its absorbed subsidiary's. Groups that have gone through, or are going through, a merger of this kind should expect the Department to come back once it has fixed the addressee, and should keep the records of the old entity's filings in order rather than treat the quashing of one notice as the end of the matter.

Why It Matters

Companies that absorb subsidiaries through merger often keep finding stray tax notices addressed to the entity that no longer exists. This ruling confirms that such notices can be quashed outright rather than merely corrected, but only buys time, not immunity, since the department can simply reissue the notice to the real successor. Groups mid-merger should document their amalgamation disclosures to tax authorities carefully and promptly.
