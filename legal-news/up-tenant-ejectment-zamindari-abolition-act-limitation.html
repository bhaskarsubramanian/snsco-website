Tenant's Long Cultivating Possession Defeats Ejectment Claim: Revenue Court Applies Statutory Bar Under Tenancy Legislation

Source: As reported in state revenue-bar circulars in February 2026. This article has been independently researched and written by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes and is not a reproduction of any single report.

A revenue court has dismissed an ejectment suit filed by landowner Devendra Singh Tomar against agricultural cultivator Bansi Lal, who had been in continuous cultivating possession of the disputed plot for over two decades, holding that the tenancy protections conferred by the state's tenancy legislation barred the landowner's attempt to treat him as a mere licensee liable to summary eviction.

The landowner had argued that Bansi Lal was permitted to cultivate the land purely as a seasonal arrangement renewed informally each year, and that no formal tenancy was ever created. The revenue court, however, examined revenue records including khasra entries recording the cultivator's possession across multiple consecutive agricultural years, and held that such continuous, unbroken cultivating possession — irrespective of the absence of a written tenancy agreement — is sufficient to attract statutory tenancy protection once the relevant qualifying period has elapsed.

The court emphasised that state tenancy legislation of this kind is protective and remedial in character, intended to prevent landowners from using the absence of a formal written lease to defeat the accrued rights of tenants who have genuinely cultivated the land season after season. It held that the burden lay on the landowner to affirmatively prove that possession was permissive and interrupted each season, which he had failed to discharge.

The ejectment suit was accordingly dismissed, with the court noting that the landowner's remedy, if any, lay in pursuing resumption proceedings strictly in accordance with the statutory grounds and procedure prescribed for resumption of tenanted land, rather than by way of an ordinary ejectment suit.

Why It Matters

Landowners who allow cultivators to work agricultural land year after year without a written arrangement should be aware that continuous cultivating possession can itself give rise to statutory tenancy rights under state tenancy legislation — and that terminating such an arrangement generally requires following the specific resumption procedure the statute prescribes, not an ordinary suit for ejectment.
