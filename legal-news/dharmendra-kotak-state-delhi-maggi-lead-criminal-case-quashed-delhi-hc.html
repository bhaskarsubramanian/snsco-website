Delhi HC Quashes 11-Year-Old Maggi Lead Prosecution, Says Fresh CFTRI Tests Eroded the Case

Source: Originally reported by Bar and Bench, theprint.in, LawBeat and lawlex.org. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Eleven years after Food Safety Officers in Delhi pulled packets of Maggi noodles off retail shelves for testing, the criminal case built on those samples has finally come to an end. The Delhi High Court has quashed the complaints, the summoning orders issued in 2015 and 2016, and all proceedings that followed against Nestle India's distributors and other persons connected with the manufacture and sale of the product, holding that making them stand trial any longer would amount to an abuse of the process of the court.

The prosecution traced back to May 2015, when Food Safety Officers collected Maggi samples in Delhi as part of the nationwide scare that followed similar findings in other states. The Food Analyst's reports said the lead content in the masala tastemaker sachet exceeded the permissible limit of 2.5 parts per million under food safety regulations, and a separate complaint alleged misbranding over the product's 'No Added MSG' label. On the strength of those reports, criminal complaints were filed and summoning orders issued against the petitioners, who approached the High Court years later under Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, invoking the Court's inherent power to prevent the misuse of the criminal process.

What changed the picture, and what the Court treated as decisive, was what happened to the scientific foundation of the original Food Analyst reports in the intervening years. The Supreme Court, hearing the broader Maggi litigation, had directed fresh testing of the product by CSIR-Central Food Technological Research Institute in Mysore under its own supervision, a step meant to settle the underlying safety question independently of the original state-level sampling. Those CFTRI reports found the relevant parameters, including lead content, within permissible limits, directly at odds with what the original Delhi samples had shown.

Justice Madhu Jain's order records that the State could point to no independent evidence of adulteration beyond the original Food Analyst reports whose reliability had, by the time of the hearing, been substantially undermined by the later referral testing conducted under the Supreme Court's own supervision. Continuing a prosecution on reports whose scientific basis has been superseded by a court-ordered retest with a contrary finding, the judgment holds, no longer serves any purpose the criminal law is meant to serve, and forcing the petitioners through a trial on that foundation would be oppressive rather than a genuine vindication of food safety standards.

The ruling closes only the Delhi prosecutions, and it does so on the particular procedural history of this case rather than as a general finding that Maggi was always safe or that the original 2015 sampling was conducted improperly. Other proceedings arising from the same episode, in other states and before other fora, are not automatically disposed of by this order, though each will face a similar question about how much weight a Food Analyst report can retain once a court-supervised referral test has produced the opposite result on the same parameter.

For food business operators who have spent years defending prosecutions launched on the back of an initial adverse Food Analyst report, the order is a useful illustration of how a later, more authoritative test result can be deployed to end a prosecution rather than merely to argue the merits at trial. Section 482 relief of this kind depends heavily on timing and on being able to show the Court that the scientific basis for the original complaint has genuinely eroded, not merely that a later test produced a more favourable number that the accused would like weighed in their favour.

The decade the case took to reach this point is itself a point for regulators and prosecutors alike. A food safety prosecution that outlives the shelf life of its own evidentiary basis by a decade serves neither the consumer interest the Food Safety and Standards Act is meant to protect nor the accused's right to a prosecution concluded within a reasonable time, and the Delhi High Court's order reads, in substance, as much a comment on that delay as it is a ruling on the lead content itself.

Why It Matters

Food business operators facing a prosecution built on a single adverse laboratory report now have a concrete precedent for using a later, court-supervised referral test with a contrary finding to seek quashing under Section 482 rather than waiting out a full trial, while regulators are reminded that a prosecution's scientific foundation can be overtaken by subsequent testing ordered in connected proceedings years after the original complaint was filed.
