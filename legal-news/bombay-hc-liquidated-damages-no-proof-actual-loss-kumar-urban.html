Bombay HC: A Developer Need Not Prove Actual Loss to Recover Liquidated Damages for Delay

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and legaldeli.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A redevelopment project that ran well behind schedule has cost its developer a confirmed arbitral award running into crores, after the Bombay High Court refused to disturb a sole arbitrator's decision to hold Kumar Urban Development Private Limited to the liquidated damages clause in its agreement with the Jal Ratn Deep Co-operative Housing Society. Justice Aarti Sathe dismissed the developer's challenge under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 on October 23, 2026, in Commercial Arbitration Petition No. 443 of 2017.

The underlying dispute traced back to delay in completing redevelopment work the developer had undertaken for the society. The arbitration agreement between the parties fixed liquidated damages for delay at Rs 10 lakh per week, and the sole arbitrator applied that rate across 62 weeks of delay to arrive at a gross figure of Rs 6.20 crore. Against that gross amount, the arbitrator set off Rs 1.10 crore the developer had already paid toward security deposits and a further Rs 2.42 crore paid as hardship compensation to the society's members, leaving a net amount, together with interest, that the developer was directed to pay.

Before the High Court, the developer's central argument rested on Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. It contended that a claimant seeking liquidated damages must still show actual loss or legal injury flowing from the breach, and that the arbitrator had awarded the full contractual rate without any such proof. The developer also pointed to two factual circumstances it said undercut any real loss to the society: the members whose flats were being redeveloped had not yet vacated their existing premises during much of the delay period, and the project itself had benefited from an increase in floor space index that, on the developer's telling, left the society better off rather than worse off.

Justice Sathe rejected that framing. The settled position under Section 74, as the court applied it, does not require a claimant to quantify its loss to the rupee before a liquidated damages clause can operate; where the nature of the loss is such that it is difficult to assess in precise monetary terms, as delay in handing over redeveloped housing typically is, the contractually stipulated rate itself can serve as a reasonable measure of compensation without separate proof of the exact loss suffered. The arbitrator's approach, applying the agreed weekly rate across the actual period of delay and crediting the developer for amounts already paid, fell squarely within that permissible method rather than outside it.

The court also declined to treat the developer's two factual points as undermining the award. Whether members had fully vacated their flats and whether an enhanced floor space index conferred some separate benefit on the society went to the weight of evidence before the arbitrator, not to any error of law or patent illegality that a court exercising the narrow jurisdiction under Section 34 could revisit. An arbitrator's assessment of such factual matters, once arrived at on a plausible reading of the record, is not something a Section 34 court reopens merely because the losing party would have weighed the same facts differently.

Taken together, the ruling is a reminder of how narrow the Section 34 lens remains even when the amount at stake is substantial. Developers and other parties bound by liquidated damages clauses in redevelopment and construction agreements often resist enforcement by arguing that the society or purchaser suffered no quantifiable loss, hoping a court will read that absence of quantified loss as a reason to cut down the award. This judgment forecloses that argument wherever the clause fixes damages for a type of delay whose real-world cost, in disrupted housing and prolonged uncertainty for residents, resists precise calculation in the first place.

For housing societies and other counterparties negotiating redevelopment agreements, the judgment also highlights the practical value of a clearly drafted liquidated damages clause pegged to a weekly or monthly rate. Once such a clause is in place and delay is established, the society does not carry the burden of separately proving what that delay cost its members in rupee terms; the agreed rate does the work, subject only to adjustment for amounts already paid, as happened here with the security deposit and hardship compensation credits.

Why It Matters

Developers facing liquidated damages claims for delayed redevelopment routinely argue that the claimant must independently prove actual monetary loss before the contractual rate can be enforced. This ruling confirms that where the loss from delay is inherently difficult to quantify, as with disrupted housing arrangements, the liquidated damages clause itself can be enforced without separate proof of loss, and that factual disputes over mitigating circumstances, such as members not yet having vacated or incidental project benefits, do not open an arbitral award to interference under the narrow scope of Section 34.
