Telangana High Court Orders Housing Board to Register Flat Sale Deeds Within 10 Days, No Penal Interest Conditions

Source: Originally reported by telanganatoday.com and deccanchronicle.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A batch of flat purchasers had gone to the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission complaining that the Telangana State Housing Board was sitting on their completed purchases, refusing to register sale deeds in their favour despite the flats being ready. The NCDRC agreed with them. In an order dated 19 June 2025, it directed the Board to go ahead and execute the sale deeds.

Instead of complying on those terms, the Board came to the Telangana High Court with a writ petition challenging the NCDRC's order, while telling the Commission in the same breath that it would carry out the direction anyway. That combination, resisting the order on one hand while repeatedly assuring compliance on the other, is what the High Court was asked to untangle when the writ petition came up for hearing. Purchasers, caught between an order they had already won and a Board that would neither comply nor formally abandon its challenge, were left without registered title to flats they had otherwise paid for.

The Board's own stated position gave the Court something concrete to work with. It had told the NCDRC it would execute the sale deeds once purchasers paid the full consideration fixed for each flat, a figure the Board put at Rs. 47.85 lakh per unit. If that was genuinely the Board's position, there was no obvious reason for registration to wait on the outcome of the writ petition, particularly once purchasers were prepared to pay that amount.

Taking the Board at its own word, the High Court, in its order dated 28 October 2026, directed it to execute the sale deeds within ten days, and to do so without insisting on any adjustments and without demanding penal interest as a precondition. The Court tied the sale deed execution to payment of the fixed consideration by the purchasers, not to resolution of the Board's separate dispute over additional charges. That distinction kept the order narrow: it did not require the Board to give up its penal interest claim, only to stop treating that unresolved claim as a reason to withhold registration altogether.

What the order did not resolve is the penal interest claim itself. The Board maintains that purchasers owe penal interest under the terms of their original agreement for the delay in completing the purchase, a position purchasers dispute, arguing that any interest calculation has to account for adjustments in their favour. Press reports on the figures involved do not agree with each other closely enough to state a precise rate or amount here. The High Court left that dispute untouched for the moment, directing instead that the operation of the NCDRC's June 2025 order would remain suspended once the sale deeds were executed on the agreed terms, and posting the matter for further hearing on 18 November 2026.

The structure of the order is itself the point for anyone tracking how courts handle a public housing authority's resistance to a consumer forum's directions. Rather than deciding the writ petition on its merits in one go, which would have left purchasers waiting through a full round of litigation before getting their deeds, the Court separated the part of the dispute where the Board had already effectively conceded, registration against payment of the fixed price, from the part still genuinely contested, the penal interest claim. Purchasers get possession-linked paperwork now; the money dispute gets argued out later.

For buyers caught in disputes with housing boards or similar statutory bodies, the practical lesson is that a writ petition challenging a consumer forum order does not necessarily stall registration if the authority itself has told the forum, on record, that it is willing to register on payment of a fixed sum. Courts asked to supervise that kind of litigation are willing to hold an authority to its own stated terms on the uncontested part of a dispute, while letting the genuinely contested part, here the penal interest claim, proceed separately rather than holding up the whole transaction.

Why It Matters

Statutory housing boards frequently resist executing sale deeds pending appeals against consumer forum orders, leaving buyers without registered title for years while the appeal runs its course. This order shows that a court supervising such a challenge can split the dispute, directing immediate registration on terms the authority itself has already offered while leaving a genuinely contested side issue, such as penal interest, for a later hearing. Buyers facing similar stalling by a public housing authority should point to the authority's own stated willingness to register on payment of a fixed price as grounds for interim relief.
