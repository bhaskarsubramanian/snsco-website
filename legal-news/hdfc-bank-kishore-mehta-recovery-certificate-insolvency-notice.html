Pre-2016 DRT Recovery Certificates Cannot Found an Insolvency Notice, Holds Supreme Court

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, SCC Online and Verdictum. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A bank holding a recovery certificate from the Debts Recovery Tribunal has long treated it as good as a decree, something it can use to chase a defaulting borrower through whatever forum promises the fastest result. The Supreme Court has now told HDFC Bank that a recovery certificate issued before November 2016 does not carry that weight for one particular purpose: triggering an insolvency notice against an individual under the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act, 1909.

The dispute traced back to 2004. The Debts Recovery Tribunal directed issuance of a recovery certificate against Kishore K. Mehta for a little over Rs 14.74 crore on 26 October that year, and the certificate itself followed on 30 November 2004. Years later, the bank sought to use that certificate as the foundation for a Section 9(2) insolvency notice against Mehta, arguing that a recovery certificate was the functional equivalent of a decree or order of a court, the kind of instrument the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act accepts as the basis for such a notice.

Mehta's representatives challenged the notice before the Bombay High Court. A single judge quashed it, a Division Bench affirmed that view, and the bank carried the fight to the Supreme Court. Justices Dipankar Datta and Satish Chandra Sharma were unpersuaded by the bank's position and dismissed the appeal.

The Court's reasoning rested on a close reading of the Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993. Parliament inserted sub-section 22A into Section 19 of that Act only in 2016, and that provision is what expressly equates a recovery certificate with a decree or order for enforcement purposes. The bench read this insertion as telling evidence that no such equivalence existed in law before 2016. If recovery certificates had always carried decree-like status, there would have been no need for Parliament to say so by amendment nearly a quarter century after the RDB Act was first enacted.

Equally important, the Court held that the 2016 amendment was not given retrospective effect. A recovery certificate issued in 2004, well before Section 19(22A) existed, could not retroactively acquire a status Parliament chose to confer only prospectively. The bank's argument that the certificate had always functioned as a decree, and that the amendment merely clarified existing law, did not persuade the bench.

The judgment also leaned on the special character of insolvency proceedings against individuals. Unless a statute plainly permits it, the Court was unwilling to stretch the category of instruments on which an insolvency notice can rest, describing the consequence of such a notice for an individual debtor in terms close to civil death. That framing explains why the bench chose the narrower, more literal reading of the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act over the bank's broader, purpose-driven one.

For lenders still sitting on decade-old recovery certificates obtained before the 2016 amendment, the ruling draws a sharp line. Those certificates remain fully executable through the ordinary machinery of the Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act and the Code of Civil Procedure provisions it imports, but they cannot, on their own, be repurposed as the trigger for insolvency notices against individual borrowers for conduct that predates the amendment. A bank wanting that route will need a certificate issued, or at least some decree or order recognised as such, after November 2016, or it will have to pursue recovery through the tribunal's ordinary execution process instead. The distinction the Court drew matters because personal insolvency law under the 1909 Act still runs on a separate track from the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, which governs corporate debtors and has its own machinery for recognising a financial creditor's claim. A bank chasing an individual guarantor or borrower outside the corporate insolvency framework cannot borrow the Code's more permissive approach to what counts as proof of debt; it has to satisfy the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act on its own, older and narrower terms, which is exactly where the bank's recovery certificate fell short here. Lenders with a portfolio of pre-2016 recovery certificates against individual guarantors would do well to audit which of those files were headed toward insolvency notices rather than straightforward execution. Any notice already issued on the strength of a certificate predating the amendment is now vulnerable to exactly the challenge Mehta's representatives brought, and banks may need to withdraw and refile such notices on a different evidentiary footing, or abandon the insolvency route in favour of execution proceedings before the Tribunal itself, where the recovery certificate's status was never in doubt.

Why It Matters

Banks and NBFCs holding old, pre-2016 DRT recovery certificates against individual guarantors or borrowers cannot treat those certificates as decrees for the purpose of launching insolvency notices under the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act. Lenders pursuing legacy recovery files from the early 2000s need to fall back on ordinary DRT execution, not personal insolvency proceedings, unless their recovery certificate post-dates the 2016 amendment to Section 19 of the RDB Act.
