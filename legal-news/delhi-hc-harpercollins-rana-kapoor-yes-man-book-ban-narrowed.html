Delhi HC Lifts Blanket Ban on Rana Kapoor Biography, Keeps Four Passages Restrained

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon, Bar and Bench, Verdictum and LiveLaw Biz. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Pavan Chand Lall wrote a biography of Rana Kapoor called Yes Man: The Untold Story of Rana Kapoor, which HarperCollins published in December 2020. It traces Kapoor's rise at Yes Bank and the bank's collapse. Kapoor, the bank's co-founder and former managing director and chief executive, sued Lall and HarperCollins for defamation, breach of privacy and injury to reputation, and asked the trial court to stop the book's sale and circulation altogether. He also went after two articles and an interview that touched on the same material.

The District Judge at Tis Hazari agreed with him in July 2024, restraining the entire book's sale, distribution and circulation while the suit was pending, barring the defamatory statements in the articles and interview, and directing their removal from websites and YouTube. Lall did not appear and was proceeded against ex parte. HarperCollins, which had no similar option to sit the case out, filed an appeal, as did Lall separately, both challenging the breadth of the order under Order XLIII Rule 1(r) of the Code of Civil Procedure.

Their argument before the Delhi High Court was that the trial court had gone further than its own findings justified. Of the statements Kapoor had flagged as defamatory, the trial court itself had found only a handful of them problematic on a prima facie basis, yet it had restrained the whole book regardless, covering hundreds of pages that had nothing to do with those statements and had already been in public circulation for years without complaint. Kapoor's side defended the order as a considered exercise of discretion, pointing out that being a public figure does not strip a person of the right to protect his reputation, and that material being available elsewhere does not make republishing it in a book any less actionable.

Justice Harish Vaidyanathan Shankar applied the settled test from Wander Ltd. v. Antox India, under which an appellate court reviewing an interim injunction does not reweigh the evidence afresh but asks whether the trial judge exercised discretion arbitrarily, ignored relevant considerations, or departed from settled principles. Measured against that standard, the blanket restraint on the book could not stand. The trial court's own reasoning had identified specific statements as problematic; an injunction has to track that finding, not sweep in content the court never examined.

On the merits of the specific statements, the court agreed with the trial court that three of them, referred to in the proceedings as statements (iv), (vii) and (x), were prima facie defamatory and could remain restrained. A fourth, statement (ii), the court treated differently, holding it was derogatory in tone but not, on a prima facie view, defamatory, and leaving its final character to be decided by the trial court rather than resolving it finally at this stage. Until that happens, publication of that particular statement stays on hold as well.

The operative order modified the trial court's injunction so that it operates only against the specific passages in which these four statements appear, rather than against the book as a whole. Beyond that, HarperCollins and Lall are free to sell, distribute and circulate Yes Man. The court was careful to describe this as an interim arrangement only, pending final decision of the defamation suit, and said the trial court should decide the case uninfluenced by anything it had said while assessing the appeal. No costs were awarded either way.

For publishers, authors and the business figures who end up written about, the order is a reminder that an interim injunction has to be built passage by passage rather than imposed on an entire work because a few lines in it are disputed. A businessman or executive who objects to how he is portrayed in a book still has to show the court, statement by statement, what crosses the line into defamation; getting the whole book pulled from shelves on the strength of a handful of contested lines is not something a court can do once an appellate bench is asked to look at the arithmetic.

Why It Matters

Businesspeople and family-business principals who are the subject of unflattering books or articles often seek a blanket restraint rather than a targeted one. This ruling confirms that Indian courts will generally confine an interim injunction to the specific passages found prima facie defamatory, not the whole publication, which matters both to subjects weighing whether to sue and to publishers and authors assessing their exposure before and after release.
