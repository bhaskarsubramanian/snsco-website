Delhi HC: Writ Can't Reopen Arbitration Testimony, Dismisses Rs 10 Crore Claim Against AIR

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon, LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

The dispute behind this writ petition goes back to 1988, when Electronic Enterprises, run by its sole proprietor S.C. Gupta, was involved in a tender for the procurement of microphones by All India Radio. That commercial disagreement was referred to arbitration, with Gupta as the claimant, in proceedings that would have given him the chance to lead evidence, cross-examine the other side's witnesses and make his case on the merits of the tender dispute in the ordinary way arbitration proceedings run. Decades later, Gupta returned to court, this time not over the tender itself but over something a witness had said while giving evidence in that arbitration.

Gupta's case was that testimony given by an All India Radio official, identified in reports as Yadav, during the arbitration amounted to defamation, and he filed a writ petition under Article 226 of the Constitution before the Delhi High Court seeking Rs 10 crore in damages from the Director General, All India Radio on that basis. The petition did not challenge the arbitral award itself through the limited grounds available under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act; instead it asked the writ court to treat the long-past testimony as an independent wrong and award compensation for it directly.

The Delhi High Court was not persuaded. The bench noted that Gupta had been a party to the arbitration in which the impugned testimony was given, and had the opportunity, available to every party in arbitral proceedings, to cross-examine the witness and challenge the testimony at the time it was recorded. Having had that chance within the arbitration itself, the court held, Gupta could not use a writ petition years later to reopen the same question of what the witness said and whether it was accurate, by recasting it as a fresh defamation claim rather than as a challenge to the arbitral record.

On that basis the court found the writ petition devoid of merit and held that it did not warrant the exercise of the court's jurisdiction under Article 226, dismissing the petition along with any pending applications in it. The order treats the question as essentially one of forum and finality: a party's remedy for what happens during arbitration, including testimony it disagrees with, lies within the arbitration and the narrow post-award remedies the Arbitration Act provides, not in an open-ended writ petition filed long after the arbitration has concluded.

The principle reaches well beyond microphones and a 1988 tender. Anyone who has gone through arbitration, commercial or contractual, and come away unhappy with how a witness's evidence was treated, or with any other aspect of how the proceedings were conducted, has to pursue that grievance through the mechanisms the arbitration itself and the Arbitration and Conciliation Act provide, principally through objections raised during the proceedings and the limited challenge to the award under Section 34. Dressing up the same grievance years later as a separate civil or constitutional claim, filed directly against the opposing party's witness or official, is unlikely to find a sympathetic writ court, as this order shows.

For a business that has been through arbitration and feels a witness overstepped or misled the tribunal, the lesson here is procedural rather than substantive: raise it, through cross-examination and objection, while the arbitration is still live, because a court years later is not going to treat the same complaint as a fresh wrong simply because it is framed as defamation instead of as a challenge to the evidence.

The age of the underlying dispute is itself part of the story. A procurement tender from 1988 working its way through arbitration and then into a writ petition decided only in November 2026 says something about how long a determined litigant can keep a grievance alive by shifting the label attached to it, moving from a contractual claim to arbitration, and then, once that avenue is exhausted, to a constitutional remedy framed as a personal wrong against an individual official rather than against the authority itself. Courts dealing with public sector bodies such as All India Radio see this pattern often enough that the reasoning in this order is likely to be cited again whenever an old commercial dispute resurfaces dressed as a fresh cause of action.

Why It Matters

A party unhappy with how arbitration proceedings went, including testimony a witness gave, cannot wait years and then bring the same grievance to a writ court dressed up as an independent civil wrong. The Delhi High Court's dismissal of this Rs 10 crore defamation claim against All India Radio confirms that a writ petition is not a substitute for the objections and cross-examination available within arbitration itself, or for the narrow challenge to an award under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act. That matters to any business weighing whether to relitigate an old arbitration through a different label.
