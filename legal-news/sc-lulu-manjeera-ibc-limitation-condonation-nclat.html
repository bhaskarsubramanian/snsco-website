IBC & Insolvency · 16 November 2026

NCLAT Cannot Stretch the 15-Day IBC Appeal Window Using the Limitation Act: Supreme Court

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Verdictum, courtbook.in and Law Chakra. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A resolution plan that had already cleared the NCLT nearly came undone over a filing deadline, and the Supreme Court has now put that question to rest. The NCLT, Hyderabad, approved a resolution plan under which Lulu International Shopping Malls would take over Manjeera Retail Holdings, the company behind the Manjeera Mall. Three unit buyers in the mall were unhappy with how the plan treated their interests and wanted to challenge the approval before the NCLAT. The trouble was timing: their appeals were e-filed a day after the outer 45-day limit allowed under Section 61(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code had already run out.

Section 61(2) gives an aggrieved party 30 days to appeal an NCLT order, extendable by a further 15 days if the appellant shows sufficient cause for the delay. Add the two together and the absolute ceiling is 45 days. Beyond that, the IBC's scheme gives the NCLAT no room to condone anything more. The unit buyers' appeals missed even this outer limit, filed on what the Supreme Court's judgment treats as the 46th day. Rather than reading the IBC's own ceiling as final, the NCLAT turned to Section 4 of the Limitation Act, 1963, a general provision that lets a litigant file on the next working day if the last day of limitation falls on a court holiday, and used it to let the appeals in.

The Supreme Court held that was the wrong move. Relying on its own earlier ruling in Tata Steel Ltd. v. Raj Kumar Banerjee, the Bench of Justices Sanjay Kumar and Sanjeev Sachdeva explained that the extra 15 days available for condonation under Section 61(2) is not part of the limitation period that Section 4 of the Limitation Act can stretch. The 30-day period is the prescribed limitation; the following 15 days are a discretionary, sufficient-cause window granted by the IBC itself, not a limitation period that general limitation law can extend further by deeming a holiday-adjacent filing to be on time. Once an appeal falls outside the full 45 days, it is simply too late, and no provision of the Limitation Act can pull it back in.

On that footing, the Supreme Court set aside the NCLAT's order that had allowed the unit buyers' appeals to proceed, and dismissed their challenge to the resolution plan as time-barred. The practical result is that the NCLT's approval of the Lulu takeover of Manjeera Retail Holdings stands undisturbed, and the resolution process that had been clouded by the pending appeals can move forward.

The judgment also deals with a separate but related point about what the NCLAT can and cannot do once it has passed an order. The unit buyers, after their appeals ran into trouble, had asked the NCLAT to recall its own order. The Supreme Court clarified that the NCLAT has no power of review under the IBC; a tribunal created by statute only has the powers the statute gives it, and nothing in the IBC hands the NCLAT a review jurisdiction. What the NCLAT does have is a narrower power to recall an order under Rule 11 of the NCLAT Rules, 2016, which lets it correct an order in limited circumstances without reopening the merits. Where a party's recall application is wrongly rejected, the remedy is to challenge that rejection before the Supreme Court itself under Section 62 of the IBC, which is the route that corrected the error in this case.

The ruling matters well beyond this one mall. IBC litigation runs on hard deadlines precisely so that resolution plans, once approved, are not left hanging for years while late-filed appeals work their way through the system. Lenders, resolution applicants, homebuyers and other stakeholders structure their expectations, and often their financing, around the finality that a confirmed resolution plan is supposed to deliver. A general tolerance for stretching the condonation window using outside limitation law would have undercut exactly that finality, since almost any late appeal could find some provision of the Limitation Act to lean on.

For anyone appealing an NCLT order, the message is blunt: count the days strictly against the 30-plus-15 ceiling in Section 61(2), and do not assume that a holiday, a weekend, or any other general limitation concept will rescue a filing made after day 45. Resolution applicants and successful bidders, for their part, now have clearer authority to resist delayed challenges that try to use general limitation principles to get a second bite at a plan the NCLT has already approved.

Why It Matters

IBC timelines only work if tribunals enforce them strictly, and this ruling confirms the NCLAT cannot borrow from the general Limitation Act to revive an appeal filed beyond the IBC's own 45-day outer limit. Resolution applicants and successful bidders gain a firmer basis to resist late challenges to approved plans, while anyone considering an appeal from an NCLT order should treat the 30-plus-15-day ceiling as absolute rather than assume any general limitation provision can extend it.
