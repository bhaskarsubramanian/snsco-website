Control Over 'How' the Work Is Performed, Not Merely 'What' Task Is Assigned, Is Key to Whether a Gig Worker Is Really an Employee: High Court

Source: As reported by LiveLaw on 5 June 2026. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes; it is not a reproduction of the original report. Readers are encouraged to consult the original source and the underlying judgment or order directly.

A High Court, in a matter concerning the classification of app-based delivery workers, has laid down a detailed application of the control test to the platform economy, holding that the relevant inquiry is not simply whether the aggregator assigns tasks or sets prices through its app, but the degree of control it exercises over how those tasks are actually to be performed, including mandated routes, real-time performance monitoring, penalty structures for deviation from platform-set standards, and restrictions on a worker's ability to simultaneously work for competing platforms.

The Court held that a genuinely independent contractor relationship is characterised by the worker's freedom to determine the manner of performance, and the mere fact that a platform organises task allocation through algorithmic assignment does not, by itself, convert what is otherwise an independent engagement into an employment relationship. However, where the degree of operational control, through mandatory acceptance rates, algorithmically enforced exclusivity, and close monitoring with financial penalties for deviation, begins to resemble the direction and supervision an employer would exercise over an employee, the label the platform applies to the relationship is not determinative.

The Court declined to lay down a bright-line rule applicable to the gig economy as a whole, holding instead that classification will necessarily be a fact-specific exercise turning on the actual degree of operational control exercised by a given platform over a given category of worker, to be assessed on the specific facts and contractual and operational structure of each platform.

The Code on Social Security's extension of certain social security benefits to gig and platform workers addresses part of this landscape by creating a distinct statutory category for such workers, but questions of full employment status, for purposes such as retrenchment protection, minimum wages, or industrial dispute remedies, continue to turn on this fact-specific control analysis where a worker disputes their classification outside the Social Security Code's specific framework.

Why It Matters

Platforms structuring gig-worker engagement should assess not just how tasks are assigned but how much operational control is exercised through algorithmic monitoring, penalty structures and exclusivity requirements. A high degree of such control can support a claim that the relationship is really one of disguised employment.
