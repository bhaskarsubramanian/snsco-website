Delhi High Court Keeps BharatPe Promoter's Consent Right Over Unity Bank's Capital Plans Alive

Source: Originally reported by Law Chakra, courtbook.in and yourstory.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Minority shareholders who negotiate a consent right into their investment documents want to know one thing above all else: will a court actually stand behind that right when the company tries to move ahead without them. Resilient Innovations Private Limited, the parent entity of fintech major BharatPe, got its answer from the Delhi High Court's Division Bench on 5 November 2026, and the answer was yes.

The dispute arose out of Resilient's shareholding in Unity Small Finance Bank Limited. At some point the bank's board wanted to put forward a proposal to increase its authorised share capital. Resilient objected, taking the position that it held a right of prior written consent before any such proposal could go to the board, and that Unity had not obtained that consent before moving ahead. A Single Judge agreed with Resilient and granted an interim injunction restraining the bank from placing the capital-increase proposal before its board without first securing Resilient's written consent.

Unity was not willing to let the order stand and carried it up on appeal under FAO(OS)(COMM) 221/2026, asking the Division Bench to lift the restraint so the board could get on with raising capital. The bank's case, as reported, rested on treating the proposed capital increase as an ordinary matter of banking and business judgment that a shareholder's consent clause should not be allowed to hold hostage, particularly given the regulatory and commercial pressures facing a small finance bank.

Appeals of this kind usually turn on how an appellate bench reads the balance between two legitimate interests. On one side sits a company's need to run its business and meet its capital requirements without a minority shareholder holding a veto over ordinary corporate decisions. On the other sits an investor's bargained-for right to have a say before the very decisions that could dilute its stake or change the company it invested in. An interim injunction of this kind exists precisely to hold that balance steady while the underlying dispute over the parties' rights is worked out at trial or in arbitration, whichever forum the shareholders' arrangement points to.

The Division Bench, comprising Justice C. Hari Shankar and Justice Vinod Kumar, was not convinced, and dismissed Unity's appeal, leaving the Single Judge's interim protection for Resilient in place. Justice Hari Shankar authored the judgment, running to 59 pages by one account, which on its own tells a litigator that the Bench engaged with the contractual and factual record in some depth rather than disposing of the appeal on a short, formal footing.

What the ruling leaves undisturbed matters more than any single line of reasoning in it. An appellate court choosing not to interfere with an interim order is often as significant, in practical terms, as a final decision on the merits, because it fixes the status quo for however long the underlying dispute takes to resolve. Here, that status quo is that Unity's board cannot take its capital-increase proposal forward without Resilient's prior written consent, for as long as the interim order stands.

For a bank that may need to raise capital on a timeline set by its regulator or its business needs, having that timeline hostage to a shareholder's consent is not a comfortable position, and it is easy to see why Unity fought the point on appeal rather than simply complying. Equally, for an investor who negotiated a consent right precisely to have a say over dilution or control of the capital structure, this is the outcome that clause was written to produce, and the Division Bench's refusal to disturb it vindicates the bargain Resilient struck when it took its stake.

The case is a reminder, for any investor negotiating affirmative-vote or consent rights into a shareholders' agreement with an Indian company, including a regulated entity such as a bank, that Indian courts will enforce those rights by injunction when a company tries to move ahead without the agreed consent, and that an appellate bench will not lightly disturb such protection once a Single Judge has granted it on a reasoned basis. Promoters and boards negotiating future funding rounds would do well to treat consent clauses of this kind as genuinely binding constraints on corporate action, not formalities to be worked around once capital is needed.

Why It Matters

Foreign and domestic investors who take minority stakes in Indian companies, including regulated entities such as banks, often rely on consent or affirmative-vote rights to protect against dilution or unilateral changes to the capital structure. This ruling shows that Indian appellate courts will uphold an interim injunction enforcing such a right against a company's board, giving investors real assurance that a negotiated consent clause will be given practical effect rather than treated as a formality the company can bypass when it needs to raise capital quickly.
