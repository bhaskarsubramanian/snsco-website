CESTAT Mumbai Quashes Rs 85.28-Lakh Customs Demand on Imported Brown Rice Protein Powder

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and TaxGuru. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

An importer who has described its goods accurately on the bill of entry and paid duty on the classification it believes is correct still has to live with the risk that a customs officer, sometimes years later, decides a different tariff heading applies and reopens the whole consignment for a higher rate. That is the dispute the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal's Mumbai bench resolved on 2 November 2026 in favour of the importer, Unibourne Food Ingredients, over a consignment of imported brown rice protein powder.

Unibourne had classified the goods under Customs Tariff Item 3504 0099, a residuary entry covering peptones and other protein substances and their derivatives not specified elsewhere, and paid basic customs duty at 20 per cent on that basis. The jurisdictional customs house at Jawaharlal Nehru Port took a different view in an order dated 26 December 2025, holding the goods were more correctly classifiable under CTH 2106 1000, a heading for food preparations not elsewhere specified, which carried a different and higher duty incidence. That reclassification produced a differential duty demand of Rs 85,28,035, along with interest, confiscation, a redemption fine and penalties.

The Tribunal did not accept that the goods belonged under the food-preparation heading. It held that brown rice protein powder, being a protein concentrate derived from processing brown rice rather than a composite food preparation ready for consumption, fell squarely within the scope of Heading 3504, and that Unibourne's classification under CTI 3504 0099 was correct. Having found the classification itself sound, the Tribunal set aside not just the differential duty but the interest, the confiscation order, the redemption fine and the penalties that had followed from the reclassification, since none of those consequential orders could survive once their underlying premise fell away.

Part of the ruling also turned on what the importer is required to prove at the time of filing. The Tribunal's reasoning records that Unibourne had correctly and specifically described the goods as brown rice protein powder in its bills of entry, which matters because a reclassification dispute of this kind often comes down to whether the customs house can point to a misdeclaration or concealment by the importer, as opposed to simply forming a different legal view of a correctly and fully disclosed product. Where the description on the documents was accurate throughout, the burden shifts more heavily onto the department to justify why a different heading should apply, and a bare difference of opinion on classification is not itself evidence of any wrongdoing by the importer.

For businesses importing specialised food ingredients and nutraceutical inputs into India, whether as an Indian trading company or as the Indian buying arm of an overseas supplier, this is a reminder that tariff classification for a product that straddles the line between a processed food item and an industrial or nutritional ingredient is rarely settled once and for all by filing a bill of entry. Harmonised System headings for protein isolates, concentrates and similar derivatives are drafted around processing method and end use, not around marketing descriptions such as 'protein powder', and customs houses at different ports, or the same port at different times, can and do take inconsistent positions on where a given product sits.

The practical lesson for an importer facing a show-cause notice on reclassification is to go back to first principles on how the product is actually made and what it technically is, rather than arguing primarily from how it is labelled or sold in the market, since that is where a tribunal will look first. Keeping the technical and manufacturing documentation that supports the original classification on file, and not merely the commercial invoice and packing list, puts an importer in a far stronger position if a reclassification dispute arises months or years after a consignment has already cleared and the duty has been paid and treated as settled.

There is also a timing point that recurring importers of the same product line should not overlook. A reclassification raised nearly a year after the goods had already cleared, as happened here, shows how long a customs house can sit on a consignment before reopening it, and an importer who keeps bringing in the same product under the same heading year after year carries that exposure forward with every fresh shipment until either a tribunal settles the question or the department formally accepts the classification in writing. Getting an advance ruling from the Customs Authority for Advance Rulings before a new product line is first imported, rather than waiting for a dispute to force the issue years later, is the more economical route for an importer that expects to bring in meaningful volumes of a borderline product.

Why It Matters

Importers of processed food ingredients and nutraceutical inputs, including Indian buying arms of overseas suppliers, face real exposure when a customs house revisits a classification years after a consignment has cleared. This ruling shows that an accurate description on the bill of entry is a strong defence against a later reclassification, and that businesses should retain technical and manufacturing documentation, not just commercial paperwork, to support the classification they originally filed.
