Power, Energy & Mining · 29 October 2026

NGT Orders Arunachal to Clear Riverbed Encroachments, Curb Illegal Mining

Source: Originally reported by downtoearth.org.in and whalesbook.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A river running through Arunachal Pradesh had, by the time this matter reached the National Green Tribunal, acquired boundary walls and private bridges along stretches of its bed, built by people who had simply treated the riverbed as available land. On 29 October 2026, the Tribunal's Eastern Zone Bench at Kolkata ordered the state to stop treating that encroachment as someone else's problem. It directed the Deputy Commissioners of every district in Arunachal Pradesh to identify and remove encroachments on the Borum river and on other rivers across the state, specifically naming boundary walls and private bridges as structures that had no business being where they were found.

The order did not stop at removal. The Tribunal also told the state's Geology and Mining department to act against illegal earth mining taking place along the banks of the Borum and other rivers, and it set a condition on any mining that is meant to continue lawfully: earth mining can proceed only after the State Environment Impact Assessment Authority has granted environmental clearance, and only once District Survey Reports are in place for the districts concerned. Without that paperwork, the mining the Tribunal is talking about simply has no lawful basis to continue.

That combination, structural encroachment on one side and unauthorised extraction on the other, is what gave the Bench its underlying concern. A riverbed narrowed by walls and bridges and simultaneously dug into for earth loses both its carrying capacity and its banks at the same time, and the Tribunal's own reasoning, as reported, framed the structures as a threat to local water flow and ecological stability rather than a mere planning irregularity. Arunachal Pradesh's rivers, running through terrain where flooding and erosion are already a seasonal fact of life, do not have much tolerance to spare for either problem on its own, let alone both together.

Private bridges are a telling detail in that list of what has to go. A boundary wall built too close to a riverbank at least stays in one place, but a private bridge thrown across a river implies traffic crossing it regularly, which in turn suggests the encroachment has become part of someone's daily routine rather than a one-off structure nobody noticed. Removing a bridge that people already rely on to get from one side of a village to the other is a harder administrative task than pulling down a wall, and the order gives the Deputy Commissioners no exception for that difficulty.

The Tribunal also reached beyond district administration into the machinery of state government itself. It directed the state Chief Secretary to place the question of framing appropriate rules before the state government, pushing the response beyond ad hoc removal drives toward a regulatory framework that would govern riverbed use and earth mining going forward rather than leaving each encroachment to be caught and cleared one at a time.

For the Deputy Commissioners now holding this order, the task is concrete and immediate: survey the riverbanks under their charge, identify what has been built where it should not have been, and remove it. For the Geology and Mining department, the task is to treat environmental clearance and a completed District Survey Report as preconditions rather than paperwork to be regularised later, since the order leaves no room for earth mining to continue on the understanding that clearance will follow once extraction is already underway.

What this order signals for mining operators and landowners along Arunachal Pradesh's rivers more broadly is that the Tribunal is not content to deal with riverbed mining and riverbed construction as separate problems handled by separate departments on separate timelines. A business or individual relying on a presence along a riverbank, whether a boundary wall built without anyone objecting or an earth-mining operation that began before clearance was in hand, is now operating against an express direction naming exactly that conduct, with the state's own administrative and regulatory apparatus told to act on it rather than wait for a fresh complaint to arrive.

Why It Matters

Riverbed land in hill states is often treated as informally available, for a boundary wall, a private bridge, or small-scale earth extraction, on the assumption that nobody is watching closely. This order puts district administrations and the state's mining regulator on direct notice to treat that conduct as unlawful rather than tolerated, and makes clear that mining along a riverbank needs environmental clearance and a District Survey Report in hand before work starts, not after. Anyone with a structure or extraction operation on or near a riverbed in Arunachal Pradesh should expect enforcement rather than continued latitude.
