RBI's Second Amendment to the 2026 Export-Import Rules Settles Who Files the Export Declaration Form

Source: Originally reported by studycafe.in, foxmandal.in and foxmandal.in (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Exporters dealing with their bank's trade desk this month are running into a tweaked set of rules that trace back to a notification the Reserve Bank of India issued on 2 November 2026. The notification carries the unglamorous name FEMA 23(R)/(2)/2026-RB, and it amends the Foreign Exchange Management (Export and Import of Goods and Services) Regulations, 2026, the consolidated framework RBI had first notified on 13 January 2026 to replace the older, scattered export control rules.

That consolidated framework had already been amended once before this. On 22 September 2026, RBI cut the standard period within which export proceeds must be realised and repatriated to India, moving it down from fifteen months to nine months for ordinary exports, and from eighteen months to twelve months for exports invoiced or settled in rupees. Both changes took effect from 1 October 2026, and they remain the operative realisation windows exporters work to today.

The Second Amendment does not touch those timelines. Its focus is narrower and, for banks and exporters who have been arguing over paperwork, more practical. It inserts a definition of a resident individual into the regulations, a term the principal regulations had used without pinning down, and it is this definition that now anchors which natural persons fall within the export compliance regime as opposed to the separate rules that apply to exporting companies, partnerships and other entities.

A second change in the amendment deals with the Export Declaration Form, the document through which an exporter tells the authorities what is going out, to whom, and for how much. Under the regulations as they stood, an exporter was expected to file this form before goods or software left the country, and the obligation sat awkwardly alongside the reality that an Authorised Dealer bank often already holds the same payment and shipment details through the banking channel used to route the export proceeds. The Second Amendment resolves that overlap for a defined set of cases by treating the EDF as deemed to have been filed once the relevant payment-related information has been furnished to the Authorised Dealer bank, so long as that information matches what the form would otherwise require.

For exporters of services specifically, the amendment goes a step further and sets out what counts as adequate filing of the EDF, an area the January 2026 regulations had left comparatively thin. Software and services exporters do not move physical goods through a customs checkpoint, so the usual anchor for declaring an export, the shipping bill, does not apply to them in the same way. The amendment ties their declaration obligation more closely to the banking trail of the payment itself, which is the practical record that exists in most service export transactions in any event.

RBI issued the Second Amendment under Sections 7 and 8 of the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, together with sub-section (6) of Section 10 and sub-section (2) of Section 47, the same statutory provisions that supported the principal regulations and the September amendment before it. Its commencement is tied back to 1 October 2026, the date on which the underlying 2026 framework and the first amendment both took effect, rather than to the date of the notification itself.

None of this is the kind of change that makes headlines outside trade finance desks, but it closes a gap that Authorised Dealer banks had flagged after the January notification: a resident individual exporting on a small scale, or a services exporter with no shipping bill to point to, had no clean textual anchor telling them how their EDF obligation would be treated once the bank already had the payment details in hand. Exporters and their banks now have that anchor, and compliance teams reconciling export files from October 2026 onward should check which of their filings fall within the deemed-submission route the amendment creates, rather than assuming the older, stricter reading of the EDF requirement still governs every transaction.

For exporters of goods and services who route payments through Authorised Dealer banks, including Indian subsidiaries and branch offices that handle their own export documentation, the amendment is worth factoring into internal compliance checklists now rather than at the time of an RBI inspection or a bank's periodic audit of export files.

Why It Matters

Exporters and the banks that handle their trade documentation now have a clearer rule on when an Export Declaration Form is treated as filed, particularly for resident individuals and services exporters who lack a shipping bill to anchor their declaration. Getting this wrong can hold up repatriation of export proceeds or invite RBI scrutiny of a bank's trade compliance file, so exporters dealing with India from abroad, and their Indian banking partners, should check which filings now qualify for the deemed-submission route before the next compliance cycle.
