Highest Bidder in Liquidation Auction Gets No Vested Right Until Sale Concludes, Holds NCLT Kochi

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and supremetoday.ai. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Asten Realtors Private Limited is in liquidation before the Kochi Bench of the National Company Law Tribunal, in proceedings numbered CP(IBC)/54/KOB/2022. As with any liquidation under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, the liquidator has to convert the corporate debtor's remaining assets into money for distribution to creditors, and one of those assets, a parcel of real estate held by the company, went to an e-auction conducted as part of that process.

Muhammed Aslam came out on top of that e-auction with a bid of Rs 4,96,27,659. On the strength of that result, he approached the tribunal seeking more time to complete his side of the transaction and asked to be impleaded as a party in the ongoing liquidation proceedings, treating his position as highest bidder as something the tribunal needed to protect while the rest of the process played out.

The tribunal was not persuaded that winning the bid, by itself, had earned him a seat at that table. In an order dated 16 October 2026, the NCLT Kochi Bench held that a person declared the highest bidder in an auction does not thereby acquire a vested or enforceable right to the asset in question. Being named the highest bidder is one step in a sale process, not the completion of that process, and a sale stands concluded only once the remaining conditions attached to it, payment within the stipulated period and formal confirmation by the liquidator among them, are actually fulfilled.

Working from that premise, the tribunal declined Aslam's request for additional time and refused to implead him as a party to proceedings over which the liquidator retained the discretion to decide how the asset was finally disposed of. The order records no finding that the e-auction itself had been conducted improperly, and it does not suggest Aslam acted in bad faith in bidding the amount he did. It holds only that an unmet condition following a winning bid leaves that bidder with an expectation he can pursue through the ordinary course of the sale process, not a right he can enforce by forcing his way into the liquidation itself through an impleadment application.

That reasoning tracks a line of authority built mostly around government tenders and contracts, where the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that being named the highest bidder, or the lowest tenderer for that matter, does not by itself bind the seller to complete the transaction, particularly where the auction or tender conditions reserve a right to cancel or modify the process at any stage before it closes. The NCLT Kochi Bench has carried that same reasoning into the liquidation setting under the IBC, where creditors waiting on sale proceeds have as much riding on a clean, flexible sale process as any government department running a tender.

The practical difference between an auction bid and a concluded sale often gets blurred once a winning number is announced, and bidders understandably start treating the asset as already theirs. This order tells them otherwise in the liquidation context specifically. A liquidator selling assets under the Code is free to insist on payment and formal confirmation before treating any e-auction as closed, and a bidder who has not cleared those conditions gains no foothold in the liquidation proceedings merely by having topped the bid sheet.

For liquidators and resolution professionals running asset sales elsewhere in the country, the order is a reminder worth keeping on file. Auction conditions that reserve flexibility over the final sale, cancellation clauses, confirmation requirements, payment deadlines, are not boilerplate that can be waived informally once a bidder emerges. They are what let a liquidator manage an incomplete sale, including walking away from a bidder who has not performed, without facing an impleadment application built on nothing more than having placed the highest number.

Creditors in a liquidation have their own stake in how firmly a tribunal enforces this distinction. Every week an asset stays tied up with a bidder who has not paid is a week the sale proceeds that creditors are waiting on stay out of reach, and a liquidator who felt bound to treat every top bid as final, regardless of whether payment followed, would have far less room to re-auction a stalled asset or negotiate a better outcome. By keeping the line between a winning bid and a completed sale firm, the Kochi Bench has protected the liquidator's hand to manage the process in the creditors' interest, rather than in the interest of whichever bidder happened to top the sheet on a given day.

Why It Matters

Liquidators selling corporate debtor assets under the IBC often face bidders who treat the top position in an e-auction as a settled deal. This order confirms that a sale is not complete, and the bidder has no enforceable claim or standing to intervene in the liquidation, until payment and formal confirmation follow. Liquidators retain room to manage an incomplete sale process without a disappointed or impatient bidder being able to force his way into the proceedings by seeking impleadment.
