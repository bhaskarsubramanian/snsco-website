Coconut Is An Oilseed, Not A Fruit, For Mandi Trading: Allahabad High Court

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon, courtbook.in, supremetoday.ai and odishabytes.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A wholesale trader who had been selling green coconuts out of the Navin Phal Sabzi Mandi Sthal in Sahibabad, Ghaziabad, has lost its challenge to being told to stop. The Lucknow Bench of the Allahabad High Court dismissed the writ petition filed by M/s Three Star Fruit Company, holding that coconut is classified as an oilseed under the Uttar Pradesh Krishi Utpadan Mandi Adhiniyam, 1964, and has no place in a market yard licensed specifically for fruits and vegetables. Justice Amitabh Kumar Rai, writing for the bench he shared with Justice Alok Mathur, dismissed the petition with no order as to costs.

The dispute traced back to how the Sahibabad market area's specified-produce list had changed over the years. Coconut featured on that list when the area was first notified in 1996, but a notification dated 18 July 2003 dropped it. A later order dated 17 September 2021 permitted coconut to be traded on payment of user charges, but only within mandi yards set aside for grain and oilseeds, not within the fruit and vegetable section. The petitioner held a licence for the latter, and argued that this later order gave it a fresh right to deal in coconut wherever it already operated.

Reading the two notifications in a sequence opposite to the petitioner's argument, the Bench held that a licence for the fruit and vegetable yard never extended to a commodity the law treats as an oilseed, and the 2021 order creating a payment route for coconut in grain and oilseed yards did nothing to widen a licence issued for a different yard altogether. The petitioner, the judgment records, "at no point of time, had any legal right to trade in green coconut" within the Sahibabad fruit and vegetable premises. The fact that the market committee had not enforced the position earlier did not, in the Court's view, convert years of tolerance into a right.

On the core classification question, the Court leaned on Supreme Court authority construing how coconut is dealt with in markets and under tax statutes, including the rulings in Popular Trading Company and in the Bharuch Coconut Trading line of cases. Those decisions have treated the dried, watery coconut sold in trade as distinct from the green, tender coconut sold as a drink or snack, for the purpose of deciding which schedule entry a commodity falls under. Applying that commercial-parlance test, the Bench held that the fact that tender coconut is sometimes bought and eaten as a fruit does not mean every form in which coconut is traded qualifies as a fruit for licensing purposes.

The petitioner's botanical argument, that coconut is scientifically a drupe and therefore a fruit, did not move the Court either. Market-yard classification under the 1964 Act, the judgment holds, follows how a commodity is bought and sold in the trade, not how a botanist would describe it. On that basis, the 1995 notification placing coconut under the "Oilseeds" sub-heading of the agricultural produce schedule was upheld as a valid exercise of the state's classification power, and not something a court should second-guess merely because another classification was also arguable.

Traders in a similar position across other UP mandi towns are left with one narrow option after this ruling: coconut can still be bought and sold, but only inside yards designated for grain and oilseeds, and only after paying the user charges fixed by the 2021 order. A fruit and vegetable licence will not cover it, however long a trader has been allowed to deal in it from that counter. For a market area like Sahibabad, which sits on the Ghaziabad side of the Delhi-NCR trading belt and feeds produce into Delhi's wholesale chain, the distinction matters in practical terms: a trader who wants to add coconut to an existing fruit and vegetable stall will need a separate yard allotment, not just a fresh understanding with the market committee.

This judgment also carries a wider point about how much weight a market-area notification carries once it has been issued and not successfully challenged within time. Traders who built a business model on an informal practice tolerated for years found that practice gave them nothing to stand on once the market committee chose to enforce the schedule as written. Anyone holding a produce licence under a state mandi law would do well to check the schedule entry for a commodity before assuming that an existing stall licence covers it, rather than relying on how long the trade has gone unchallenged.

Why It Matters

Market-area notifications under state Krishi Utpadan Mandi laws fix which commodities a yard licence covers, and courts will read that schedule literally rather than by botanical classification or years of unchallenged practice. Traders operating out of NCR-adjacent mandi towns such as Ghaziabad who deal in produce with disputed classification, coconut being one recurring example, should check the specific yard their licence covers before expanding into a new commodity, since an oilseed or grain classification will not be read into a fruit and vegetable licence merely because the trade has gone on for years.
