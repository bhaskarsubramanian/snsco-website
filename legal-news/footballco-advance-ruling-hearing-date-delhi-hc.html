Delhi HC Nudges Advance Rulings Board To Fix Hearing Dates Within 15 Days Of Filing

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, TaxGuru and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A Netherlands-incorporated applicant that heard nothing from India's tax authorities for months after seeking an advance ruling has at least got a court to say the silence should not have lasted this long. The Delhi High Court, hearing a writ by FootballCo Intermediate Cooperatief U.A. against the Board for Advance Rulings, stopped short of ordering a decision by any fixed date, but it put on record that an applicant waiting to know when its case will even be heard is a problem the Board needs to fix.

FootballCo had filed its application on 3 July 2026 under Section 384 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, seeking a ruling on the tax treatment of its Indian dealings before the transaction in question actually went through, which is the entire point of approaching the Board rather than waiting for an assessment and fighting it later. The application was assigned to Board for Advance Rulings-I, Delhi, by a communication dated 9 July 2026. From there, according to the writ petition, nothing happened. No admission order came. No hearing date was communicated. The applicant was left to assume that its request was sitting somewhere in a queue with no visibility into when, or whether, it would be taken up.

That is precisely the uncertainty an advance ruling is supposed to remove, and the irony was not lost on the petitioner. A mechanism built to give a taxpayer, particularly a foreign one unfamiliar with how long Indian tax litigation can run, some certainty about its position before it commits to a transaction, ends up adding uncertainty of its own when the applicant cannot even get a sense of the timeline.

The Division Bench of Justices Dinesh Mehta and Aditi Choudhary did not grant the mandamus FootballCo was asking for, which would have compelled the Board to decide the application within a set period. The court's reasoning was practical rather than sympathetic to inaction: roughly two months remained within the statutory timeline the Income-tax Act, 2025 allows the Board to decide such applications, so directing disposal by a specific earlier date would have meant compressing a process Parliament had already given a defined outer limit for.

Having declined the mandamus, the Bench still went further than simply dismissing the petition. It recorded the view that an applicant is entitled to at least know when its matter will be heard, even if the final ruling itself takes the full time the statute allows. The court suggested that the Board explore a mechanism to inform applicants of their likely or next hearing date within fifteen days of an application being filed, so that the silence FootballCo experienced for weeks on end does not repeat itself for the next applicant.

The suggestion is not a binding direction with teeth behind it, and the Board is free to decide how, or whether, to implement it. But coming from the Delhi High Court, which handles the bulk of advance ruling litigation given where the Board's principal bench sits, it carries weight as a marker of what the court expects going forward, and it gives future petitioners something concrete to point to if the same silence recurs.

For foreign companies and their Indian subsidiaries weighing whether an advance ruling is worth the wait compared to simply taking a position and litigating it out later if the assessing officer disagrees, procedural transparency of this kind matters as much as the substantive ruling itself. A process that takes months to even schedule a hearing, without any update to the applicant in the meantime, pushes some companies toward just filing returns on their own interpretation and keeping funds aside for a dispute, rather than seeking the certainty the ruling mechanism was designed to provide.

The underlying tax question FootballCo actually raised was not decided in this order and remains pending before the Board. What the ruling leaves behind is a procedural marker rather than a substantive one: a reminder that the advance ruling route, still relatively young under the Income-tax Act, 2025, needs its own house in order on timelines if it is to be a genuinely attractive alternative to post-facto litigation for cross-border taxpayers.

Why It Matters

Advance rulings exist to give a taxpayer, especially a foreign company unfamiliar with Indian tax litigation, certainty before a transaction rather than a dispute after it. This order does not decide FootballCo's tax question, but it puts the Board for Advance Rulings on notice that leaving an applicant without any word on a hearing date for months defeats the purpose of the mechanism, and it gives future applicants a basis to press for earlier scheduling.
