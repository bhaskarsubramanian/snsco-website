IBC & Insolvency · 17 November 2026

NCLT Kolkata: A Form B Notice Is Not the Same as Invoking the Guarantee

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, knnindia.co.in and supremetoday.ai. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A bank that holds a personal guarantee often assumes the guarantee is live the moment the underlying loan turns bad, and that a demand notice under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code is simply the next administrative step. The Kolkata Bench of the National Company Law Tribunal has now told the State Bank of India that the two things are not the same, and dismissed its insolvency petition against a guarantor because the bank skipped a step it thought it had already taken.

The petition was filed under Section 95(1) of the Code against Rajesh Sonthalia, who had stood as personal guarantor for Enfield Solar Energy Limited. SBI told the tribunal that Rs. 97.28 crore was outstanding as on 31 May 2025 and that it had done everything the Code required before moving against the guarantor. Sonthalia's defence was narrower than a dispute over the amount owed: he argued that SBI had never actually invoked the guarantee in the manner the guarantee deed itself demanded, and that without invocation there was nothing for a Section 95(1) petition to rest on.

SBI pointed to a notice it had issued on 25 May 2022 as proof that invocation had occurred. The tribunal went back to the guarantee deed to test that claim and found the notice wanting. The deed's clause on payment demands, the bench held, governed demands made to recover money already due; it was not drafted as, and could not be read as, a standalone clause invoking the guarantee itself. A notice that ticks the first box does not automatically tick the second, and SBI had produced nothing beyond the 2022 notice to show that a separate invocation had taken place.

From there the bench turned to the demand notice SBI had issued in Form B under Rule 7(1) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority for Insolvency Resolution Process for Personal Guarantors to Corporate Debtors) Rules, 2019. Banks routinely treat that notice as doing double duty, both demanding payment and putting the guarantor on notice that the guarantee is being called upon. The tribunal rejected that shortcut outright, holding that a Form B notice is a procedural step that follows invocation under the Code's scheme and cannot stand in for the invocation itself. It leaned on an NCLAT ruling to the same effect, treating the sequence as settled rather than open to debate.

The consequence followed mechanically once the tribunal had made that finding. Without a valid invocation on record, there was no debt that had crystallised against the guarantor in the manner Section 95(1) contemplates, so the Form B notice issued on the strength of the 2022 letter could not support the petition that came after it. The tribunal dismissed SBI's petition as not maintainable, leaving the bank free to invoke the guarantee properly and start again, but not to treat the time and paperwork already spent as sufficient.

The ruling will matter well beyond this one guarantor. Guarantee deeds in India are rarely drafted as a single undertaking; they typically separate the clause describing when a demand for payment may be made from any clause, express or implied, that deals with invoking the guarantee as a distinct legal act. Lenders who have grown used to firing off one notice that is meant to cover both bases now have a tribunal ruling telling them that courts will read the deed clause by clause, and that convenience is not a substitute for what the document actually says.

For personal guarantors facing recovery action, the decision offers a concrete point to test before conceding that an insolvency petition is in order. It is worth asking, clause by clause, whether the notice a bank is relying on actually did what the guarantee deed required of an invocation, or whether it only did what the deed required of a payment demand. The two are not interchangeable, and a bank that has only done the latter has, on this ruling, not yet earned the right to bring a Section 95(1) petition at all.

The dismissal is also a reminder that the burden of proving invocation sits with the creditor, not the guarantor. SBI's case did not fail because the underlying debt was disputed or because the guarantee itself was unenforceable; it failed because the bank could not show a document doing the specific job of invocation. Creditors moving against guarantors in Kolkata, and before benches that follow this reasoning elsewhere, would do well to keep a separate, clearly worded invocation notice on file rather than relying on the demand notice to carry that weight as well.

Why It Matters

Banks and other lenders holding personal guarantees, including from NRI or HNI guarantors of Indian corporate borrowers, cannot treat a Form B demand notice under the IBC as automatically invoking the guarantee. The guarantee deed's own invocation requirement must be satisfied first and separately, or an insolvency petition against the guarantor can be dismissed as not maintainable regardless of how large the underlying debt is.
