Misuse of Authority by Bank Officials Undermines Customer Trust, Holds Gujarat High Court, Denying Bail

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw and supremetoday.ai. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Yogendra Vijay Bahadur Singh held the title of Cluster Sales Head at Axis Bank. The allegation against him, which the Gujarat High Court was asked to weigh at the anticipatory bail stage, is that he used that position to help reactivate a dormant account belonging to a widow, bypassing the bank's own internal protocols to do it, in coordination with another bank official.

The widow's account mattered to her beyond the ordinary sense in which any account matters to its holder. She had been receiving financial assistance under a government scheme, routed through that account, and the allegation is that the reactivation, and what followed it, diverted funds that scheme was meant to protect. Singh sought anticipatory bail, framing the dispute as essentially a banking matter between a customer and an institution, the kind of disagreement usually worked out through internal grievance channels or, at worst, civil proceedings rather than custodial interrogation.

Dormant accounts exist as a protective category precisely because an account that has seen no activity for a long stretch is treated as more vulnerable to exactly this kind of diversion, and banks generally build in extra verification steps before reviving one. The allegation here is that those steps were skipped rather than satisfied, which is what separates the case from an ordinary disputed transaction where a bank's process was followed but a customer later disagreed with the outcome.

Faced with that framing, the Court declined to treat the matter as an ordinary customer dispute. It reasoned that misuse of authority by bank officials strikes at the foundation of how financial institutions actually function, since customers hand over money and personal financial detail to a bank precisely because they trust that the people administering their accounts will follow the institution's own safeguards rather than override them. Where an official is alleged to have bypassed those safeguards for a vulnerable customer's account, reactivating it outside the bank's ordinary process rather than through it, the Court found that custodial interrogation, not anticipatory bail, was the appropriate course while the allegations are investigated.

Denying the application, the Court left Singh exposed to arrest and interrogation on the underlying allegations, rejecting the submission that the matter could be resolved as a banking dispute without further custodial inquiry into how the account was actually reactivated and where the funds that followed were directed.

The order is a reminder, aimed as much at banks as at individual officials, that courts are not inclined to treat allegations of internal-protocol bypass as mere operational lapses once a vulnerable customer's funds are shown to have moved as a result. Banks increasingly rely on internal authorisation layers precisely to prevent a single official from unilaterally reactivating dormant accounts or approving unusual transactions, and this order signals that where those layers are shown to have been circumvented, the official responsible cannot expect a court to treat the resulting loss as a civil or regulatory matter alone.

Customers relying on government welfare payments routed through a bank account are, in practice, among the least likely to notice unauthorised activity quickly, since many such accounts see limited independent monitoring by the account holder between disbursement cycles. That vulnerability is part of why the Court treated the alleged conduct here as more than an internal process failure; an official exploiting the gap between disbursement and detection is exploiting a weakness the welfare scheme itself cannot easily guard against on its own.

For banks, the practical lesson is in audit trails: an institution that can show an official acted outside, rather than through, its approval processes is in a stronger position both to disown the conduct internally and to support a criminal complaint against the individual responsible, rather than being left to absorb reputational and financial exposure for conduct its own systems were designed to prevent. For compliance teams, the order is worth citing internally as a sign that courts will look past a formal customer-dispute label where a bank official's own conduct, not the underlying transaction, is what is actually in question.

Why It Matters

Banks depend on internal authorisation controls to stop individual officials from unilaterally acting on sensitive accounts, particularly dormant accounts linked to government welfare payments. This order shows that courts will treat an official's alleged bypass of those controls as a serious breach of trust warranting custodial interrogation, rather than as an internal banking dispute to be resolved through grievance channels. Banking compliance and litigation teams should treat this as support for pursuing both internal disciplinary action and criminal complaints against officials who circumvent authorisation protocols, rather than limiting the response to account-level remediation for the affected customer.
