Allahabad HC: Family Courts Can Relax Evidence Rules, Not the Right to Cross-Examine

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw and drishtijudiciary.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A mother who had been fighting for custody of her two sons found her plea rejected by the Family Court on the strength of an affidavit she never got to test. The Allahabad High Court has now set that order aside, holding that a Family Court cannot decide a contested custody claim on one party's affidavit evidence while denying the other side any chance to cross-examine the person who swore it. The bench of Justices Saral Srivastava and Sudhanshu Chauhan made the point in plain terms: the relaxed procedure Parliament gave to Family Courts was never meant to let a parent's say-so go unchallenged.

The parties, reported only as P and P to protect the identity of the children involved, had married in Varanasi and had two sons born a few years into the marriage. By the time the matter reached the Family Court, the relationship had broken down and the mother sought custody. The father opposed her plea through a written statement and a supporting affidavit, but did not step into the witness box. His evidence was eventually closed because of his own repeated defaults in appearing. The Family Court nonetheless went on to decide the custody question against the mother, treating the father's affidavit as sufficient material on its own.

The High Court's answer turned on Section 14 of the Family Courts Act, 1984, which lets a Family Court receive any report, statement, document, information or other matter it considers may assist it in dealing with a dispute, whether or not that material would be admissible under the Indian Evidence Act. LiveLaw's report of the ruling records the Court's reasoning that this provision loosens the technical rules of admissibility a civil court would otherwise apply, but it does not touch the separate and more basic requirement that a party be given a fair opportunity to meet the case against them. Proof and natural justice, the Court held, survive Section 14 intact even where strict admissibility does not.

Applied to the facts, that distinction decided the appeal. The father's affidavit was admissible under the relaxed regime because Section 14 permitted the Family Court to look at it. But admissibility is not the same as reliability, and reliability could only be tested by cross-examination. Because the father avoided the witness box and his evidence was closed on account of his own defaults, the mother never had the chance to question him on the very statements the Family Court used to decide against her. The High Court treated that gap as fatal, holding that accepting untested affidavit evidence over a party's objection, where the opposing party has had no opportunity to cross-examine, amounts to a denial of natural justice regardless of how liberal the Family Court's evidentiary powers otherwise are.

The ruling sits comfortably alongside other Allahabad High Court decisions this year that have pulled Family Courts back toward basic fairness even while confirming their wide procedural latitude. A September ruling in a Section 125 CrPC maintenance matter held that a named co-adulterer must be heard before any adverse finding is recorded against him, on the same logic that summary or informal procedure is not a licence to bypass a hearing. Read together, these rulings tell Family Courts that Section 14 is a tool for cutting through paperwork and technical objections to documents, not a shortcut past the opposing party's right to challenge the evidence actually being used to decide the case.

For family law practitioners, the practical lesson is to insist on cross-examination whenever the other side's case rests on an affidavit making contested factual claims, particularly allegations going to a parent's fitness or conduct. A Family Court that closes a party's evidence because of that party's own defaults cannot then use the affidavit filed by that same party as a ready substitute for tested testimony, at least not where the opposing side specifically sought the opportunity to cross-examine and was refused it. Litigants on the receiving end of an adverse custody or maintenance order built on an affidavit they never got to question now have a clear Allahabad High Court precedent to invoke.

The decision also offers a reminder for the lawyers who draft Family Court pleadings. Simply filing an affidavit and then letting the deponent default out of appearing is not a safe route to a favourable order, since the opposing party can get that evidence excluded from consideration on appeal by showing it was accepted without a chance to test it. Filing an affidavit is step one; standing by it under cross-examination, or accepting the consequences of not doing so, is the part that actually proves a case before a Family Court.

Why It Matters

Family Courts routinely rely on affidavits and informal material under Section 14 of the Family Courts Act, and this ruling draws a clear line between that relaxed admissibility and the separate right to cross-examine on contested facts. Parents, and the lawyers representing them, now have Allahabad High Court authority that a custody or maintenance order built on untested affidavit evidence, where cross-examination was sought and refused, cannot stand.
