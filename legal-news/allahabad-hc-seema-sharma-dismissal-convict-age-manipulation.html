Allahabad HC Upholds Dismissal of Court Clerk Who Altered a Convict's Age in Records

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw and theprint.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A court record is only as reliable as the person keeping it, and the Allahabad High Court has just upheld the dismissal of an Assistant Record Keeper who treated that trust as something to be rewritten for a convict's benefit. The ruling is a plain statement that tampering with judicial records, even years after the fact, will not be forgiven because the employee herself did not benefit financially from it.

Seema Sharma worked as an Assistant Record Keeper at the Muzaffarnagar district judgeship. The case against her concerned a record from a 1977 sessions trial, in which a man named Juber had been convicted of murder and sentenced to life imprisonment in November 1978. The original trial record gave his age as 19 years at the time. Decades later, in response to a departmental questionnaire dated 13 September 2000, the same record showed his age as 38 years, a change that had no basis in the original file.

The alteration was not a clerical slip without consequence. The Uttar Pradesh government runs a scheme allowing premature release of prisoners who have crossed the age of 60, and the change to Juber's recorded age was aimed squarely at helping him qualify for that scheme years before he would otherwise have been eligible. Departmental proceedings were initiated against Seema Sharma over this manipulation, and she was dismissed from service as a result. She challenged that dismissal through a writ petition before the Allahabad High Court.

Justice Anish Kumar Gupta was unsparing in rejecting her challenge. The judgment described the alteration as direct interference with the administration of justice, treating it as serious misconduct in the truest sense rather than a bureaucratic irregularity. The court held that no lenient view could be taken of such conduct, whatever personal circumstances the employee might point to in mitigation. On that basis, the writ petition was dismissed and the dismissal from service was left standing.

The judgment does not suggest that Seema Sharma gained money from what she did, and nothing in the reporting of the case indicates any payment changed hands. That absence of a direct financial motive did not help her case. The High Court's reasoning rests instead on what the falsified record was capable of doing: letting a life convict access an early release scheme on the strength of an age that was simply invented. An administrative record that decides whether a prisoner walks free years ahead of schedule is not a place where a clerk gets to exercise personal discretion about what the file should say.

For anyone who works with court or prison records, whether as a record keeper, a clerk or an administrative officer, the case draws a hard line around what counts as serious misconduct justifying dismissal rather than a lesser penalty. Altering an entry that affects a convict's eligibility for release goes to the heart of why those records exist at all, and the ruling leaves no room for the argument that the passage of time, or the absence of personal gain, should soften the consequence.

The case also illustrates how long these disputes can run through the system. The questionnaire that exposed the altered age was answered in September 2000, the dismissal followed through departmental proceedings after that, and the writ petition challenging it carried the number Writ-A No. 71889 of 2010, meaning it took roughly a decade after dismissal, and years more after that, before the Allahabad High Court finally disposed of it in November 2026. For institutions managing disciplinary matters involving record manipulation, the delay is itself a reminder that departmental decisions on dismissal, once properly reasoned on the gravity of the misconduct, are likely to be upheld by courts even after such a long interval, provided the underlying finding of tampering is not in doubt.

None of this depended on proving that Seema Sharma personally profited, which is the part of the ruling that matters most for anyone drafting a departmental charge sheet over record tampering. A disciplinary authority does not need to establish a bribe or a kickback to justify dismissal; showing that the record was deliberately falsified, and that the falsification was capable of producing a specific, serious consequence such as an undeserved early release, is enough on its own. Courts reviewing such dismissals on writ jurisdiction will look at whether the departmental finding of tampering itself holds up, not at whether a motive beyond the tampering has also been proved.

Why It Matters

Government and judicial employees who handle official records sometimes assume that altering an entry without personal financial gain will be treated leniently, but this ruling confirms that courts view tampering with a convict's age in prison records as a grave form of interference with the justice system, warranting dismissal regardless of motive or the years elapsed since the act. It is a caution for anyone in a record-keeping role: the seriousness of an alteration is judged by what it enables, not by what the employee personally gained from making it.
