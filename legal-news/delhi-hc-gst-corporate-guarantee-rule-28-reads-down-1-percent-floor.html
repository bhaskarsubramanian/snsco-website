Delhi HC: GST Hits Free Guarantees, Not the 1% Floor

Source: Originally reported by Bar and Bench, LiveLaw Biz, LiveLaw Biz (2) and Law Chakra. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A Division Bench of the Delhi High Court, hearing a batch of writ petitions led by Sterlite Power Transmission Limited, answered two questions that group companies and their lenders have been arguing about for three years. On 17 November it held that a parent or promoter company standing guarantee for a related company's bank loan is a taxable supply under GST even when no guarantee fee is charged, but that the government's rule fixing the taxable value at a flat one percent of the guaranteed amount cannot be enforced as an inflexible minimum.

The dispute goes back to lenders insisting on comfort from the promoter group before sanctioning working capital or term loans to an associate company. Many groups gave such guarantees without charging anything, on the view that a guarantee between related companies with no fee attached could not be a 'supply' at all, and that GST needs consideration to attach. The department took a different position under Rule 28(2) of the CGST Rules, treating one percent per annum of the guaranteed sum as the deemed value regardless of what was actually charged, or whether anything changed hands.

On taxability, the Bench leaned on Schedule I of the CGST Act, which deems transactions between related persons to be supplies even without consideration. Extending a corporate guarantee, it held, is a service capable of being valued, and the absence of a fee does not take it outside GST once the parties are related. This puts the Delhi court at odds with a line of Bombay High Court orders that had gone the other way on similar facts, and closer to the Gujarat High Court's view in Torrent Power Limited that intra-group guarantees are taxable.

The valuation question drew a different response from the Bench, which parted ways with the Gujarat court's own reasoning. Gujarat had read down the words 'whichever is higher' in Rule 28(2) to keep the provision from reaching guarantees given before 26 October 2023. Delhi instead attacked the one percent figure itself: treated as an unrebuttable minimum applied without regard to the facts, it held, the rule is arbitrary and offends Article 14, since two guarantees of the same face value can carry very different economic weight depending on what other security the lender already holds. The Bench therefore read Rule 28(2) down to a rebuttable starting point. A taxpayer may displace the one percent figure by placing cogent and verifiable material before the assessing officer to show the guarantee's actual open market value is lower, and the officer is required to examine that material rather than apply the deemed rate mechanically.

That conclusion was paired with confirmation of the rule's prospective reach. Rule 28(2) catches guarantees that are executed, renewed, extended or enhanced on or after 26 October 2023; an existing guarantee that nobody touches thereafter does not fall within it merely because it continues on the books. The Bench allowed the batch only in part, leaving some petitioners to carry the valuation exercise back to the department on their own facts, which is why the outcome reads as a mixed result rather than an outright win for the companies that filed it.

Three High Courts have now taken visibly different paths through the same Rule 28(2). Bombay has found no GST where no consideration passes, Gujarat has limited the fixed method to guarantees from after October 2023, and Delhi has kept the levy but struck down the floor as a rigid minimum. A day before the Delhi judgment, on 16 November, the Supreme Court issued notice on an appeal against the Gujarat ruling and stayed recovery of the disputed demands pending that appeal, so the question is still open at the top rather than settled by any one High Court.

For an Indian company that has stood guarantee for a group entity's loan without charging a fee, including an Indian subsidiary of a foreign parent or a family business backing a sister concern, this judgment is not the last word, only the latest one. Lender sanction letters, the security already available to the lender and any comparable market quotes for similar guarantees are now the material that will decide the tax bill, not an assumed one percent, and groups that have simply paid on the deemed value without testing it may want to revisit that practice while the larger question sits before the Supreme Court.

Why It Matters

Corporate groups routinely have one company stand guarantee for another's bank loan without charging a fee, a practice common to Indian subsidiaries of foreign parents and to family businesses backing sister concerns. This ruling confirms GST can still apply to such guarantees, but takes away the department's ability to insist on one percent of the guaranteed sum as an automatic value; a guarantor can now contest that figure with real evidence, which affects how such arrangements should be priced and documented going forward.
