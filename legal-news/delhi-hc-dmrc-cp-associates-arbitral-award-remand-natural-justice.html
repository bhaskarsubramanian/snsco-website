Delhi HC: Single Judge Must Deal With Each Objection to an Arbitral Award, Not Just Reproduce It

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon and LiveLaw Biz. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

C.P. & Associates Pvt. Ltd. was awarded a contract in 2008 for architectural finishing work at seven elevated stations on the Inderlok-Mundka line of Phase-II of the Delhi Metro, a line that runs through some of the most congested parts of west and northwest Delhi. The contract, executed on 14 October 2008, was worth Rs 33,76,81,216.12 and was to be completed within fourteen months of the letter of acceptance issued that July. It never got there. The two sides blamed each other for the delay, DMRC saying the contractor moved too slowly and brought inadequate resources to site, the contractor saying it never got timely work fronts or drawings to work with. DMRC issued a fourteen-day notice on 21 May 2010 and terminated the contract less than three weeks later, on 4 June 2010.

The contractor did not move to arbitration immediately. Its claims went first to DMRC's own engineer under the dispute clause of the contract, a reference made only on 11 May 2013, nearly three years after termination. Arbitration followed in August that year. By the time the tribunal ruled, on 28 September 2018, it had thirty-two claims before it. Seven were allowed, three were partly allowed, and twenty-two were rejected outright. The contractor was unhappy enough with the result to challenge it under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, picking out eight specific findings for attack, among them unpaid work under the bill of quantities, forfeiture of the performance bank guarantee, rejected extra items, a housekeeping recovery, and the arbitration costs themselves.

A Single Judge dismissed the Section 34 petition on 4 May 2023. What the Division Bench found, on appeal under Section 37, was not that the Single Judge had got the law wrong, but that the order never actually engaged with what the contractor was arguing. It recorded the governing principles correctly enough and set out the parties' broad contentions, but then moved straight to reproducing passages from the award and concluding that the award matched the contract. The claim-by-claim objections the contractor had raised, on the forfeiture, on the housekeeping deduction, on the liquidated damages for the third and fourth extensions of time, were never taken up one by one and tested against the record.

DMRC's defence on appeal leaned on the narrow room Section 37 leaves for interference. The Bench did not dispute that principle. It referred to the Supreme Court's rulings in NHAI v. M. Hakeem and Fiza Developers for the point that courts exercising this jurisdiction are not meant to re-examine the merits of an award, only to check that the Section 34 process was properly gone through. That is exactly where the gap lay here. An appellate court reviewing a Section 34 order for patent illegality or perversity has nothing to review if the Section 34 order itself never decided the questions put to it. The Bench drew on HUDA v. Mehta Construction Co. and the Bombay Slum Redevelopment line of authority to hold that it could not, in an appeal, decide for the first time whether each objection met the high bar for setting aside an award. Doing so would deprive the contractor of the one level of scrutiny the statute actually promises it.

The Bench was direct about what this amounted to on the contractor's side: a failure by the Single Judge to deal with the specific Section 34 contentions that had been argued, which it treated as a breach of natural justice rather than a mere shortfall in drafting. It set aside the 4 May 2023 order in its entirety and sent the Section 34 petition back for fresh consideration, with each of the eight contested findings to be examined on its own terms. The judgment carries no view, expressed or implied, on which way those findings should go. The remand is procedural, not a verdict on the merits of the arbitral award.

For contractors and public sector corporations alike, cases built around metro and highway contracts tend to run for well over a decade from termination to a final word on an arbitral award, as this one has, from a 2010 termination to a 2018 award to a 2026 remand that still leaves the Section 34 petition open. The ruling is a reminder that getting a Section 34 petition dismissed is not the end of the road for the party resisting it if the order doing the dismissing skipped over the objections actually pressed. A judgment that states the law correctly but answers none of the specific points raised invites exactly this kind of remand, adding years rather than closing the file.

Why It Matters

Government and public sector entities executing infrastructure contracts face Section 34 challenges to arbitral awards as a matter of course, and the pace at which those challenges get resolved often decides how long a contractor waits to get paid or a corporation waits to close its books. This ruling signals that a Section 34 order which recites the law but does not work through each specific objection is vulnerable on appeal regardless of how the underlying award reads, adding years to disputes that are already old by the time they reach a court.
