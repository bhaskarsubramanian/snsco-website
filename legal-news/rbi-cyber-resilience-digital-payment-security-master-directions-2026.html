RBI Folds Non-Bank Payment Operators Into a Single Cyber Resilience Rulebook

Source: Originally reported by TaxGuru, TaxGuru (2), resources.probe42.in and lexplosion.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

The Reserve Bank of India issued its Cyber Resilience and Digital Payment Security Controls Master Directions, 2026 on 5 November 2026, replacing a 2024 framework that had already set out cyber governance expectations for non-bank payment system operators. The new Directions, numbered RBI/DPSS/2026-27/475, fold what had been a set of separate instructions on board-level oversight, system audits and vendor risk into one consolidated rulebook, and extend coverage to third-party switch application service providers that white-label ATM operators rely on but that the 2024 framework had not squarely addressed.

A predecessor framework, issued on 30 July 2024, already required non-bank payment system operators to appoint a Chief Information Security Officer, maintain board-level oversight of cyber risk, and manage risk arising from third-party vendors. The 2026 version keeps that structure in place but widens its reach to cover the switch application service providers that sit behind shared ATM networks, requiring white-label ATM operators to write cyber security obligations directly into their contracts with these providers and to give the Reserve Bank supervisory access to them, closing a gap where a critical piece of payment infrastructure sat one contractual layer removed from the regulator's direct reach.

Entities covered span most of the non-bank payment ecosystem: the National Payments Corporation of India, card networks, payment aggregators, prepaid payment instrument issuers, and white-label ATM operators, among other authorised non-bank payment system operators. For an industry that has grown rapidly since the 2024 framework was issued, bringing all of these categories under one consolidated set of Directions, rather than a patchwork of circulars issued at different times, gives compliance teams a single reference point for what the Reserve Bank expects of their cyber governance.

Compliance timelines differ by the size of the operator. Coverage of the Directions indicates that large and medium payment system operators are expected to comply from the date the Directions are published on the Reserve Bank's website, while small operators have been given until 1 April 2028 to fall in line. That is a tighter timeline for large and medium operators than the 2024 framework set, which had staggered compliance across April 2025 for large operators, April 2026 for medium ones, and April 2028 for small operators, suggesting the Reserve Bank expects operators already covered by the 2024 framework to have the groundwork in place.

Some of the operational detail reported alongside the Directions, including a six-hour window for reporting cyber incidents and a requirement to apply critical security patches immediately once a known attack vector is identified, appeared in only one source reviewed for this report and could not be independently corroborated against a second account before publication. Readers relying on the exact reporting window or patching timeline should check the Directions as published on the Reserve Bank's website rather than secondary summaries.

For payment aggregators, PPI issuers and white-label ATM operators already building out compliance under the 2024 framework, the practical task now is to map what they have in place against the consolidated 2026 text and identify what the new switch-provider and contractual-access requirements add. For smaller operators with until April 2028, the longer runway gives more room to plan, but the direction of travel, toward tighter contractual obligations on third-party infrastructure providers and faster compliance for the larger players, is unlikely to reverse before then.

White-label ATM operators are likely to feel the switch-provider requirement most directly, since many of them route transactions through a shared third-party switch rather than running that infrastructure themselves. Under the 2026 Directions, it will not be enough for such an operator to point to its own internal cyber controls; it will also have to show that its contract with the switch provider carries the security obligations the Directions require, and that the Reserve Bank can reach that provider directly for supervisory purposes rather than only through the operator. Renegotiating existing vendor contracts to add those terms, where they are not already there, is likely to be the single largest compliance task the 2026 Directions create for this category of operator.

Why It Matters

Non-bank payment operators, from UPI-linked apps to white-label ATM networks, sit outside the direct supervisory net that applies to banks, even though a breach at one of them can disrupt payments for millions of users. Consolidating cyber resilience rules into one set of Directions, and extending them to third-party switch providers behind shared ATM networks, closes a gap in RBI's oversight and gives large and medium operators a near-immediate compliance deadline rather than the staggered one the 2024 framework allowed.
