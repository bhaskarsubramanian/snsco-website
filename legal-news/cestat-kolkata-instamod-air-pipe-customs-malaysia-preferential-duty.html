CESTAT Kolkata: A Customs Demand Cannot Rest on Emails or a DRI Alert Never Shown to the Importer

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, TaxGuru and jurishour.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Instamod Air Pipe Pvt. Ltd. cleared a shipment of aluminium pipes, fittings, valves and brackets in February 2018, claiming preferential duty under the India-Malaysia Comprehensive Economic Cooperation Agreement on the strength of a certificate of origin from the exporting country. The Directorate of Revenue Intelligence did not open an investigation into the clearance until May 2021, more than three years later, and the department did not issue a show-cause notice until February 2023, almost five years after the goods had already left customs control. The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal's Kolkata Bench has now set aside the entire demand that followed.

The department's case rested on a demand for differential customs duty of ₹12.59 lakh under Section 28(4) of the Customs Act, along with interest under Section 28AA and an equivalent penalty under Section 114A, plus confiscation of the goods and a redemption fine. Section 28(4) lets the department invoke an extended, five-year limitation period only where it can show collusion, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts by the importer. The Tribunal found that the department had not established any of that against Instamod, and without it, the ordinary limitation period had long since run out by the time the notice was issued.

Two further planks of the department's case fell away on their own facts. The adjudicating authority had leaned on a set of emails to question the genuineness of the Malaysian certificate of origin, but those emails were never mentioned in the show-cause notice and were never shown to the importer for an explanation before the order was passed. The Tribunal treated that as a basic failure of natural justice: material an assessee has no chance to see or answer cannot be used to decide the case against it. Separately, the department had pointed to a DRI alert as a red flag on the consignment, but the Tribunal found the alert concerned different products altogether and had no bearing on the goods Instamod had actually imported.

On the central question of whether the certificate of origin itself was defective, the Tribunal held that the department had failed to show that Instamod knew of any problem with the certificate or had colluded with its Malaysian supplier to obtain one improperly. A certificate of origin issued by the exporting country's competent authority carries a presumption of regularity that a bare suspicion, built on material the importer was never shown, does not displace. With the extended limitation period unavailable and the factual basis for suspecting the certificate undermined, the demand for differential duty, interest and penalty could not survive, and the Tribunal set aside the confiscation and redemption fine as well, noting that the goods were in any case no longer available for confiscation.

The order sits alongside a run of recent CESTAT Kolkata rulings pushing back on customs demands built on investigations that start years after goods clear port and on evidence the importer never had a chance to answer. For importers trading under India's free trade agreements with ASEAN countries, including Malaysia, the ruling is a reminder that a certificate of origin cannot be second-guessed on the back of material kept outside the show-cause notice, and that the department's own delay in opening an investigation can itself defeat a demand once the extended limitation period is in question.

For customs practitioners in Kolkata and importers clearing goods through the port under preferential trade agreements, the case is a useful marker of how far a show-cause notice has to go in disclosing its evidentiary basis before any extended demand can stand. An investigation opened years after clearance, and a notice that leans on alerts or correspondence the importer never saw, both now carry a tribunal-level finding against them from the Eastern Zonal Bench.

The pattern in this order is not unique to Instamod's case. CESTAT Kolkata has, in recent months, set aside a run of demands where the department built its case on material gathered well after a consignment cleared port, then tried to fit that later material into an extended limitation period meant for genuine fraud or collusion. Each time, the Tribunal has asked the same basic question: did the department actually show, at the time of the notice, that the importer knew of or participated in the alleged wrongdoing, or did it simply assume bad faith because years had passed and a discrepancy had since turned up. Instamod's case answers that question the same way the others have, and gives importers moving goods through Kolkata port one more order to cite when a stale investigation surfaces as a sudden demand.

Why It Matters

Importers using preferential duty under India's free trade agreements face customs demands years after clearance, often built on intelligence alerts or internal correspondence never disclosed in the show-cause notice. This ruling confirms that such material cannot sustain a demand, that an unrelated DRI alert cannot be stretched to cover different goods, and that the department's own delay in starting an investigation can defeat reliance on the extended five-year limitation period.
