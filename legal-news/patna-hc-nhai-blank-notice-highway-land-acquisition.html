A Notice With No Number, No Date and No Land Details Is No Notice at All: Patna HC Fines NHAI ₹5,000

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw and LiveLaw (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

National Highway 527C needed land from a petitioner in Bihar, and NHAI sent him a communication telling him, in effect, to act on it. The trouble was that the communication carried no letter number, no date, and no description of which land it concerned or how much of it. The petitioner could not have complied with it even if he had wanted to, because it did not say what compliance required.

He had built on the part of his holding that remained unacquired, and the blank notice left him worried that NHAI or the district administration would treat it as a valid demand and move against his structures without ever telling him precisely what land they claimed and on what basis. With no reference number to track and no description of the land at stake, he had no way of knowing what response, if any, the notice actually called for. He approached the Patna High Court seeking protection against any such action until a proper demarcation was carried out.

Before Justice Rajiv Roy, both the State and NHAI did something that single judges in land acquisition matters do not often get from either side: they conceded the obvious. The State and NHAI fairly acknowledged that the communication could not be treated as a valid notice at all. There was no serious attempt to defend the document on its own terms, because it could not be defended; a notice that omits its own reference number, date and the extent of land it purports to cover fails at the threshold, before any question of compliance or limitation even arises.

With that concession on record, the Court's order followed naturally. It directed NHAI to issue a proper notice, one that actually identifies the land, cites a reference and carries a date, and then to carry out a joint demarcation of the petitioner's unacquired land together with the petitioner and the State authorities. Until that demarcation fixed the boundary between what had been acquired and what had not, the petitioner's structures on the unacquired portion were not to be touched.

The Court did not stop at simply correcting the paperwork. It imposed costs of Rs 5,000 on NHAI, payable to the petitioner within four weeks, for the mental agony the blank notice had caused him over land he had held and built on for years without any clarity about its status. The Court went further and said NHAI could recover that amount from the official responsible for issuing the defective communication in the first place, after fixing responsibility internally. A modest sum in absolute terms, but a pointed one: the cost of carelessness in land acquisition paperwork is meant to land on the desk that generated it, not simply get absorbed as a departmental expense.

Land acquisition for national highway projects runs on a tight statutory sequence of notifications, declarations and individual notices, each step meant to give an affected landowner a real and informed chance to respond before the next one locks the process further in place. A separate Patna High Court ruling around the same period went further still, quashing an entire compensation award under the National Highways Act for NH-106 because individual notice under Section 3G(3) had never reached the landowners at all, holding that the absence of personal notice was fatal to the whole acquisition process rather than a curable defect.

Read together, the two rulings tell project authorities the same thing from different angles: a defective or missing individual notice is not a technicality a court will wave through once construction pressure builds. One case stopped a compensation award in its tracks; the other stopped short of that but still extracted an admission, a correction order and a personal cost. For a landowner anywhere along a highway corridor who receives a communication that does not clearly say what land is wanted and why, the lesson is to ask the obvious question before assuming the notice binds anything at all.

Why It Matters

National highway projects routinely rely on individual notices to landowners at various stages of acquisition, and this ruling confirms courts will not let a defective notice, whether blank or missing altogether, stand as the basis for any coercive action against a landowner's structures or claims. Project authorities need to treat notice drafting as a compliance step with real consequences, not paperwork, since both the notice and the official who issued it can be held to account.
