Delhi HC: Wife's Refusal to Sign Mutual Divorce Papers After Mediated Deal Isn't Contempt

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon, LiveLaw and Verdictum. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Vikas and Sonu had a divorce petition pending before the Family Court at Karkardooma when the court referred them, as it routinely does, to its mediation centre. On 17 October 2025 the two signed a settlement there, agreeing to seek divorce by mutual consent under Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act. The terms were specific: a First Motion petition to be filed by 10 November 2025, a Second Motion to follow once the statutory waiting period ran out, and a payment of Rs 3,60,000 from Vikas to Sonu, split into two equal instalments of Rs 1,80,000 each, one due when statements were recorded on the First Motion and the other at the Second Motion.

Eleven days later, on 28 October 2025, both parties appeared before the Family Court in the pending HMA petition and had their statements recorded, endorsing the settlement. Vikas then withdrew his original divorce petition, treating the settlement as the agreed route forward. What he says happened next is that his lawyers prepared the First Motion papers and the supporting affidavits and sent them to Sonu's counsel for signature, and that she simply would not sign despite being asked repeatedly. He says he borrowed the first instalment of Rs 1,80,000, has been paying interest on that loan since, and even got a demand draft ready in December 2025 and renewed it again in March 2026 when nothing moved. Sonu's side did not dispute entering into the settlement. Her counsel told the court plainly that she had since had a change of heart and was no longer inclined to go through with a mutual consent divorce, which is why she would not put her signature to the joint petition.

Vikas filed a contempt petition over this, arguing that refusing to honour a settlement recorded before the Family Court itself amounted to wilful disobedience of that court's order. Justice Neena Bansal Krishna did not see it that way. The starting point for her was the Supreme Court's long-standing position in Sureshta Devi v. Om Prakash, which holds that mutual consent for a Section 13B divorce has to survive all the way through to the decree, not just at the point the petition is filed. Either spouse remains free to pull out before that final stage, whatever they signed earlier in the process.

The Court also leaned on a more recent authority closer to home, a Division Bench ruling in Chhavi Agarwal v. Anurag Goel, which had already settled the point that refusing to go ahead with the Second Motion does not by itself amount to wilful disobedience of anything. Contempt jurisdiction, the Bench had said there, cannot be used as a backdoor way of forcing a reluctant spouse into a mutual consent divorce. Justice Bansal Krishna applied that reasoning squarely to Sonu's case. Her having second thoughts and deciding against a mutual consent divorce, the Court held, did not amount to wilful and deliberate disobedience of anything, and the essential ingredients of civil contempt under Section 2(b) of the Contempt of Courts Act were simply not made out.

On the money side, the Court noted that Sonu had not actually received anything under the settlement. The first instalment was tied to the First Motion stage, and since that stage never happened, the obligation to pay, and correspondingly any obligation on her part that might have been triggered by payment, had not arisen either. The Court was careful to say that settling a dispute before a mediation centre and placing that settlement on the Family Court's record does not, on its own, establish wilful disobedience of a court order merely because one side later balks at performing it.

None of this left Vikas without options. The Court expressly recorded that he remained free to pursue other remedies available to him for enforcing the settlement or protecting his legal rights, just not through contempt proceedings aimed at compelling Sonu to divorce him by mutual consent. The contempt petition was dismissed along with all pending applications.

Mediated settlements in matrimonial disputes are common enough in Delhi's Family Courts, and this ruling is a plain statement of where their limits lie. A settlement that commits both spouses to a mutual consent divorce does not itself bind either of them to see that divorce through, because the law treats ongoing consent, right up to the Second Motion, as something neither party can be made to give against their will. A spouse left out of pocket or out of a bargained-for outcome has civil remedies to chase, recovery of money paid, perhaps a claim grounded in the settlement itself, but contempt of court is not one of them.

Why It Matters

Family Courts across Delhi routinely refer matrimonial disputes to mediation, and settlements reached there often promise a mutual consent divorce as part of the package. This ruling confirms that such a promise does not survive a change of heart, however inconvenient that is for the other spouse who may have already paid or performed part of the bargain, and that contempt petitions are not a shortcut around a spouse's statutory right to withdraw consent before the decree is passed.
