NCLT Delhi Fines SREI Rs 1 Crore For Using IBC To Pressure JBJ Technologies Over Disputed Guarantees

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

SREI Equipment Finance Limited had filed a Section 7 petition against JBJ Technologies Limited, asking the National Company Law Tribunal's New Delhi Bench to admit the company into insolvency on the strength of two corporate guarantees. JBJ Technologies denied ever executing or authorising either guarantee. It then did something a company accused of default rarely manages to pull off: it got the petition dismissed and the petitioner penalised, not merely non-suited.

JBJ Technologies moved an application under Section 65 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, read with Rule 11 of the NCLT Rules, 2016, asking the Tribunal to throw out SREI's petition as having been filed fraudulently and for a purpose other than the genuine resolution of insolvency. Section 65 exists precisely for this situation, where insolvency proceedings are suspected of being used as a recovery tool against a company that disputes the underlying debt altogether, rather than as a mechanism to resolve a genuine default.

A Section 7 petition is ordinarily meant for a financial creditor with a clean, well-documented default to place a corporate debtor into the resolution process quickly, without the Tribunal going into disputed questions of fact the way a civil court would. That speed is exactly what makes the provision attractive to a creditor willing to use the threat of insolvency as leverage, since the prospect of a company's board losing control of management, even temporarily, tends to concentrate minds faster than a recovery suit ever could. Section 65 is the Code's answer to that risk, letting a tribunal look behind the petition itself once a debtor shows the proceeding was never really about resolving insolvency.

The Tribunal's reasoning, as recorded in the order, rested on the guarantee documents themselves. The columns meant to carry JBJ Technologies' signature and corporate stamp as guarantor were missing them. SREI pressed ahead with the insolvency petition despite JBJ's denial of the guarantees and without producing the board resolution that would have authorised them in the first place. The order also notes that SREI did not place certain correspondence bearing on the dispute before the Tribunal.

Taken individually, each of these gaps might have been explained away. Taken together, the Tribunal held, they showed a pattern: a financial creditor relying on guarantees it could not properly establish, pushing ahead regardless of a clear denial, and withholding material correspondence, all pointing toward insolvency being used to pressure JBJ Technologies into paying rather than to resolve an actual default. On that finding, the petition met the test for fraudulent or malicious initiation under Section 65.

The consequence was twofold. The Tribunal dismissed SREI's Section 7 petition outright, so JBJ Technologies never went into the corporate insolvency resolution process on the strength of the disputed guarantees. It then imposed a penalty of one crore rupees on SREI, the kind of monetary consequence Section 65 is meant to carry so that filing an insolvency petition is not treated as a cost-free way to squeeze a company that disputes what it owes.

Reports of the order do not record whether SREI intends to appeal to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal, and no further development had surfaced by the time this piece was prepared. The order stands, for now, as a one crore rupee penalty and a dismissed petition. Nothing in the reporting suggests the Tribunal went on to examine whether JBJ Technologies owed SREI money on any other, undisputed basis; the ruling is confined to the guarantees SREI actually relied on to seek admission into insolvency.

For companies on either side of a guarantee dispute, the order is a useful marker of where the line sits. A financial creditor holding a corporate guarantee it cannot produce properly executed, facing an outright denial from the alleged guarantor, takes a real risk in filing under Section 7 regardless. Equally, a company genuinely denying a guarantee now has a concrete, recent precedent for what it needs to show to get an insolvency petition thrown out as an abuse of process rather than merely defended on the merits.

Why It Matters

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code gives financial creditors a fast route to pressure a corporate debtor, which is exactly why Section 65 penalises its fraudulent or malicious use. This order shows a tribunal applying that provision with a one crore rupee penalty, not just dismissal, which matters to any business that has had an insolvency petition filed against it over a disputed or improperly executed guarantee, and to any lender weighing whether its paperwork can withstand that kind of scrutiny before filing.
