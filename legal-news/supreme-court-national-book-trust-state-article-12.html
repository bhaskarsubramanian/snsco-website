Supreme Court: National Book Trust Is 'State' Under Article 12 Despite Society Registration

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw and Verdictum. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Whether a government-funded body counts as 'State' under Article 12 of the Constitution decides whether the people dealing with it can drag it into a writ petition at all, and the Supreme Court has just confirmed that registering such a body as a society is no shield against that finding. The appeal in this case came from National Book Trust, India, and it lost.

National Book Trust was set up through Government of India resolutions dated 15 June 1957 and 28 September 1962, and it has functioned since under the administrative umbrella of the Ministry of Education. A.R. Abdul Gaffar's case against the Trust reached the Delhi High Court, where a Full Bench held that the Trust is an 'other authority' within the meaning of Article 12, bringing it within the reach of writ jurisdiction in the same way a government department would be. National Book Trust appealed that finding to the Supreme Court.

The bench of Justice Prashant Kumar Mishra and Justice Augustine George Masih dismissed the appeal and affirmed the Full Bench's view. The Court's reasoning turned on how much control the government actually exercises over the body in practice, not on the label the body carries on paper. It found that the National Book Trust is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by the government, and that this control is both particular to the Trust and pervasive across how it operates. On that finding, the Trust qualifies as an 'other authority' under Article 12.

Registering a body as a society, the Court was equally clear, does not take it outside Article 12 where the government's control over it is pervasive in this way. A society's separate legal existence on paper does not answer the real question, which is who actually controls its finances, its functions and its administration day to day. The judgment also addressed, and rejected, the argument that a body generating its own revenue should escape Article 12 scrutiny on that basis alone; revenue generation is a relevant fact but not a determinative one once pervasive government control is otherwise established.

At the same time, the Court drew a boundary around what the Article 12 finding actually changes. Being declared 'State' does not turn National Book Trust into a government department, and it does not convert its employees into civil servants. The practical effect of the ruling is narrower than it might first sound: it opens the Trust to constitutional scrutiny and writ remedies for actions that affect fundamental rights, without rewriting the Trust's employees' service conditions or giving them the status and protections that come with being part of the civil service.

For anyone dealing with an autonomous body set up and funded by the government but structured as a registered society, this distinction is the one worth carrying away. Employees, contractors, grantees and members of the public who deal with such bodies can rely on this ruling to argue that the body is answerable through a writ petition, since the Supreme Court has again confirmed that financial and administrative dependence on government, not the legal form a body takes, decides Article 12 status. Service claims that depend on being recognised as a government employee are a different matter entirely, and this judgment does not help there.

The case is also a reminder of how long constitutional questions of this kind can sit in the system before reaching final resolution. The underlying appeal was numbered as Civil Appeal No. 14369 of 2015, meaning roughly eleven years passed between the appeal being filed and the Supreme Court finally disposing of it in November 2026, with the Delhi High Court's Full Bench ruling under challenge for all of that time. Bodies structured like National Book Trust, and anyone litigating against them, now have a current, authoritative Supreme Court statement on where they stand under Article 12, even if the practical consequences for their own employees' service status remain governed by separate rules.

Why It Matters

Many government-funded bodies are set up as registered societies partly in the hope of operating outside the reach of writ jurisdiction that applies to government departments, and this ruling confirms that registration alone will not achieve that if the government's financial and administrative control over the body is pervasive in practice. For anyone with a grievance against such a body, whether an employee, a contractor or a member of the public affected by its decisions, the judgment strengthens the case for bringing a writ petition rather than being told the body is purely private.
