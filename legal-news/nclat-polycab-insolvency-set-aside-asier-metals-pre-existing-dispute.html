IBC & Insolvency · 30 October 2026

NCLAT Frees Polycab From Insolvency, Says Vendor's Rs 2.78 Crore Claim Was Already Disputed

Source: Originally reported by Bar and Bench, sahi.com, businessupturn.com and aninews.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Asier Metals Private Limited filed a Section 9 petition against Polycab India Limited claiming operational dues of about Rs 2.78 crore. The NCLT at Ahmedabad admitted the petition on 7 October 2026, started the corporate insolvency resolution process against one of the country's larger listed cable and wire manufacturers, and put an interim resolution professional in charge in place of the company's own board. Bharat Ajay Jaisinghani, a suspended director of Polycab, appealed to the NCLAT within days.

The appellate tribunal moved quickly on the interim relief. On 9 October 2026 it stayed the NCLT's admission order and the insolvency process that had followed from it, and directed that the disputed amount be deposited, without prejudice to either side's rights, while the appeal was heard out. That kept Polycab's board in charge of the company and its stock trading without the shadow of an ongoing CIRP, but it left the real question open: had the NCLT been right to admit the petition at all.

On 30 October 2026, a three-member bench headed by Officiating Chairperson Justice Yogesh Khanna answered that question against the operational creditor. The bench held that the NCLT had admitted the petition without properly examining Polycab's own correspondence with Asier Metals, correspondence that the company said recorded a dispute over the claim well before the Section 9 notice was issued. Under Section 9(5)(ii)(d) of the Code, an adjudicating authority must reject an operational creditor's application where the corporate debtor has given notice of a dispute, or where the record otherwise shows a dispute pending between the parties before the demand notice went out. The NCLAT found that test was met here and that the NCLT had brushed the correspondence aside instead of weighing it.

What the ruling does is narrow but immediate. It sets aside the admission order, closes the CIRP that had begun against Polycab, and lets the company go back to functioning through its own board rather than an interim resolution professional. What it does not do is decide who actually owes what to whom. The NCLAT was careful to say the underlying dispute over the Rs 2.78 crore claim remains open between Polycab and Asier Metals, and that any resolution of that liability will have to come from a forum better suited to trying the dispute on its facts, not from an insolvency court whose only job at the admission stage is to check whether a dispute exists, not to decide who wins it.

That distinction is the part of the order with the wider reach. Operational creditors sometimes use a Section 9 petition less as a genuine insolvency filing and more as a pressure tactic, betting that the threat of a CIRP, with its moratorium and loss of management control, will push a solvent company to settle a disputed bill rather than fight it out. The Code was not written for that purpose, and the pre-existing dispute bar in Section 9(5)(ii)(d) exists precisely to stop it from being used that way. Where a corporate debtor can point to correspondence, however informal, that shows it contested the claim before the demand notice arrived, an adjudicating authority is not free to treat the dispute as an afterthought raised only to defeat the petition.

For listed companies facing a vendor dispute of modest size relative to their balance sheet, the order is a reminder that an insolvency petition is not a shortcut past ordinary civil or commercial litigation over the amount owed. It is also a caution to NCLT benches hearing Section 9 applications: admitting a petition without engaging with the debtor's reply, simply because the operational creditor's own ledger shows an unpaid invoice, invites exactly the kind of reversal Polycab obtained here. The company's shares, which had fallen on news of the original admission, recovered part of that loss once the NCLAT's order came through, a reaction that says as much about how markets read an insolvency filing as it does about the merits of the underlying claim.

Why It Matters

The order reinforces that Section 9(5)(ii)(d) of the IBC is a real check, not a formality, on using insolvency filings to collect disputed vendor bills from solvent companies. An NCLT admitting a petition must actually weigh the debtor's correspondence showing a pre-existing dispute rather than treat an unpaid invoice as proof of default. For corporate debtors facing vendor claims, keeping a documented record of disagreement before any formal demand notice arrives remains the clearest defence against an insolvency filing being used as leverage.
