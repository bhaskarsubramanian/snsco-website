Patna HC: A Permanent Lok Adalat Cannot Record a Compromise Over Land

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw and Verdictum. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

People in Bihar's small towns settle land disputes before Permanent Lok Adalats far more often than most litigants in Delhi or Kolkata would expect, because the forum is quick, local and does not feel like a civil court. The Patna High Court has now reminded everyone why that convenience comes with a limit: a Permanent Lok Adalat set up under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 has no power to decide, or even to record a compromise over, a dispute about land title or possession.

The case traced back to a 2011 award passed by the Permanent Lok Adalat at Sasaram in Rohtas district in a dispute over partition of land. The petitioner, Bigan Shah, did not ask the High Court to strike the award down outright. He asked it to modify the award, which suggests he accepted the forum's authority to decide the matter and only disputed how it had decided it. The Court took a harder line than either side had argued for.

Justice G. Anupama Chakravarthy went back to the statute itself rather than to the terms of the 2011 award. Sections 22-A and 22-B(1) of the Legal Services Authorities Act confine a Permanent Lok Adalat's jurisdiction to disputes connected with public utility services, a category built around services such as transport, postal, telegraph, electricity, water supply, insurance and public health, not disputes over who owns or possesses a parcel of agricultural or residential land. A partition dispute between private parties over land does not fall within any of those categories, however local or small in value the dispute might be (this follows from Sections 22-A and 22-B of the Act rather than Section 22-C).

Because the subject matter itself was outside the Permanent Lok Adalat's jurisdiction, the Court held, no compromise recorded by that forum in such a dispute could stand, regardless of whether both sides had agreed to it at the time. Consent between the parties can waive many procedural objections, but it cannot hand a tribunal a jurisdiction the statute never gave it. The 2011 award was accordingly declared void from the outset rather than merely open to being corrected, which meant the petitioner's request to modify its terms fell away along with the award itself.

The ruling sits comfortably with a line of earlier Patna High Court decisions on the same forum, which have held that a Permanent Lok Adalat can only adjudicate pre-litigation disputes connected to a public utility service, and that a dispute with no connection at all to such a service falls outside its remit no matter how the parties frame their application. What makes this decision useful beyond Bihar is how plainly it treats the defect: not as an irregularity that a willing party can cure by consent, but as a complete absence of power that voids whatever the forum purported to decide.

For families and buyers dealing with land in small towns and district headquarters, where a Permanent Lok Adalat award is sometimes treated almost like a registered compromise decree, this ruling is a caution worth remembering before relying on one. A title chain that includes a Permanent Lok Adalat award covering a land partition, a boundary dispute or a possession claim carries a real risk that the award itself is a nullity, and that risk does not go away just because years have passed since the award was passed or because both original parties treated it as final at the time.

Due diligence on land anywhere in India, Bihar included, should therefore treat a Permanent Lok Adalat award in the chain of title the way a buyer would treat an order passed by a forum with no jurisdiction at all, which is to say with real suspicion until a civil court's decree or an undisputed revenue record confirms the same position independently. A buyer who discovers such an award in a seller's papers would do better to ask for the underlying civil suit record, if one exists, than to accept the award on its face.

The decision also has a narrower lesson for anyone currently before a Permanent Lok Adalat in a dispute that touches land, even where the broader transaction looks like it concerns a public utility connection, a right of way to a utility line, or a service dispute with a land component folded in. Where the real subject matter is title or possession, this ruling gives a ready basis to argue that the forum should decline jurisdiction altogether rather than attempt to resolve the land question as part of a wider settlement.

Why It Matters

Permanent Lok Adalats are widely used across Bihar and other states to settle disputes quickly outside the civil court system, and parties sometimes assume any award from such a forum is as good as a court decree. This ruling confirms that a Permanent Lok Adalat has no jurisdiction over land title or possession disputes at all, so any compromise it records on such a subject is void, a point anyone relying on an old Lok Adalat award in a property title chain needs to check before treating it as settled.
