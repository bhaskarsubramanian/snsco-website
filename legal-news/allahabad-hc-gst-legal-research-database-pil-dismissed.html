Allahabad High Court Dismisses PIL Against 18% GST on Online Legal Research Databases

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Verdictum, Bar and Bench and LawBeat. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

An advocate named Aniket Gupta walked into the Allahabad High Court asking it to strike down the 18% GST charged on subscriptions to legal research platforms such as SCC Online, Manupatra and LexisNexis, along with services like LiveLaw and Bar and Bench. He never got the court to look at that question. A division bench of Chief Justice Arun Bhansali and Justice Kshitij Shailendra dismissed his writ petition on 26 October 2026 at the admission stage itself, without going anywhere near the merits of the tax rate he was attacking.

The rate in question is not new. These platforms are classified under Service Accounting Code 998431 and have attracted GST at 18% since the tax was introduced in 2017, grouped alongside services such as online audio and video content and online gaming. Gupta's case was that this classification was unreasonable, since legal research tools are a professional necessity for lawyers rather than entertainment or leisure services, and that taxing them at the same rate as a streaming subscription or an online game ignored that distinction entirely.

That argument never got a hearing. What the bench fastened on instead was timing. If the rate had been in place since 2017, the court wanted to know what had changed in 2026 to make Gupta suddenly discover a grievance against it. His petition offered no explanation for the gap, and the bench's questioning of this delay, described by reports as pointed, formed the entire basis for dismissal. The order reportedly stopped short of recording any view on whether the classification itself was fair or arbitrary, leaving that question exactly where it found it.

This is a familiar pattern in writ practice, and one that trips up petitioners more often than the substance of their claims does. A court exercising its extraordinary jurisdiction under Article 226 is not bound to entertain a challenge simply because a statute or notification remains in force; delay and laches is an independent ground on which relief can be refused, distinct from and prior to any inquiry into merits. Where a rate has stood unchallenged for close to a decade, a petitioner needs a credible account of why he is raising it now, not merely an assertion that the rate is wrong. Gupta's petition apparently supplied no such account, and the bench treated that gap as fatal on its own.

The practical argument he raised, that lawyers cannot do their jobs properly without verifying case law and citations through these databases, and that treating a professional tool as a luxury good was unsound policy, may well resurface in a differently framed challenge. Nothing in this order forecloses that. What it does mean is that the specific route Gupta chose, a public interest petition filed nearly nine years after the rate first applied, was not going to get a hearing on substance, regardless of how the classification argument might have fared if it had been tested.

For firms and individual practitioners who absorb this GST as a cost of maintaining research subscriptions, the dismissal changes nothing about their current tax position. The 18% rate stands exactly as it did before the petition was filed. Anyone wanting to contest the classification of these services under the GST regime will need either a fresh petitioner with a better explanation for approaching the court at this stage, or a different procedural route entirely, such as an application before the GST Council or a representation to the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs seeking reclassification, rather than a writ petition that a court can dispose of on delay alone.

The episode is also a reminder of how much weight an Indian court can place on the question of why a petitioner waited, independent of whether the underlying grievance has force. Practitioners advising clients who wish to challenge a long-standing fiscal classification should treat the explanation for delay as a threshold requirement to be built into the petition from the outset, not an afterthought to be supplied if the respondent raises it. Here, the absence of any such explanation meant the actual tax question, whether a professional research tool belongs in the same GST bracket as online gaming, was never reached at all.

Why It Matters

The 18% GST on legal research subscriptions remains unchanged, and the court recorded no view on whether classifying these services alongside online gaming and streaming is justified. Firms and advocates who think the classification is wrong will need a petition that explains, credibly, why the challenge is being brought only now, since the Allahabad High Court has shown it will dismiss a long-delayed fiscal challenge on that ground alone without touching the substance.
