NCLAT: Going-Concern Sale Is Governed by Rules on the Liquidation Order Date, Not a Later Amendment

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, Taxscan, TaxGuru and IBC Laws. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Garden Court Distilleries Private Limited went into liquidation on 10 October 2025, when the NCLT's Ahmedabad Bench admitted the company under Section 33(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code. That order itself recorded that the committee of creditors had already decided to first explore selling the company as a going concern rather than breaking it up for piecemeal asset sales, the preferred route under the liquidation regulations wherever a buyer for the whole business can realistically be found.

Four days after the liquidation order, on 14 October 2025, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India amended Regulation 39C of the liquidation process regulations, the provision bearing on how a going-concern sale is to be conducted. When a successful bidder later approached the NCLT seeking reliefs to actually implement the going-concern sale that had by then been identified, the NCLT, in an order dated 17 June 2026, applied the amended version of Regulation 39C and rejected the bidder's application on that basis, treating the later amendment as governing the sale process even though the liquidation itself had commenced before the amendment existed.

The bidder and the financial creditor, State Bank of India, carried that rejection up to the NCLAT in two connected appeals. Their argument was that a going-concern liquidation sale is not a free-standing event separate from the liquidation order that set it in motion, it is simply the mode of liquidation the committee had already chosen inside that order, so the regulations governing the sale had to be the regulations in force on 10 October 2025, the date the liquidation order itself was passed, not whatever version of Regulation 39C happened to exist by the time implementation reliefs came up for decision months later.

That framing persuaded the tribunal. It held that a going-concern liquidation sale commences from the date of the Section 33 liquidation order, not from the later auction notice or any subsequent procedural step, and that an amendment notified after that date cannot retrospectively alter the legal character or foundation of a liquidation process already under way. The tribunal grounded this in its own earlier ruling in Ashok Kumar Gulla, which had taken the same position on the general principle that the regulations applicable to a liquidation are fixed by the regulations in force when the liquidation order issues.

Having found the NCLT's reliance on the amended Regulation 39C misplaced, the NCLAT set aside the 17 June 2026 order and sent the matter back to the Adjudicating Authority, directing it to decide the successful bidder's application for reliefs within two weeks, using the regulatory framework as it stood on the liquidation order date rather than the amended version the NCLT had wrongly applied. The tribunal added a practical observation to underline the stakes of further delay, noting that failing to get the distillery running again as a going concern could cost more than 800 workers their jobs and disrupt the local farmers whose produce feeds the business.

For liquidators, successful bidders and creditors dealing with a going-concern sale that straddles a regulatory amendment, the ruling fixes a clear anchor point: the regulations governing the sale are those in force on the date the Section 33 liquidation order itself was passed, regardless of how much later the actual sale implementation comes up for decision or how the rules have changed in the meantime. A liquidation process does not pick up a later amendment's requirements simply because the sale takes time to conclude, and parties structuring a going-concern bid around a particular regulatory regime can rely on that regime remaining fixed from the liquidation order date forward.

The two week timeline the NCLAT imposed on remand also reflects a broader impatience appellate forums have shown recently with liquidation processes that drag on while a viable going-concern buyer sits waiting, particularly where, as here, continued delay threatens jobs and a supply chain reaching beyond the corporate debtor itself into the surrounding local economy.

Why It Matters

The ruling fixes that the regulatory framework governing a going-concern liquidation sale is locked in on the date the Section 33 liquidation order is passed, not on the date a subsequent IBBI amendment happens to take effect, giving liquidators, bidders and creditors a clear basis to resist an adjudicating authority applying a later amendment to a sale process already under way, and pressing for a quick remand decision where delay threatens the business's ongoing viability.
