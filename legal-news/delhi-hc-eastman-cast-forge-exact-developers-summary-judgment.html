Delhi HC Grants Summary Judgment For Rs 4.07 Crore Refund In Gurugram Commercial Project Default

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon and delhihighcourt.nic.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A commercial buyer that paid over four crore rupees for six units in a Gurugram project and then waited years for either possession or its money back has obtained a decree without a trial. The Delhi High Court allowed a summary judgment application under Order XIII-A of the Code of Civil Procedure and decreed the suit filed by M/s Eastman Cast and Forge Ltd. against M/s Exact Developers and Promoters Pvt. Ltd., the developer of a project called The Ace in Manesar.

The facts, as recorded in the judgment, were straightforward. Eastman had booked six commercial units in the project and paid Rs. 4,07,81,000 towards them. Possession never came. Instead of litigating the delay, the parties signed a settlement agreement on 18 July 2023 under which the developer agreed to refund the principal amount in five instalments. The developer paid nothing under that schedule either, and Eastman filed the present suit on 27 February 2025 for recovery of the principal with pendente lite and future interest.

Jurisdiction was the developer's main defence. Clause 62 of the original builder-buyer agreement gave exclusive jurisdiction to courts in Gurugram, and the developer argued the Delhi High Court had no business hearing the dispute. The Court rejected that argument on the ground that the suit was not founded on the builder-buyer agreement at all, but on the later settlement agreement, which was executed in Delhi, a fact the developer did not deny. Since the defendant also carries on business in Delhi, the Court held it had jurisdiction under Section 20(c) of the Civil Procedure Code regardless of what the original agreement's jurisdiction clause said.

On the merits, the developer raised what the judgment describes as an alleged oral alternate arrangement, namely that Eastman had agreed to take possession of the flats instead of a cash refund. The Court found nothing in the settlement agreement supporting any such term, and treated the absence of a written reference to it as fatal to the defence at the summary-judgment stage. Applying the test the Supreme Court has laid down for Order XIII-A applications, that a defence must show a real, not merely arguable, prospect of success at trial, the Court held that "the defendant has no real prospects of successfully defending the claim" and that the defence raised was, in its words, "moonshine or a mirage."

The decree that followed gives Eastman the full principal of Rs. 4,07,81,000, with pendente lite and future interest running at 9 per cent per annum from 18 July 2023, the date the settlement agreement was signed. That rate is lower than the 12 per cent Eastman had asked for, which suggests the Court exercised its own discretion on the interest component even while granting the underlying claim in full and without a trial. A decree sheet has been directed to be drawn up to formalise the judgment into an executable decree.

For buyers of commercial space in NCR projects who have already moved past the builder-buyer agreement stage and signed a settlement for refund, the ruling is a useful marker of how courts will treat a developer's later default on that settlement. Once a developer has agreed, in a fresh and separately executed document, to pay a fixed sum on a fixed schedule, an argument that goes back to some informal understanding predating or outside that settlement is unlikely to survive a summary judgment application unless it finds support in the settlement's own text. Buyers negotiating such settlements would do well to make sure every agreed alternative, including any possession-in-lieu-of-refund arrangement, is written into the settlement document itself.

The ruling also illustrates how Order XIII-A, introduced into commercial litigation to let genuinely undefended claims be decided without the delay of a full trial, is being used in builder-default disputes once the claim has moved from a possession dispute to a straightforward recovery under a settlement. Where the amount owed and the default are both on record and undisputed, a developer cannot expect an oral or unwritten defence to buy it the time a full trial would otherwise take.

Why It Matters

Commercial and residential buyers who move from a delayed-possession dispute to a written settlement for refund often assume a full trial still lies ahead if the developer later defaults on that settlement. This ruling shows that once a settlement fixes a sum and a schedule, a developer's later defence based on some informal or oral alternative arrangement is unlikely to survive scrutiny at the summary judgment stage unless it is recorded in the settlement itself, and that a jurisdiction clause in the original builder-buyer agreement will not control a suit founded on a later, separately executed settlement signed elsewhere.
