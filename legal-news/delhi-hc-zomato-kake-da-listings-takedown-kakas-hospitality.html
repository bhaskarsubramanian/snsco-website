Delhi High Court Orders Zomato to Take Down 132 'Kake Da'-Styled Listings Mimicking a Registered Mark

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Kakas Hospitality has been fighting to protect the name KAKE-DA-HOTEL for years, a mark with roots in a Connaught Place eatery and a history that includes a 2011 decree letting certain family members continue using it. The fight has since moved from the restaurant itself to the online platforms that list it, because the real threat to a food brand's distinctiveness today is not a rival signboard across the street but dozens of near-identical listings on the same delivery app that a customer is scrolling through on their phone.

This dispute reached Justice Tejas Karia's court this month on an application to deal with a new batch of listings. Kakas Hospitality had already secured an interim injunction in April 2026, restraining certain defendants from using marks built around the core term KAKE-DA, and that order was continued in July. What brought the parties back was evidence that new listings carrying names such as Kaka Da Dhaba, Kake Da Chicken and Kake Da Express had appeared on Zomato after the injunction, many of them run out of cloud kitchens that made it difficult for the plaintiff to identify who was actually behind the listing.

Kakas Hospitality's case was that these new listings were not independent infringers starting fresh, but the same defendants, or persons acting for them, resurfacing under slightly different names to get around the existing injunction. Roughly 81 of the flagged listings operated as cloud kitchens with no physical storefront, which meant there was no easy way to walk up to a shop and identify who was running the business, unlike a traditional restaurant premises where a process server could simply show up.

Justice Karia accepted that this pattern justified a fresh takedown direction rather than leaving the plaintiff to chase each new listing through fresh impleadment applications. He directed Eternal Limited, which operates the Zomato platform, to take down, block, delist or deactivate the 132 identified listings within two weeks of the order, and to disclose whatever particulars it holds about the persons operating those listings, so the plaintiff can actually identify and, if necessary, implead them in the ongoing suit.

The order sits within a now-familiar line of Delhi High Court rulings treating food delivery platforms as a practical choke point for enforcing trademark injunctions in the restaurant sector, the same approach the court has taken in cases involving well-known pizza and other food brands facing copycat listings on Zomato and Swiggy. Rather than treating the platform as a neutral intermediary outside the fight, courts have been willing to direct it to act on specific, identified listings once a plaintiff makes out a case that an existing injunction is being evaded through minor variations in a listing's name.

For restaurant brand owners, the practical value of this kind of order is speed. A takedown direction addressed to the platform operator, covering a defined list of URLs or listings, moves faster than hunting down the operator of an anonymous cloud kitchen through conventional process-serving, particularly where the same infringer appears to be relaunching under new listing names as soon as one is taken down.

This matter has been listed for further hearing in 2027, which means the underlying infringement claims against the identified and as-yet-unidentified defendants remain to be tried. What this order resolves, for now, is only the immediate harm of the listings staying live on the platform while that process plays out, which in a market as fast-moving as online food delivery is often the harm that matters most to a brand owner trying to protect its name.

The ruling is a reminder to any business relying on a regional or heritage food brand name that registering the mark is only the first step; policing it on aggregator platforms, where a slight respelling can put a copycat listing in front of thousands of customers overnight, now requires the same vigilance that was once reserved for physical signboards and menus.

Why It Matters

The order shows Delhi High Court's growing willingness to direct food delivery platforms to delist specific infringing listings as an interim measure, even where the operators behind those listings are unidentified cloud kitchens, rather than requiring a brand owner to chase down each new infringer one at a time before any relief can be granted at all. It also shows courts treating a relaunch under a slightly altered name as evasion, not a fresh dispute.
