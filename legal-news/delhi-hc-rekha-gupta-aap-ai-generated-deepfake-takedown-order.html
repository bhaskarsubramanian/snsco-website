Delhi HC Orders Takedown of AI-Generated Posts in CM Rekha Gupta's Defamation Suit

Source: Originally reported by Verdictum, Bar and Bench, Law Chakra and courtbook.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

On 6 November 2026, Justice Manoj Jain of the Delhi High Court passed an ex parte interim order directing the removal of six social media posts that a civil suit filed by Chief Minister Rekha Gupta describes as AI-generated content depicting her as a liquor vendor, a hawker and a delivery agent. The order came in a defamation suit Gupta filed against the Aam Aadmi Party and other defendants, registered as CS(OS) 1019/2026 along with connected applications.

Gupta's plaint sought a permanent injunction restraining the defendants from publishing or circulating the content, along with damages. The posts she complained of were not photographs or ordinary caricatures but images and video built using artificial intelligence tools, altering her likeness to show her performing roles unconnected to her office. When the matter came up before Justice Jain, AAP had not entered appearance and was not represented, so the court heard only Gupta's side before passing interim relief.

The court held, on a prima facie view, that the content went beyond what political commentary or satire ordinarily permits under Indian law. Reporting of the order quotes the court describing the effect of the posts as making the Chief Minister, in its words, an object of ridicule and insult. On that basis, Justice Jain found a real risk of injury to her reputation and personal dignity that could not be undone simply by awaiting a full trial, which is the usual ground on which courts grant urgent relief without hearing the other side first.

Relief under the order is specific rather than open-ended. It directs the takedown of six identified URLs carrying the posts in question, not a general restraint on the defendants' social media activity or on AI-generated political content as such. Nothing in the order bars AAP or anyone else from criticising Gupta's conduct as Chief Minister through ordinary text, cartoons or commentary. Because the order was passed without AAP being heard, it remains provisional in the fullest sense. The case is listed for further consideration on 4 December 2026, when the party will have the opportunity to contest the suit, respond to the takedown direction and argue its own case on whether the content falls within protected political expression rather than defamation.

Suits of this kind turn on a now-familiar three-part test for interim injunctions in Indian civil procedure: whether the plaintiff shows a prima facie case, whether the balance of convenience favours granting relief before trial, and whether refusing relief would cause harm that money alone could not fix later. Courts across the country have increasingly applied this test to synthetic and AI-altered content aimed at public figures, treating the speed at which such material spreads online as itself a reason the balance of convenience and irreparable harm tests cut in the plaintiff's favour at the interim stage.

What the order does not do is settle, finally, where the line sits between permissible political satire and actionable defamation when the tool used is generative AI rather than a cartoonist's pen or a photo edit. That question remains open for 4 December, when AAP is expected to put its own case before the court, including any defence that the content was commentary on public conduct rather than a false statement of fact about the Chief Minister personally. How Indian courts eventually draw that line will matter well beyond this one suit, since generated imagery of this kind is now cheap to produce and easy to circulate against any public figure, business leader or private individual who attracts online attention.

For now, the practical effect is narrow and time-limited. The six posts identified in the order are to come down, the broader suit continues, and both sides will return to argue the merits once the defendants have had a chance to respond. Litigants on either side of a dispute involving AI-generated or deepfake content aimed at a public figure can expect Indian courts to keep using the same ex parte, narrowly tailored takedown route seen here, rather than broader prior restraints, while the underlying defamation claim is worked out at a fuller hearing.

Why It Matters

The order shows how readily Delhi courts will grant urgent, narrowly worded takedown relief against AI-generated or deepfake content aimed at a named individual, even before the other side is heard, once a plaintiff shows the content goes beyond ordinary commentary and risks reputational harm that a later damages award cannot fully repair. Businesses, public figures and their counsel dealing with synthetic media impersonation should note that Indian courts are treating speed of online spread as a factor favouring interim relief, and that such orders remain provisional until the opposing party is heard.
