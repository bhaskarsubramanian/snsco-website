MCA Widens Its Overseas Listing Route Beyond Plain Equity Shares to Convertible Debentures and Preference Shares

Source: Originally reported by vinodkothari.com, teamleaseregtech.com, theweek.in and corplawupdates.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Indian companies looking to raise capital through a direct listing abroad have, until now, been confined to one instrument: the plain equity share. The route itself is fairly young. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs opened it in 2024 through the Companies (Listing of Equity Shares in Permissible Jurisdictions) Rules, letting unlisted Indian companies list equity shares directly on specified stock exchanges outside India, including exchanges in the GIFT City International Financial Services Centre, without first listing in India. A notification dated 3 November 2026, G.S.R. 968(E), now widens that door considerably.

The amendment's drafting technique is almost housekeeping in appearance: wherever the 2024 rules used the word 'shares', the word is now replaced with 'instruments', and the rules themselves are renamed accordingly. But the substance behind that word change is significant. A new definition of 'equity instruments' is inserted, and it covers not just equity shares but convertible debentures and preference shares issued by an Indian company as well. Companies that could previously only send plain equity abroad can now structure an overseas listing around hybrid instruments too, instruments that institutional investors in several permissible jurisdictions are considerably more comfortable pricing and holding than a straight equity stake in an unlisted Indian issuer.

That widening travels alongside a separate eligibility change in the same amendment: a company with negative net worth, which the 2024 rules would otherwise have kept off this route altogether, can now use it if it undertakes to allot at least 75 per cent of the offering to Qualified Institutional Buyers. The amendment also shortens the cooling-off period for companies that have paid off overdue bank or lender dues, from two years to a little over six months, before they can use the route, while tightening the position the other way for companies that have not filed their annual returns or financial statements, who now have to clear those filings first.

The amendment also replaces Form LEAP-1 in the Second Schedule to the 2024 rules with a revised form, bringing the paperwork in line with the broader category of instruments the rules now cover. Form LEAP-1 is the form through which an Indian company notifies the Registrar of its intention to list, and a form drafted around plain equity shares would have sat awkwardly against a listing built around convertible debentures or preference shares.

What the amendment leaves untouched matters just as much as what it changes. The list of permissible jurisdictions under the 2024 rules, the specific set of overseas stock exchanges an Indian company may list on using this route, has not been expanded by this notification. Commentary on the amendment is also clear that it does not touch the listing regulations of the overseas exchange or the IFSC exchange itself, and does not alter the separate requirements under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019 that govern how foreign investors can hold and trade such instruments. A company planning to use the expanded route still has two different rulebooks to satisfy, the Companies Act framework MCA has just widened, and the FEMA framework that sits alongside it and has not moved.

That gap matters practically because, as some commentary on the amendment points out, the definition of 'equity instruments' inserted into the Companies Act rules is not fully aligned with how the FEMA Non-Debt Instruments Rules define and treat similar instruments. A convertible debenture that qualifies for the new overseas listing route under the Companies Act rules will still need to be checked separately against the FEMA definitions and any sectoral or pricing conditions that apply there, rather than assumed to clear both frameworks on the strength of the Companies Act amendment alone.

For Indian companies weighing an overseas listing, particularly those that had looked at the 2024 route and concluded that straight equity was not the right instrument for their capital structure or investor base, the amendment opens a door that was previously closed. For their counsel and compliance teams, the practical task now is cross-checking any proposed convertible debenture or preference share listing against both the amended Companies Act rules and the unchanged FEMA position, and building the QIB allocation condition into the offer structure from the outset rather than retrofitting it later.

Why It Matters

Companies planning to raise capital abroad, including those backed by foreign private equity or venture investors looking for an exit route through an overseas listing, now have a wider menu of instruments to list directly outside India without a prior domestic listing. The QIB allocation condition and the unchanged FEMA requirements mean the route still needs careful structuring, so promoters and foreign investors weighing an overseas listing should treat this as widening the options, not as removing the compliance work involved in using them.
