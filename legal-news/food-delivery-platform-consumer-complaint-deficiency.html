Food Delivery Platform Held Liable for Deficiency in Service Over Repeated Non-Delivery of Paid Orders

Source: As reported in consumer-law commentary and case digests through 2026. This article has been independently researched and written by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes, summarising the general legal position rather than reproducing any single report. Readers should verify current developments against the underlying order or judgment and primary sources.

A District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission has held a food delivery platform liable for deficiency in service after a consumer's paid orders were repeatedly marked as delivered in the platform's system despite not being received, with the platform's customer support offering only partial, inconsistently calculated refunds across each incident. The complaint was filed by the consumer, referred to in the order as the complainant, against the platform, identified as the opposite party, and against the platform's delivery partner separately impleaded as a co-opposite party.

The platform argued that, as an intermediary connecting consumers with restaurants and delivery partners, its liability for non-delivery was limited, and that responsibility rested primarily with the delivery partner. The Commission rejected this framing on the facts of the case, noting that the consumer had paid the platform directly, the platform controlled the order-tracking and refund process end-to-end, and the platform's own system logs — produced during the proceedings — showed inconsistent delivery-status entries that the platform could not satisfactorily explain.

The Commission held that where a platform collects payment, controls the transaction and delivery-tracking process, and administers refunds through its own systems, it cannot deflect the entirety of deficiency-in-service liability onto a delivery partner merely because the last-mile delivery was physically performed by that partner. It directed a full refund for each of the disputed orders, along with compensation for the repeated inconvenience and the time spent pursuing an adequate resolution.

The order adds to a developing body of consumer commission decisions examining platform liability in the intermediary economy, generally holding that a platform's degree of control over payment, tracking and grievance redressal — not merely its self-description as an intermediary — determines the extent of its deficiency-in-service exposure.

Why It Matters

Consumers pursuing a non-delivery complaint against a food delivery or similar platform should preserve the app's order-tracking screenshots and each refund communication received — inconsistencies between what the app shows and what was actually received are often central to establishing the platform's own service failure.
