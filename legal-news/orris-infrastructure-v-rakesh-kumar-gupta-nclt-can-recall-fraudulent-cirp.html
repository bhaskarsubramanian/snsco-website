IBC & Insolvency · 30 September 2026

NCLT Can Recall a CIRP Admission Obtained by Fraud, But Need Not Always Shut Down the Insolvency: Supreme Court

Source: Originally reported by Verdictum, deccanherald.com, supremetoday.ai and theindianlawyer.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

An operational creditor called Straight Edge Contracts filed a Section 9 petition against Three C Shelters claiming an unpaid operational debt of roughly 29.96 crore rupees. The NCLT admitted the petition in 2020 and the corporate debtor went into insolvency. Later, the corporate debtor's side came back and told the same NCLT that the whole debt was manufactured, a mirage created to engineer an admission that suited certain parties rather than to recover a genuine claim, and asked the Tribunal to recall its own admission order.

The NCLT actually found collusion on the facts, but held its hands were tied procedurally. It took the view that once a Section 9 petition has been admitted and the corporate insolvency resolution process has commenced, the Tribunal has no inherent power to recall that admission, fraud or no fraud; the aggrieved party's only route was an appeal. The NCLAT disagreed on the power question and went to the other extreme, setting aside the CIRP altogether once it concluded the initiation was tainted. Orris Infrastructure, which had become involved as a resolution applicant or interested stakeholder in the process by that stage, carried the matter to the Supreme Court, as did other appellants, giving the case its clutch of connected civil appeals.

The Supreme Court's answer sits between the two tribunal positions, and that middle path is the real contribution of the judgment. On power, the Court sided with the NCLAT: an adjudicating authority is not powerless once it discovers that its own admission order was obtained through fraud or collusion between the applicant and persons connected to the corporate debtor. Jurisdictional facts, the Court said, are conditions precedent to a tribunal properly assuming jurisdiction in the first place. Where those facts are shown to have been manufactured, the order built on them does not acquire some immunity from correction simply because a CIRP has since been set in motion and third parties have started to rely on it. The NCLT can recall its own admission order in such a case.

But the Court declined to treat recall and termination of the CIRP as the same thing, which is where it parted from the NCLAT. Recalling the admission order because it was obtained by fraud is one question. What happens to the insolvency process that has, by the time the fraud comes to light, often already drawn in a resolution professional, a committee of creditors, and sometimes resolution applicants who have spent money and time preparing bids, is a separate question with its own stakeholders to protect. The Court held that on recalling an admission found to be fraudulent, the Tribunal must disallow the original fraudulent applicant from continuing to participate and may trigger proceedings against that applicant under Section 65 of the Code, which penalises fraudulent or malicious insolvency applications. That punishes the wrongdoer directly.

Whether the CIRP itself should then be wound up is a different call, to be made only after the Tribunal hears the resolution professional and takes the views of the committee of creditors and other stakeholders who came into the process in good faith. If continuing the resolution process serves the larger interest of actually resolving the corporate debtor's insolvency, rather than simply undoing everything because the trigger event was tainted, the Tribunal may let it continue. The appeals succeeded on this footing: the Supreme Court restored the matter to the adjudicating authority to work through that two-stage exercise, rather than accepting the NCLAT's blanket termination.

The practical upshot for insolvency practitioners is a clearer map for a fact pattern that comes up more often than the IBC's architecture seems to anticipate, a CIRP whose very trigger turns out to have been staged. Counsel for a corporate debtor who suspects the triggering debt was manufactured now has a clean basis to seek recall rather than being told, as the NCLT initially held here, that the only remedy lies in appeal against the original admission. Equally, resolution professionals and genuine creditors who have already committed time and resources to a CIRP by the time fraud surfaces have an argument, grounded in this judgment, that the process need not collapse around them automatically; the Tribunal has to weigh their position before deciding whether to pull the plug.

Why It Matters

Fraudulent or collusive triggering of insolvency proceedings, often to pressure a promoter or to park an asset beyond the reach of genuine creditors, is a recurring complaint in Indian insolvency practice. This ruling confirms the NCLT's inherent power to recall its own admission order on proof of fraud, removing an earlier procedural doubt, while protecting resolution professionals, committees of creditors and bona fide resolution applicants from having an otherwise viable CIRP terminated automatically merely because its trigger was later found tainted.
