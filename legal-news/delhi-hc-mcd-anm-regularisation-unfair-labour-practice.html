Delhi HC Upholds Regularisation of 23 ANMs From Date of Appointment, Calls Years on Contract Unfair Labour Practice

Source: Originally reported by delhihighcourt.nic.in and advocategandhi.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Twenty-three women joined the Health Department of the erstwhile Municipal Corporation of Delhi as Auxiliary Nurse Midwives between August and October 2001, hired on contract against posts the Corporation itself had sanctioned. Sixteen of them went through that round of engagement; the rest followed later on the same contractual basis. None of them was ever made permanent. By the time the dispute reached the Delhi High Court this October, some of these women had been working on paper as temporary staff for close to a quarter century.

The ANMs raised an industrial dispute in November 2010, asking to be regularised as ANMs from their respective dates of initial appointment rather than from whatever later date the Corporation chose to recognise. An Industrial Tribunal agreed with them and passed an award directing regularisation with retrospective effect. By the time the award came up for enforcement, the undivided MCD itself no longer existed. The 2012 trifurcation had split it into three corporations, and the 23 women were now spread across them: thirteen under the North Delhi Municipal Corporation side of that division, three under the East Delhi Municipal Corporation, and seven under the South Delhi Municipal Corporation. Each of these successor bodies filed its own writ petition challenging the award, and the High Court heard the connected petitions together.

The municipal corporations' central argument was procedural and, on its face, a familiar one in government service litigation: regular appointments to municipal posts are supposed to go through the Delhi Subordinate Services Selection Board, and an Industrial Tribunal cannot direct regularisation outside that channel, however long someone has worked on contract. If accepted, that argument would have meant the Tribunal's award was void regardless of how many years the women had put in or how routine their duties had become.

Justice Manoj Kumar Ohri rejected that defence on the facts the Corporation's own side had placed on record. The management's witnesses, when examined before the Tribunal, had admitted that the ANMs were taken on after advertisements and a selection process, and that they had gone on to work against posts the Corporation had sanctioned for that very purpose. Once an employer's own evidence shows a selection process and sanctioned posts, a DSSSB-only argument loses most of its force; the complaint is no longer about regularising someone who entered through the back door, but about an employer that ran a front-door process and then declined to act on its result for over a decade.

The judgment treats the underlying conduct as going beyond an isolated oversight. Keeping employees on contractual terms for ten to sixteen years, against posts the employer had itself sanctioned and filled through a proper process, was held to amount to an unfair labour practice rather than a simple administrative delay. That framing matters because it shifts the burden in these disputes: once a tribunal finds the hallmarks of a real, sanctioned post and a real selection process, prolonged contractual status stops looking like caution on the employer's part and starts looking like the practice being penalised.

The Court was careful to describe its own role in reviewing the award. Sitting in writ jurisdiction under Article 226 over an Industrial Tribunal's award, a High Court does not re-weigh the evidence or substitute its own view of the facts for the Tribunal's; it checks whether the Tribunal's conclusions were perverse or legally unsustainable on the record before it. Finding neither defect here, the Court declined to disturb the Tribunal's findings on selection, sanctioned posts, or the length of service, and dismissed both writ petitions.

The practical result is that the regularisation runs from each ANM's original date of joining, not from the date any corporation later chose to treat her as permanent, and not from the date of the Tribunal's award either. For employees whose joining dates stretch back to 2001, that retrospective window carries real consequences for seniority, pension reckoning and pay fixation, on top of the basic question of permanent status.

For Delhi's municipal bodies and other public employers who routinely staff sanctioned posts through contractual hiring, the ruling narrows the room to rely on a selection-channel technicality once their own record shows a proper process was followed. An employer that advertises, selects and places staff against posts it has sanctioned, then leaves them on contract for years, cannot later point to the absence of a DSSSB-routed order as the reason regularisation should not follow.

Why It Matters

Delhi's three municipal corporations, and other statutory employers who hire through selection but keep staff on contract against sanctioned posts for years, now face a tribunal and High Court willing to treat that pattern as unfair labour practice rather than routine delay. The retrospective regularisation ordered here, running from each employee's original joining date, signals that the longer an employer defers acting on its own selection process, the costlier the eventual correction becomes in seniority and pay terms.
