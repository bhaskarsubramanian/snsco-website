Resistance to Delivery of Possession Under a Decree Must Be Independently Adjudicated, Not Summarily Overridden: High Court on Order XXI Rule 97

Source: As reported in civil-law commentary and case digests through 2026. This article has been independently researched and written by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes, summarising the general legal position rather than reproducing any single report. Readers should verify current developments against the underlying judgment and primary sources.

Obtaining a decree for possession of immovable property is often only the first stage of a successful outcome — execution of that decree, particularly where a person other than the judgment-debtor is found in occupation of the property, frequently generates a fresh round of litigation of its own. The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 anticipates precisely this scenario through Order XXI Rules 97 to 101, which govern what happens when a decree-holder's attempt to take possession is resisted, whether by the judgment-debtor or by a third party claiming an independent right.

A High Court has clarified the procedure applicable in such cases. Where a decree-holder is resisted or obstructed in obtaining possession, the decree-holder may apply to the executing court complaining of such resistance. Where the resistance is by a person other than the judgment-debtor who claims to be in possession in their own right — for example, a tenant, a subsequent purchaser, or a family member asserting an independent claim — the executing court cannot simply override that resistance and direct delivery of possession without first adjudicating, as a question arising in execution under Section 47 of the CPC, whether the resisting party's claim to independent possession is genuine.

The Court held that this adjudication is mandatory, not discretionary, and that a resisting party who genuinely claims an independent right is entitled to a proper hearing on that claim within the execution proceedings themselves, rather than being forced to file a fresh, separate suit. Conversely, the Court cautioned that this procedure should not be misused as a delaying tactic by a judgment-debtor or a person acting in collusion with them, and that an executing court retains the ability to reject a plainly frivolous or collusive claim of independent possession after a summary but genuine inquiry.

The ruling is a useful reminder for both decree-holders and third parties in possession that execution of a possession decree is not always a mechanical formality, and that a genuinely disputed claim of independent possession must be resolved through the specific procedure the CPC provides, rather than through informal means.

Why It Matters

A decree-holder facing resistance during execution, and a genuine third-party occupant resisting delivery of possession, should both be prepared to substantiate their respective positions before the executing court under Order XXI Rule 97 — this stage can be as contested, and as consequential, as the original suit.
