Delhi HC: Gratuity Stays Withheld While a Retired Employee's Criminal Appeal Is Pending

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, LiveLaw (2), courtbook.in and drishtijudiciary.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Jamuna Dass worked as a Tax Assistant in the Office of the Commissioner of Income Tax and retired on 31 December 2011. Three weeks later, on 23 January 2012, a Special Judge, CBI, convicted and sentenced him. Dass appealed that conviction, and Criminal Appeal 344/2012 has sat pending ever since. The question that reached the Delhi High Court in W.P.(C) 2936/2021 was narrow but practical: can the government go on withholding his gratuity for all that time, or must it release the money once he has retired and no departmental proceeding remains pending against him.

A Division Bench of Justices C. Hari Shankar and Vinod Kumar answered in the government's favour. Rule 69(1)(c) of the Central Civil Services (Pension) Rules, 1972, lets the government withhold gratuity where departmental or judicial proceedings are pending against a retired employee. Dass had argued that the rule's reference to pending proceedings covers only departmental inquiries, not a criminal appeal sitting in a different court altogether. The Bench rejected that reading. Rule 69(1)(c), it held, applies equally to pending judicial proceedings, and a criminal appeal against conviction counts as exactly that.

The practical effect is that the clock on Dass's gratuity does not start running until his appeal is finally decided one way or the other. Nothing in the ruling converts the withholding into a permanent forfeiture. Gratuity withheld under this rule stays recoverable the day the appeal concludes in the retired employee's favour, and the government's case has never been that a pending appeal, by itself, extinguishes the right to the money. It only delays payment.

This sits alongside, and is distinguishable from, an earlier 2025 ruling by the same High Court in a different matter, where the government had tried to withhold retiral benefits on the strength of a bare FIR with nothing more. That 2025 decision held an FIR alone will not do. Withholding under the pension rules needs either a pending departmental proceeding or a judicial proceeding where cognizance has actually been taken, on a police report or a complaint. Dass's case went well past that threshold: he had been tried, convicted and sentenced, and his own appeal against that conviction was what kept the matter alive.

For government employers the ruling gives a reasonably clean rule to apply at retirement: check whether a conviction and a pending appeal exist, not just whether a complaint has been filed somewhere. For retired employees fighting a conviction, it is a reminder that an appeal, filed to protect their liberty and their record, carries a cost most people do not immediately think about: their gratuity stays locked up for as long as that appeal takes to resolve, which on this record had already stretched past thirteen years.

One compliance website covering the ruling described it as applying to all Central Civil Services retirees facing any pending proceeding, which overstates what the Bench actually decided on these specific facts: a conviction already recorded, and an appeal against that conviction still open. Employers and employees relying on this ruling should anchor to those facts rather than to the broader restatement, and check the text of the order itself before extending it to weaker cases, such as a department withholding gratuity because an employee faces an unrelated departmental inquiry that has not reached the same stage.

The case also illustrates how long the pension rules let a withholding run in practice. Dass retired in December 2011 and was convicted barely three weeks later. Fourteen years on, with the appeal still pending before the appellate court, the gratuity he earned across his career remains out of his hands, and the Delhi High Court's ruling confirms the government can keep it that way until the appeal is finally decided, whichever way it goes. Employees who find themselves in Dass's position have no shortcut through the pension rules to force an earlier release. Their only real lever is to push the pending appeal itself to a quicker resolution, since the withholding is tied entirely to the life of that appeal and not to any separate timeline of its own.

Why It Matters

Government departments often sit on a retired employee's gratuity for years without a clear basis to do so. This ruling confirms Rule 69(1)(c) of the CCS Pension Rules covers a pending criminal appeal and is not limited to a departmental inquiry, so a convicted retiree cannot demand release of gratuity while that appeal remains undecided. HR and pension desks in government offices can point to this precedent rather than relying on a bare FIR, which a separate ruling has already held is not enough on its own.
