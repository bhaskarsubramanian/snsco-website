Allahabad High Court Orders UP Trial Courts To Decide Maintenance Execution Pleas Within Six Months

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, courtbook.in and Mondaq. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Data Ram waited 645 days before challenging a maintenance order passed against him, and by the time his revision reached the Allahabad High Court, that delay alone decided the case. The Principal Judge, Family Court, Kasganj, had directed him on 17 September 2024 to pay his wife Rs. 3,000 a month and Rs. 1,000 each to their two minor children under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, with the liability running from 18 April 2022, the date she had first moved the court. Data Ram did nothing about that order for close to two years. When he finally filed Criminal Revision (Defective) No. 2005 of 2026, he asked the High Court to condone the delay, and that request is where the matter actually turned.

The explanation he offered did not survive scrutiny. The Court found nothing in his application that accounted for the length of the delay, let alone each stretch of it. A party who sits on a right for 645 days needs more than a general plea of hardship or oversight before a court will excuse the gap, and the bench found no such explanation here. It dismissed the condonation application, and once that fell, the revision itself stood barred by limitation and was rejected without the Court going into whether the underlying maintenance order was correct.

That would ordinarily have closed the file. Instead, the Court used Data Ram's case as the occasion to address a problem it clearly saw running through maintenance litigation across the state: orders get passed, and then little happens for years because the execution side of the process stalls in the trial courts. A wife or child awarded maintenance under Section 125 has, in practice, won only half the fight. Getting the trial court to actually enforce payment against a reluctant husband or father is a separate contest, and often the longer one.

The bench grounded its direction in the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in Periyammal v. Rajamani, which told every High Court to collect data on pending execution petitions and ensure they are decided within six months, and in the Allahabad High Court's own Registrar General circular of 24 May 2025, issued to carry out that direction. Taking stock of how little that circular appears to have changed on the ground, the Court directed all trial courts across Uttar Pradesh to decide execution applications in maintenance cases within six months of filing. It added a line meant as a warning rather than an aspiration: failure to meet that timeline may amount to contempt of both the Supreme Court and the High Court.

That last part matters more than the six-month figure by itself. Disposal timelines for pending litigation are not new, and Uttar Pradesh's own judicial officers have heard similar directions before, on subjects ranging from bail applications to matrimonial trials, many of which lost force once the attention around them passed. By tying non-compliance expressly to contempt jurisdiction, its own and the Supreme Court's, the Allahabad High Court gave presiding officers in family and magisterial courts a concrete reason to treat execution applications as a docket priority rather than a residual category attended to whenever time permits.

For lawyers handling maintenance work in the state, the order gives something to point to the next time an execution petition drifts past its first anniversary without a hearing date fixed. For a husband or father weighing whether to simply stop paying and wait out the system, it removes one of the quieter incentives for delay, since the trial court itself now carries a deadline it did not have before.

Practitioners acting for maintenance claimants in Uttar Pradesh would do well to note the filing date of every execution petition they put in and track it against the six-month mark the Court has now fixed. A petition sitting idle past that point is no longer merely slow; it falls outside a standing direction of the High Court, which gives counsel a sharper basis to seek an expedited hearing than a bare request for priority listing would. Whether presiding officers treat that distinction as real pressure or as one more circular to be filed away will only become clear over the next few reporting cycles.

Why It Matters

Maintenance orders under Section 125 CrPC are frequently won on paper and then left unenforced for years once execution proceedings stall in the trial courts. This direction gives Uttar Pradesh litigants and their counsel a concrete institutional deadline, backed by the threat of contempt against the court itself rather than only the defaulting spouse, to push execution applications forward. Family law practitioners should flag petitions nearing the six-month mark and cite this order when seeking expedited hearing, since it turns previously discretionary case management into a standing judicial direction with consequences attached to non-compliance.
