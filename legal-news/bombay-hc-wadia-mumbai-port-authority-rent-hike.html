Bombay HC Quashes Mumbai Port Authority's Retrospective Rent Hikes on Long-Term Tenants

Source: Originally reported by supremetoday.ai, LiveLaw, LiveLaw (2) and uniindia.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

More than a hundred tenants holding long leases from the Mumbai Port Authority, formerly the Mumbai Port Trust, had watched their rent bills jump well beyond what they expected, and beyond what the Port Authority's own earlier settlement with them had promised. A batch of writ petitions led by Arsheesh Jamshed Wadia challenged that jump in the Bombay High Court. A Division Bench of Justices Bharati Dangre and Manjusha Deshpande has now quashed the retrospective part of the increase.

The dispute traces back to a fight the tenants' families had already fought once, and won, before the Supreme Court. In 2004, in Jamshed Hormusji Wadia v. Board of Trustees of the Port of Mumbai, the Supreme Court held that the Port Trust, as an instrumentality of the State, had to fix rents fairly and reasonably rather than simply dictate terms to a captive set of lessees, and it upheld a set of compromise proposals fixing the rates those tenants would pay going forward.

Those 2004 compromise rates governed for years without controversy. The present round of litigation arose because the Port Authority tried to revise the rents with retrospective effect, reaching back to charge tenants differential arrears for years that had already passed under the earlier, settled rates. The Bench held that the 2004 compromise rates continued to govern the petitioners right up to 31 March 2024, and that the Port Authority's attempt to fix higher rates retrospectively for the period before that date could not be sustained.

Retrospective demands of this kind can land on a tenant years after the fact, often as a lump arrears bill covering a period the tenant had already budgeted for and closed out under the old rate. That is precisely what had happened here: tenants who believed their rent liability for past years was settled and done found themselves facing fresh demands recomputed at a higher rate for periods already gone by. The Bench's finding that the 2004 rates bound the Port Authority through March 2024 means those recomputed demands, to the extent they look backward rather than forward, have no legal basis to stand on.

The ruling does not freeze Port Authority rents forever at the 2004 numbers. The Bench made clear that from 1 April 2024 onward, the Port Authority is free to fix new rents, provided the fixation is fair, reasonable and not profiteering in nature. That is the same standard the 2004 Supreme Court judgment set, applied forward rather than backward. What the Port Authority cannot do is reach back into a period already governed by the settled rates and recompute what tenants owed for years already closed.

Tenants served with back-rent demands under the revised rates now have a ruling telling them, to the extent those demands cover the pre-April 2024 period, that they do not stand. Those facing a post-April 2024 revision will still need to test any new figure against the fairness and reasonableness standard the Bench reaffirmed, rather than against the 2004 numbers themselves.

Government bodies that lease out land on long terms, from port trusts to municipal corporations, face the same temptation the Mumbai Port Authority gave in to here: raise the rent and claim the increase reaches back to cover years already gone, rather than only years still ahead. This ruling draws a line for any such body tempted to do that. A State lessor can fix a new, fair rent going forward. It cannot use that same fairness standard as cover for rewriting what a tenant already owed for a period a settled rate had already closed.

There is also a lesson here for tenants sitting on old, court-sanctioned settlements with a government lessor and assuming those settlements protect them indefinitely without any need to watch for a fresh rate revision. The 2004 Wadia compromise gave these tenants certainty for two decades, but it was never going to be permanent, and the Bombay High Court's ruling confirms that the Port Authority can lawfully move to a new rate once the settled period ends. Tenants relying on an old compromise should treat the date on which that compromise's protection runs out as a date to watch, not as a permanent shield, and should be ready to scrutinise the next rate the lessor fixes against the fairness and reasonableness standard rather than assume the old number simply continues.

Why It Matters

State instrumentalities that lease land long-term, port trusts, railways, municipal bodies, sometimes try to claw back revenue by revising rents retrospectively rather than only prospectively. This ruling reaffirms that a settled rate, once fixed through a court-sanctioned compromise, binds the lessor for the period it covers, and a later revision can only operate from the date it is actually made. Tenants served retrospective demands under a revised rate structure have a concrete precedent to resist them, while lessors retain full freedom to fix fair rents for the future.
