Agricultural Entry in Revenue Records Alone Doesn't Block a SARFAESI Sale: Allahabad High Court

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, LiveLaw Biz and TaxGuru. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Rama Chaurasia and her relative Kiran Chaurasia bought a plot in village Sarosa Bharosa, in the Alamnagar area of Lucknow, back in 1987. In 2003 they mortgaged it with Canara Bank to secure roughly Rs 30 lakh in credit extended to a family concern, M/s Chaurasia Traders. The business ran into default, and the bank invoked the SARFAESI Act to take possession of the mortgaged property and put it up for sale to recover the outstanding dues. Possession was taken, a public auction notice was issued, and the property was eventually sold to a third-party bidder, setting off the round of litigation that this judgment finally closes.

Their response had two limbs, and both travelled all the way to the High Court. The first was that the land was still agricultural, so the bank had no business proceeding against it under a statute that excludes agricultural land from its reach. The second, raised once the sale process was underway, was that they were entitled to pay off the dues and redeem the property even after the auction notice had been published, so long as the sale certificate had not yet been issued.

The Debts Recovery Tribunal at Lucknow accepted the agricultural-land argument in August 2010, in a securitisation application filed under Section 17 of the SARFAESI Act, and set aside the bank's action. It noted that the bank had not produced any order under Section 143 of the UP Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act converting the land from agricultural to non-agricultural use, and treated that gap as decisive. The Debts Recovery Appellate Tribunal at Allahabad took a different view on appeal and reversed that order in July 2011, restoring the bank's action and, with it, the sale already carried out. The borrowers then moved the High Court under Article 226 against that reversal, a writ petition that remained pending for close to fifteen years before being decided this month.

Justice Irshad Ali rejected the argument that the khatauni entry settled the matter. A revenue record shows how land is classified for revenue purposes, the Court held, but that classification is not conclusive proof of how the land is actually being used on the ground. The absence of a Section 143 order does not, by itself, defeat a bank's action under the SARFAESI Act either. A borrower who wants the benefit of the agricultural exemption under Section 31(i) has to show real, continuing agricultural use, not simply point to an old entry and the silence of the land records on conversion.

On the second limb, the Court worked through the chronology the bank had pleaded and found that the public auction notices went out several months before the borrowers' offer to pay off the dues. A borrower's statutory right to stop a SARFAESI sale by tendering the entire outstanding amount runs out once the auction notice is published; it does not survive, as the borrowers had argued, all the way up to the point the sale certificate is issued. An offer made after the notice is out comes too late to arrest the sale.

The borrowers also pointed out that their eventual offer was higher than the price the bank had accepted from the successful bidder, and argued that this alone should have persuaded the bank to call off the sale. The Court was not persuaded. A subsequent higher offer, made after an auction bid has already been accepted, does not oblige a secured creditor to abandon that sale and start the process again.

Finding no jurisdictional error, no perversity and no breach of a mandatory procedural requirement in the Appellate Tribunal's 2011 order, the High Court dismissed the writ petition and let that order stand. The borrowers were left with the Tribunal's order against them intact, and with it the sale that had already gone through more than a decade earlier. For lenders recovering dues against land that still carries an agricultural entry in the UP revenue records, and for borrowers hoping an old khatauni entry or a late offer will unwind a completed auction, the ruling draws both lines plainly: the exemption needs proof of actual use on the ground, and the statutory right of redemption has a fixed cut-off date, tied to publication of the auction notice, that neither a later tender nor a better offer can move back.

Why It Matters

Banks and NBFCs recovering secured loans in Uttar Pradesh against land still recorded as agricultural often face this exact objection at the SARFAESI stage. The ruling confirms that a bare revenue entry, without evidence of actual cultivation, will not stall a sale, and that a borrower's window to redeem the property by tendering full dues closes once the auction notice is published, not when the sale certificate is finally issued.
