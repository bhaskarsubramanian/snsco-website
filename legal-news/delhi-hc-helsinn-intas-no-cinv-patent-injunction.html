Delhi High Court Restrains Intas From Selling Generic Anti-Nausea Combination Drug in Helsinn Patent Suit

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, LiveLaw Biz (2) and supremetoday.ai. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Helsinn Healthcare SA, the Swiss company behind a patented combination drug used to control chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, moved fast once it learned a rival was preparing to launch a generic version, and the Delhi High Court moved just as fast in response. Within two days of a market surveyor flagging the launch, the company was in court, and it walked out with an interim order blocking the sale.

The dispute centres on Indian Patent IN 426553, which covers a fixed-dose combination of netupitant and palonosetron, two drugs typically combined into a single dose to prevent the nausea and vomiting that chemotherapy patients experience. Helsinn and its co-plaintiff told the Court that a market surveyor had alerted them on 7 November 2026 that Intas Pharmaceuticals Limited was preparing to launch a combipack under the brand NO-CINV, consisting of one 300 mg netupitant tablet and one 0.5 mg palonosetron tablet, which Helsinn said would infringe its patent. Helsinn sent Intas a cease-and-desist notice that same day.

Acting on that alert, Helsinn approached the Delhi High Court within two days, seeking an ad interim injunction before the product could actually reach the market. Justice Manmeet Pritam Singh Arora held that infringement of the patent's claims was prima facie evident and, notably, was not contested by Intas when the matter was argued. The Court restrained Intas, and anyone acting on its behalf, from manufacturing, using, selling, offering for sale, distributing, advertising, exporting or otherwise dealing in NO-CINV, or in any other medicinal product infringing Patent IN 426553, until the next hearing in the suit, CS(COMM) 1219/2026, and directed Intas to file a detailed statement of its existing inventory, including batch numbers, manufacturing dates and expiry details.

Intas, for its part, raised a validity challenge at the ad interim stage, arguing that combining two already-known substances into a single fixed-dose tablet lacked the inventive step needed to sustain a patent. The Court held that this defence called for a detailed examination of the patent specification, the claims and the Controller's own orders on the patent, which was not something it could resolve at the ad interim stage. It also drew on its own earlier order in a separate suit, Helsinn v. Hetero Healthcare, decided in 2024, to hold that packaging two known molecules together as an integrated combipack did not, at least prima facie, give Intas a tenable defence to infringement. The findings on both infringement and validity remain prima facie, and Intas remains free to press its invalidity defence when the suit is heard on merits.

The order forms part of a longer-running pattern of Helsinn litigation over the same molecule combination against different generic makers. The company has previously sued other manufacturers over similar combipacks built around netupitant and palonosetron, with mixed results; in at least one earlier matter against a different defendant, the Delhi High Court later vacated an ad interim injunction it had granted, after finding the plaintiff had not shown an imminent threat or a sufficient territorial connection to justify interim relief. That history means the restraint on Intas, like the others before it, is provisional rather than a final word on whether Helsinn's patent will hold up against each generic challenger it sues.

For pharmaceutical companies operating in India, foreign and domestic alike, the case is a reminder of how quickly a patent holder can secure an ad interim restraint once it has concrete evidence that a competing launch is imminent, well before the generic product has actually reached pharmacy shelves. Two days was enough for Helsinn to go from a surveyor's tip to a court order. For generic manufacturers planning a launch around a patented combination, the lesson cuts the other way: the window between a competitor's first notice of an impending launch and a restraining order can be extremely short, and an invalidity defence prepared only after the injunction is granted argues from behind.

The next hearing in CS(COMM) 1219/2026 will test whether Intas's novelty challenge to the fixed-dose combination can dislodge the interim restraint, a question the Court left entirely open when it granted the ad interim order. Until then, NO-CINV stays off the market, and Helsinn's broader campaign to enforce IN 426553 against one generic entrant after another continues.

Why It Matters

Patent holders can secure an ad interim restraint against a generic launch within days of learning of it, often before any invalidity defence is even filed, as this order shows for a chemotherapy anti-nausea combination drug. Foreign innovator companies litigating patents in India get a concrete illustration of how fast interim relief can move once concrete evidence of an impending launch surfaces. Generic manufacturers planning a launch around a patented combination need their validity case ready well before any competitor alert reaches a market surveyor, since the gap between notice and an injunction can be measured in days.
