Telangana HC: An Assignor Cannot Sue on a Patent He Sold

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A patent infringement suit that had run for almost a decade in a Hyderabad commercial court came to an end at the Telangana High Court on a point that had nothing to do with infringement at all, and everything to do with who was entitled to sue in the first place. The Court held that a man who had assigned away his patent years before filing the suit had no standing to bring it, however genuine his grievance about the invention's use might otherwise have been.

Abburi Ramaiah had instituted the suit in 2017 before the Commercial Court at Hyderabad, alleging infringement of Patent No. 239632 and seeking a permanent injunction along with damages of Rs. 1 crore. Cadila Healthcare Limited, since renamed Zydus Lifesciences Limited, resisted the suit on a threshold objection rather than on the merits of infringement, arguing that Ramaiah had already transferred the patent to Samastha Laboratories Private Limited under a deed executed on 12 April 2014 and therefore had no right left to enforce.

Ramaiah's answer to that objection was that the assignment to Samastha was still pending before the Patent Office when he filed the suit, and that an unregistered assignment could not yet take away his standing to sue. The Commercial Court appears to have accepted enough of that logic to let the suit proceed, prompting Cadila to move the High Court in revision.

The Bench of Justices T. Vinod Kumar and Anil Kumar Jukanti went to the patent register itself, which recorded Samastha Laboratories as the patentee with effect from 16 April 2014, only days after the assignment deed. Against that record, the Court noted, Ramaiah had neither specifically denied the assignment in his counter-affidavit nor produced any document to back his claim that registration was still pending. An objection raised only in argument, without anything in the pleadings or evidence to support it, was not enough to keep the suit alive.

On the law, the judgment relied on Section 68 of the Patents Act as it stands after the 2005 amendment, which no longer makes registration a condition for an assignment to take legal effect between the parties to it. Reading that together with Section 2(p), which recognises as 'patentee' either the original grantee who continues to hold the patent or a third party who has since acquired ownership by assignment, the Court held the two statuses cannot sit with two different people at the same time. Once Ramaiah had assigned the patent, in the Court's words he ceased to be the patentee regardless of whether the transfer had been formally entered in the register, and could not maintain an infringement suit built on a right he no longer held.

The Court was unsparing about how the suit had been pleaded, describing parts of the plaint as containing camouflaged statements that created what it called an illusionary cause of action, a reading that goes beyond rejecting the assignor's standing to question the candour of the case put forward. It also noted, almost as an aside, that the patent itself had expired in 2024, which would have closed off any claim for infringement occurring after that date regardless of who held it.

For patent holders who move an invention between group entities or sell it on, the lesson here sits outside the detail of pharmaceutical patents, Section 2(p) or Section 68. A written assignment deed takes effect between assignor and assignee from the date it is executed, not from the date it is recorded at the Patent Office, and an assignor who later discovers someone copying the invention has no personal right left to sue over it once that deed is signed, whatever paperwork is still working its way through the registry. Anyone taking an assignment, meanwhile, now has a Telangana High Court ruling to point to if the assignor, or someone claiming through the assignor, tries to relitigate rights that were sold away years earlier.

Why It Matters

Patents are routinely assigned between inventors, group companies and licensees, and registration of the assignment at the Patent Office can lag well behind the deed itself. This ruling confirms that the assignment takes effect from execution, not registration, so an assignor loses the right to sue for infringement from that date even if the Patent Office has not yet updated its records, a point relevant to any company acquiring patents through corporate transactions or licensing deals.
