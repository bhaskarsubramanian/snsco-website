Delhi HC: Unregistered GPA Sale Conveys No Title to JJ Colony Plot, Upholds Possession Decree

Source: Originally reported by delhihighcourt.nic.in, courtkutchehry.com and lawyerenews.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A man who occupied a plot in JJ Colony, Madangir on the strength of a general power of attorney has lost his appeal before the Delhi High Court, which found that the document gave him no title to resist a decree for possession. Justice Rajneesh Kumar Gupta dismissed the regular first appeal on 3 November 2026, after reserving judgment on 14 October, and left undisturbed a trial court decree of 31 January 2023 that had directed the appellant and his family to vacate the property within two months.

The dispute traces back to a civil suit filed in 2017 for possession and a mandatory injunction. The plaintiff's case was that the plot had been allotted under the Jhuggi Jhopri Removal Scheme, a resettlement programme under which the Delhi government and its urban shelter agencies allot small plots to slum dwellers displaced from encroached land, typically on terms that restrict free transfer for a period. The appellant resisted the suit by claiming rights under a general power of attorney said to have been executed by an earlier occupant, treating it as effectively a sale in his favour.

The trial court did not accept that defence and decreed possession in the plaintiff's favour. On appeal, the same argument was pressed again: that the GPA, coupled with long possession, gave the appellant a right that the civil court could not simply brush aside. The High Court rejected this line of reasoning, holding that an unregistered general power of attorney does not amount to a conveyance of immovable property and cannot by itself defeat a claim to recover possession under a scheme allotment.

This follows a settled line of authority going back to the Supreme Court's ruling in Suraj Lamp and Industries v. State of Haryana, which held that so-called GPA sales, where a power of attorney and a will are used in place of a registered sale deed, do not transfer ownership under Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act or Section 17 of the Registration Act. Delhi courts have applied that rule consistently in disputes over JJR Scheme plots, where informal transfers through power of attorney documents are common precisely because the original allotment terms discourage outright sale.

Justice Gupta's order treats the appellant's possession as derivative at best and legally insufficient to override the plaintiff's superior claim traced through the scheme allotment and the documentary record produced at trial. The appellant had no registered instrument, no documented chain of transfer recognised by the allotting authority, and nothing beyond the power of attorney to show how he came to occupy the property. Against that, the trial court had found the plaintiff's title better supported, and the appellate court saw no reason to reweigh that finding afresh.

The appeal's dismissal means the original two-month timeline for vacating the property, set by the trial court back in January 2023, now stands without further judicial hurdle in the appellant's way, subject to whatever process is needed to execute the decree. For an appellant who had resisted the suit for close to a decade through trial and appeal, the result closes off the one line of defence he had relied on throughout.

The judgment is a reminder of how often litigation over JJR Scheme and similar resettlement plots in Delhi turns on exactly this question: whether a power of attorney, however old or however long acted upon, can stand in for a registered sale deed. Courts have answered that question the same way for over a decade, and this ruling adds one more instance to a fairly uniform body of precedent rather than breaking new ground.

For residents of such colonies, the practical lesson is straightforward. A power of attorney, no matter how formally drafted or notarised, does not substitute for registration when the underlying transaction is in substance a sale. Anyone buying into a JJR Scheme plot through such a document, rather than through a transfer recognised by the allotting authority, takes on the risk that a court will treat the paper as worthless against a party holding better documented title.

Why It Matters

Thousands of plots in Delhi's resettlement colonies changed hands informally through power of attorney documents rather than registered deeds, often because the original allotment terms restricted sale. This ruling reaffirms, in the specific context of a JJR Scheme plot, that such documents give the holder no ownership that a civil court will protect against a party with better-documented title, which matters directly to anyone occupying or planning to buy such property in Delhi's resettlement colonies.
