NCLAT Delhi: 30-Day IBC Appeal Limitation Runs From Open Court Pronouncement, Not Order Upload

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, IBC Laws and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

An appeal filed just thirteen days past what the appellant believed was the deadline has been thrown out by the NCLAT's Principal Bench in New Delhi, which held that the clock for filing an insolvency appeal starts running the moment an order is pronounced in open court, whatever the date it is later uploaded on the registry's portal. The ruling in Ajay Singh Narania v. Harish Kant Kaushik & Ors. reiterates a rule that has caused more than a few insolvency appeals to fail on a technicality that litigants keep misjudging.

The underlying order came from the NCLT, Mumbai, dated 27 March 2026. Under Section 61(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, an appeal to the NCLAT must be filed within thirty days of that order, with a further fifteen days available if the appellant can show sufficient cause for the delay, and no more beyond that. The appellant's reckoning placed the thirty-day period as starting later than it actually did, apparently on the assumption that time ran from when the order became available on the Tribunal's website rather than from the date it was pronounced.

On the Bench's calculation, limitation commenced on 27 March 2026 and expired on 26 April 2026. The appeal was filed only on 21 May 2026, which put it beyond not just the primary thirty-day window but also the additional fifteen days the NCLAT is permitted to condone under the proviso to Section 61(2). An application seeking condonation of what the appellant called a thirteen-day delay was accordingly rejected, since on the correct reckoning the delay exceeded what the statute allows the Tribunal to excuse at all.

This follows the Supreme Court's reasoning in V. Nagarajan v. SKS Ispat and Power Ltd., which held that a litigant has to file an appeal within thirty days of pronouncement, extendable by a further fifteen on sufficient cause being shown, and that the limitation period is not deferred merely because a certified or uploaded copy of the order arrives later. Commentators have pointed out that Sections 61(1) and 61(2) of the IBC deliberately omit the kind of language found in Section 421(3) of the Companies Act, which ties limitation to when the order is made available to the party aggrieved, so the NCLAT has treated the omission as intentional rather than an oversight to be read around.

The practical difficulty this creates is well known among insolvency practitioners. NCLT orders are often pronounced in open court on one date and uploaded to the e-filing portal, sometimes with a certified copy following still later, days or weeks afterward. A party relying on the upload date rather than the pronouncement date to calculate its appeal window can lose the right to appeal entirely before it has even seen the order in writing, simply because it assumed the clock started later than it did.

The NCLAT's order in this case gives no indication that the delay arose from any fault on the Tribunal's part in making the order available. It reads as a straightforward application of the pronouncement rule to a short delay that the appellant might otherwise have expected to be condoned, had it fallen within the fifteen-day cushion Section 61(2) allows. Because the Bench calculated the actual delay as running past even that cushion, there was no discretion left to exercise.

For parties involved in insolvency litigation, whether operational creditors, financial creditors, or corporate debtors facing an adverse order, the lesson is to treat the date of pronouncement in open court as the only safe starting point for counting an appeal deadline. Waiting for a certified copy or an upload notification before calculating the limitation period, as this appellant appears to have done, carries a real risk that the appeal will be time-barred before it is even filed, with no room left for the Tribunal to extend it further.

It is also a reminder of how little room the IBC's appellate scheme leaves for argument once the forty-five-day outer limit, thirty days plus the fifteen-day condonable period, has passed. Unlike ordinary civil appeals, where courts retain some flexibility to excuse delay on a case-by-case basis under the Limitation Act, Section 61(2) draws a hard line. Practitioners advising clients on insolvency appeals would do well to calendar the pronouncement date the moment an adverse order is announced, rather than waiting for paperwork to catch up, because by the time it does, the window to appeal may already be closed.

Why It Matters

Parties who lose before the NCLT often wait for a certified or uploaded copy of the order before calculating when their appeal is due, assuming that is when the clock starts. This ruling confirms that assumption is wrong: the thirty-day window under Section 61(2) of the IBC runs from the date of pronouncement in open court, and miscalculating that date, as happened here, can cost a party its right to appeal altogether, with no discretion left for the Tribunal to condone the delay.
