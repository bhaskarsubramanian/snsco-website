Unmarried Sister Qualifies as Legal Heir for Farmer's Accident Death Benefit: Allahabad High Court

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, outlookmoney.com and juryscan.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Km. Vidyawati's brother farmed land in Uttar Pradesh. When he died in an accident on 18 May 2019, the state's own welfare scheme for farmers should have paid his household a lump sum built for exactly this kind of loss. Instead, his unmarried sister spent years in court establishing that she counted as family at all.

The scheme in question, the Mukhyamantri Kisan Evam Sarvahit Bima Yojana, is a Uttar Pradesh government programme that pays an accidental death benefit to a farmer's household, administered on the ground through Oriental Insurance Company. Km. Vidyawati, as her brother's dependent and unmarried sister, filed a claim as his legal heir after his death. Oriental Insurance turned her down, pointing to the policy's own description of covered family members, a list that did not, in its reading, extend to a sister.

That rejection is what brought Writ Petition No. 28051 of 2023 before the Allahabad High Court. Oriental Insurance's position rested on treating the scheme's list of named relations the way an ordinary personal accident policy treats its schedule of beneficiaries: as a closed class, outside of which no claim can succeed, however genuine the dependency. On that reading, a widow, a minor child or a surviving parent could claim, but an unmarried sister living on her brother's income could not, simply because the drafting had not named her. Km. Vidyawati's position throughout was that she had in fact depended on her brother, that no other claimant had come forward, and that the scheme existed to soften exactly this kind of loss for whoever in the household actually bore it, regardless of whether a draftsman had anticipated her particular relationship to the deceased.

The High Court rejected that approach. A welfare scheme meant to compensate a farmer's household for an accidental death, the Court held, cannot be read so narrowly as to exclude a dependent, unmarried sister merely because the insurer's list of named relations happens not to mention sisters. The benefit is payable to the farmer's legal heirs, and an unmarried sister without independent means of her own falls within that category as surely as a spouse or a child would. Insurers administering a state welfare scheme, the order suggests, cannot import exclusions drawn from ordinary commercial underwriting practice to cut down a class of beneficiaries the scheme itself was designed to protect.

Underlying the Court's reasoning is a distinction that recurs across welfare-scheme litigation generally: a commercial personal accident policy is a bargain struck between an insurer and a named policyholder, and its list of beneficiaries reflects what that bargain actually priced in, whereas a government welfare scheme is funded and designed to answer a social need, with the insurer acting as administrator rather than as the party whose risk appetite set the scheme's boundaries. Reading the second kind of instrument through the lens of the first, the Court suggested, inverts the relationship between the scheme's purpose and its paperwork, letting an administrator's drafting choices override the welfare objective the state itself set out to achieve.

Having found the rejection unsustainable, the Court quashed it and directed Oriental Insurance Company to pay Km. Vidyawati Rs. 5,00,000 as the accidental death benefit due on her brother's death.

The ruling matters beyond this one family. State governments across India run accident and welfare insurance schemes for farmers, often contracting their administration to general insurers who then apply standard policy wording to claims arising under a scheme with a different, more protective purpose. Where that wording narrows the class of claimants below what the scheme itself intends, this order gives claimants a basis to argue that the welfare object of the scheme should control, not the insurer's boilerplate list of named relatives. For dependants who do not fit neatly into the categories an insurer's form anticipates, unmarried sisters, dependent siblings, or other relatives who kept a farming household running, the judgment is a reminder that a rejection on eligibility grounds is worth testing in writ jurisdiction rather than treated as final.

For insurers and scheme administrators, the practical lesson is to revisit claim-processing guidelines for government-backed farmer welfare schemes so that eligibility criteria track the scheme's own stated beneficiaries rather than a generic personal accident template. A rejection letter that simply cites an absent name on a standard form, without engaging with who the scheme was actually built to protect, now carries a real risk of being set aside.

Why It Matters

Government farmer welfare and accident insurance schemes are routinely administered by general insurers who apply ordinary policy exclusions to claims under schemes with a wider, welfare-driven purpose. This order tells insurers and scheme administrators that eligibility must be read against the scheme's own object, not a standard list of named relatives, and gives dependent relatives outside that list a concrete precedent to rely on. Firms advising insurers on scheme administration, or claimants on rejected welfare claims, should treat eligibility denials based purely on named-relative lists as open to challenge.
