Allahabad High Court's Lucknow Bench Tells Centre to Fix the Indefinite Freezing of Cyber Fraud Victims' Bank Accounts

Source: Originally reported by outlookindia.com, dailypioneer.com and Law Chakra. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A builder in Uttar Pradesh has been locked out of his own bank accounts since 2020, not because he did anything wrong, but because his son clicked a link sent by a cybercriminal and, in the process, shared Aadhaar details and other personal information. An FIR was lodged over the resulting fraud, and in the course of that investigation, the petitioner's bank accounts were frozen. Six years on, with the fraud investigation seemingly stalled and the freeze still in place, he had no way to run his business, and he turned to the Allahabad High Court's Lucknow Bench for relief.

This is a fact pattern that has become familiar to courts across the country as digital payment fraud has grown: a victim or a person merely connected to a suspect account finds their own, unrelated funds frozen as a side effect of an investigation into someone else's fraud, with no clear mechanism to get the freeze lifted once the immediate investigative purpose has been served. The petitioner's case was that his accounts had nothing to do with the underlying fraud beyond the fact that his son's data had been compromised, yet the freeze had outlasted any plausible investigative need by years.

The bench of Justices Rajan Roy and Subhash Vidyarthi did not simply direct the banks to lift the freeze and move on. It took the broader view that this is a systemic problem rather than a one-off grievance, and impleaded the Union of India through the Ministry of Home Affairs as a party, asking the Centre to work out a solution for cyber fraud victims whose accounts remain frozen indefinitely with no administrative pathway to resolution. The court also directed the petitioner to add the Additional Chief Secretary of the Uttar Pradesh Home Department, the Lucknow Police Commissioner and the station house officer of the relevant police station as parties, spreading responsibility across the agencies that would need to act on any fix.

Counsel for two of the respondent banks told the court that one of them had already contacted the cybercrime cell for instructions and, having received none, had gone ahead and discontinued the freeze on its own. That detail mattered to the court's reasoning: it showed that at least one bank had concluded the freeze no longer served any investigative purpose, while other accounts tied to the same petitioner apparently remained frozen for want of any similar initiative, underlining the lack of a consistent, time-bound process across banks and investigating agencies.

The matter has been listed again after four weeks, treated as a fresh hearing rather than a mere adjournment, which suggests the court wants the Centre to come back with something more concrete than a status report. This builds on a line of Lucknow and Allahabad Bench rulings through 2026 that have pushed back against blanket, open-ended freezes in cybercrime investigations, including an earlier ruling that police cannot freeze an entire account without linking the full balance to the suspected crime, and that freezing powers under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita are confined to the amount actually traceable to the fraud.

For account holders who find themselves caught up in a cyber fraud investigation through no fault of their own, whether as the original victim, a family member, or simply someone who transacted with a compromised account, the practical problem has rarely been whether a freeze was justified at the outset. It has been the absence of any clear timeline or forum for getting an unjustified freeze lifted once the investigative need has passed, leaving account holders to approach a High Court directly, years after the fact, simply to get access to their own funds restored.

If the Centre does respond with a concrete mechanism, it would matter well beyond this one petitioner. Non-resident account holders, whose NRE and NRO accounts can be flagged in similar fraud-linked freezes while they are outside the country and poorly placed to chase a resolution through local police stations, face an even harder version of the same problem, since they cannot simply walk into a bank branch or a police station to sort out the freeze in person.

Why It Matters

The order pushes the Centre toward designing an actual administrative mechanism for lifting bank account freezes imposed during cybercrime investigations once their investigative purpose has lapsed, rather than leaving account holders with no option but years of litigation to recover funds never shown to be connected to any fraud at all. Banks, too, are left without a consistent protocol for deciding when to lift a freeze on their own.
