A Revenue Entry Made While She Was a Child Can't Start the Clock: J&K&L High Court Revives Inheritance Appeal

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, dailyexcelsior.com, kashmirlife.net and newsbytesapp.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Land disputes in Jammu's hill districts have a habit of outliving the people who start them, and this one is a case in point. A woman named Themu, daughter of one Sonu, sued claiming she was entitled to half of 58 kanals and 11 marlas recorded under Khewat No. 1 in Machail village of Padder tehsil, Kishtwar, as her late father's only daughter. The trial court agreed with her and decreed the suits in her favour on 9 March 2004. More than eight years later, on 7 November 2012, the District Judge, Kishtwar reversed that decree in appeal, on the ground that her claim was barred by limitation. It has taken until this October, and a second appeal before the High Court of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh, for Themu to get her decree back.

What made the District Judge throw out a decree that the trial court had granted was a revenue entry, a mutation recorded in favour of one Shib Devi, that had been entered at a point when Themu was still a minor. The appellate court treated that entry as sufficient notice to Themu that her title was being denied, and reasoned that limitation for her suit began running from that date, long before she filed it as an adult. On that logic, her claim was decades too late.

Justice Shahzad Azeem did not accept that reasoning. The judgment leans on Section 6 of the Limitation Act, which exists precisely to protect people who cannot be expected to act on their own rights while they are minors, and holds that a revenue entry recorded against a minor's interest does not automatically amount to the kind of clear, unequivocal denial of title that is needed to set limitation running. A mutation is, after all, an administrative record kept by revenue officials for their own purposes; treating it as a formal assertion against a child who had no capacity to respond to it is a different matter altogether.

Beyond the legal point, there was a plain gap in the defendants' proof. The High Court noted that nobody on the other side had led any evidence establishing exactly when the alleged denial of Themu's title had occurred, or what her actual date of birth was, both of which were essential to work out whether the limitation period, even if it did start running at some point during her minority, had actually expired by the time she sued. Without that evidence, the District Judge's finding that the suit was time-barred rested on an assumption rather than proof.

Paternity was the other front on which the District Judge's judgment fell short. The appellate court had rejected the evidence establishing that Themu was indeed Sonu's daughter, which happens to be the very fact her entire claim to inherit depended on. The High Court held that rejection was mistaken too, which meant the limitation finding was not the only thing wrong with the judgment under appeal. Having found both pillars of the reversal unsound, Justice Azeem set aside the District Judge's judgment and decree of 7 November 2012 and restored the trial court's original decree of 9 March 2004 in the two clubbed suits.

None of this turns on any special feature of Muslim or Hindu personal law governing succession in the region; it is a straightforward application of the general limitation framework to a fact pattern that recurs constantly in hill-district litigation, where a family member who was a child when a disputed mutation was recorded grows up and sues only once she is in a position to do so. Treating the childhood mutation as if it were served notice on an adult would, in effect, let defendants defeat a minor's claim before she was even old enough to know it existed.

Inheritance disputes rooted in decades-old mutations are common across Jammu's hill districts, where land records were often updated informally and claimants frequently were children when the disputed entries were made. This judgment gives those claimants a clear line of defence against the limitation plea that landholders in possession typically raise: a mutation entered during a claimant's minority is not, without more, proof that the clock started ticking that early. Anyone resisting such a claim will now need actual evidence of when a clear denial occurred and of the claimant's date of birth, not just the existence of an old revenue record.

Why It Matters

Limitation is the most common weapon used to defeat long-delayed inheritance claims in hill districts where land mutations go unchallenged for years. This ruling narrows that weapon considerably by refusing to treat a revenue entry made during a claimant's childhood as automatic proof that limitation began running then, and by insisting on actual evidence of both the date of denial and the claimant's date of birth before a suit can be dismissed as time-barred.
