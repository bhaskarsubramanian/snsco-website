Real Estate & RERA · 14 July 2026

Supreme Court Freezes Builder Directors' Bank Accounts and Issues Warrants Over Two-Decade RERA Non-Compliance

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw on 14 July 2026. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes — it is not a reproduction of the original report. Readers are encouraged to consult the original source and the underlying judgment or order directly.

The Supreme Court has taken the unusually coercive step of freezing the bank accounts of the directors of Parsvnath Developers Ltd. and Parsvnath Hessa Developers Ltd., and issuing warrants against them, after the Haryana-based developer failed for years to comply with orders of the Haryana Real Estate Regulatory Authority (HRERA) directing it to hand over possession to homebuyers of the Parsvnath Exotica project in Gurugram.

A Bench led by the Chief Justice was hearing writ petitions filed by senior citizen homebuyers who paid the full sale consideration for their flats nearly two decades ago and have still not received possession, despite HRERA orders in their favour dating back to 2021-22 that the developer has simply not complied with. The Court's frustration extended beyond the developer to the State machinery itself, observing that Haryana authorities appeared to have either colluded with the builder or failed to discharge their statutory duty to enforce RERA's own orders — a rare direct rebuke of a state regulator's enforcement failure rather than merely the developer's default.

Beyond freezing the directors' accounts and issuing warrants, the Court restrained Parsvnath from creating any third-party rights in the project or handing over possession of any unit to anyone until further orders — closing off the possibility that the developer could keep monetising the same stalled project while ignoring the homebuyers who already paid for it years ago. The episode has become a pointed illustration of a structural problem the Court itself has flagged: a RERA order is only as good as the mechanism available to enforce it, and where state authorities decline or fail to use their enforcement powers, homebuyers are left with a paper remedy while builders run out the clock.

For homebuyers stuck in a similar bind — a RERA order in hand but no real-world compliance — the case is a reminder that HRERA/RERA orders are not self-executing and that enforcement often requires escalation well beyond the regulator itself, up to and including writ proceedings that put direct, personal pressure on a developer's promoters and directors rather than only on the corporate entity. For developers, the message is equally direct: prolonged, unexplained non-compliance with a RERA order now carries a real risk of personal, coercive consequences for directors, not just corporate liability that can be managed through insolvency or restructuring.

Why It Matters

A RERA order in your favour is not the end of the road if the builder simply ignores it — persistent non-compliance can be escalated to coercive relief against the developer's directors personally, including account freezes and warrants.
