SEBI Orders Avadhut Sathe Trading Academy To Refund Rs 546 Crore Over Unregistered Advice

Source: Originally reported by Business Standard, zeebiz.com, casansaar.com and smefutures.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Avadhut Sathe built a following teaching retail investors how to trade. SEBI's final order, passed on 23 October 2026, treats that teaching business as something else entirely: an unregistered investment advisory and research analyst operation that collected money from clients it was never licensed to advise. The order directs Avadhut Sathe Trading Academy Private Limited and its founder to jointly refund close to Rs 614 crore, counting interest, and closes out a case that has been running since SEBI first moved against the academy in December 2025.

The regulator's case was never about whether Sathe's trading calls worked. It was about who is allowed to charge for them. SEBI's finding is that the academy's paid programmes amounted to investment advice and research analysis dressed up as education, services that the SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations and the SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations reserve for entities that have registered with the Board and accepted the compliance burden that comes with it. An unregistered adviser does not get to keep fees collected for advice it had no licence to give, whatever the quality of that advice turned out to be.

SEBI's first move came as an ex-parte interim order cum show cause notice in December 2025, impounding roughly Rs 546.16 crore from the academy, Sathe and his wife Gouri Avadhut Sathe pending a full inquiry. An ex-parte freeze of that size is a blunt instrument, and the noticees went to the Securities Appellate Tribunal to soften it. SAT obliged only in part. On 22 January 2026, it allowed the academy to keep operating rather than shutting down outright, but conditioned that relief on depositing Rs 100 crore in a fixed deposit with a lien marked in SEBI's favour, a halfway measure that preserved SEBI's security over the disputed money while the show cause proceedings ran their course.

That is the backdrop against which October's final order has to be read. SEBI did not simply confirm the interim freeze; it worked out, after hearing the noticees, exactly how much had come from the clients SEBI says the academy was never entitled to advise, and ordered that amount handed back rather than retained. The refund figure comes to about Rs 546.17 crore in principal, with a further Rs 67.87 crore in interest, bringing the total to roughly Rs 614 crore. Under the order, that interest component goes not to SEBI itself but to its Investor Protection and Education Fund, the statutory account SEBI uses to compensate investors and fund awareness work rather than to its own budget.

SEBI built in a compliance mechanism rather than leaving repayment to trust. The noticees have five months from receiving the order to complete the refunds, and once they are done, a chartered accountant with peer-review status has thirty days to certify a completion report confirming that the money actually reached the clients it was taken from. That two-step structure, independent certification following actual repayment, is SEBI's standard answer to the practical problem that a refund order is only as good as someone checking whether the money moved.

Separate from the refund, SEBI imposed monetary penalties totalling Rs 11.5 crore, split unevenly across the three noticees: Rs 6 crore on the academy itself, Rs 4 crore on Avadhut Sathe personally, and Rs 1.5 crore on his wife Gouri Avadhut Sathe, payable within forty-five days of the order. The market-access bars track the same hierarchy of culpability. The academy and Sathe are barred from the securities market for two years or until they comply with the refund direction, whichever is later, tying the length of the ban directly to compliance rather than letting it run out on a fixed calendar regardless of whether the money has actually gone back. Gouri Sathe, treated as less central to the running of the business, draws a shorter six-month bar. Bank accounts belonging to the academy and to Sathe himself stay frozen for debits until the refund amounts are actually deposited, which keeps the pressure on even after the order is signed and the appeal clock starts running.

For the broader market in trading education and paid stock-tip channels, the order is less about one finfluencer's business model than about where the line sits between teaching and advising. A course that explains how markets work is not the same thing, in SEBI's eyes, as a paid programme built around specific calls on what to buy and when, and dressing the second kind of activity in the language of education does not convert it into something outside SEBI's registration requirements. Firms and individuals running similar paid trading communities, mentorship programmes or signal services without an investment adviser or research analyst licence are the ones most directly exposed by the reasoning in this order, and the staged penalty and ban structure here gives a fairly detailed template for what SEBI does once it decides unregistered advice has been dressed up as something else.

Why It Matters

The order sets out, in unusual detail, how SEBI treats paid trading education that functions as investment advice: refund of the fees collected, interest routed to the Investor Protection Fund, a CA-certified compliance check, and bans tied to actual repayment rather than a fixed calendar. Anyone running a paid trading course, mentorship programme or signal service without an investment adviser or research analyst registration is exposed to the same reasoning, regardless of how the business is marketed.
