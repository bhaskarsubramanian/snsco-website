UP Discom Loses Arbitration Challenge After Missing Section 34 Deadline By Refiling In The Wrong City First

Source: Originally reported by Indian Kanoon. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A Uttar Pradesh electricity distribution company that lost an arbitration over a sub-station contract has now lost its chance to challenge that award at all, after its lawyers filed the challenge petition in the wrong court first and ran out of time refiling it in the right one. The Delhi High Court's Commercial Appellate Division dismissed two connected appeals filed by Dakshinanchal Vidyut Vitran Nigam Ltd, the discom that serves large parts of western Uttar Pradesh, against M/s Creative Entrepreneurs LLP, the contractor it had engaged for a turnkey 33/11 KV sub-station project at Kasganj.

The underlying contract went back to 2015 and 2016, when the discom issued a letter of intent and then three work orders for the sub-station work. A payment dispute followed, and the contractor took it to the Micro and Small Enterprise Facilitation Council under Section 18 of the MSMED Act. When conciliation there failed, the matter went to arbitration at the Delhi Arbitration Centre, which delivered an award dated 1 April 2024 against the discom. The award was served on the discom on 6 May 2024.

Under Section 34(3) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, a party has three months from the date of receipt of the award to file an application to set it aside, with a further thirty days available only if the court is satisfied there was sufficient cause for the delay, and nothing beyond that. The discom filed its objection petition on 21 May 2024, within time, but filed it before the Commercial Court in Agra rather than in Delhi, where the arbitration had been seated. The Agra court dismissed the petition for want of territorial jurisdiction. By the time the discom refiled the same objection before the Commercial Court at Rohini in Delhi, it was 10 June 2026, well over two years after the award. The Rohini court dismissed the refiled petition as time-barred on 24 August 2026, and the discom appealed that dismissal under Section 37 of the Act.

On appeal, the Bench accepted that Section 14 of the Limitation Act, which allows a litigant to exclude the time spent pursuing a remedy in good faith before a court that turns out to lack jurisdiction, does apply to a Section 34 petition despite the strict three-month-plus-thirty-day language of that provision. That finding went some way toward the discom's position. It did not go far enough. Even after excluding the period the objection had spent before the Agra court, the gap between the dismissal there and the refiling in Delhi still placed the Rohini filing outside the window Section 34(3) allows, and Section 5 of the Limitation Act, which lets a court condone delay beyond a statutory period for sufficient cause in other kinds of proceedings, does not apply to Section 34 at all, a position the Bench treated as settled by Supreme Court authority.

The Bench was equally clear about what it was not deciding. The discom's substantive arguments on the award, including whether the MSMED Act's facilitation-council route even applies to a turnkey works contract of this kind, and whether the interest rate the arbitrator awarded was excessive, were never examined. An appeal under Section 37 against an order made under Section 34, the Court held, is confined to whether the Section 34 court exercised its own jurisdiction correctly, not an occasion to revisit the merits of an award that the Section 34 court never got to examine because the petition challenging it was out of time. Finding no error in the Rohini court's reasoning, the Bench dismissed both appeals.

Public sector discoms and other government-linked entities that face arbitration under the MSMED Act's facilitation-council mechanism can draw a practical lesson from this appeal: it is about where to file, not how. Getting the venue of a Section 34 petition wrong carries none of the forgiveness available in ordinary civil litigation. Section 14 can rescue the time spent in a court that lacked jurisdiction, but only if the refiling itself happens quickly once that court's lack of jurisdiction becomes clear, since Section 5's power to condone delay is simply unavailable once the three-month-plus-thirty-day limit under Section 34(3) runs out. For any entity with a dispute seated in one city but more used to litigating from its own headquarters elsewhere, this is a sharp reminder to confirm the seat of arbitration before filing a challenge petition, not after.

Why It Matters

State discoms and other public-sector entities facing arbitral awards under the MSMED Act's facilitation-council route often litigate out of their own headquarters without first confirming where the arbitration was seated. This ruling confirms that Section 14 of the Limitation Act can rescue time lost by filing a Section 34 challenge in the wrong court, but only if the party refiles quickly in the correct one, since Section 5's general power to condone delay has no application to Section 34 petitions once the statutory window closes, leaving an appellate court unable to even reach the merits of the underlying award.
