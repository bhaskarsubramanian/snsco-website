Delhi High Court Permanently Bars 'Z-CONTIN' Heart Drug Mark as Too Close to Mundipharma's CONTIN Family

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and medicaldialogues.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Modi-Mundipharma has spent years in Delhi courtrooms protecting a single suffix. The company owns a family of marks built around the word CONTIN, used across its sustained-release drug formulations, and it treats any new brand that borrows that suffix as an attempt to trade on a reputation it built over decades. The latest round involved a cardiac drug, and the stakes were higher than the usual trademark skirmish: the medicine in question is a nitroglycerin tablet, prescribed for angina, where a patient confusing one brand for another at the pharmacy counter is not a minor inconvenience.

The suit was filed against Arlak Biotech Private Limited and Kuresia Pharma Private Limited, who were marketing their own nitroglycerin formulation under the name Z-CONTIN. Modi-Mundipharma argued that the CONTIN element was the distinctive core of its mark, not a generic pharmaceutical suffix free for anyone to use, and that Z-CONTIN was close enough in sound and appearance to invite exactly the kind of mix-up that drug regulators and courts have repeatedly warned against in cardiac medication.

Justice Vikas Mahajan did not require a full trial to resolve the dispute. He passed what amounted to a summary judgment, finding that the defendants had not put forward a case strong enough to justify the expense and delay of oral evidence. The court held that continued sale of nitroglycerin tablets under the Z-CONTIN name would likely confuse the public about an association with Modi-Mundipharma's registered CONTIN marks, and that this was enough to make out infringement and passing off without needing to test every disputed fact at trial.

The order permanently restrains Arlak Biotech and Kuresia Pharma from manufacturing, selling, marketing or in any way dealing with nitroglycerin tablets, or any other pharmaceutical product, under the Z-CONTIN mark or any other mark deceptively similar to CONTIN. The court also awarded Modi-Mundipharma nominal damages of Rs 2 lakh, along with costs of the litigation, a modest sum by commercial litigation standards but one that signals the court treated the infringement as made out rather than merely arguable.

This is not Modi-Mundipharma's first outing on this exact question. The company has fought and won similar disputes over marks such as Cardiocontin and Nitrocontin, where courts have taken the position that the ordinary deceptive-similarity test gets tightened further when the product is a cardiac drug. A mix-up over a shampoo brand is one thing; a mix-up at a pharmacy counter involving a heart medication carries a different order of risk, and Delhi High Court judges hearing this line of cases have said so in as many words in earlier rounds.

For other pharmaceutical companies, domestic and foreign, the ruling is a reminder that owning a registered mark built around a common pharma-sounding suffix does not make that suffix available to competitors just because many drug names share similar endings. Courts are willing to draw the line in the trademark holder's favour when the resemblance is close and the underlying product is one where confusion carries a health risk, and they are increasingly willing to do so by way of summary judgment rather than putting a plaintiff through a multi-year trial to get there.

The case also illustrates how the summary judgment provisions under the Commercial Courts regime are being used in trademark practice: where a defendant's written statement does not raise a triable defence, the court can decide the suit on affidavit evidence and pleadings alone, sparing both sides years of evidence recording. For brand owners who have already litigated the same point once, this considerably shortens the second and third rounds against copycat marks.

Generic drug makers often assume that because many cardiac and pain-management formulations share a common suffix across the industry, a new entrant can safely adopt a name built around that suffix so long as the full brand name differs by a letter or two. This order treats that assumption as risky rather than safe, at least where the senior mark's owner can show a long, consistent history of enforcing the same suffix against earlier entrants. For smaller formulation companies weighing a brand name before launch, the practical lesson is to clear a proposed mark against the full family of registrations held by established players in the category, not merely against the exact mark being copied; a search limited to identical marks would have missed this risk entirely, since Z-CONTIN and CONTIN are not identical strings, only similar enough to confuse a patient at a pharmacy counter.

Why It Matters

The ruling confirms that Indian courts apply a stricter test for deceptive similarity in pharmaceutical trademarks, particularly cardiac medication, and that a brand owner who has already established its mark's distinctiveness in earlier litigation can get a quicker summary judgment against a later copycat rather than a full trial. It is a caution for smaller drug makers that clearing a new brand name against identical marks alone is not enough due diligence before launch.
