Supreme Court: Once a Resolution Plan Goes to the NCLT, the Committee of Creditors Cannot Take Its Approval Back

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz, IBC Laws, Law Trend and advocatekhoj.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Committees of creditors in insolvency proceedings sometimes get cold feet after they have already voted to approve a resolution plan, usually because the corporate debtor's fortunes improve just enough to make continuing the plan look like a bad bargain compared to what could be recovered otherwise. The Supreme Court has now shut that door firmly. In Bank of India v. S.M. Kamal Pasha, decided on 26 October 2026, the Court held that a CoC cannot withdraw its approval of a resolution plan once that plan has been placed before the National Company Law Tribunal for sanction.

The appeal arrived at the Supreme Court after a round trip through both tribunals below. The Committee of Creditors, acting through the Resolution Professional, had approved a resolution plan and then had a change of heart once the corporate debtor's financial position improved. The Resolution Professional accordingly moved the NCLT with an application seeking to withdraw the CoC's approval, and the NCLT allowed it, effectively putting the already-approved plan back in play.

That order did not survive appeal. The NCLAT set aside the NCLT's decision permitting withdrawal and revived the pending application for approval of the resolution plan, taking the view that an approved plan cannot simply be unwound because the CoC later decides it no longer likes the deal. Bank of India, representing the CoC's position, carried the matter further to the Supreme Court.

Justices Pardiwala and Vinod Chandran read Sections 12A, 30 and 31 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code together and concluded that the statute allows withdrawal only up to the point of CoC approval, not after. Once the CoC has voted to approve a plan and it has gone before the Adjudicating Authority, both the CoC and the successful resolution applicant are bound by it pending the NCLT's own decision under Section 31. The Court declined to treat an improvement in the corporate debtor's financial fortunes as a ground for undoing that position, holding that profitability alone cannot justify refusing to implement a plan that otherwise satisfies the requirements of Section 30(2).

The logic is less about punishing a CoC for changing its mind than about protecting the integrity of the resolution process itself. A resolution applicant bids for a stressed company on the basis of a plan the CoC has approved, and invests time and capital structuring that bid around the approved terms. If the CoC could simply walk back its approval whenever the underlying business looked a little healthier than expected, every resolution applicant would have to discount its bid for the risk of the rug being pulled after approval, which defeats the predictability the Code is meant to supply at exactly the stage where certainty matters most.

Section 12A of the Code, which the Court read alongside Sections 30 and 31, deals with withdrawal of the corporate insolvency resolution process itself on an application backed by ninety per cent of the voting share of the CoC, and the Court was careful to keep that provision in its proper lane. Withdrawing the entire CIRP under Section 12A is a different act from a CoC trying to unpick its own approval of a plan already sitting with the Adjudicating Authority, and treating the two as interchangeable would let a CoC achieve indirectly, through a plan withdrawal, what Section 12A was never designed to permit at that late a stage.

The practical consequence for the parties before the Court is that the plan-approval application the NCLAT revived now goes back on track for the NCLT to decide on its own merits under Section 31, rather than being derailed by the CoC's intervening change of heart. The corporate debtor's improved numbers may well be relevant to how the plan is eventually implemented, but they are not a reason to let the CoC avoid implementing the bargain it already struck.

For CoC members and resolution professionals, the ruling draws a bright line around the point of no return in a CIRP. Creditors who are unhappy with a plan have every opportunity to vote it down before approval, or to negotiate better terms while the plan is still being finalised. What they cannot do, on this ruling, is approve a plan, watch the corporate debtor's numbers improve, and then try to extract a better deal or abandon the plan altogether by withdrawing consent after the file has already gone to the NCLT. Lenders structuring exit strategies around possible withdrawal after approval will need to rework that assumption.

Why It Matters

The ruling forecloses a tactic some creditors have tried when a corporate debtor's finances recover after a resolution plan is approved but before the NCLT sanctions it: withdrawing consent to chase a better recovery. By fixing CoC approval, not NCLT sanction, as the point after which withdrawal is no longer available, the Court gives resolution applicants and the insolvency process generally more certainty that an approved plan will actually be implemented.
