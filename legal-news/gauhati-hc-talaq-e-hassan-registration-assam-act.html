Talaq-e-Hassan Is Not Prohibited in India, But Registration Must Follow the 2024 Assam Act: Gauhati High Court

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, SCC Online, theprint.in and siasat.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A man who had already pronounced talaq three times under the Talaq-e-Hassan procedure found himself stuck not on whether the divorce was valid, but on who in Assam was supposed to put it on paper. He had made his three pronouncements on 22 March, 26 April and 27 May 2026, a month apart each time as the procedure requires, and then went looking for a registrar to formalise what he had done. The registrar he approached in Barpeta turned him down, and that refusal is what landed the matter before Justice Arun Dev Choudhury of the Gauhati High Court.

The problem was not with the talaq itself. The judge held that Talaq-e-Hassan, the practice of pronouncing divorce once a month over three consecutive months, is not currently prohibited in India. That finding alone settles the substantive question for anyone relying on this form of divorce in Assam: the practice stands as a recognised, lawful route to dissolving a Muslim marriage, at least until Parliament or a competent court says otherwise.

The registration side of the case was messier. The petitioner had filed an application on 2 June 2026 under Section 12 of the new law, seeking to have his divorce registered. The registrar he went to at Barpeta, however, was functioning under the old framework, the Assam Moslem Marriages and Divorces Registration Act of 1935, which has since been repealed. A registrar operating under a statute that no longer exists was never going to be the right address for a registration request built on the successor law.

Justice Choudhury's answer was to redirect, not to resolve the merits of registration himself. He held that the petitioner needed to go to the jurisdictional Marriage and Divorce Registrar appointed under the 2024 Assam Act, the body actually empowered to register a Talaq-e-Hassan divorce once it is satisfied that the procedure under that Act has been followed. The court did not order registration outright; it ordered the petitioner to the correct door and left the registrar under the new Act to examine the application on its own terms.

This distinction matters because of how Assam has restructured its machinery for registering Muslim marriages and divorces. The 2024 Act replaced the 1935 framework entirely, which means registrars and procedures that functioned perfectly well under the old law no longer have any legal standing to act on new applications. A person seeking registration now has to know which authority currently holds that power, and a mistake on that point, as this petitioner discovered, can cost months even when the underlying divorce itself is not in question.

The ruling leaves Talaq-e-Hassan's legal status settled for practical purposes in Assam, at least as matters stand, while making clear that validity and registration are two separate hurdles. A pronouncement can be entirely lawful and still go unregistered if the person seeking registration approaches the wrong office, particularly in a state where the registration framework itself changed mid-stream. For lawyers advising clients on Muslim personal law matters in Assam, the case is a reminder to check which statute currently governs registration before filing anything, rather than assuming the old registrar network under the 1935 Act is still live.

The judgment also illustrates how a transition between two statutes can generate litigation that has nothing to do with the substance of the law and everything to do with procedure. The petitioner here was not fighting over whether his marriage had ended; he was fighting over paperwork, and only because the registrar he happened to approach was sitting under a repealed law. Practitioners handling similar registrations in Assam now have a clear judicial signal pointing them to the 2024 Act's registrar as the only competent authority going forward.

What the case does not do is settle how long such a redirection takes in practice, or what happens if the newly appointed registrar also refuses registration on some fresh ground. The petitioner still has to satisfy that registrar that his pronouncements followed the required monthly gaps and that the underlying marriage records check out, so the litigation may not be over even though the jurisdictional question is now answered. Clients going through a similar process should budget for that second stage rather than assuming a High Court order naming the correct registrar closes the matter outright.

Why It Matters

The ruling confirms that Talaq-e-Hassan remains a lawful, unprohibited form of divorce in India, while clarifying that registration in Assam must now go through the registrar appointed under the 2024 Assam Muslim Marriages and Divorces Registration Act, not any authority still operating under the repealed 1935 Act. Lawyers and litigants seeking registration of such divorces in Assam should verify which statute currently governs before approaching a registrar, to avoid the delay this petitioner faced.
