Habeas Corpus Cannot Decide a Transnational Child Custody Dispute: Delhi High Court

Source: Originally reported by Verdictum, supremetoday.ai and shoneekapoor.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A father and a grandmother approached the Delhi High Court with a writ of habeas corpus asking for the return of a minor boy who had been living with his mother in Berkeley, California, since 2018. Jasjit Singh Mangat, the father, framed the case as an illegal removal of his son from India without his knowledge. The mother disputed that framing entirely, telling the Court that the move to the United States had been a mutual decision between the parents and that the extended family had been kept informed throughout.

By the time the petition reached the Division Bench, the boy had been in California for close to eight years, had been born in the United States, held American citizenship, and also carried an Overseas Citizens of India card. He was no longer an infant whose whereabouts were in doubt. The real dispute was not where the child physically was, since nobody contested that, but with whom he ought to live and under what arrangement going forward, which is a very different question from the one a writ of habeas corpus is built to answer.

Justices Prathiba M. Singh and Madhu Jain used that distinction to dispose of the petition. A habeas corpus proceeding, the Bench explained, is summary in character and meant for cases of unlawful detention, not a vehicle for resolving contested custody claims that turn on years of lived circumstances, schooling, settled routine and the child's own attachments. Converting a writ petition into a prolonged custody trial, taking evidence on welfare and working out a long-term parenting arrangement, sits outside what the writ jurisdiction is designed to do, however pressing the underlying family dispute might be for the parties involved.

Pointing the father toward the statutory route instead, the Bench noted that the ordinary remedy in a custody dispute of this kind lies under the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act or the Guardians and Wards Act, depending on which applies to the family. Those proceedings allow a court to examine the child's welfare properly, call for reports, hear both parents and reach a reasoned custody or visitation order, rather than deciding the matter on the limited affidavit record that a habeas corpus petition typically generates. A guardianship court can also appoint a counsellor, direct a home study or seek the child's own wishes through an age-appropriate process, none of which a summary writ hearing is equipped to do on affidavits alone.

Flagging the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights as a forum the family could use in the meantime, the Bench suggested mediation there with a view to working out a parenting plan rather than letting the dispute run as adversarial litigation across two jurisdictions. For a child who has spent nearly his entire life abroad, an imposed order from an Indian writ court without any welfare inquiry would have risked disrupting a settled life on the strength of procedure rather than substance, and the Bench appears to have been alive to that risk even while declining to go into the merits itself. Mediation before a specialised child-rights body also avoids the adversarial posture that a habeas corpus hearing tends to force on both parents from the outset, which rarely serves a child's interests when the underlying dispute is really about access and parenting arrangements rather than wrongful confinement.

Cross-border custody disputes of this kind are becoming more common as Indian families spread across multiple countries, and this order gives such families a clear line to work with: habeas corpus remains open where a child's location or safety is genuinely in doubt, but once the child's whereabouts are known and settled, the fight shifts to custody on the merits, and that fight belongs in a guardianship forum equipped to hear it properly rather than before a writ court working from affidavits alone.

That approach carries a practical cost for a parent left behind in India, since guardianship proceedings move more slowly than a writ petition and require a fuller evidentiary record. Lawyers advising such a parent need to assess early, before any petition is drafted, whether the facts genuinely support an urgent writ for unlawful confinement or instead call for a properly pleaded guardianship or custody petition capable of engaging with the child's welfare in the detail a court will expect. Filing the wrong remedy costs years, as this case shows, and a court faced with a settled, long-resident child abroad is unlikely to disturb that status quo through a writ without the kind of inquiry only a guardianship proceeding can provide.

Why It Matters

NRI parents often reach for habeas corpus as the fastest route when a child has moved abroad with the other parent, assuming the writ jurisdiction can force a return. This ruling confirms Indian courts will decline that short cut once the child has been settled abroad for years, pushing families instead toward guardianship proceedings and bodies like the NCPCR. Counsel advising such families should plead the correct remedy from the outset rather than lose years to a procedurally mismatched writ petition.
