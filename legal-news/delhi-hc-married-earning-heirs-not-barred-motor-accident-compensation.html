Delhi HC: Marriage Alone Won't Bar a Motor Accident Claim

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Verdictum and courtbook.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Insurance companies defending motor accident claims have long argued a simple point: once a son or daughter marries, or a sibling starts earning and lives separately, that family member stops being a dependant and should get little or nothing when a parent or sibling dies in a road accident. The Delhi High Court has now told United India Insurance that this argument does not hold as a rule of thumb, deciding a batch of cross-appeals where the insurer wanted awards cut and the claimants wanted them raised.

The appeals arose out of Motor Accident Claims Tribunal awards passed in favour of family members, including a married woman who had lost both her father and her sister in the same road accident. The insurer argued that because she was married, employed and living in her own household, she could not be treated as dependent on either deceased and her compensation should be reduced accordingly. The claimants, for their part, argued the Tribunal had undervalued the loss using too conservative a method. Several connected appeals raised variations of the same question, with insurers in each case pointing to the claimant's marital status, employment or separate residence as grounds to cut the award, and claimants pointing to the actual closeness of the relationship and the support that had in fact been exchanged.

Justice Anish Dayal rejected the idea that dependency can be decided by sorting relatives into fixed boxes of dependant and non-dependant. The question, the judgment holds, has to be answered on the facts of each case, looking at what the relationship actually involved rather than at labels like married or unmarried, employed or unemployed, living together or living apart. A married, earning daughter, son, spouse or sibling is not excluded from compensation merely because of any one of those facts standing alone.

The Court went further on who may even bring a claim. Under Section 166 of the Motor Vehicles Act, it held, every legal representative of a deceased victim is entitled to maintain a compensation claim, and none of them needs to first prove financial dependency simply to get through the door. Dependency affects how much a particular claimant should recover relative to others, not whether that claimant has a claim at all. That distinction matters because tribunals and insurers have sometimes treated proof of dependency as a threshold the claimant must clear before any amount is even considered.

On how to value the claim of a relative who is not a primary dependant, the judgment rejected the notional loss of estate method the Tribunal had borrowed from an earlier precedent, Keith Rowe v. Prashant Sagar, calling that approach inconsistent with the structured method the Supreme Court has laid down in Sarla Verma and Pranay Sethi. Using the Sarla Verma and Pranay Sethi framework instead, the Court enhanced the compensation payable to the married woman who had lost her father and sister by more than twelve lakh rupees over what the Tribunal had awarded.

To guide future apportionment among multiple heirs with different degrees of dependency, the judgment set out illustrations rather than leaving tribunals to guess, including one example of a fifty-twenty-five-twenty-five split between a widow and two married children. That kind of worked example is likely to be picked up by tribunals across Delhi dealing with the same recurring fact pattern of a mixed household of dependent and self-supporting heirs.

The ruling sits against a line of earlier Delhi High Court orders that had taken a stricter view, treating siblings in particular as unlikely to qualify as dependants absent specific evidence to the contrary. Those earlier orders are not overruled outright, since the Court frames its approach as fact-specific rather than as a blanket rule in the claimants' favour, but insurers can no longer treat marital status, a job or a separate address as a shortcut to denying a claim, and claimants can no longer assume a sibling's claim will be waved through without proof of the relationship's real content.

What this means in practical terms is a heavier burden of proof on both sides at the tribunal stage. A claimant who is married, earning or living apart from the deceased will need to put in evidence, bank transfers, shared expenses, visits, or other material showing the actual degree of support or closeness, rather than relying on the family relationship alone to carry the claim. An insurer that wants to resist such a claim will equally need more than the bare facts of marriage or employment; it will have to show, on the specific record, why the relationship did not in substance involve dependency. Tribunals across Delhi handling the common fact pattern of a household with both dependent and self-supporting heirs now have the Sarla Verma and Pranay Sethi framework, applied through this judgment's illustrations, as the method to follow instead of ad hoc apportionment, and what the Tribunal and the insurer will have to examine going forward is the real nature of each relationship rather than a checklist of marital status, employment or address.

Why It Matters

Families pursuing motor accident claims often include a mix of dependent and earning, married relatives, and insurers routinely resist paying the latter on the ground that marriage or employment ends dependency. This ruling confirms that every legal representative can claim under Section 166 regardless of dependency, that dependency itself turns on facts rather than fixed categories, and that tribunals must apply the Sarla Verma and Pranay Sethi method rather than a notional loss of estate calculation when valuing such claims.
