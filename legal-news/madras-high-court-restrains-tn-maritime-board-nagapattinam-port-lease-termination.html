Madras High Court Stops Tamil Nadu Maritime Board From Ending a Port Shop Lease Before Its Term, Pending Arbitration

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw Biz and supremetoday.ai. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A shop lease at a state-run port terminal might seem a small thing to fight over in the High Court, but the Tamil Nadu Maritime Board found itself restrained from doing exactly what it wanted with one, after the Madras High Court held that the Board could not use a tendering policy that already existed at the time it granted the lease as a fresh reason to end it early.

The Board had allotted vacant commercial space at the Nagapattinam Passenger Terminal to PRG Shipping, after assessing the company's qualifications, rather than through an open tender. The arrangement began as a one-year tenure and was later extended by two years, with an approval letter dated 15 December 2025 confirming that the extended period would run until 23 December 2027. Barely six months into that extended term, the Board issued termination letters to PRG Shipping on 12 June 2026, and followed up a week later, on 19 June, with fresh e-tender notices inviting bids for the very same premises.

PRG Shipping's case was that the timing was no coincidence. It alleged that a ferry operator at the terminal had complained about space constraints and had effectively pressured the Board into clearing it out before its agreed term was up, so that the space could be re-let on terms the ferry operator preferred. The Board's defence, put through its Additional Advocate General, was more institutional: the allotment agreement allowed termination without cause, and in any event an open tender would be more transparent and would likely fetch higher rent for the Board than the negotiated arrangement PRG Shipping was operating under.

Justice K. Kumaresh Babu was not persuaded that a policy preference for tendering, one that existed before PRG Shipping was ever allotted the space, could now be dressed up as a ground to cut short a lease the Board itself had extended only months earlier. If transparency and better rent through tendering were always available to the Board, the judge reasoned, that consideration cannot suddenly become a valid reason to terminate once a tenant is in possession under a lease the Board had already renewed with full knowledge of how the space was being allotted.

The order restrains the Board from acting on its termination letters and from proceeding with the fresh e-tender for the Nagapattinam premises, keeping PRG Shipping in place while the underlying dispute goes to arbitration. The applications before the Court were framed as arbitration applications, which fits the limited question the Court had to answer: not who should run the shop at Nagapattinam in the end, but whether the status quo should hold while an arbitrator works that out, given the Board's own extension of the lease only months before it moved to end it.

What makes the ruling useful beyond this one port lease is its treatment of a public body's attempt to retrofit an existing policy into a contractual termination clause. State port and maritime boards, like many public authorities handling land and shop allotments, often sit on standing tender policies that coexist, sometimes uneasily, with negotiated allotments made for particular reasons. This order signals that a tenant who has already been given a specific tenure, especially one extended after the policy in question was already on the books, cannot be displaced simply because the authority later decides the general policy should now prevail over the specific arrangement it made.

For commercial tenants holding negotiated allotments from port authorities, municipal bodies or other statutory boards, the case is a reminder to document the circumstances of any extension or renewal carefully, since a later attempt to terminate on policy grounds will be judged against what the authority already knew and had already decided when it last confirmed the tenure. An authority that extends a lease with its eyes open to its own tendering policy will find it difficult to invoke that same policy as an emergency exit a few months later.

The interim relief keeps PRG Shipping trading at the terminal for now, with the question of whether the Board had any other valid ground to end the lease left for the arbitrator to decide. Until that arbitration concludes, the Board's e-tender for the Nagapattinam shop space stays off the table.

Why It Matters

Public authorities that allot commercial space outside open tender, then later invoke a pre-existing tender policy to terminate early, are a recurring source of disputes for traders operating out of ports, municipal markets and similar public premises. This ruling gives such tenants a concrete argument: an authority cannot rely on a policy that already existed at the time of allotment or renewal as a fresh ground for premature termination, and interim relief pending arbitration is available to preserve possession while that question is resolved.
