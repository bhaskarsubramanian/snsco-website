Delhi HC: PMLA Attachment Cannot Survive a Resolution Plan Approved Under IBC Section 32A

Source: Originally reported by TaxGuru, Law Chakra, LiveLaw Biz and thetaxcorp.in. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A fixed deposit worth Rs 3.01 crore, sitting under an Enforcement Directorate attachment since August 2021, is finally going back to its owner. The Delhi High Court's Division Bench of Justice Navin Chawla and Justice Arun Bhardwaj set aside an order of the Prevention of Money Laundering Act Appellate Tribunal on 6 November 2026, ruling in Dash Exports Private Limited v. Directorate of Enforcement that an attachment made before a resolution plan is approved cannot survive that plan once the conditions of Section 32A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code are met.

The dispute traces back to a provisional attachment order the ED passed on 25 August 2021 over property at Village Habibpur in Gautam Buddh Nagar, Uttar Pradesh, as part of a money laundering investigation connected to the corporate debtor. Dash Exports Private Limited is itself the corporate debtor, whose insolvency resolution process had commenced on 26 February 2020. Asteroid Shelter Homes Private Limited, the resolution applicant, had its plan approved by Dash Exports' Committee of Creditors with 99.22 per cent voting share on 27 November 2020, and the National Company Law Tribunal approved that plan on 29 September 2021, covering the property the ED had attached. Dash Exports approached the PMLA Appellate Tribunal seeking release of the fixed deposit once the plan was approved. The Tribunal turned the plea down.

Timing drove both the Tribunal's reasoning and the ED's position before the High Court. Section 32A(2) of the IBC bars any action against the property of a corporate debtor covered by an approved resolution plan, including attachment, once the plan takes effect and its conditions are satisfied. The ED's argument was that this protection reaches only attachments made after the plan is approved, not ones already sitting on the property when the Tribunal confirms it. On that reading, an attachment that predates plan approval would be untouched by Section 32A, leaving the ED free to hold the property regardless of what the resolution process achieved.

The Division Bench rejected that reading outright, calling it an artificial distinction the statute does not support. Section 32A's purpose, the Bench held, is to give a resolution applicant a clean company, free of the sins of its past management and free of claims, attachments and prosecutions tied to offences committed before insolvency commenced. Reading the provision to spare only post-approval attachments while leaving pre-approval ones standing would gut that purpose, since in practice almost every attachment relevant to a corporate debtor facing insolvency happens before, not after, a resolution plan is approved. The protection operates prospectively from the date of approval, releasing the property from whatever attachment sat on it once the statutory conditions are satisfied, irrespective of when that attachment was first imposed.

Having found the Tribunal's distinction untenable, the Bench set aside its order and directed release of the Rs 3.01 crore fixed deposit to Dash Exports within two weeks. The ruling hands the resolution applicant immediate, concrete relief rather than sending the matter back for fresh consideration, which is itself worth noticing: appellate courts dealing with PMLA appeals often remand for the Tribunal to apply the correct test, but here the Bench was satisfied the facts left only one possible outcome once the law was correctly stated.

For promoters, lenders and resolution applicants dealing with a stressed company under investigation, the order narrows a gap enforcement agencies have tried to use for years. A company picked up under the IBC often carries the baggage of its previous management's conduct, including transactions the ED is investigating under the PMLA. Resolution applicants have long worried that an attachment registered before their plan is approved could survive the process and tie up assets they paid good money to acquire free and clear. This ruling tells them that worry should not drive their bid price, at least within the Delhi High Court's jurisdiction, so long as the conditions Section 32A actually sets are met.

The judgment does not throw the door open to every attachment linked to a corporate debtor. Section 32A sets out specific conditions before its immunity applies, and the Enforcement Directorate keeps the ability to argue, in a future case, that those conditions were not satisfied on the facts before it. What this ruling forecloses is only the particular shortcut the Tribunal had accepted here, that an attachment's date alone could place it outside Section 32A's reach. Lenders and resolution applicants assessing deals involving a corporate debtor with an open ED investigation would still be wise to verify, deal by deal, that the plan and its approval meet every condition the provision demands before counting on the attachment disappearing on its own.

Why It Matters

Private equity funds, NBFCs and corporate groups that bid for distressed Indian companies under the IBC routinely price in the risk that a government agency's attachment, often tied to conduct by a company's previous promoters, could survive the resolution process and trap acquired assets. This ruling confirms that a Section 32A-compliant resolution plan extinguishes an Enforcement Directorate attachment even where that attachment predates the plan's approval, giving resolution applicants, including foreign investors and NBFC lenders financing such acquisitions, firmer ground to value distressed assets without discounting purely for timing.
