Bank's Failure to Act Promptly on a Reported Payment Fraud Before Funds Were Withdrawn Is Deficiency in Service: State Commission

Source: As reported in consumer-law commentary and case digests through 2026. This article has been independently researched and written by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes, summarising the general legal position rather than reproducing any single report. Readers should verify current developments against the underlying order or judgment and primary sources.

A State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission has held that a bank's delay in acting on a customer's prompt report of unauthorised online payment fraud, during which the disputed funds were withdrawn from the recipient account before any freeze could be applied, amounted to deficiency in service under the Consumer Protection Act. The complaint was filed by the customer, referred to in the order as the complainant, against the bank, identified as the opposite party, after an unauthorised transfer was made from the complainant's account through a phishing scheme.

The customer had reported the fraudulent transaction to the bank's helpline within a short window after discovering it, well within the timeframe that Reserve Bank of India guidelines treat as limiting a customer's own liability for an unauthorised electronic transaction reported promptly. The bank's internal records, produced before the Commission, showed a significant gap between the time of the customer's report and the time an actual freeze request was processed internally, during which the funds were withdrawn from the beneficiary account.

The Commission held that the applicable regulatory framework places an obligation on banks to act with reasonable promptness once an unauthorised transaction is reported, precisely because delay materially reduces the chances of recovering the funds, and that an unexplained internal processing gap of the kind shown here amounted to deficiency in service distinct from the customer's own zero or limited liability for the fraud itself. It directed the bank to credit the disputed amount back to the customer's account, along with compensation for the delay.

The ruling illustrates that a consumer complaint arising from payment fraud can proceed on two distinct footings — the customer's limited liability for a promptly reported unauthorised transaction under the regulatory framework, and the bank's own deficiency in service for any unreasonable delay in acting on that report — and that establishing the latter can secure relief even where liability questions around the fraud itself are complex.

Why It Matters

A customer who discovers unauthorised payment fraud should report it to the bank's helpline immediately and preserve the exact time and reference number of that report — the gap between the report and the bank's actual freeze action is often the single most important fact in a subsequent deficiency-in-service claim.
