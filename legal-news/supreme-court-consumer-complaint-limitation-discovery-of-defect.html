Limitation for a Consumer Complaint Over a Latent Defect Runs From Discovery, Not the Date of Purchase: Supreme Court

Source: As reported in consumer-law commentary and case digests through mid-2026. This article has been independently researched and written by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes, summarising the general legal position rather than reproducing any single report. Readers should verify current developments against the underlying judgment and primary sources.

Section 69 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 prescribes a two-year limitation period for filing a consumer complaint, running "from the date on which the cause of action has arisen." A recurring question before consumer fora is what that date actually is where the defect complained of is latent: that is, not apparent or reasonably discoverable at the time of purchase or delivery.

The Supreme Court has reaffirmed the settled "discovery rule" applicable in such cases: where a defect in goods or a deficiency in service is inherently latent and could not have been discovered through ordinary diligence at the time of purchase or provision, the cause of action. And therefore the limitation clock. Arises from the date the consumer discovers, or with reasonable diligence ought to have discovered, the defect, not from the date of the original transaction.

The Court cautioned that this is not a general licence to delay filing complaints. The discovery rule applies specifically to defects that are genuinely latent in nature, such as structural defects in a building that manifest only after some years, or a manufacturing defect in a durable good that surfaces only under particular conditions of use. Where a defect was reasonably apparent or discoverable at the time of purchase, the ordinary limitation period continues to run from that earlier date, and a consumer cannot artificially extend the limitation period by delaying an inspection that could reasonably have been undertaken earlier.

The ruling is of particular relevance to disputes over construction quality, home appliances, and other goods and services where a defect may reasonably take time to manifest, and gives consumer fora a clearer analytical framework for assessing limitation objections raised by opposite parties at the threshold.

Why It Matters

Consumers who discover a defect well after purchase should document precisely when the defect was, or reasonably could have been, discovered: that date, not the original purchase date, is likely to be treated as the starting point for the two-year limitation period where the defect was genuinely latent.
