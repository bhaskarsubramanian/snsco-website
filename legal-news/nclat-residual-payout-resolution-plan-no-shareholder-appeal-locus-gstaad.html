NCLAT: Residual Payout Under Resolution Plan Doesn't Give Shareholders A Right To Appeal

Source: Originally reported by IBC Laws, LiveLaw Biz and LiveLaw Biz (2). This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

Gstaad Hotels Private Limited went through the entire corporate insolvency resolution process, ending with the National Company Law Tribunal's Mumbai bench approving a resolution plan from Shree Naman Developers Private Limited on 3 September 2026, valued at roughly Rs 1,611 crore. That plan, once approved, extinguished the company's entire existing equity share capital, as resolution plans commonly do when a new promoter steps in to revive an insolvent company. Two of the company's minority shareholders, NKAY Investment and Sunil Madhavdas Ahuja, were unwilling to let the matter rest there and appealed to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal against the plan's approval.

Before the appellate tribunal could even look at whether the plan itself was fair or complied with the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, it had to decide a more basic question: did these shareholders have the right to appeal at all? Section 61 of the Code allows an appeal to be filed by a person aggrieved by an order of the adjudicating authority, and the appellants' case rested substantially on the fact that the approved plan provided them a residual payout under the distribution waterfall in Section 53, after secured and other creditors had been paid. They argued that being entitled to this residual amount made them an aggrieved party with standing to challenge the plan.

A bench of Justice Mohd Faiz Alam Khan and Technical Member Naresh Salecha rejected that argument. Receiving a payout under a resolution plan, the tribunal held, does not by itself convert a shareholder into a person aggrieved by that very plan for the purposes of Section 61. The two things, in the tribunal's reasoning, sit on different footing: one is the consequence of a plan already approved and binding, and the other is a separate legal right to challenge the approval itself, which cannot be manufactured merely because the plan happens to leave something on the table for existing shareholders.

The bench went on to restate why shareholders occupy a limited position in the resolution process as Parliament designed it. Insolvency resolution under the Code runs on a creditor-in-control model, where the committee of creditors decides whether and on what terms a company is to be revived, and shareholder approval is not a precondition for a plan's validity. Extinguishing the existing equity, as this plan did, does not change that structure or create a fresh cause of action for the shareholders whose stake is wiped out, in the absence of fraud or a malicious initiation of the insolvency process, neither of which NKAY Investment or Ahuja had alleged here. Without a sustainable claim to standing, the tribunal said it had no occasion to examine the resolution plan's merits at their instance, and dismissed both appeals on that ground alone.

This is not an isolated question. Shareholders and former promoters of companies that go through insolvency often try to use an appeal under Section 61 as a way to hold up implementation of a plan that has already wiped out their stake, and tribunals have had to repeatedly mark out how far that route can be used. For a resolution applicant such as Shree Naman Developers, or for any acquirer, domestic or foreign, buying a company out of insolvency, the practical value of a plan approved by the committee of creditors depends on that approval being reasonably final once the statutory appeal window and grounds are exhausted by parties who actually have standing to invoke them.

Gstaad Hotels' insolvency has already generated more than one round of litigation, including a separate challenge by a former director reported to have reached the Supreme Court on different grounds. This particular appeal, brought by shareholders hoping a residual payment would unlock a right to contest the plan itself, did not get past the threshold question of who may bring it.

The order also leaves an unresolved practical question for drafters of resolution plans. A plan that sets aside a small residual sum for existing shareholders, after every creditor class has been paid, is treated by lenders and resolution professionals as a sign of fiscal prudence and transparency, since it shows the committee of creditors has accounted for every rupee generated by the debtor's assets. This ruling confirms that making such provision does not expose the plan to a wider risk of being reopened at a shareholder's instance, which should, if anything, make resolution applicants more comfortable building in a modest residual payout rather than structuring plans to leave shareholders with nothing at all merely to avoid giving them an argument on standing.

Why It Matters

Promoters and shareholders of an insolvent company often try to use an appeal under Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code to stall a resolution plan after their equity has been wiped out. NCLAT's ruling confirms that simply being entitled to a residual payment under the plan's distribution waterfall does not, on its own, give a shareholder standing to challenge the plan's approval. That matters to resolution applicants and acquirers, Indian and foreign, who price a takeover on the assumption that an approved plan will not be reopened by parties without a genuine grievance recognised under the Code.
