CCPA Fines Hogar Technologies Rs 1 Lakh for Hiding Walkie-Talkie Licence Rules

Source: Originally reported by aninews.in, Business Standard, TaxGuru and newsable.asianetnews.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A walkie-talkie listing that does not mention the frequency the device operates on, or the licence a buyer needs before switching it on, looks like a minor omission. The Central Consumer Protection Authority has now put a price on that omission. On 25 October 2026, a bench of Chief Commissioner Nidhi Khare and Commissioner Anupam Mishra fined Hogar Technologies and Innovations LLP, a Kerala-based electronics seller, Rs 1 lakh for advertising and listing walkie-talkies on its own website without disclosing the operating frequencies or the licensing conditions attached to lawful use of the devices.

Walkie-talkies in India are not sold like any other gadget. Depending on the frequency band and power output, a buyer may need authorisation from the Wireless Planning and Coordination Wing before the device can be legally operated, and selling one without making that clear leaves the buyer holding equipment they cannot lawfully switch on. The CCPA's case against Hogar turned on exactly that gap: its website listings described the devices' features and price but said nothing about the regulatory conditions a purchaser would have to meet.

Hogar's defence rested on its own compliance rather than the consumer's. The company told the authority that it held a valid Dealer Possessing Licence and that its walkie-talkie models carried Equipment Type Approval certification, and argued that its website functioned mainly as an information and lead-generation page rather than a retail storefront, since it took no payments online. On that basis, it argued the disclosure obligations that apply to a seller advertising goods for sale should not attach to it in the same way.

The CCPA did not accept the distinction. Holding the necessary approvals as a dealer, the authority reasoned, is a different question from telling a prospective buyer what they are getting into. A consumer reading a walkie-talkie listing has no way of knowing, from the listing alone, that the device needs a licence to operate lawfully, and a seller's own paperwork does nothing to fix that information gap for the person on the other end of the transaction. The authority was careful to frame its order narrowly: it said in terms that these proceedings were not meant to decide whether Hogar had actually complied with telecom licensing requirements, only whether its advertising had misled consumers about what compliance those requirements demand.

That framing matters for how the order should be read going forward. The CCPA is not setting itself up as a second telecom regulator auditing sellers' paperwork. It is applying a more familiar consumer-protection principle, that an advertisement which omits information a buyer needs to use a product lawfully is itself misleading, regardless of whether the seller's own compliance file is in order. A seller can hold every licence the Wireless Planning and Coordination Wing requires and still fall foul of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 if its listings do not pass that information on to the person deciding whether to buy.

Hogar is not an isolated target. The order forms part of a wider enforcement push against walkie-talkie sellers and marketplaces that began with larger penalties against e-commerce majors, including Rs 10 lakh each imposed on Amazon, Flipkart, Meesho, Meta and TradeIndia over similar listings earlier in the campaign. Reports place the cumulative penalties issued against the twelve entities covered by this drive at around Rs 58 lakh, with Hogar's Rs 1 lakh order the latest addition to a growing list rather than a standalone action.

For any business selling regulated equipment online, walkie-talkies included, the practical lesson is that a product listing has to do more than describe the item and quote a price. Where a device cannot be used lawfully without an additional authorisation, the listing itself has to say so, in terms a buyer can act on before paying rather than discovering afterward. Holding the right paperwork in a filing cabinet is necessary but not sufficient; the same information has to reach the consumer at the point of sale, and a seller that treats its own compliance as a substitute for that disclosure is taking on exactly the risk that caught up with Hogar.

Why It Matters

Sellers of regulated equipment often assume that holding the right approvals themselves is enough, and that disclosure to the buyer is a separate, lesser concern. This order treats the two as distinct obligations: a seller's own licensing compliance does not excuse a listing that fails to tell the consumer what regulatory conditions attach to using the product. Any business advertising equipment that needs a licence to operate, not just walkie-talkies, should check that its listings carry that information plainly, not just that its own paperwork is in order.
