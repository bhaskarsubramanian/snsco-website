Power, Energy & Mining · 17 November 2026

APTEL Restores Full Tariff For Wind Power Beyond Normative CUF, Sets Aside APERC Cuts

Source: Originally reported by solarquarter.com, outlookbusiness.com, businessupturn.com and 5paisa.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

On 17 November 2026, the Appellate Tribunal for Electricity decided a batch of 23 connected appeals filed against orders of the Andhra Pradesh Electricity Regulatory Commission, and ruled against the discoms on every one of them. The appeals had been filed by wind power generators, among them Beta Wind Farm Private Limited, a unit of Orient Green Power, and four wind subsidiaries of JSW Energy: JSW Vayu (Tungabhadra), JSW Vayu (Indravati), JSW Wind Power (Pennar) and JSW Wind Power Limited. Each had challenged an APERC order that cut the rate payable to them for electricity generated beyond a normative capacity utilisation factor the commission had fixed for their wind projects.

What the generators disputed was how APERC treated generation beyond that benchmark. Wind output varies through the year and across years depending on wind speed, and a normative CUF is ordinarily used as a planning assumption, to help a regulator work out an average tariff, not as a cap on how much energy a generator may actually deliver and get paid for under its power purchase agreement. APERC's orders, however, computed the CUF on a cumulative basis and then applied a sharply reduced rate, reported at around fifty paise a unit in the Beta Wind Farm matter, to whatever a generator supplied once its cumulative CUF crossed the fixed threshold, even though the contracted tariff for that generation ran several times higher.

The generators argued this amounted to rewriting their power purchase agreements through a tariff order. Nothing in the underlying agreements, they said, allowed a discom to pay a punitive rate for power actually generated and supplied, merely because a planning estimate of average output had been crossed in a particular period. A normative CUF, on their case, sets the basis for the tariff calculation itself; it does not create a ceiling beyond which a discom can simply pay less for electricity it has taken onto the grid and used.

APTEL agreed. It set aside APERC's cumulative CUF computation and the reduced per-unit rate attached to generation beyond it, and directed that the generators be paid the applicable contracted tariff for the entire quantity of power supplied, not a discounted rate for the portion above the benchmark. The tribunal also directed that amounts the discoms had withheld on the earlier basis be released, with interest.

The practical numbers are significant for the companies involved. Orient Green Power told stock exchanges that Beta Wind Farm expects to recover close to Rs 12.98 crore from the Andhra Pradesh distribution utility as a result of the order. JSW Energy's subsidiaries, which had separately challenged an APERC order on the same issue, are positioned to recover amounts withheld under that order as well, since the tribunal's reasoning applied across the batch. Both companies made their disclosures under the listing obligations that require a material litigation outcome to be reported to exchanges within a short window.

Such disputes are not unique to these two groups. State commissions in more than one jurisdiction have tried, at various points, to use a normative CUF figure set for tariff determination as a tool to limit actual payouts once a wind project outperforms that figure, and tribunals have repeatedly had to clarify that the two functions are not the same. For anyone buying into or financing a renewable energy platform in India, including institutional and overseas investors assessing receivables risk on operating wind assets, this line of orders matters because it bears on how reliably a generator can expect to be paid for what its turbines actually produce, as against what a regulator once assumed they would.

APERC retains the option of challenging the order before the Supreme Court, as discoms have done in comparable tariff disputes from other states, so this may not be the last word on this batch of appeals. For now, though, it restores to these wind generators the tariff their power purchase agreements promised for every unit they fed into the grid, rather than the reduced rate the state commission had tried to apply retrospectively.

Why It Matters

Wind and solar developers negotiate power purchase agreements on the basis of a contracted tariff for the electricity they deliver, not a reduced rate once a planning assumption about average output is exceeded. APTEL's order confirms that a normative capacity utilisation factor fixed for tariff calculation cannot be converted into a cap on what a generator is paid for electricity actually supplied and taken onto the grid. That matters for lenders, acquirers and investors pricing receivables risk on operating renewable energy assets in Indian states well beyond Andhra Pradesh alone.
