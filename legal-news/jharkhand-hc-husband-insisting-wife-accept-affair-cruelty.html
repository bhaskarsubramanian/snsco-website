Jharkhand HC: Forcing a Wife to Accept a Husband's Affair as a 'Younger Sister' Is Cruelty of the Gravest Kind

Source: Originally reported by LiveLaw, Bar and Bench and newsx.com. This article has been independently researched and rewritten in full by S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors for informational purposes: it is not a reproduction of the original reports. Readers are encouraged to consult the original sources and the underlying judgement directly.

A wife who had been turned away by the Family Court at Hazaribagh has won her divorce on appeal, with the Jharkhand High Court holding that a husband who insists his wife accept his extramarital relationship, and live alongside his partner under the guise of treating her as a 'younger sister,' inflicts cruelty of the gravest kind on his wife. A Division Bench of Justices Sujit Narayan Prasad and Pradeep Kumar Srivastava delivered the ruling on October 26, 2026, allowing the wife's appeal against a Family Court judgment dated November 17, 2023 that had dismissed her divorce petition filed on the ground of cruelty under Section 13(1)(i-a) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955.

The wife's case before the Family Court and on appeal was that her husband had maintained a relationship with a widow even after signing a bond promising to end it, and that he had physically assaulted her when she objected. Central to her complaint was that he did not merely conduct the affair behind her back; he pressed her to accept the other woman into their domestic life, asking the wife to treat his partner as a 'younger sister' so the three of them could coexist under one roof. The husband denied the allegations and offered a counter-narrative, claiming that medical prescriptions the wife produced, from a clinic owned by her own father, had been fabricated to build a false case against him.

Below, the Family Court had sided with the husband, dismissing the wife's petition and leaving the marriage intact. The High Court found that approach unsustainable on the record before it. It held that the Family Court had appreciated the evidence in a piecemeal manner, picking at individual pieces of testimony in isolation rather than weighing the matter as a whole, and had overlooked the settled principle that matrimonial cruelty is to be tested on a preponderance of probabilities rather than proof beyond reasonable doubt. Read as a whole and against that standard, the Bench found the wife's version of events clearly established.

On the substantive question of what counts as cruelty, the High Court's reasoning went beyond the bare fact of an affair. Infidelity by itself, the Bench's approach suggests, is a well-recognised ground of matrimonial fault, but what elevated this case to cruelty of the gravest kind was the husband's demand that his wife actively accommodate the relationship within the household, recasting his partner as a family member the wife was expected to live alongside. Compelling a spouse to participate in, or at least tacitly endorse, the very relationship that wrongs her shatters matrimonial trust in a way ordinary infidelity, standing alone, may not.

Allowing the appeal, the Division Bench set aside the Family Court's dismissal and granted the wife a decree of divorce on the ground of cruelty. In doing so, it rejected the husband's attempt to discredit the wife's medical evidence by pointing to her father's ownership of the clinic that issued the prescriptions, treating that connection as insufficient, on its own, to brand the documents fabricated without something more concrete to show tampering or collusion.

The ruling adds to a developing body of Jharkhand High Court matrimonial jurisprudence that looks past the label of 'infidelity' to ask what, specifically, a husband or wife did with that infidelity inside the marriage. A spouse who conducts an affair quietly and a spouse who insists the other partner accept and live with the arrangement are not, on this reasoning, equally culpable; the latter conduct compounds the original breach of fidelity with an ongoing demand that corrodes the aggrieved spouse's dignity and autonomy within her own home.

For family courts weighing similar petitions, the judgment is a reminder that corroborating evidence, such as medical records, cannot be dismissed merely because of who happens to be connected to the source, absent some concrete indication of fabrication. It is also a caution against assessing testimony issue by issue instead of as a connected narrative, since a piecemeal reading can miss a pattern of cruelty that only emerges when the evidence is read together.

Why It Matters

Family courts frequently treat a spouse's infidelity and any associated demand on the aggrieved partner as a single, undifferentiated allegation of cruelty, without examining what specifically was demanded of the wronged spouse within the marriage. This ruling draws a sharper line, holding that compelling a spouse to accept and cohabit with the unfaithful partner's lover is cruelty of the gravest kind in its own right, independent of the underlying infidelity. It also reinforces that matrimonial cruelty is assessed on a preponderance of probabilities and on the evidence as a whole, not by picking apart individual pieces of testimony or discounting evidence solely because of a family connection to its source.
