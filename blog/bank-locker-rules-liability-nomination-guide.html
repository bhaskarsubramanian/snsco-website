Bank Locker Rules in India: What the Bank Owes You, Nomination and Rent Default

What a bank is and is not liable for if your locker's contents are lost, how nomination works on death, and what happens if locker rent goes unpaid.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A bank's liability for the contents of a safe deposit locker is real but limited. Under the Reserve Bank of India's revised instructions, a bank that loses an article in a locker through its own negligence, a fire, theft, robbery, building collapse or fraud by its staff has to pay compensation up to one hundred times the locker's annual rent. Outside those specific causes, and in particular for loss caused by a natural calamity the bank could not have prevented, the bank's liability is far narrower, and a customer who simply says valuables are missing without more does not automatically get compensated for whatever they claim was inside.

This guide sets out when a bank is liable and when it is not, what happens to a locker's contents when the holder dies, how a bank may lawfully break open a locker for unpaid rent, and where to take a dispute if the bank refuses to pay.

The Current Rules, and Why They Changed

The Reserve Bank revised its locker instructions in August 2021, directing scheduled commercial banks, cooperative banks, small finance banks, payments banks and local area banks to put a standard locker agreement in place and to disclose their liability clearly to customers. Banks were required to phase in the new agreements with existing locker holders through 2023, with fifty per cent of agreements renewed by mid-year and all of them by the end of that year.

The revision followed the Supreme Court's February 2021 judgment in Amitabha Dasgupta v United Bank of India, where the Court directed compensation of Rs 5 lakh to a customer whose locker had been broken open without just cause, and separately directed the Reserve Bank to frame detailed rules covering every aspect of how banks manage locker and safe deposit facilities, since no such regulatory framework existed at the time. The 2021 instructions are the regulator's response to that direction, and the standard locker agreement every bank now uses traces back to it.

When the Bank Is Liable, and the Hundred-Times Cap

A bank is liable for loss of locker contents where the loss results from its own fire, theft, burglary or robbery, from the collapse of the building, or from fraud by its own employees. In these situations, the bank's compensation is capped at an amount equal to one hundred times the annual rent charged for that locker, regardless of what the customer says was actually inside, because banks do not keep a record of locker contents and are not expected to compensate for items they never had a chance to verify.

This is a real improvement over the position banks took for decades, where many agreements disclaimed all liability for locker contents regardless of the cause of loss. The one hundred times figure is still modest against the value some customers keep in a locker, which is exactly why the rules place so much weight on due care by the bank, since a customer whose loss exceeds the cap has to show something beyond the formula, typically a clear case of the bank's own negligence, to recover more through a court or consumer forum rather than relying on the formula alone.

What the Bank Is Not Liable For

Loss caused by a natural calamity such as an earthquake, flood or lightning strike, where the bank itself was not negligent in protecting the locker room against a foreseeable risk, falls outside the bank's liability. A customer's own negligence, such as sharing the locker key or access credentials with someone else, similarly does not create liability on the bank's part if that leads to a loss.

The locker agreement also records that the bank does not know and does not keep any list of what a customer keeps inside, so it cannot be made to compensate for a specific item of jewellery or a stated sum of cash simply on the customer's word that it was there. This is also why banks prohibit keeping cash, firearms, hazardous substances or similarly restricted items in a locker, since the facility is meant for documents, jewellery and similar valuables rather than as a general storage or cash-holding arrangement.

Nomination and What Happens When the Locker Holder Dies

A locker holder can nominate one or more persons to receive access to the locker's contents on death, and where a valid nomination exists, the bank has to release the contents to the nominee within a defined period, generally within about fifteen days of being given the necessary documents such as the death certificate, without insisting that the nominee first obtain a succession certificate or letters of administration.

Where there is no nomination, the bank will ordinarily insist on legal heirship documents, such as a succession certificate, a probated will, or a letter of administration, before releasing the contents, and this can take considerably longer where the family does not already hold one of those documents. For this reason, a locker holder who has not nominated anyone, or whose nomination is outdated because of a death, divorce or estrangement within the family, should treat updating the nomination as a priority rather than something to attend to eventually.

Can the Bank Break Open Your Locker for Unpaid Rent?

Yes, but only after following a defined process, and not on the strength of one missed payment. Banks generally treat rent that has remained unpaid for an extended period, commonly around three years, as grounds to proceed toward breaking open the locker, and the locker agreement a customer signs sets out the exact trigger and notice periods that bank applies, which can differ from one bank to another.

Before breaking open a locker, a bank is expected to send the locker holder a notice of the proposed action and a further notice before actually carrying it out, so that the holder has a real opportunity to clear the dues or remove the contents. When the locker is eventually opened, the bank is expected to do so in the presence of its own officials and independent witnesses, to record the exercise, and to prepare an inventory of what was found, with the holder's acknowledgement obtained if they are present or informed afterwards. A bank that skips these steps, or that cannot show independent witnesses were present, weakens its own position if a dispute later arises over what the locker contained.

What to Check Before You Sign a Locker Agreement

Most customers sign a locker agreement at the branch counter without reading it closely, and that is a mistake worth avoiding, since the agreement is the document that will actually govern a dispute rather than the general RBI rules alone. Check the clause on liability and compensation to see whether it tracks the hundred-times-rent formula and the specific causes of loss the bank accepts responsibility for, since the wording a particular bank uses can still be narrower than what the regulator intends if it is not updated.

Check also what the agreement says about the consequences of unpaid rent, since the trigger period and the notice requirements before a break-open are set out there rather than in a single uniform RBI clause, and two different banks can legitimately apply different timelines. Finally, confirm the nomination section is filled in correctly and matches current family circumstances, and ask the branch for a copy of the executed agreement for your own records rather than assuming you can get one later without difficulty.

Where to Take a Dispute if the Bank Refuses to Pay

A customer dissatisfied with how a bank has handled a locker, whether over a disputed loss, a refusal to release contents to a nominee, or an unfair break-open, can first raise a written complaint with the bank's own grievance redressal officer. If that does not resolve the matter within the period the bank's policy allows, the next step is usually a complaint to the Reserve Bank's Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, which covers locker-related service deficiencies among other banking complaints, or a consumer complaint before the appropriate consumer commission where the claim is framed as a deficiency in service.

Choosing between the ombudsman route and a consumer complaint depends on what the customer is seeking. The ombudsman can direct compensation within defined limits and does not involve filing fees, while a consumer commission can go further in assessing compensation but takes longer and requires the formal process of filing and proving a consumer complaint.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much compensation can I get if the bank loses my locker's contents to theft?

Where the loss is due to the bank's own negligence, fire, theft, robbery, building collapse or staff fraud, compensation is generally capped at one hundred times the locker's annual rent. If your actual loss is higher, you would need to show the bank's negligence separately through a court or consumer forum to recover more than the formula gives.

Is the bank liable if my locker is damaged in a flood or earthquake?

Generally no, unless the bank was itself negligent in protecting the locker room against a risk it should have guarded against. Loss from a genuine natural calamity, where the bank took reasonable precautions, usually falls outside the bank's liability under the current rules.

Can my nominee access my locker immediately after my death, or does the family need a succession certificate first?

Where a valid nomination exists, the bank is expected to release the contents to the nominee within about fifteen days of receiving the required documents, without insisting on a succession certificate first. Without a nomination, the bank will usually ask for proof of legal heirship before releasing anything.

Can a bank just break open my locker if I miss a rent payment?

No. Banks generally require an extended period of unpaid rent, commonly around three years under typical locker agreements, and have to give prior notice before proceeding, and the locker is expected to be opened in front of independent witnesses with an inventory recorded. The exact notice periods depend on your own bank's locker agreement.

What can I do if my bank refuses to compensate me for a locker loss?

Start with a written complaint to the bank's grievance redressal officer. If that does not resolve it, you can complain to the Reserve Bank's Integrated Ombudsman Scheme or file a consumer complaint before the consumer commission, depending on how much you are seeking and how quickly you need a decision.
