Selling Food as Organic Without Certification: What FSSAI's Rules Actually Require

FSSAI's organic food rules require real NPOP or PGS-India certification before the word organic or the Jaivik Bharat logo can go on any label.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A packaged food brand wants to add the word organic to its label because consumers pay a real premium for it, and the product is, by the company's own account, grown without synthetic pesticides. FSSAI does not accept that account on its own. It requires a specific certification behind the claim, and 2026 has been the year the regulator actually started enforcing that line against household names, not just small, informal sellers at a weekend farmers' market.

Food Safety and Standards (Organic Foods) Regulations, 2017 set out exactly what a business needs before the word organic, or the dedicated Jaivik Bharat logo, can appear anywhere on a product, and recent enforcement action shows the regulator is reading that requirement literally rather than treating it as a formality.

The Basic Rule: No Certification, No Organic Claim

The Food Safety and Standards (Organic Foods) Regulations, 2017, notified on 29 December 2017, require that any food marketed as organic in India carry third-party certification under one of two recognised systems, and that uncertified product cannot use the word organic, any common variant of it, or the dedicated Jaivik Bharat logo on its packaging, website or any other marketing material directed at an Indian consumer.

The regulation binds every food business operator dealing in organic food, whether a large packaged-goods company or a small farm selling at a local market, with certification as the default position and only a narrow, specifically defined exemption carved out for genuinely small direct sellers. A business that assumes its own honest belief in its product's organic qualities is enough to justify the claim is working from the wrong premise entirely, since the regulation asks for a certificate on file, not a sincere intention or a good growing practice that nobody has actually verified.

Two Certification Routes: NPOP and PGS-India

A food business can certify under the National Programme for Organic Production, administered through APEDA and accredited certification bodies, built around third-party inspection and primarily used for larger-scale and export-oriented operations, or under the Participatory Guarantee System for India, a group-based, peer-review certification model developed specifically to be accessible to small and marginal farmers who cannot absorb the cost and documentation burden of full third-party certification year after year.

Both routes are recognised as equally valid under the 2017 regulations for domestic sale, and a business should pick whichever fits its scale and sales channel, since using neither and still marketing a product as organic is the violation FSSAI has been enforcing against, not a mere choice-of-certification question left to the company's discretion. Switching between the two routes mid-year, or claiming a certification process is merely pending, does not give a seller cover to use organic branding before the certificate actually exists.

The Jaivik Bharat Logo Is Not Decorative Branding

The regulations introduced the Jaivik Bharat logo specifically so a consumer could visually distinguish a genuinely certified organic product from one merely using organic-sounding language or imagery on the pack. Carrying the logo without the underlying NPOP or PGS-India certification behind it is treated as a distinct and particularly visible form of the same underlying violation, since the logo is meant to be a reliable shorthand for certified status, not marketing language a brand can apply to signal a general wellness positioning to a shopper scanning a shelf quickly.

FSSAI's enforcement action against Dabur India in August 2026 specifically flagged organic products displaying the Jaivik Bharat logo without a valid FSSAI organic endorsement behind it, treating the logo misuse as a contravention distinct from, and in addition to, the company's separate labelling claims that triggered the same prohibition order. The two findings reinforce each other for enforcement purposes but are legally separate violations, each independently sufficient to trigger action regardless of whether the other claim is ever proved.

The Small-Producer Exemption, and Its Real Limits

FSSAI has clarified that a genuinely small original producer or producer organisation selling organic produce directly to the end consumer, rather than through a retailer or distributor, is exempt from NPOP or PGS-India certification up to an annual organic turnover of Rs 12 lakh, with a separate, higher threshold of Rs 50 lakh applying to aggregators or intermediaries who collect produce from such small producers and sell it on directly.

This exemption does not extend to using the Jaivik Bharat logo. A producer selling under this relaxation can describe produce as organic in a direct sale to the end consumer, but cannot put the certified-organic logo on packaging, since the logo is reserved for certified product regardless of sale volume. A business operating above these thresholds, or selling through any intermediary rather than genuinely direct to the consumer, cannot rely on this relaxation at all and needs full certification under one of the two recognised routes.

What Recent Enforcement Actually Looked Like

FSSAI's enforcement through 2026 has concentrated on the gap between marketing language and actual certification, rather than waiting for an outright false certificate to surface before acting. In June 2026, the regulator issued notices to several brands, including smaller organic-focused companies, over product names and branding that could lead a consumer to assume organic status the products did not actually hold the required certification or logo for, treating a suggestive brand name as functionally equivalent to a direct claim.

In August 2026, the regulator went further against Dabur India with a prohibition order covering products carrying misleading claims, including a specific organic claim, and flagged a separate instance of the Jaivik Bharat logo appearing on products without the underlying organic endorsement. Both episodes show that FSSAI is treating organic-adjacent branding and naming, not just outright false certification claims, as within its enforcement net, so a business does not need to print the word certified next to organic falsely to attract scrutiny over this issue.

Penalties Are Misbranding Penalties, Not a Separate Offence

There is no standalone organic fraud offence under the FSS Act. Selling an uncertified product under organic branding is enforced as misbranding under Section 52 of the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, which carries a monetary penalty that can extend up to Rs 3 lakh, along with a power for the Adjudicating Officer to direct corrective action, including removing the offending claim from packaging or, in a stronger case, destroying the mislabelled stock altogether at the seller's own cost.

The Supreme Court has confirmed that the FSS Act does not prescribe imprisonment for misbranding, so the consequence for this particular violation stays civil and monetary rather than criminal, and has also held that a person penalised under Section 52 cannot also be prosecuted under the now-repealed Prevention of Food Adulteration Act for the same conduct, closing off any risk of facing two separate regimes for one single act of misbranding on the same product.

What to Check Before Using the Word Organic

A food business should first confirm which certification route, NPOP or PGS-India, actually fits its production scale and sales channel, and put the certification in place before any packaging is printed or any website goes live with the claim. Second, if relying on the small-producer exemption, confirm the turnover genuinely stays under the relevant threshold and that the sale is actually direct to the end consumer, since selling through even one layer of distribution removes the exemption entirely regardless of how small the business otherwise is.

Third, keep the Jaivik Bharat logo off any packaging unless the certification behind it is current and covers that specific product, since logo misuse has been a specific, separately flagged basis for recent enforcement action against well-known brands. Fourth, review any broader wellness or purity claims against what the certification actually supports, since FSSAI's recent action reads such claims together with the organic-specific labelling requirement rather than treating them as harmless marketing sitting beside the real compliance question on the same pack.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I call my product organic if I grow it myself and sell directly to customers?

Only if your annual organic turnover stays within the small-producer exemption, currently referenced as Rs 12 lakh, and the sale is genuinely direct to the end consumer. Even then, you cannot use the Jaivik Bharat logo, since that is reserved for certified product.

What is the difference between NPOP and PGS-India certification?

NPOP is a third-party, inspection-based certification generally suited to larger or export-oriented operations, while PGS-India is a group-based peer-review system designed to be more accessible for small and marginal farmers who cannot absorb a full inspection regime. Both are equally valid for domestic organic sale under current rules.

What happens if I use the Jaivik Bharat logo without actual certification?

This is treated as a specific, visible form of misbranding under the 2017 Organic Foods Regulations and the FSS Act, and FSSAI has recently taken prohibition action against a major company for exactly this, alongside separate claims-related violations on the same product.

Can FSSAI send me to jail for a false organic claim?

No. Misbranding under Section 52 of the FSS Act carries only a monetary penalty, which can extend up to Rs 3 lakh, along with a power to order corrective action or destruction of the mislabelled stock, not imprisonment of any kind.

Does organic certification also cover claims like natural or 100% pure?

No. Those are separate claims from organic status specifically, and FSSAI has scrutinised and acted against both kinds of claims together where a product's actual composition does not support them, so each claim needs its own basis, not just an organic certificate.
