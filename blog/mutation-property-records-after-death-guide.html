You Mutated the Property in Your Name After Your Father Died. Does That Make You the Owner?

Mutation of revenue records after a death does not create or prove title, and a genuinely disputed inheritance still needs a civil suit to settle it.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family loses its head, the eldest son goes to the local revenue office or municipal corporation, submits a death certificate and some supporting papers, and gets the property mutated into his own name within a few weeks. The siblings, busy with grief and their own lives, do not object at the time. Years later, when the property needs to be sold or a loan needs to be raised against it, somebody asks for the title documents, and the family discovers that the mutation entry everyone treated as settling the matter never actually settled anything.

This surprises people every time, because a mutation certificate looks official, carries a government seal, and gets accepted without question by banks checking collateral or municipal offices issuing property tax bills. None of that converts it into proof of ownership, and the Supreme Court has said so in terms clear enough that there is very little room left to argue otherwise.

What Mutation Actually Is

Mutation is the process of updating the name recorded against a property in the revenue record, commonly called the Record of Rights, jamabandi, khatian or similar depending on the state, or in a municipal corporation's property tax register for urban property. It happens after a sale, a gift, a partition, or a death, and the office making the change typically wants a death certificate along with inheritance-related papers, such as a legal heir certificate, a succession certificate, a will, or a probate order, depending on what the applicant actually has and what the local office insists on.

The function of this record has always been fiscal rather than adjudicatory. Revenue and municipal authorities need to know whom to bill for land revenue or property tax and whom to treat as the person in possession for administrative purposes, and the mutation register exists to serve that narrow purpose. It was never designed, and courts have consistently held it was never intended, to operate as a title register the way a formal land registration system in some other countries does.

Balwant Singh v. Daulat Singh: The Governing Rule

The leading authority is Balwant Singh v. Daulat Singh (D) by LRs, (1997) 7 SCC 137, where the Supreme Court considered a case in which the lower courts had treated a 1954 mutation as if it had itself transferred title and possession to the person in whose favour it was recorded. The Supreme Court rejected that approach, holding that mutation of property in revenue records neither creates nor extinguishes title to the property, nor does it carry any presumptive value on the question of title, since such entries exist only for the purpose of collecting land revenue.

That holding has been treated as settled law ever since, cited and reapplied by the Supreme Court itself in later cases and by High Courts deciding disputes where one party relies on a mutation entry as if it were conclusive proof of ownership. The practical consequence is that a party cannot win a title dispute simply by pointing to a mutation entry in their favour, however old that entry is and however unopposed it went at the time it was made.

The Will Problem: Jitendra Singh and Disputed Succession

A particular version of this issue comes up constantly in succession disputes: one heir applies for mutation relying on a will, the revenue office mutates the property in that heir's name, and other family members only object later, if at all, once a sale or a loan surfaces the issue. In Jitendra Singh v. State of Madhya Pradesh, the Supreme Court addressed exactly this fact pattern and held that a mutation entry does not confer any right, title or interest in favour of the person in whose name it stands, and that where title is claimed on the basis of a will specifically, the party relying on that will has to approach the appropriate civil court and get the question of title actually decided there before mutation on that basis can be treated as settling anything.

This matters because revenue and municipal officers processing mutation applications are not equipped, and are not expected, to adjudicate a contested will, verify testamentary capacity, or rule on whether a will was validly executed. They process the paperwork placed before them, which is exactly why a mutation resting on a disputed will carries even less legal weight than an ordinary inheritance-based mutation where there is no real dispute about who the heirs are.

What Other Heirs Can Still Do, and the Limitation Trap

A sibling or co-heir who did not object when one family member got the property mutated into their own name has not thereby lost their share. The remedy is a civil suit, typically for a declaration of title, for partition, or for both, asking the civil court to determine actual ownership and shares independently of what the revenue records currently show, and courts applying Balwant Singh and its line of authority have been consistently willing to look past an existing mutation entry to decide that underlying question properly.

The real trap is not the mutation itself but limitation and delay. A co-heir who waits many years after becoming aware that the property was mutated solely in another's name, and who allows that person to deal with the property as sole owner, sell portions of it, mortgage it, or otherwise act in ways inconsistent with shared ownership, risks running into limitation problems or acquiescence arguments by the time a suit is finally filed, even though the mutation entry alone never extinguished their legal right. The lesson is to raise the objection, or at least put it on record, reasonably promptly rather than assuming the underlying right survives indefinitely regardless of how long inaction continues.

Practical Steps for a Family After a Death

If the inheritance is genuinely undisputed among the heirs, getting the property mutated promptly, in the names of all the actual heirs rather than just one, is still worth doing even though it does not create title, because it keeps the revenue and tax records aligned with reality and avoids giving any single heir the ability to later claim sole ownership on the strength of a mutation entry that happens to exist only in their name.

If a will is involved and any heir disputes its validity, or even quietly doubts it without saying so openly, do not treat mutation based on that will as the end of the matter. Raise the dispute and consider seeking probate or a declaratory suit sooner rather than later, since the eventual fight, if it comes, will be decided in a civil court on evidence about the will's execution and the testator's capacity, not by whoever got to the revenue office first. A family that understands mutation for what it actually is, an administrative record for tax purposes, tends to avoid the expensive surprise of discovering decades later that an entry everyone assumed was final was never legally final at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

If my name is on the mutation register, am I the legal owner of the property?

Not necessarily. The Supreme Court has held repeatedly, starting with Balwant Singh v. Daulat Singh, that mutation neither creates nor extinguishes title and has no presumptive value on ownership. It only serves the fiscal purpose of knowing whom to bill for revenue or tax.

My brother got the property mutated in his name alone based on our father's will, which I think is fake. What can I do?

Mutation based on a disputed will does not settle title. Under Jitendra Singh v. State of Madhya Pradesh, you can approach a civil court to challenge the will and seek a declaration of your rights, and the revenue mutation entry will not stand in the way of that challenge.

I never objected when my sibling mutated the property after our mother's death. Have I lost my share?

Not automatically, since mutation alone does not extinguish your right. But waiting too long, especially while your sibling deals with the property as sole owner, can create real limitation or acquiescence problems, so it is better to raise your claim through a civil suit sooner rather than later.

Do I need to get the property mutated at all if all the heirs agree on the inheritance?

It is still worth doing, since it keeps revenue and property tax records accurate and avoids letting any one heir later claim sole ownership based on an entry that happens to exist only in their name, even though mutation itself never creates title.
