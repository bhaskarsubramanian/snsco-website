Seat vs Venue of Arbitration: Why Two Words in Your Contract Can Decide Which Court Has Jurisdiction

A guide to the legal distinction between the seat and venue of arbitration under Indian law, why courts treat the seat as the anchor for supervisory jurisdiction, and how to draft the clause to avoid ambiguity.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 11 March 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Two Different Concepts, Often Confused in Drafting

In everyday commercial drafting, 'seat' and 'venue' of arbitration are sometimes used loosely, even interchangeably. Legally, they are not the same thing. The 'seat' (also called the 'place' of arbitration in the juridical sense) is the location whose courts have supervisory jurisdiction over the arbitration: the courts that can hear applications for interim relief under Section 9, appointment of an arbitrator under Section 11, or a challenge to the award under Section 34. The 'venue', by contrast, is simply the physical location where hearings are convenient to hold: it carries no independent jurisdictional weight of its own.

The BALCO Framework

The Supreme Court's Constitution Bench decision in Bharat Aluminium Co. v. Kaiser Aluminium Technical Services Inc. (BALCO) established that once the seat of arbitration is determined, the courts of that seat have exclusive supervisory jurisdiction over the arbitration: to the exclusion of courts where the cause of action may otherwise have arisen, or where a 'venue' happens to be named for convenience. Later decisions have refined this further, holding that where a contract names a place as the 'venue' with no other contrary indicia, that place is often treated as the seat by default, since parties rarely go to the trouble of specifying a hearing location without meaning it to also anchor the arbitration.

How Courts Resolve Ambiguous or Conflicting Clauses

Problems arise where a contract uses inconsistent language: naming one city as 'venue', referencing institutional rules headquartered elsewhere, and including a separate exclusive jurisdiction clause pointing to a third city's courts. In such cases, courts read the arbitration clause and the contract as a whole, giving weight to any exclusive jurisdiction clause and to the substance of the parties' overall arrangement, rather than fixating on a single word like 'venue' used in one sentence of the agreement.

Practical Drafting Guidance

The safest approach is to avoid the ambiguity altogether: state expressly that a named city is the 'seat and venue' of the arbitration, or, if hearings are genuinely intended to be held elsewhere for convenience while a different city remains the seat, say so explicitly: for example, 'the seat of arbitration shall be Delhi; hearings may, for convenience, be held at Mumbai or such other place as the tribunal may determine.' Careless drafting that names different cities for 'seat' and 'venue' without further clarity is a common and avoidable source of jurisdictional disputes.

Frequently Asked Questions

If my contract names 'Delhi' as the venue and says nothing about the seat, is Delhi the seat?

Often, yes: where there is no other contrary indication in the contract, courts have tended to treat a named 'venue' as the seat by default, since it would otherwise serve no purpose. But this is not an automatic rule and depends on the contract as a whole.

Can the seat of arbitration be different from where the parties or the subject matter of the dispute are located?

Yes: parties are free to choose a seat unconnected to their location or the subject matter of the dispute, and the seat's courts will still have supervisory jurisdiction once validly agreed.

Does the seat determine which law governs the contract itself?

No: the seat determines the 'curial law' governing the arbitration procedure and the supervisory courts; the substantive law governing the contract can be, and often is, a different system of law entirely, if the parties have so agreed.

What should I do if my existing contracts have ambiguous seat/venue language?

Consider amending the arbitration clause in future contracts to state the seat expressly, and be alert, in any dispute arising under an existing ambiguous clause, to argue the seat question early: ideally before or at the Section 9 or Section 11 stage, since it affects which court you should even be approaching.
