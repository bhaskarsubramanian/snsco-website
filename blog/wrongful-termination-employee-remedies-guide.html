Wrongfully Terminated? The Legal Remedies Available to an Employee in India

A guide for employees on what makes a termination 'wrongful' under Indian labour law, the difference in remedies available to a 'workman' versus other employees, and the practical first steps after a disputed termination.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 12 December 2025 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

What Makes a Termination 'Wrongful'

A termination can be challenged as wrongful on several distinct grounds: where it violates the notice or process requirements in the employment contract or applicable certified standing orders; where it is not preceded by a fair domestic inquiry despite being for alleged misconduct; where statutory retrenchment compensation and procedure under the Industrial Disputes Act were not followed for a workman; or where the termination is otherwise discriminatory or in breach of a specific statutory protection, such as during a protected period of maternity leave.

Why 'Workman' Status Matters

Whether an employee qualifies as a 'workman' under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 significantly affects the available remedy: a workman whose termination violates the Act's retrenchment or dismissal safeguards can seek reinstatement and back wages through conciliation and a reference to the Labour Court or Industrial Tribunal, a specialised and often more employee-protective forum. Employees in predominantly managerial, supervisory or administrative roles who fall outside 'workman' status generally must instead pursue a breach of contract claim in the civil courts, where the standard remedy is monetary compensation for the unserved notice period rather than reinstatement.

The First Practical Steps After a Disputed Termination

An employee facing a termination they believe is wrongful should promptly collect and preserve documentation, the appointment letter, any termination communication, performance records, and correspondence relevant to the circumstances of the termination, and assess, ideally with legal advice, whether they qualify as a workman for purposes of the applicable industrial relations framework, since this materially shapes which forum and remedy are available.

Settlement Versus Litigation

Many termination disputes are resolved through negotiated full-and-final settlements rather than litigation, and employees should read any settlement or release document carefully before signing, since courts have generally held that accepting a full and final settlement can preclude a subsequent challenge to the legality of the termination itself: meaning the settlement stage is often the last practical opportunity to negotiate terms.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a 'workman' and other employees for termination purposes?

A 'workman' under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 is broadly determined by the actual, predominant nature of the duties performed rather than job title, and workman status opens access to the Labour Court/Industrial Tribunal framework, including potential reinstatement: employees outside this category typically pursue a civil breach-of-contract claim instead.

Can a wrongfully terminated employee get their job back?

Reinstatement is a potential remedy for a workman whose termination is found to violate the Industrial Disputes Act's safeguards, decided by a Labour Court or Tribunal on the facts: for employees outside workman status pursuing a civil claim, the more typical remedy is monetary compensation rather than reinstatement.

Is there a time limit to challenge a wrongful termination?

Yes, limitation periods apply and vary by the specific forum and cause of action involved: employees should seek advice promptly after a disputed termination rather than delaying, since an unreasonable delay can itself become a ground for resisting the claim.

Does signing a full and final settlement end the ability to challenge the termination?

Often yes: courts have generally held that accepting a full and final settlement operates as a binding compromise covering claims arising from the employment, including the legality of the termination, so employees should review such documents carefully, ideally with advice, before signing.
