Signing a Power of Attorney Abroad for Use in India: Apostille, Consularisation and Stamping

How an NRI should execute a power of attorney abroad for use in India: apostille versus consularisation, and the stamping step that follows.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An NRI managing a property sale, a bank matter or a court case in India from abroad almost always needs a power of attorney, and almost as often gets the execution wrong in some procedural detail that surfaces only when the document is actually presented to a sub-registrar, a bank or a court years later. The document itself might be perfectly drafted, with the right powers and the right beneficiary named, and still get rejected because the authentication chain behind it, apostille or consular attestation, was not done correctly, or because the stamping step that Indian law separately requires was missed entirely.

This is one of those areas where getting the paperwork sequence right matters as much as getting the legal drafting right. What follows is the actual chain a power of attorney executed outside India needs to go through before an Indian authority will reliably act on it.

Why a foreign-executed document needs authentication at all

An Indian sub-registrar, bank or court has no independent way of knowing that a document signed in London, Dubai or New York was actually signed by the person it claims to be signed by, before a notary or official who actually had the authority to attest it. Authentication exists to solve exactly this problem: it creates a verifiable chain showing the signature and the attesting official's own authority are genuine, so that an Indian authority can rely on the document without separately verifying the foreign notary.

Where the country of execution is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention of 1961, which India also joined, the chain is relatively short. A notary or other competent local official attests the document, and then the country's own designated apostille authority, often a foreign ministry or an equivalent department, affixes a standard apostille certificate. That apostille is then accepted by Indian authorities without any further legalisation by an Indian embassy or consulate.

Where the country of execution is not a Convention member, the apostille route is not available, and the document instead needs consular attestation, meaning it has to be presented to the Indian embassy or consulate in that country, which attests it after the local notarisation and any local foreign ministry attestation the sending country separately requires. This consular route generally takes longer and costs more than apostille, and it is worth confirming current requirements with the specific Indian mission before relying on older guidance, since procedures and designated competent authorities can change.

Apostille does not replace stamping and adjudication in India

A common and costly misunderstanding is treating an apostilled power of attorney as fully ready for use the moment it lands in India. It is not. The Indian Stamp Act, 1899 separately requires that a power of attorney executed outside India, where it relates to property situated in India or to any matter to be done in India, be stamped once it is received in India, since the document was executed on plain, unstamped paper abroad.

The practical timeline most commonly cited is that the document must be presented to the Collector of Stamps for stamping within roughly three months of its receipt in India. Missing this window can mean the document is treated as insufficiently stamped, which creates complications later if the document ever needs to be relied upon in a court proceeding or produced before a registering authority, since an insufficiently stamped document typically cannot be acted upon or admitted in evidence until the deficient duty and any penalty is paid.

The stamp duty itself varies by state and depends on the specific powers the document grants, with a power of attorney authorising sale of immovable property generally attracting materially higher duty than one authorising routine administrative acts such as operating a bank account or representing someone in a non-property court matter. This figure should be confirmed with the relevant state's current stamp schedule or through adjudication by the Collector of Stamps before relying on any fixed percentage, since state schedules differ and get revised.

When registration is also required, separately from stamping

Stamping and registration are two different requirements and are sometimes confused. Under Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908, a power of attorney is compulsorily registrable where it is being used to authorise someone to present a document for registration on the executant's behalf, or in certain other situations the Act specifically names, typically where the power of attorney itself effectively operates to transfer an interest in immovable property.

A straightforward power of attorney authorising an agent in India to sell property on the NRI's behalf is commonly registered in practice, even where registration is not always strictly mandated for every version of such a document, because registered powers of attorney are generally given more weight and less scrutiny by sub-registrars and banks than unregistered ones, and because registration creates a reliable, searchable public record that reduces the risk of a later dispute about whether the power was validly granted or later revoked.

Registration of a foreign-executed power of attorney in India can happen in one of two ways: the NRI can appear in person before the Indian sub-registrar, which is often impractical, or, more commonly, the document can be authenticated through the apostille or consular route described above and then presented for registration in India by the person named as attorney, following the specific procedure the relevant state's registration rules prescribe for documents executed outside India.

Drafting choices that reduce the risk of rejection later

An NRI drafting a power of attorney for use in India should be specific rather than sweeping about what powers are actually granted. A general, catch-all power of attorney authorising the agent to do anything whatsoever on the NRI's behalf is more likely to draw scrutiny from a bank or sub-registrar, and more likely to be misused, than a power of attorney that names the specific property, the specific transaction, or the specific category of acts the agent is authorised to carry out.

The document should also specify its own duration or the conditions under which it expires, since an open-ended power of attorney with no stated end date can create long-term risk if the relationship with the attorney later sours or if the NRI simply forgets the document exists. Equally important is naming a clear, documented process for revocation, including a requirement that the revocation itself be communicated in writing to anyone who has been dealing with the attorney, such as a bank or a builder, since a power of attorney that has been revoked privately but never actually communicated to third parties can continue to bind the NRI as far as those third parties are concerned.

Photographs of the NRI and the attorney, specimen signatures, and passport or identity details are commonly attached to the document today, partly because Indian banks and sub-registrars have become considerably more cautious about power-of-attorney-based property transactions following past instances of fraud, and documents without this kind of supporting identification are more likely to be questioned or delayed.

Common points of failure in practice

The most frequent failure is timing: an NRI gets the document apostilled or consularised correctly, but does not get it stamped within the window Indian law expects once it reaches India, either because the document sat unused for months or because whoever received it in India did not know stamping was a separate step from apostille.

Another frequent failure is using an apostille where consular attestation was actually required, typically because the country of execution's status under the Hague Convention was assumed rather than checked, or because the specific document type needed a different treatment than a general document would. Sources on this point are not always consistent about which countries are and are not Convention members for every purpose, so this should be confirmed directly with the Indian mission in that country or the relevant foreign ministry before relying on an assumption.

A third, quieter failure is drafting the power of attorney too broadly or too vaguely for the specific transaction, which leads a sub-registrar or bank to ask for a fresh, more specific document at the point the transaction is actually attempted, by which time the NRI may be unavailable to execute a replacement quickly. Getting the scope right at drafting stage, with professional advice on the specific transaction in mind, avoids this entirely.

Frequently Asked Questions

I am an NRI in a country that is part of the Hague Apostille Convention. Do I still need to visit the Indian embassy?

Generally no, for the authentication step itself. An apostille from the competent authority in that country is accepted by Indian authorities without further consular legalisation. You may still need to interact with an Indian mission for unrelated purposes, such as getting documents attested for other reasons, but the apostille itself replaces consular attestation for Convention countries.

Does getting my power of attorney apostilled mean it is ready to use in India immediately?

No. Apostille only authenticates the execution. Once the document reaches India, it still needs to be stamped under the Indian Stamp Act within the usual window after receipt, and depending on what it authorises, it may also need registration. Treating apostille as the final step is one of the most common and costly mistakes.

Can my power of attorney be registered in India without me being physically present?

In most states, yes, provided it has been properly authenticated through apostille or consular attestation abroad and the applicable procedure for foreign-executed documents is followed when presenting it for registration in India. The exact procedure differs by state, so check the specific state's registration rules before assuming the process is identical everywhere.

Should I give my attorney in India a general power to do anything on my behalf, to avoid drafting a new document for every transaction?

This is generally not advisable. A broad, open-ended power of attorney is more likely to be questioned by banks and sub-registrars and carries higher risk if misused. It is usually better to draft a document specific to the intended transaction or category of acts, even if that means executing a fresh document for a different transaction later.
