Land Acquired in Noida and Greater Noida: Additional Compensation and Abadi Plot Claims Explained

How farmers whose land was acquired in Noida and Greater Noida can pursue enhanced compensation and abadi plot allotment, and where it stands.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Land acquisition around Noida and Greater Noida has produced one of the longest-running compensation disputes in the country, running from the original acquisitions under the Land Acquisition Act, 1894 through decades of litigation over how much more the authorities owe the farmers whose land was taken. Two separate kinds of claims run through this history: a request for enhanced monetary compensation decided through a court reference, and a claim to an abadi plot, a developed residential plot handed back to the farmer as compensation in kind rather than only in cash. Both have moved at different speeds, and a farmer or heir looking at an old acquisition file needs to know which claim is actually open before assuming either one has lapsed or been settled.

This guide sets out how the enhanced compensation reference works, what the abadi plot policy has looked like over time including its more recent revival, and the limitation traps that have closed the door on relief for landowners who did not act within the reference period, even where other landowners from the very same acquisition later received more.

The original dispute and the 64.7% enhancement

A large share of the acquisitions for Noida and Greater Noida go back decades, and a full bench of the Allahabad High Court, in a ruling delivered in 2011, found serious defects in how several villages' land had been acquired. Rather than unwind the acquisition and hand the land back, since substantial development had already taken place, the court allowed the authorities to retain the land but directed additional compensation, an enhancement reported at up to 64.7% over what had already been paid, along with an allotment of developed abadi land to eligible landowners, subject to a notified percentage and a per-square-metre cap.

The Supreme Court broadly upheld this approach rather than disturbing the High Court's enhancement, while making clear that the figure and the circumstances of that case were specific to it and should not be read as a precedent automatically applicable to every other acquisition in the region. That distinction matters because it is the basis later rulings used to deny the same enhancement to landowners whose own acquisitions, even in neighbouring villages, were not part of the specific litigation that produced the 64.7% figure.

Why some landowners from the same villages still missed out

That enhancement reached landowners who were parties to the litigation that produced it, directly or as members of a class the court's order covered. Landowners from the same villages who had not filed their own petition or reference, and were not otherwise brought within the scope of that litigation, found courts unwilling to extend the same enhanced figure to them automatically, even though the underlying acquisition and the unfairness complained of was identical. Reports describing this gap put the number of affected landowners in the thousands, and a further appeal seeking to extend the benefit to them was dismissed.

This history illustrates a point that comes up repeatedly in land acquisition disputes: the compensation a court orders in one reference generally benefits the parties to that reference, not every landowner from the same acquisition notification. A landowner who skips filing a reference or does not otherwise join the relevant proceedings risks being left with the original award even where a neighbour with an identical holding, acquired under the identical notification, ends up with substantially more because that neighbour actually litigated the point.

The abadi plot policy: discontinued, then revived

Abadi plot allotment, giving back a developed residential plot as part of the compensation package rather than cash alone, has a complicated history across the three authorities in the region. Greater Noida has operated versions of this scheme at different points, and farmers have pressed for a standard 10% abadi plot entitlement on the extent of land acquired, a demand that has featured in protests going back to the late 1990s. The Yamuna Expressway Industrial Development Authority separately discontinued its own abadi plot provision in 2016 before reportedly moving toward reinstating a smaller entitlement, with later reports describing proposals to raise that percentage further, though the exact current figure needs to be confirmed with the specific authority before a farmer relies on it.

Compensation rates and the abadi plot percentage are not uniform across Noida, Greater Noida and YEIDA, and they have changed over time even within the same authority, including a reported revision of the per-square-metre cash rate alongside an alternative lower cash rate for those who opt into the abadi plot component instead. A landowner dealing with a live acquisition, as opposed to a decades-old one already litigated, should get the authority's current notified rate and abadi policy in writing rather than relying on what a neighbour received in an earlier round, since the terms offered have shifted more than once.

Filing a reference for enhanced compensation: the Section 18 route

A landowner dissatisfied with the compensation awarded by the Land Acquisition Collector can seek a reference to the civil court under Section 18 of the Land Acquisition Act, 1894, asking the court to determine whether the award should be enhanced. This reference has to be sought within the period the Act allows after the award or after notice of it, and the request is made to the Collector, who then refers the matter to the court if the conditions for a reference are met. Missing this window is one of the more common ways landowners end up stuck with the original award even where a strong case for enhancement exists on the merits.

Where the reference succeeds and compensation is enhanced, a landowner who falls within the same acquisition notification but did not file a Section 18 reference can turn to Section 28A, inserted into the Act in 1984 specifically to let non-referring landowners claim the benefit of another landowner's enhancement without having litigated their own reference. This route carries its own strict limitation, reported as three months from the date of the court's award on the reference that produced the enhancement, and courts have held this period is applied firmly rather than treated as a formality, so landowners who hear about a favourable judgment well after it is delivered may already be out of time to benefit from it.

What a farmer or heir should check before assuming a claim is dead

Because this litigation has run for decades, many of the people now dealing with these files are heirs rather than the original landowner, and records can be incomplete or scattered across the Collector's office, the development authority and the civil court. The first step is establishing which acquisition notification the land fell under and whether any reference, whether under Section 18 or Section 28A, was ever filed in respect of that specific holding, since the answer determines whether there is still a live legal avenue or whether the matter was closed years ago with no action taken.

Where a reference was filed and is still pending, or where a fresh acquisition is underway, a landowner should keep a close watch on notices from the Collector and the authority rather than relying on word of mouth from the village about what others received, given how much the compensation rate and the abadi plot terms have varied across different rounds of acquisition in this region. Where the acquisition long predates the current statute and the only live question is enforcement of an award already made, such as recovering compensation a court has ordered but the authority has not paid, that is generally a separate execution proceeding rather than a fresh reference, and the Supreme Court has continued to direct payment in old, specific cases even decades after the underlying acquisition.

Frequently Asked Questions

I am a farmer from a village near Noida whose land was acquired decades ago. Can I still claim the 64.7% enhancement other villagers received?

Only if your land fell within the specific litigation or acquisition notification that produced that enhancement and you were a party to it, or you can still bring a timely Section 28A claim off the back of it. Courts have refused to extend that figure automatically to landowners outside that litigation, even from the same general area, so this needs checking against your specific file.

What is an abadi plot, and is it the same as cash compensation?

An abadi plot is a developed residential plot allotted as compensation in kind, usually set as a percentage of the land acquired, rather than cash alone. Policies on the percentage and eligibility have changed over time and differ between Noida, Greater Noida and YEIDA, so the current terms should be confirmed with the specific authority.

How long do I have to seek enhanced compensation after an award?

A Section 18 reference for enhancement must be sought within the period the Land Acquisition Act allows after the award, and a Section 28A claim riding on someone else's enhancement carries a separate, strict limitation reported at three months from that court's award. Both deadlines have been applied firmly, so delay is one of the biggest risks to this kind of claim.

My neighbour from the same acquisition got more compensation through a court case. Can I automatically get the same amount?

Not automatically. You would generally need to bring a timely claim under Section 28A to benefit from someone else's enhancement if you did not file your own reference, and that claim is itself subject to a short limitation period running from the date of the enhancing judgment.
