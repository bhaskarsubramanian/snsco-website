ECB Loans From a Foreign Parent to an Indian Subsidiary: The 2026 Rules

How an Indian subsidiary can borrow from its foreign parent under India's ECB rules after the RBI's February 2026 overhaul, and what it must report.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 14 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An Indian company can borrow directly from its foreign parent or a group affiliate under the external commercial borrowings framework, and as on 13 November 2026 that framework looks different from the one treasury teams were used to. The Reserve Bank of India folded the separate ECB rules into its main foreign exchange borrowing and lending regulations through an amendment that took effect in February 2026, widening the pool of permitted lenders, loosening the fixed cost ceiling and changing how a borrower reports the loan once it is drawn.

What follows sets out who can still borrow and lend under an ECB, what the maturity and cost conditions now say, and where a foreign parent lending into its Indian subsidiary still needs an authorised dealer bank in the loop and a Loan Registration Number before the money moves. It also flags where the secondary commentary on the February 2026 change does not agree on the numbers, because a company relying on this route for the first time should check the Reserve Bank's own notification rather than any single advisory note, including this one.

What an ECB is, and why a subsidiary would use one

An external commercial borrowing is simply a commercial loan raised by an Indian resident from a non-resident lender, governed under Section 6 of the Foreign Exchange Management Act and the regulations made under it. It sits apart from foreign direct investment, which brings in equity, needs a valuation exercise and a Form FC-GPR filing, and dilutes the existing shareholders. It also sits apart from a rupee-denominated loan raised domestically, which does not touch FEMA at all. A group that wants to fund its Indian subsidiary's working capital, capital expenditure or the refinancing of costlier domestic debt, without issuing fresh shares, typically looks at an ECB precisely because the money comes in as debt and goes back out as debt, on terms the group itself can fix within the regulatory limits.

The rules apply whether the lender is an unrelated foreign bank or the subsidiary's own foreign parent, though a related-party loan draws closer scrutiny on points such as the ratio of the ECB to the lender's shareholding in the borrower. Most eligible borrowers raise an ECB under the automatic route, meaning no case-by-case Reserve Bank approval is needed before the loan is drawn, with the approval route reserved for structures or amounts that fall outside the automatic route's conditions. A company that has never raised an ECB before should not assume its situation is automatic simply because its parent is willing to lend; the borrower category, the lender category and the end-use all have to fit the current Schedule.

The February 2026 overhaul of the ECB framework

The change came through the Foreign Exchange Management (Borrowing and Lending) (First Amendment) Regulations, 2026, issued as Notification No. FEMA 3(R)(5)/2026-RB and dated 9 February 2026. Commentary from EY and from Maheshwari and Co. gives the regulations effect from their publication in the Official Gazette, reported as 16 February 2026, and a circular directing authorised dealer banks to apply the change followed within days, though the exact date of that circular is given differently across sources (16 or 18 February) and should be confirmed with the bank handling the transaction rather than taken from any one write-up.

In substance, the Reserve Bank moved the ECB provisions out of a standalone Master Direction and into Schedule I of the main FEM (Borrowing and Lending) Regulations, so the list of eligible borrowers and recognised lenders, the maturity and cost conditions, and the end-use restrictions now sit in one consolidated place rather than a separate document a practitioner had to cross-check. Whether the Reserve Bank will still publish a user-friendly consolidated Master Direction purely for reference is not settled on the commentary available, so a borrower's compliance team should work from the amended Regulations themselves, not from habit.

Who can lend, and the widened pool of eligible lenders

Before this amendment, a foreign lender other than a recognised category such as a foreign equity holder generally had to be resident in a country whose financial regulator met Financial Action Task Force or International Organization of Securities Commissions standards. Both EY's alert and the Bar and Bench commentary on the amendment report that this jurisdiction condition has been removed, which widens the pool of entities a multinational group can route a loan through without first checking where that lending entity sits for FATF or IOSCO purposes.

The category covering a foreign equity holder or group company lending to its own Indian subsidiary continues to exist, tied to conditions linked to the lender's shareholding and to a prescribed ECB-to-equity ratio for that relationship. Nothing in the commentary reviewed for this guide suggests that specific ratio has itself been changed by the February 2026 amendment; what has changed is the removal of the jurisdiction test for lenders outside that related-party category, and a broadening of which Indian entities qualify as eligible borrowers in the first place. A group treasury should still work out, loan by loan, which lender category its parent or affiliate falls into before assuming the wider rules automatically apply to it.

Maturity, cost and end-use after the amendment

The minimum average maturity period for most ECBs remains three years on the commentary available, with a shorter window reported for manufacturing sector borrowers and for refinancing that respects the original loan's maturity. This guide does not repeat the specific dollar cap some advisory notes attach to the manufacturing exception, because that figure appeared in only one synthesis of the amendment and could not be checked against a second independent source; a borrower in that sector should get its authorised dealer bank's confirmation of the current cap in writing before structuring around it.

A fixed all-in-cost ceiling, previously expressed as a benchmark rate plus a specified spread, has been replaced by a cost of borrowing that is expected to track prevailing market conditions rather than a hard number set in the Regulations. Penal interest and prepayment charges were previously capped at not more than two percentage points over the contracted rate under the earlier rules; multiple law-firm summaries of the February 2026 amendment report that this fixed cap has been removed, with such charges now expected to follow prevailing market conditions rather than a hard ceiling. End-use restrictions are also reported to have loosened, but no two sources reviewed here agree closely enough on which specific uses are now open for this guide to list them; a borrower should treat any previously barred end-use, such as general working capital beyond the older limits, as still closed until its bank confirms otherwise under the amended Schedule.

Reporting the loan: Form ECB, the LRN and event-based filing

The mechanics have not changed in their basic shape. The borrower and lender agree commercial terms, the borrower's authorised dealer Category I bank reviews the draft agreement against the current eligibility and pricing conditions, and the loan is reported to the Reserve Bank on Form ECB to obtain a Loan Registration Number before any drawdown. A loan that obtains its LRN on or after 16 February 2026 is reported to fall under the amended Regulations, while an ECB already running on an earlier LRN continues broadly on its original terms unless it is refinanced.

What has changed, on the reported commentary, is the shift of Form ECB 2 from a fixed monthly filing to an event-based filing tied to specified triggers in the loan's life, rather than a return due every month regardless of activity. This is a welcome reduction in paperwork if the reported change holds, but a compliance team should keep filing monthly until its AD bank confirms in writing that the event-based regime applies to that particular facility, since a missed Form ECB 2 is a reporting contravention under FEMA that otherwise has to be regularised through the Reserve Bank's compounding mechanism rather than ignored.

Where this goes wrong in practice

The recurring mistake is treating an ECB and an equity infusion as interchangeable because the money is coming from the same parent either way. An ECB does not need a Form FC-GPR filing, but the loan proceeds cannot usually be used to acquire shares in another Indian company, to repay an existing rupee loan beyond the specific exceptions the Schedule allows, or to fund real estate activity that is not genuinely linked to the borrower's business, and a group that mixes up the two routes ends up having to unwind a drawdown. A second mistake is calculating the average maturity on the loan's final repayment date rather than the weighted average of all scheduled repayments, which understates the true maturity on a loan with a bullet structure and front-loaded instalments.

A third, more basic error is assuming a related-party loan needs less documentation because the lender and the borrower's board effectively answer to the same ultimate owner. The authorised dealer bank will still want the loan agreement, the board resolutions on both sides where relevant, and confirmation of the lender's category, and it will not process the LRN application on the strength of a group circular alone. A subsidiary planning to refinance an older, costlier ECB under the new cost of borrowing standard should get its bank's written view on the applicable ceiling before signing the refinancing term sheet, not after, since unwinding a signed agreement that breaches a condition is considerably more work than renegotiating a draft.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a foreign parent company lend money directly to its Indian subsidiary without setting up a separate finance entity?

Yes. This is exactly what the external commercial borrowings route is for. The parent lends as a non-resident lender, the subsidiary borrows as an Indian resident, and the loan is reported to the Reserve Bank through an authorised dealer bank, which issues a Loan Registration Number before the funds are drawn down.

What is a Loan Registration Number and why does an ECB need one?

It is the identification number the Reserve Bank allots to a reported ECB once Form ECB has been reviewed by the authorised dealer bank and forwarded. No drawdown should happen before the LRN is issued, and all later reporting on that loan, including the periodic or event-based return, is filed against this number.

Has the interest rate ceiling on ECBs been removed entirely after the February 2026 amendment?

The fixed all-in-cost ceiling with its benchmark-plus-spread formula has been replaced by a cost of borrowing concept tied to market conditions rather than a hard number in the Regulations, on the commentary available. Penal interest and prepayment charges, previously capped at two percentage points over the contracted rate, are reported to no longer be subject to that fixed cap and are now expected to track prevailing market conditions instead. The exact residual limits should be confirmed with an authorised dealer bank before pricing a loan.

What is the minimum period before an ECB taken by an Indian subsidiary can be repaid?

The minimum average maturity period for most ECBs is reported to remain three years, with a shorter period available to manufacturing sector borrowers on terms a borrower should verify directly with its bank. Repaying earlier than the applicable minimum average maturity is a breach unless a specific exception applies.

What happens if an Indian company finds it used ECB funds for a purpose the Regulations do not permit?

An end-use breach is a contravention under FEMA. It is typically regularised through the Reserve Bank's compounding process on application, rather than left unreported, and the earlier a borrower approaches its authorised dealer bank once the breach is identified, the more straightforward that process tends to be.
