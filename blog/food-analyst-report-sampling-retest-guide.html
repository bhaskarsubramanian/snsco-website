Challenging a Food Analyst's Report Under the FSS Act: Sampling, Retesting and Prosecution Defences

How a food sample is split, when a food business operator can demand retesting, and why courts have quashed FSSAI prosecutions over sampling defects.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 22 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A food business operator usually hears about a problem with a batch long after it has left the factory. A Food Safety Officer visits, draws a sample, and weeks later a notice arrives saying the Food Analyst has found the product 'unsafe' or 'sub-standard' and that prosecution is being launched. By then the shelf life of the product may be over, the batch may be fully sold, and the operator's first instinct is to argue about the quality of the food itself. That is usually the wrong fight to pick first.

Most prosecutions under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 rise or fall on whether the sampling, splitting and testing procedure was followed exactly as the Act and the Rules require. Courts treat this legislation as penal in character, which means the procedural safeguards built around the sample are read strictly, not loosely. An operator who understands the sequence of what should have happened, and checks it against what the record actually shows, often finds the case was never properly built.

How a food sample is actually divided

When a Food Safety Officer draws a sample under Section 38 of the Act, the sample is not sent to a single laboratory and left at that. Section 47(1)(c) requires the officer to divide the sample into four parts. One part goes to the Food Analyst for the initial report. Two parts go to the Designated Officer, who holds them in case a dispute arises later. The fourth part is kept available for the food business operator, who can have it sent to a NABL-accredited or FSSAI-notified laboratory of the operator's own choosing if there is a disagreement with the Food Analyst's finding.

This four-way split exists precisely because the first report is not meant to be the final word. The structure assumes that an operator who disputes the finding will want an independent check, and it builds that check into the sampling process itself rather than leaving it to be argued for the first time in court. An operator who is not told about this split, or whose request for the fourth part is ignored or delayed past the point where testing is still meaningful, has a procedural objection worth raising early, not as an afterthought once the trial is underway.

The Food Analyst, once the first part is tested, must forward the report to the Designated Officer. If the report finds a contravention, it becomes the basis for the Designated Officer to decide whether to launch prosecution under Section 42, or in more serious cases to refer the matter for adjudication. None of this analysis happens in a vacuum. Section 43 requires that the laboratory carrying out the testing be accredited for the relevant scope of analysis, and a Bombay High Court decision quashing a prosecution against Nestle over its Maggi noodles turned on exactly this point: the Referral Food Laboratory that examined the sample on the operator's appeal was not shown to be NABL-accredited as Section 43 requires, so its adverse finding could not be relied upon to found a prosecution at all, even though the first laboratory's analysis had found the product compliant.

The appeal and referral route when reports disagree

An operator is not stuck with the Food Analyst's report simply because a request for the fourth part was made. There is a separate route for actually disputing the finding once it is in hand. Under Section 46(4) read with the procedure in the Rules, an operator can appeal the Food Analyst's report to the Designated Officer, and this appeal has to be filed within a limited window. More than one High Court order dealing with retesting disputes has treated this as a genuinely short window, and one Madhya Pradesh High Court matter recorded the prosecution's own argument that an application made after the scheduled thirty-day period had lapsed came too late, leaving the Designated Officer no jurisdiction to send the sample for a fresh test.

Where the operator's own test and the original Food Analyst's report come back different, the matter does not simply get argued out between the two reports. It goes to a Referral Food Laboratory, whose finding is treated as the one that settles the scientific question. This is worth knowing because defence strategy sometimes assumes a second favourable report automatically wins the case. It does not. What it does is trigger the referral mechanism, and the referral report is what a prosecution has to answer to, or rely on, by the time the matter is argued.

The practical lesson for an operator is to treat the notice of sampling as the start of a clock, not as background paperwork to be filed away. Whoever receives that notice should immediately check whether a fourth-part sample is still physically available, whether the product's shelf life allows for meaningful retesting, and whether the window for an appeal to the Designated Officer is still open. Waiting until a summons arrives from a magistrate's court is usually too late to exercise any of these rights.

Why timing has quashed more prosecutions than the lab results themselves

A recurring pattern in recent High Court orders is that the prosecution fails not because the food was actually safe, but because the procedural clock was not respected. In 2024, the Bombay High Court set aside a penalty order after finding that the time limit for analysis had not been followed, holding in clear terms that because the Act is penal, the time limits for analysis have to be scrupulously followed, and directing FSSAI to issue clearer guidance on the point.

Limitation arguments turn on a date that is easy to get wrong. The Allahabad High Court, in a 2024 ruling involving a dairy operator, held that the date of commission of the offence for limitation purposes is the date the Food Analyst's report is received, not the date the sample was originally collected. An operator's lawyer who calculates limitation from the sampling date rather than the report date can end up conceding a case that was actually time-barred.

Shelf life creates its own version of the same problem. In a more recent Bombay High Court matter involving Britannia Dairy, the prosecution notice reached the company only after the product's shelf life had already run out, which meant the company had lost any real opportunity to exercise its right to have the retained sample retested. The court quashed the case on that basis, treating the word 'shall' in the relevant Rule as making the retest opportunity mandatory rather than optional, and therefore something the prosecution had to actually preserve, not merely offer on paper.

The consumer's separate right to test under Section 40

Section 40 gives an ordinary purchaser, as distinct from a Food Safety Officer, a right to have food tested independently. A consumer who buys a product and suspects something is wrong can pay a fee to have a Food Analyst examine it, provided the food business operator is told of this at the time of purchase. If the report comes back showing non-compliance, the purchaser is entitled to get that fee refunded, and the report goes on to the Designated Officer in the same way an officer-initiated sample would.

This route matters for food businesses because it means a dissatisfied customer does not need to wait for a regulator to act. A single consumer complaint, properly tested under Section 40, can trigger the same prosecution machinery that a routine FSSAI inspection would. Businesses that handle customer complaints casually, without checking whether a sample was retained and whether the complainant has invoked this provision, sometimes find out only when the Designated Officer's notice arrives that the process has already moved well past the stage where an informal settlement was realistic.

What a notice under FSSAI should prompt you to check immediately

The first thing to verify on receiving any sampling notice is whether the four-way split under Section 47(1)(c) was actually carried out, and whether the fourth part, meant for the operator's own testing, was offered in a way that still left enough shelf life for a meaningful result. If the notice arrives close to or after the product's expiry, that timing itself can be the strongest point in the operator's favour, as the Britannia decision shows.

The second check is the identity and accreditation of the laboratory that actually tested the sample. Section 43 requires accreditation for the specific scope of testing involved, and the Nestle ruling shows that a report from the wrong kind of laboratory is not a minor irregularity, it can be fatal to the entire prosecution. The third check is the date of the Food Analyst's report itself, since that date, not the date of sampling, usually starts the limitation clock for the offence.

None of this is a substitute for actually getting the underlying food testing right at the manufacturing stage. But in practice, a well-run compliance process that tracks these procedural dates alongside its own quality control gives a business a genuine defence that does not depend on relitigating whether the product was in fact safe.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I refuse to let a Food Safety Officer take a sample from my premises?

No. Officers have statutory power to draw samples under Section 38, and refusal or obstruction is itself an offence. What an operator can and should insist on is that the sampling and splitting procedure under Section 47 is followed properly, including being told about the fourth part reserved for independent testing.

What happens if the fourth part of my sample was never offered to me?

This is a genuine procedural defect worth raising, ideally in writing at the earliest opportunity rather than only at trial. Courts have treated the retesting right as mandatory, and a prosecution built on a report where this right was denied or rendered meaningless by delay has been quashed on that basis in reported High Court decisions.

How long do I have to seek retesting once I get the Food Analyst's report?

The window has been treated by at least one High Court as a strict thirty-day period for seeking the sample's referral or retest, calculated from when the scheduled process allows. Because the exact deadline depends on the specific Rule being invoked and the facts, an operator should start the process the day the report is received rather than waiting to confirm the precise date.

Does it matter which laboratory tested my sample?

Yes, significantly. Section 43 requires the laboratory to hold accreditation covering the specific type of test performed. A report from a laboratory outside that accreditation can be found unreliable as a matter of law, regardless of what the report actually says about the product.

A customer complained and says they got the product tested themselves. What should I do?

Treat it seriously and promptly. Section 40 lets any purchaser have food tested by a Food Analyst at their own cost, and a report showing non-compliance can be forwarded to the Designated Officer just as an officer-led sample would be. Ask for details of when and how the sample was drawn and tested before responding.
