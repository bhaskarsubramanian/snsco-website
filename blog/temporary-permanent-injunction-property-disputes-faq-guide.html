Temporary vs Permanent Injunctions in Property Disputes: An FAQ Guide

A practical FAQ guide explaining the difference between a temporary (interim) injunction and a permanent injunction in a property dispute, when each is available, and what a plaintiff must show.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Two Different Remedies, Often Confused

In a property dispute, the terms 'temporary injunction' and 'permanent injunction' are often used loosely, but they are legally distinct remedies granted at different stages of a suit, on different standards of proof, and for different purposes. Understanding which one applies, and when: is important for anyone considering, or defending, an injunction suit over property.

Temporary (Interim) Injunctions

A temporary injunction, governed by Order XXXIX of the Code of Civil Procedure, is an interim measure that a court can grant at any stage of a pending suit, even before the final outcome is decided, to preserve the status quo: most commonly, to prevent a party from being dispossessed, or from carrying out construction on disputed land, while the suit is being heard. Courts apply a well-established three-fold test: whether the plaintiff has a prima facie case, whether the balance of convenience favours granting the injunction, and whether the plaintiff would suffer irreparable injury if it is refused.

Permanent Injunctions

A permanent injunction is final relief, granted only at the conclusion of a full trial, once the plaintiff has proved their case on the merits: typically, that they are in settled, lawful possession of the property and that the defendant is threatening or has committed an unlawful interference with that possession. Unlike a temporary injunction, it is not based on a prima facie assessment but on final findings of fact after evidence has been led by both sides.

What a Plaintiff Must Establish for Either

For both forms of injunction, a plaintiff must generally establish settled possession as of the relevant date: an injunction protects existing possession, it does not establish a disputed right to possession, which instead requires a suit for possession and proof of title. A plaintiff who is not actually in possession, or whose possession is itself disputed or of recent, precarious origin, is generally not entitled to an injunction.

Ex-Parte Interim Injunctions

Courts can, in genuinely urgent cases, grant a temporary injunction ex parte: that is, without first hearing the opposing party, but such orders are meant to be exceptional, are typically short-lived pending a full hearing of both sides, and require the court to record specific reasons for proceeding without notice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between a temporary and a permanent injunction?

A temporary injunction is interim relief granted during a pending suit to preserve the status quo, decided on a prima facie basis. A permanent injunction is final relief granted only after a full trial, based on proved facts.

Do I need to prove my full case to get a temporary injunction?

No: a temporary injunction requires only a prima facie case, along with balance of convenience and irreparable injury in your favour, not final proof of your entire claim.

Can I get an injunction if I'm not currently in possession of the property?

Generally no: an injunction protects existing, settled possession. If you are out of possession, the appropriate relief is usually a suit for possession, requiring proof of title, rather than an injunction alone.

Can a court grant an injunction without hearing the other side first?

Only in genuinely urgent circumstances, and such ex-parte orders are meant to be short-lived, pending a full hearing of both parties, with the court recording specific reasons for proceeding without notice.
