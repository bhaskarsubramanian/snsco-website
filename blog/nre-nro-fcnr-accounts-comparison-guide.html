NRE vs NRO vs FCNR Accounts: Which One Actually Fits Your Situation?

NRIs are offered three different types of Indian bank accounts with materially different rules on repatriation, taxation and currency: this guide explains how NRE, NRO and FCNR accounts differ and when to use each.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Why NRIs Need More Than One Type of Account

A Non-Resident Indian is generally not permitted to continue operating an ordinary resident savings account once their residential status changes: Indian banking regulation requires accounts to be redesignated appropriately, and this is where the three principal NRI account types come in, each designed for a different purpose and carrying materially different rules on which currency the account is held in, whether and how easily funds can be sent back abroad, and how interest earned is taxed.

NRE (Non-Resident External) Account

An NRE account is denominated in Indian Rupees but funded from the NRI's foreign earnings, and its defining feature is full and free repatriability, both the principal deposited and any interest earned can be freely transferred abroad, without restriction, at any time. Interest earned on an NRE account is exempt from Indian income tax, making it attractive for parking foreign-earned savings the NRI wants to keep readily transferable and largely outside the Indian tax net. The account can be held as savings or fixed deposit, and since it is Rupee-denominated, it carries exchange-rate risk: the actual foreign-currency value of the deposit fluctuates with the Rupee's movement against the NRI's home currency.

NRO (Non-Resident Ordinary) Account

An NRO account is the vehicle for managing income earned within India: rent from Indian property, dividends from Indian investments, pension, or the proceeds of selling Indian assets. Unlike an NRE account, funds in an NRO account are not freely repatriable: repatriation is subject to specific limits and conditions (broadly, a cap on the amount repatriable in a financial year without additional RBI permission, along with the requirement to obtain a Chartered Accountant's certification confirming applicable Indian taxes have been paid on the funds before repatriation). Interest earned on an NRO account is taxable in India, generally subject to tax deduction at source at the rate applicable to non-residents (subject to any lower rate available under an applicable DTAA, discussed in our companion guide, and the documentation needed to claim it).

FCNR (Foreign Currency Non-Resident) Account

An FCNR account is held in a specified foreign currency (commonly USD, GBP, EUR, and several others), rather than in Rupees, and is available only as a term/fixed deposit, not a savings account. Its principal advantage is that it eliminates exchange-rate risk on the deposit itself, since both the deposit and the eventual maturity value remain in the foreign currency the NRI chose: useful for an NRI who wants to earn interest on foreign savings without taking on Rupee exchange-rate exposure. Like an NRE account, both principal and interest on an FCNR account are freely repatriable, and interest is exempt from Indian income tax for as long as the depositor retains non-resident status.

Choosing Between Them

The practical choice generally comes down to the source and intended use of the funds. Foreign earnings the NRI wants to keep readily transferable, in Rupees, and tax-efficient in India: NRE. Income actually generated within India (rent, dividends, pension): NRO, accepting the repatriation limits and Indian tax that come with it. Foreign savings the NRI wants to deposit for a fixed term without taking on Rupee exchange-rate risk: FCNR. Many NRIs maintain more than one of these accounts simultaneously, precisely because different categories of their own money, foreign earnings, Indian rental income, foreign fixed deposits, are each better suited to a different account type.

A Common Mistake: Funding the Wrong Account

A recurring and avoidable problem arises when Indian-sourced income (rent, dividends) is mistakenly deposited into an NRE account instead of an NRO account, or when an NRI attempts to fund property purchases or other transactions from an account whose funds don't actually have the repatriation status the transaction requires. Because NRE and FCNR accounts are meant to hold foreign-sourced funds and NRO accounts are meant to hold India-sourced funds, keeping the source of funds and the account type properly matched from the outset avoids compliance complications and repatriation difficulties later: retroactively correcting a misfunded account is considerably more cumbersome than funding it correctly in the first place.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between an NRE and an NRO account?

An NRE account holds foreign-earned funds with full, free repatriability and tax-exempt interest; an NRO account holds India-sourced income (rent, dividends, pension) with limited, conditional repatriability and interest that is taxable in India.

What is an FCNR account and why would an NRI choose it?

An FCNR account is a fixed-deposit account held in a specified foreign currency rather than Rupees, eliminating exchange-rate risk on the deposit. Like an NRE account, both principal and interest are freely repatriable and interest is tax-exempt for a non-resident.

Can Indian rental income be deposited into an NRE account?

No, not properly: Indian-sourced income such as rent should be deposited into an NRO account. Depositing India-sourced income into an NRE account, meant for foreign-earned funds, is a common and avoidable compliance mistake.

Is interest on an NRO account taxable in India?

Yes, generally subject to tax deduction at source at the applicable non-resident rate, though a lower rate may be available under an applicable DTAA with proper documentation, including a Tax Residency Certificate and Form 10F.

Can NRO account funds be freely sent abroad?

Not freely: repatriation from an NRO account is subject to specific annual limits without additional RBI permission, and generally requires a Chartered Accountant's certification confirming applicable Indian taxes have been paid on the funds.

References & Further Reading

This guide provides a general comparison of NRE, NRO and FCNR accounts. Specific interest rates, repatriation limits and tax treatment are periodically updated by the RBI and tax authorities, and current rules should be confirmed before opening or funding an account.
