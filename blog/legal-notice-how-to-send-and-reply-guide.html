Sending and Replying to a Legal Notice in India: What It Must Say and What Happens Next

When a legal notice is mandatory rather than optional, what it should contain, and why ignoring one can cost you more than a reply would.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A legal notice is, at bottom, a formal letter stating a grievance, the relief wanted, and a deadline to respond before legal proceedings follow. For most civil claims, sending one is a matter of practice and leverage rather than law. For a narrow but important set of claims, it is a precondition without which a court will not even entertain the case. As the law stands today, the two situations that come up most often are a suit against the government, where Section 80 of the Code of Civil Procedure makes notice mandatory, and a cheque bounce complaint, where the notice under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act is what actually starts the criminal offence running.

Getting the notice itself right, and knowing what a reply does and does not achieve, matters more than people assume, because both documents tend to resurface in court months or years later, quoted back at the party who signed them.

When notice is mandatory rather than merely customary

Outside a handful of statutory situations, Indian law does not require a plaintiff to send a notice before filing a civil suit. Lawyers send one anyway, because it sometimes resolves the dispute without litigation, it creates a documented record of the claim and the date it was asserted, and it occasionally draws out an admission or a counter-position that shapes the eventual pleadings. None of that makes the notice legally compulsory for an ordinary suit for damages or possession.

Two statutes change that by making notice a condition precedent to the claim itself. Section 80 of the Code of Civil Procedure bars a suit against the government or a public officer acting in their official capacity unless notice has first been given. Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act makes the demand notice part of the definition of the offence itself: without a notice correctly timed and worded, there is no completed offence to prosecute, whatever the state of the underlying cheque. A client who skips the notice in either situation does not just lose an opportunity to settle; they lose the right to sue or prosecute at all until the notice requirement is satisfied.

Suing the government: Section 80 of the Code of Civil Procedure

Section 80 requires that, before a suit is instituted against the central or state government, or against a public officer for an act done in their official capacity, a notice be delivered or left at the relevant office at least two months in advance. The notice must state the plaintiff's name, address and the cause of action, together with the relief claimed. The Supreme Court has described the requirement as mandatory and admitting of no implied exceptions, which in practice means a suit filed even a few days inside the two-month window is liable to be thrown out on this ground alone, regardless of the merits.

The one statutory escape is Section 80(2), which allows a suit for urgent or immediate relief to be filed without prior notice, but only with the leave of the court. Even then, the court cannot actually grant interim or final relief in that suit until the government or officer has had a reasonable opportunity to show cause, so the exception buys the plaintiff the ability to file and keep a limitation-sensitive claim alive, not an immediate order. Courts have warned against using the urgency route as a routine device to dodge Section 80, and expect the claimed urgency to be real and specifically pleaded, not asserted as a formality to get past the registry.

Cheque bounce notices under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act

A cheque bounce notice works on a tight and commonly misunderstood timetable. Once the payee learns that the cheque has been returned unpaid, the demand notice calling for payment must be sent within thirty days of that knowledge. It should identify the cheque, the amount, the date of dishonour and the reason given by the bank, and call on the drawer to pay within the statutory period.

The drawer then has fifteen days from receiving that notice to make the payment. Only if the fifteen days pass without payment does an offence under Section 138 actually arise; a complaint filed before that window closes is premature and will fail regardless of how clear the underlying dishonour is. From the date that cause of action arises, the complaint itself must be filed within one month under Section 142(1)(b), though courts have a discretion to condone a short delay on sufficient cause shown in a separate application.

Getting the notice's dates wrong is one of the most common ways a genuinely good Section 138 case collapses. A notice sent too late after learning of the dishonour, or a complaint filed either before the fifteen days run out or after the one-month window closes without a condonation application, gives the defence an easy technical win that has nothing to do with whether the cheque actually bounced.

What a notice should contain and how it should be served

A sound notice states the facts in chronological order, identifies the specific breach or default, quantifies the relief or sum demanded, cites the provision under which it is sent where one applies, and gives a clear deadline. Vague or exaggerated language weakens it; a notice that overstates the claim or threatens remedies the sender has no real intention of pursuing tends to be read later, in court, as evidence that the sender's own version was unsteady from the outset.

Service matters as much as content. The usual practice is registered post with acknowledgment due, often supplemented by email or courier so there is more than one proof of dispatch and, ideally, delivery. Where delivery is refused or the notice returns unclaimed, courts have generally treated a correctly addressed notice sent by registered post as sufficiently served, on the reasoning that a party cannot avoid a notice by refusing to collect it, though the sender should still keep the postal track record and the returned envelope as proof of the attempt.

Replying to a notice: silence is not a safe strategy

No law compels a recipient to reply to a legal notice, and an unanswered notice does not by itself create a liability that did not otherwise exist. That said, most advocates treat a considered reply as close to essential, for a simple reason: a court faced with a notice that went unanswered tends to read the silence as some evidence that the recipient had nothing credible to say in response at the time, even though the legal effect of silence alone is limited and cannot substitute for actual proof of the claim.

A reply should engage with each specific allegation rather than issuing a blanket denial, since a later defence that contradicts the position taken in the reply is far weaker than a defence that lines up with it. It should also be sent within whatever deadline the notice itself sets, or within a reasonable time if none is stated, typically understood as somewhere between fifteen and thirty days depending on the complexity of the dispute. Where the claim genuinely has no merit, a short, firm reply denying it is usually safer than ignoring the notice altogether and hoping it goes away.

How the notice and reply resurface later in court

Both the notice and any reply are ordinary documents that get placed on the court record once litigation starts, and their contents are examined closely for inconsistencies with the pleadings filed later. A plaintiff whose notice claimed one sum and whose plaint claims a different one invites an obvious cross-examination point. A defendant whose reply admitted a fact that the written statement later denies faces the same problem in reverse.

Admissions made in a notice or a reply are governed today by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 with effect from 1 July 2024 and carries forward the same basic treatment of admissions as relevant and, in many situations, strong evidence against the person who made them. The safest working assumption for a client drafting either document is that every sentence in it may be read back to them in open court, which is reason enough to have an advocate review the wording before it goes out, whichever side of the notice they happen to be on.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I always need to send a legal notice before filing a civil suit?

No. Outside specific statutes, Indian law does not generally require a pre-suit notice for an ordinary civil claim. It becomes mandatory mainly when suing the government or a public officer under Section 80 CPC, or when the claim is a cheque bounce complaint under Section 138, where the notice is part of how the offence itself is defined.

What happens if I file a suit against a government department without the two months' notice?

The suit is liable to be dismissed or the plaint rejected on this ground alone, since courts have treated the Section 80 requirement as mandatory with no implied exceptions. The only route around it is Section 80(2), filing with the court's leave for urgent relief, and even then the court cannot grant relief until the government has had a reasonable chance to respond.

Is it mandatory to reply to a legal notice I have received?

No statute compels a reply, but not replying carries real risk. Courts can treat unexplained silence as some indication that the recipient had no credible answer at the time, which can weigh against them later, even though silence alone is not enough to prove the claim against them.

I sent the Section 138 demand notice late. Can I still file the complaint?

If the notice itself was sent beyond thirty days of learning of the dishonour, the underlying offence may not be made out at all, since courts have held that a notice outside the permitted period means Section 138 is not attracted. This is different from a late complaint, where condonation of delay in filing, not in the notice, can sometimes be sought.

Does sending a legal notice stop my limitation period from running?

Generally no. A notice is sometimes a precondition to suing, but sending it does not independently add time to a limitation period that is otherwise running under the Limitation Act, 1963, unless a specific provision such as an acknowledgment of debt applies on the same facts.

References & Further Reading

The provisions and rulings below were relied on for this guide. The position can change with amendments and later decisions, so check the current text and the latest case law before acting on any of it.

  1. Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Section 80 (notice before suit against government or public officer) and Section 80(2) (urgent relief exception)
  2. Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, Section 138 (dishonour of cheque, including the proviso on notice and payment periods) and Section 142(1)(b) (limitation for filing complaint)
  3. Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, provisions on admissions (replacing the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 with effect from 1 July 2024)
  4. Bihari Chowdhary v. State of Bihar (Supreme Court, on the mandatory nature of Section 80 CPC notice)
  5. Limitation Act, 1963 (general position that a notice does not itself extend a running limitation period)
