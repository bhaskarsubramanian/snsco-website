Is an Employer Legally Required to Issue a Relieving or Experience Letter?

A short, practical answer to a question that comes up in almost every exit — and why the answer is more nuanced than a straightforward 'yes' or 'no'.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 August 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

The Short Answer: No Statute Mandates It — But That Isn't the Whole Picture

There is no central labour statute, and generally no state Shops and Establishments Act, that expressly obliges an employer to issue an experience letter or relieving letter to a departing employee. Absent a specific term in the appointment letter or a company's own HR policy promising one, an employer is not, strictly as a matter of statute or the individual employment contract, legally compelled to hand over such a document merely because an employee has resigned or been terminated.

That said, treating this as a purely discretionary courtesy with no downside risk would be a mistake. Two separate pressures push employers toward issuing these letters as a matter of practice even without a statutory mandate: the weight Indian courts and labour commissioners give to established industry practice, and the fact that a departing employee has other, more roundabout ways of establishing the same facts a relieving letter would confirm.

Why Industry Practice and Natural Justice Still Matter

Issuing an experience or relieving letter confirming the basic facts of employment — designation, dates of service, and (where the employer chooses) a neutral statement of conduct — has become such a widespread industry convention that several Labour Courts and Commissioners have, in specific cases, directed employers to issue one on the reasoning that withholding a document that merely confirms objectively verifiable facts, without more, sits uneasily with basic fairness in the employment relationship. This line of authority does not create a blanket statutory right, but it does mean an employer who refuses outright, particularly without a documented and defensible reason, faces a real prospect of being directed to issue one if the matter is escalated.

Where an employer has a genuine, serious basis for withholding a relieving letter — documented misconduct, an ongoing internal or external investigation, or reputational damage caused by the departing employee's conduct — that is a legitimate factor a court or commissioner is likely to weigh. But 'genuine and documented' is doing real work in that sentence: a bare assertion of dissatisfaction with an employee's performance, without a documented factual basis, is a much weaker footing to withhold a relieving letter than a properly conducted disciplinary process that actually substantiated specific misconduct.

The Practical Reality: Employees Have Other Ways to Prove Employment

Employers weighing whether to withhold a relieving letter should also recognise that doing so rarely prevents the employee from establishing the underlying facts of their employment through other means. Income tax returns filed during the employment period, Employees' Provident Fund contribution records and the corresponding EPFO documentation, bank statements showing salary credits, and the appointment letter and resignation correspondence themselves all independently corroborate designation, tenure, and compensation — meaning a subsequent employer or institution can generally verify employment history even without a formal relieving letter, just through a slightly more roundabout process.

This matters for the employer's own risk calculus: withholding a relieving letter over genuine, documented misconduct concerns is a defensible business decision, but withholding it purely as informal leverage or punishment — where the underlying facts of tenure and designation are not actually in dispute — provides comparatively little practical protection while creating a documented grievance an employee can point to if they escalate the matter.

A Practical Middle Path

Where an employer has genuine, documented concerns about an employee's conduct but wants to avoid unnecessary escalation, a considered middle path is often available: issuing a factual, neutral confirmation of employment — designation and dates of service only, without characterising the reason for departure or offering any assessment of conduct — while separately retaining internal documentation of the substantive concerns for use if they become relevant in another context (a reference check, a subsequent dispute, and so on). This approach satisfies the practical and reputational pressure to provide basic confirmation of employment without requiring the employer to make any positive representation about the departing employee's conduct that it is not prepared to stand behind.

Ultimately, whether to issue a full, neutral, or no relieving letter at all is a considered management decision, best made with the specific documentation of any misconduct concerns in hand, rather than a decision to be made reflexively in either direction. Given that the employee retains a legal route (a notice, followed if necessary by an approach to the relevant Labour Commissioner or court) to press the issue, employers are well advised to have a documented, defensible rationale ready before declining a request outright.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an employee sue for a relieving letter if the company refuses to issue one?

There is no direct statutory cause of action, but an employee can send a legal notice or approach the relevant Labour Commissioner or court, and in some cases has succeeded in obtaining a direction, particularly where the underlying facts of employment are not disputed and the employer has no documented misconduct-based justification.

Does withholding a relieving letter stop a former employee from proving they worked at the company?

No — income tax records, EPFO/PF contribution history, salary bank credits, and the original appointment and resignation correspondence all independently establish the employment relationship.

Is there a difference between an experience letter and a relieving letter?

In practice the terms are often used interchangeably, though a 'relieving letter' typically confirms the last working day and formal release from obligations, while an 'experience letter' more specifically certifies the role, duration, and sometimes performance — employers can choose to issue one, both, or neither, subject to the considerations above.
