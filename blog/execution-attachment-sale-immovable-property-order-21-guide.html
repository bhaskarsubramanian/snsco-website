Executing a Decree Against Immovable Property: Attachment, Proclamation and Sale Under Order XXI CPC

How a decree is enforced against a judgment debtor's immovable property in India: attachment, the sale proclamation, and the mandatory steps courts police.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Winning a money decree is the easier half of civil litigation in India. Getting paid is the part that drags on, and where the judgment debtor's only visible asset is a house or a plot of land, the decree holder has to go through a specific, rule-bound sequence under Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 before that property can actually be sold and the proceeds handed over. Skip a step, or get the notice wrong, and courts have repeatedly set aside the eventual sale, sending the decree holder back to square one years into the process.

This is a different stage of execution from recovering a debt out of a bank account or salary through garnishee proceedings, and different again from dealing with a judgment debtor who physically resists being put out of a property once it has already been sold or awarded. This guide covers the specific sequence for attaching and selling immovable property itself: the order of attachment, the mandatory notice before the sale terms are settled, the proclamation of sale, and the objections a debtor can still raise even after all of that has happened.

Step one: the order of attachment

Attachment of immovable property in execution is made, under Order XXI Rule 54, by an order of the court prohibiting the judgment debtor from transferring or creating any charge on the property. The order does not itself transfer anything to the decree holder or give possession; it simply freezes the debtor's ability to deal with that specific property while execution proceeds. Courts have held that an attachment becomes an effective bar against alienation only once the proclamation of the attachment order is actually made and a copy affixed as the rule requires, which means a defective or incomplete proclamation of the attachment itself can be challenged later as never having validly stopped the debtor from dealing with the property in the meantime.

A 1976 amendment to the Code added sub-rule (1A), requiring the court to give the judgment debtor notice to appear on a specified date to take notice of the date fixed for settling the terms of the proclamation of sale, before the court goes ahead and settles those terms. Courts, including the Supreme Court, have treated this notice requirement, together with the parallel notice requirement under Rule 66(2) at the proclamation stage itself, as mandatory rather than a mere formality, and a sale conducted after skipping it is vulnerable to being set aside on that ground alone, regardless of how the auction itself was conducted.

Step two: deciding what to sell

Before the court settles the terms of a sale, Order XXI Rule 64 requires it to decide what property, or what part of the property, actually needs to be sold to satisfy the decree. This is not a rubber-stamp step. Courts have held that putting an entire property up for sale where only a portion of it would realise enough to satisfy the decretal amount is itself a ground to set the sale aside, since the rule is meant to protect the judgment debtor from losing more than necessary to clear the debt.

In practice, this requires the executing court to have a reasonably informed view of the property's value and the likely sale realisation before fixing what is to be sold, which is also where a decree holder's valuation evidence, or the debtor's own objection that the whole property need not be sold, becomes relevant at this early stage rather than only after the auction has happened.

Step three: the proclamation of sale

Once the court has decided what is to be sold, Order XXI Rule 66 requires a proclamation of the intended sale, drawn up in the language of the court, stating the property to be sold, any known encumbrance, the amount for which the sale is ordered, and other particulars a bidder would need to make an informed bid. The terms of this proclamation have to be settled after notice to the decree holder and the judgment debtor, except where notice was already validly given at the earlier Rule 64 stage, and the proclamation itself is then published and a copy affixed in the manner the rules prescribe.

A minimum gap is required between the date of the proclamation and the date of the actual sale, commonly described in practitioner commentary as thirty days under Order XXI Rule 68, though the precise computation can be affected by local High Court amendments, so the current rule in the specific state should be checked before fixing or relying on a sale date. Courts have consistently held that non-compliance with the mandatory requirements around settling and publishing the proclamation, particularly the notice requirements under Rules 54(1A) and 66(2), can render the resulting sale void, which is why a decree holder pushing for a quick sale date without proper notice to the debtor is usually creating a problem for itself rather than saving time.

Step four: the auction and confirmation of sale

The sale itself is conducted as a public auction in the manner the rules and the court's own practice direction prescribe, with the highest bidder, subject to any reserve price the court has fixed, becoming the auction purchaser. The sale is not final the moment the hammer falls. The judgment debtor, or any other person affected, can apply under Order XXI Rule 90 to set the sale aside on the ground of a material irregularity or fraud in publishing or conducting the sale, provided the applicant shows that the irregularity or fraud actually caused substantial injury, since a purely technical defect that caused no real prejudice is not, by itself, enough to undo a completed sale.

Judgment debtors also retain, in many cases and within a specific time limit, the right to apply to set aside the sale by depositing the decretal amount together with the specified additional costs and interest the rules prescribe, which is a separate route from alleging irregularity and does not require showing any defect in how the auction itself was conducted. A decree holder and an auction purchaser should both expect this window to exist and plan around it rather than treating the auction's conclusion as the end of the matter.

What a debtor can still do, and what a purchaser should check

A judgment debtor who believes an error occurred anywhere in this sequence, a missing notice under Rule 54(1A), a proclamation that failed to disclose a known encumbrance, an entire property sold when a part would have sufficed under Rule 64, or a material irregularity under Rule 90, has a real and frequently successful avenue to have the sale set aside, and courts do not treat these as mere technicalities once the defect is actually established. This is precisely why a decree holder in a hurry to realise the decree gains nothing by cutting corners on notice or proclamation: an improperly conducted sale simply restarts the clock.

An auction purchaser, for their part, should independently verify that the proclamation correctly disclosed the property's encumbrances and that the statutory notices were in fact given, since a purchaser who buys at a sale later set aside for a defect in the execution process can be left in a long dispute over the deposit, even where the purchaser personally did nothing wrong. Checking the execution court's record of compliance with Rules 54(1A), 64 and 66(2) before bidding, not merely relying on the auction notice itself, is the practical safeguard against buying into someone else's procedural dispute.

After the auction: confirmation, the sale certificate, and getting possession

A completed auction is still not the end point. Where no application to set the sale aside under Rules 89, 90 or 91 is made within the time allowed, or such an application is made and refused, the court passes an order confirming the sale under Order XXI Rule 92, and only at that point does the sale become absolute. Where an attachment itself is under objection, courts have treated that as a bar on confirming the sale until the objection is finally decided, which means a pending dispute over the attachment, not just the sale, can hold up confirmation.

Once the sale is absolute, Order XXI Rule 94 requires the court to issue a sale certificate identifying the property and the purchaser, and the certificate's own date is fixed as the date the sale became absolute, not the date the certificate is actually signed and handed over, a distinction that matters because courts have noted real delay in practice between a sale becoming absolute and the certificate actually being issued. The auction purchaser's right to apply for possession of the property under Rule 95 arises only after that certificate has been issued, so a purchaser who wants to move into or otherwise take over the property should treat the sale certificate, not the auction itself, as the real trigger for that next step, and should expect the certificate's issuance to be the stage most likely to be delayed in a busy execution court.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the first step in executing a decree against a debtor's house or land?

The executing court attaches the property under Order XXI Rule 54 of the CPC, by an order prohibiting the judgment debtor from transferring it or creating a charge on it. This attachment only becomes an effective bar on dealing with the property once the attachment order is properly proclaimed and a copy affixed as the rule requires.

Can a court sell an entire property to recover a small decretal amount?

Not properly. Order XXI Rule 64 requires the court to decide what property, or what portion of it, needs to be sold to satisfy the decree before settling the sale terms, and selling an entire property where a part would have sufficed is a recognised ground for setting the resulting sale aside.

How much notice is required before immovable property is sold in execution?

Courts are required to give notice before settling the terms of the sale proclamation under Rules 54(1A) and 66(2), and a minimum gap, commonly described as thirty days under Rule 68, is required between the proclamation and the actual sale, though the exact period can be affected by state-level High Court amendments.

Can a sale be set aside after the auction has already happened?

Yes. Under Order XXI Rule 90, an affected party can apply to set the sale aside for material irregularity or fraud in publishing or conducting it, provided they show substantial injury from the defect, and separately, a judgment debtor can in many cases apply to set the sale aside by depositing the decretal amount with the prescribed costs and interest within the time allowed.

When can an auction purchaser take possession of the property?

Only after the sale is confirmed under Order XXI Rule 92 and a sale certificate is then issued under Rule 94. An application for possession under Rule 95 can be made only once that certificate has actually been issued, and courts have noted real delay between a sale becoming absolute and the certificate being handed over in practice.
