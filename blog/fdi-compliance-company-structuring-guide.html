Structuring a Company for Foreign Investment: FDI Compliance Basics Under FEMA

A guide for companies planning to raise foreign investment on the FDI compliance framework under FEMA: permitted routes, sectoral caps, pricing guidelines and reporting obligations that shape company structuring.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 25 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Automatic Route vs. Government Route

Foreign direct investment into an Indian company is permitted either under the automatic route, where no prior government approval is required and the investment is subject only to sectoral conditions and post-facto reporting, or the government route, where prior approval from the relevant administrative ministry or department is required before the investment can be made. Which route applies depends on the sector the investee company operates in, and, in certain cases, on whether the investment originates from an entity based in a country sharing a land border with India, which attracts a mandatory government-approval requirement regardless of sector.

Sectoral Caps and Entry Conditions

Even where the automatic route applies, many sectors carry a sectoral cap limiting the maximum percentage of foreign shareholding permitted, and some carry additional entry conditions, such as minimum capitalisation, licensing prerequisites, or restrictions on the instruments through which foreign investment can be made. Structuring a company's capital and shareholding pattern without first confirming the applicable sectoral cap and entry conditions for its actual business activity is one of the most common early-stage FDI compliance missteps.

Pricing Guidelines for Share Issuance and Transfer

FEMA pricing guidelines generally require that shares issued to, or transferred by, a non-resident be priced at not less than fair value as determined under an internationally accepted pricing methodology, on the issue side, and, on a transfer from a resident to a non-resident, at not less than that fair value as well: with the direction reversed (not more than fair value) for a transfer from a non-resident to a resident. Structuring a round or secondary transfer without confirming pricing compliance can create downstream reporting and compounding issues even where the underlying commercial terms are perfectly legitimate.

Reporting Obligations

Indian companies receiving foreign investment must file the prescribed reports with the Reserve Bank of India through the FIRMS portal. Principally Form FC-GPR on allotment of shares to a non-resident, and Form FC-TRS on a transfer of shares between a resident and non-resident, within the prescribed timelines. Delayed or missed filings can require compounding of the contravention with the RBI before further FDI-linked corporate actions can proceed cleanly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between the automatic and government routes for FDI?

Under the automatic route, no prior government approval is needed and the investment is subject to sectoral conditions and reporting; under the government route, prior approval from the relevant ministry or department is mandatory before the investment can be made.

Does investment from a bordering country always need government approval?

Yes: investment from an entity based in, or beneficially owned by a person based in, a country sharing a land border with India requires prior government approval regardless of the sector involved.

What are FDI pricing guidelines meant to prevent?

They prevent share issuance or transfer between residents and non-residents at prices that are not reflective of fair value, which could otherwise be used to move value across the exchange control boundary outside the reported investment framework.

What happens if FC-GPR or FC-TRS filings are missed?

The company faces a contravention under FEMA that typically requires compounding with the Reserve Bank of India, and unresolved filings can complicate future foreign-investment transactions involving the company.
