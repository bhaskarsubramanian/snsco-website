When Is a Foreign Company Tax Resident in India? POEM Test

How the POEM test can make a foreign company tax resident in India, the active-business safe harbour, and what residence then costs it.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 16 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A company incorporated outside India does not automatically escape Indian tax residence. Under the Income Tax Act, 1961, a foreign company is treated as resident in India, and taxed in India on its worldwide income rather than only on its India income, if its place of effective management for the relevant year is in India. The test looks past the certificate of incorporation to where the company's key management and commercial decisions are actually made, and a foreign subsidiary run almost entirely by its Indian promoters from a Mumbai or Gurugram office, with board meetings held abroad only on paper, is exactly the fact pattern this provision was written for.

The test carries a built-in safe harbour for genuine operating businesses and a facts-based inquiry for everyone else, and the two routes produce very different outcomes for a multinational group's intermediate holding or trading companies set up offshore. This guide sets out both routes, the consequence of failing the test, and what the Income-tax Act, 2025, due to apply from the tax year beginning 1 April 2026, changes and does not change.

What the statute actually asks

Section 6(3) of the Income Tax Act, 1961 provides that a company is resident in India in any year if it is an Indian company, or if its place of effective management in that year is in India. Place of effective management is defined, for this purpose, as the place where key management and commercial decisions necessary for the conduct of the business of the company as a whole are, in substance, made. The emphasis on 'in substance' is deliberate: the test is not satisfied or defeated merely by where board meetings are formally minuted to have occurred, if the real decision-making can be shown to happen somewhere else.

The Central Board of Direct Taxes issued Circular No. 6 of 2017 to lay down guiding principles for applying this test, after an earlier attempt at a stricter standard was deferred for a year following industry concern that it would sweep in ordinary offshore structures. The circular remains the operative departmental guidance on how an assessing officer is expected to approach a POEM inquiry, and taxpayers and officers alike generally work from its two-stage structure rather than from the bare statutory text alone.

The active business outside India safe harbour

The circular's first question is whether the foreign company is engaged in active business outside India. A company qualifies if its passive income, broadly income from related-party transactions, dividends, interest, royalties and capital gains, does not exceed fifty per cent of its total income, and if, on the same fifty per cent threshold, less than half of its total assets are situated in India, less than half of its employees are based in India or are Indian residents, and less than half of its payroll expense is paid to such employees. These figures are generally taken as an average of the relevant year and the two years before it, rather than a single year's snapshot, which protects a company from failing the test because of one unusual year.

A company that clears this active-business gateway gets a strong presumption in its favour: its place of effective management is presumed to be outside India where the majority of its board meetings are held outside India, even if some strategic decisions are influenced by an Indian parent, so long as the board itself is not a rubber stamp for decisions already made in India. Separately, Circular No. 8 of 2017 exempts a foreign company altogether from the guiding principles where its turnover or gross receipts in the relevant year are Rs 50 crore or less, which keeps small offshore entities out of this analysis entirely regardless of where they are actually run from.

When the gateway test fails: the facts and circumstances inquiry

A company that cannot clear the active-business threshold, typically a holding company, an intermediate investment vehicle, or a trading entity whose real activity and decision-making sit substantially in India, falls into a facts-and-circumstances inquiry that the circular describes as locating the company's 'head and brain'. This looks at where the board in substance exercises its powers, as opposed to where it formally meets; whether the board routinely follows the directions of a person resident in India such as a promoter or an Indian parent company's executives; where the company's day-to-day operational decisions, as distinct from a formal approval, are actually taken; and where the heads of key functions such as finance and operations are based.

This is an inherently fact-specific exercise, and the circular itself warns against a mechanical or isolated reading of any single factor, such as treating the location of a single video-conference call as decisive. In practice, assessing officers building a POEM case tend to rely heavily on email trails, internal approval chains, and the seniority and location of the people who actually drove a transaction, rather than on the minutes of a board meeting held at a registered office abroad that did little more than record a decision already made elsewhere.

What happens once a foreign company is found resident

The consequence of failing the test is substantial: a foreign company found to have its place of effective management in India is taxed in India on its global income, not merely on income sourced in India, and becomes subject to the compliance obligations of an Indian tax resident, including return filing, transfer pricing documentation for its dealings with group entities, and exposure to India's general anti-avoidance rule where the structure itself looks designed to avoid tax rather than to serve a genuine commercial purpose. For a group that set up an offshore holding or trading company precisely to keep certain income outside the Indian tax net, a POEM finding can undo the planning entirely and bring years of income within Indian assessment, with interest running on the resulting demand.

Where India has a double taxation avoidance agreement with the company's country of incorporation, most current treaties resolve a dual-residence conflict for a company using a tie-breaker built around effective management rather than a mutual agreement procedure in every case, though the exact tie-breaker language and procedure varies by treaty and should be checked against the specific agreement in point rather than assumed from another treaty's text.

Keeping the evidence that a POEM inquiry will actually test

Because the facts-and-circumstances inquiry turns on contemporaneous records rather than a company's own later account of how it was run, the practical defence against a POEM finding is built well before any notice arrives. Boards that genuinely deliberate, rather than approve decisions already taken elsewhere, should be able to show it: detailed minutes recording discussion and disagreement rather than a bare recital of resolutions, board packs circulated and reviewed before the meeting rather than produced on the day, and a named set of directors who can demonstrate independent judgment on the matters put to them, including the ability to reject a proposal rather than ratify it as a formality.

Groups that centralise treasury, pricing or strategic decisions in an Indian office for cost or coordination reasons, as many do, should expect that convenience to be weighed against the residence test, and should think about whether operational decisions genuinely specific to the foreign company are still made, and documented, at the level of that company's own management rather than folded entirely into the parent's Indian functions. A structure that cannot point to any decision actually made outside India beyond the formal act of incorporation is the clearest case for a POEM finding, regardless of how carefully its board minutes are drafted after the fact.

What changes under the Income-tax Act, 2025

The Income-tax Act, 2025, which applies from the tax year beginning 1 April 2026 and does not disturb the assessment of income for earlier years, re-enacts the company residence test without changing its substance. The provision now sits in a different sub-section of the renumbered Section 6, rather than in Section 6(3), and commentary tracking the renumbering places the company test, including the place of effective management limb, in that renumbered sub-section; the same two-stage structure, active business safe harbour and facts-based inquiry for everything else, continues to apply, and the CBDT's 2017 circulars on how the test is administered have not been reported as withdrawn or replaced. A company or its advisers citing the provision for a year beginning on or after 1 April 2026 should cite the 2025 Act's renumbered provision rather than the 1961 Act's Section 6(3), while a dispute over an earlier year continues to be governed by the 1961 Act as it stood for that year.

Groups reviewing an existing offshore structure against this test should treat the exercise as a forward-looking compliance check, not a one-time filing decision: the active business ratios are tested year on year on a trailing average, so a business that qualified for the safe harbour in one period can lose it if its passive income or India-based activity grows relative to the rest of the group, and the inquiry should be revisited whenever the group's operating model or the location of its senior decision-makers changes materially.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does holding board meetings outside India automatically keep a foreign company out of Indian tax residence?

Only if the company first clears the active business outside India threshold; for such a company, a majority of board meetings held outside India creates a strong presumption of non-residence. For a company that fails the active-business test, the location of board meetings is only one factor in a wider facts-and-circumstances inquiry and is not decisive on its own.

Is every small offshore subsidiary at risk of a POEM inquiry?

No. CBDT Circular No. 8 of 2017 exempts a foreign company from the POEM guiding principles altogether where its turnover or gross receipts for the year are Rs 50 crore or less, regardless of where it is actually managed from.

What is the practical difference between a company failing the active business test and one that passes it?

A company that passes the active business test gets a strong presumption of non-residence tied mainly to where its board meets. A company that fails it is assessed on a broader set of facts, including who actually drives decisions, where senior management is based, and whether the board merely ratifies decisions already made in India, which is a harder and more uncertain test to satisfy.

If a foreign holding company is found resident in India, is it taxed only on its Indian income?

No. Once resident, it is taxed on its worldwide income rather than its India income alone, and becomes subject to Indian compliance obligations including return filing, transfer pricing documentation for dealings with group companies, and potential scrutiny under the general anti-avoidance rule if the structure appears designed mainly to reduce tax rather than to serve an independent commercial purpose.

Does the Income-tax Act, 2025 change the POEM test?

The substance of the test is unchanged; the provision is simply renumbered within Section 6 and applies from the tax year beginning 1 April 2026 (the new Act replaces the assessment-year system with a tax-year system from that date). Income for periods before that continues to be governed by Section 6(3) of the 1961 Act, and the 2017 CBDT circulars continue to be the operative guidance on how the test is applied.
