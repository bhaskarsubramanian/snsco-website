Illegal Salary Deductions: What an Employer May and May Not Deduct Under the Code on Wages

What an employer can lawfully deduct from wages under the Code on Wages, 2019, the caps on fines and recoveries, and how to claim back a deduction.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An employer cannot deduct money from an employee's salary for any reason it chooses. Since 21 November 2025, when the Code on Wages, 2019 came into force and replaced the Payment of Wages Act, 1936, every deduction an employer makes from wages has to fall within a specific list the Code permits, whether that is a fine for misconduct, recovery for damage to company property, repayment of an advance, or a statutory deduction such as provident fund or income tax. Anything outside that list, however the employer labels it on the payslip, is not a lawful deduction and can be recovered.

This guide sets out what the Code actually allows an employer to take out of an employee's pay, the caps that apply to fines and other deductions, the procedure an employer has to follow before imposing a fine or recovering for damage, and the route an employee has to get an unlawful deduction reversed.

What Changed on 21 November 2025

The central government brought all four labour codes, including the Code on Wages, 2019, into force with effect from 21 November 2025, repealing the Payment of Wages Act, 1936 along with the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965 and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976. The rules on what an employer can deduct from wages, which used to sit in Section 7 of the old Payment of Wages Act, are now carried forward, with some changes, into Chapter III of the Code on Wages.

For an employee whose deduction was made before that date, the Payment of Wages Act and the case law built around it still apply to that deduction. For anything deducted on or after 21 November 2025, the Code's own provisions, discussed below, are what govern the dispute, including the forum where a complaint has to be filed and the time limit for filing it.

The General Rule: No Deduction Without the Code's Authorisation

Section 18 of the Code states that wages can only be paid after making deductions that the Code itself authorises, and lists out the specific heads under which a deduction is permitted, such as fines, absence from duty, damage to or loss of goods entrusted to the employee, recovery of advances, house accommodation and amenities provided by the employer, and recovery towards income tax or other statutory dues. A deduction that does not fit one of these heads is not a deduction at all in the Code's language, it is simply an unlawful withholding of wages.

The Code also draws a distinction between a deduction and a genuine loss of wages for a reason that is not a deduction, such as the employer withholding an increment, denying a promotion, or ordering a legitimate suspension pending an inquiry. Those situations reduce what an employee earns but are not treated as deductions subject to the caps described below, which matters because an employee cannot challenge a withheld increment using the deduction-claim route meant for fines or recoveries.

Across all heads taken together, the total deductions made from an employee's wages in a single wage period cannot exceed fifty per cent of the wages payable for that period, with a relaxation where part of the deduction is being paid over to a cooperative society. An employer that deducts beyond that ceiling in one pay cycle has gone beyond what the Code allows, regardless of how legitimate each individual deduction might otherwise be.

Fines: The Three Per Cent Cap and the Procedure Before Imposing One

Section 19 deals specifically with fines for misconduct, and it is tighter than the general deduction rule. The total fine imposed on an employee in any one wage period cannot exceed three per cent of the wages payable to that employee for that period, and this cap applies to the fines taken together, not separately to each act of misconduct within the month. No fine can be imposed on an employee below fifteen years of age at all.

Before any fine is imposed, the employee has to be given a written notice specifying the act or omission and an opportunity to explain why the fine should not be imposed. The acts and omissions for which a fine may be imposed, and the scale of fines, have to be approved in advance and displayed where employees can see them, and a fine cannot be recovered from an employee in instalments. An employer is also required to keep a register of the fines imposed and to apply the amounts collected for the benefit of the employees as a class, not keep them as general revenue.

An employer who deducts a flat sum described as a fine, without the prior notice, the approved scale, or the three per cent cap, has not imposed a fine the Code recognises, and the amount deducted is recoverable regardless of whether the underlying misconduct actually happened.

Deductions for Damage, Loss or Unauthorised Absence

Where an employee is responsible for damage to, or loss of, goods or money entrusted to them, the Code allows a deduction, but only to the extent of the actual damage or loss, and only after the employee has been given a chance to show cause against the proposed deduction. An employer that deducts an amount it has simply decided on, without connecting it to an assessed loss and without hearing the employee first, is exposed on both counts if the employee challenges it.

Deductions for absence from duty are treated differently from fines and are generally proportionate to the period of absence, calculated against the wages for the period actually worked. Where several employees who were jointly responsible for a task absent themselves together in circumstances suggesting a concerted action, the Code permits a somewhat higher proportionate deduction, but this is a narrow exception tied to that specific situation and is not a general licence to penalise ordinary absence more heavily than the days actually missed.

Advances, Loans and Recoveries From Pay

An employer can recover an advance of wages paid before employment began from the employee's first payment of wages, and can recover later advances in instalments spread over a reasonable period, but an advance given specifically to cover travelling expenses is treated differently and cannot simply be clawed back from the next pay cheque in the same way. Recovery of a loan the employer gave for something like a two-wheeler or education also has to stay within the overall deduction ceiling described above.

Employees sometimes assume that because they signed a loan or advance agreement agreeing to a particular recovery schedule, the employer can deduct whatever that agreement says regardless of the Code's caps. That assumption is wrong. A private agreement between employer and employee cannot override the statutory ceiling on deductions in a wage period, and an employer recovering an advance in a way that pushes total deductions past the permitted limit is still in breach of the Code even if the employee agreed to the recovery schedule in writing.

Claiming Back an Unauthorised Deduction

An employee who believes a deduction was unauthorised, excessive or made without the required procedure can approach the claims authority appointed under the Code, generally an officer designated by the appropriate government for that purpose. Section 45 allows such an application to be filed within three years from the date the claim arose, a considerably longer window than the one-year period most employees associate with the old Payment of Wages Act, though the authority can also condone a late filing where the employee shows sufficient cause for the delay.

The claims authority, once it decides a deduction was wrongly made, can direct refund of the amount along with compensation, and has the same powers as a civil court for summoning and examining witnesses and compelling the production of documents. An order of the claims authority can be appealed to the appellate authority notified for that jurisdiction, and Section 49 sets the time limit for that appeal at ninety days from the date of the order.

Because the authority designated to hear these claims, and the specific forms and procedure for filing, are notified separately by the central government for establishments it regulates and by each state government for establishments within its jurisdiction, an employee should check with the local labour department or inspector for the exact authority and forms that apply in that state, rather than assuming the procedure is identical everywhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my employer deduct a full day's pay because I came in a few minutes late?

Only within limits. A deduction tied to actual time lost should be proportionate to the time missed, and if it is instead imposed as a fine for misconduct, it falls under the three per cent wage-period cap on fines and needs prior notice and an approved scale. A flat full-day deduction for a few minutes' lateness is unlikely to survive a challenge.

Can my employer deduct money for damaged equipment without asking me anything first?

No. The Code requires the employer to give the employee an opportunity to show cause before deducting for damage or loss, and the deduction cannot exceed the actual loss. A deduction made without that opportunity is open to challenge even if the employee was in fact responsible for the damage.

How long do I have to complain about an old salary deduction?

Under the Code on Wages, an application to the claims authority generally has to be filed within three years from when the claim arose, longer than the shorter period under the earlier Payment of Wages Act. The authority can still allow a late application where sufficient cause for the delay is shown.

Can my employer recover a loan it gave me directly from my salary every month?

Yes, in instalments over a reasonable period, but the recovery still has to respect the overall cap on deductions in any one wage period. An employer cannot rely on a loan agreement to deduct more than the Code allows in a single pay cycle, even if the employee signed that agreement.

What can I do if deductions take away more than half my monthly salary?

Deductions across all permitted heads generally cannot exceed fifty per cent of wages payable for that period, subject to a relaxation for cooperative society payments. An employee facing deductions beyond that limit can raise the excess before the claims authority appointed under the Code and seek a refund.
