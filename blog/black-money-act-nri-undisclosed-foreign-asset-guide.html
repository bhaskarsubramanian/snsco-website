The Black Money Act and NRIs: What Counts as an Undisclosed Foreign Asset

Resident Indians, including those who recently became resident again after years abroad, face steep penalties for undisclosed foreign assets under the Black Money Act — this guide explains who it applies to and what must be disclosed.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

Who the Black Money Act Actually Applies To

The Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015 is frequently discussed in connection with NRIs, which creates a common misconception: the Act's disclosure obligations apply to persons who are 'resident' (specifically, 'resident and ordinarily resident') for Indian income tax purposes, not to genuine NRIs whose foreign income and assets fall outside India's tax net in the first place because they are non-resident. The practical relevance for the NRI community runs mainly in two directions: NRIs who return to India and become resident, and resident Indians (including those with an NRI history) who retain foreign assets from a period spent abroad without properly disclosing them once their residential status changes.

What Must Be Disclosed

A person who qualifies as resident and ordinarily resident in India for a given assessment year is required to disclose specified foreign assets and foreign income in Schedule FA (Foreign Assets) of their income tax return, regardless of how small the value or income involved. This includes foreign bank accounts, foreign financial interests (including shares, ESOPs and other equity), foreign immovable property, foreign trusts in which the person has an interest, and any other specified category of foreign asset or foreign income. The Act's penalty and prosecution provisions apply where such assets or income are not properly disclosed. Indian courts have specifically rejected the argument that a low account balance or immateriality of the asset excuses non-disclosure — the obligation to disclose in Schedule FA does not turn on materiality, and a High Court in 2026 upheld a penalty on exactly this basis, rejecting a taxpayer's plea that non-disclosure of a foreign bank account was a bona fide oversight given its low balance.

The RNOR Transition Window

A returning NRI does not become subject to full resident-and-ordinarily-resident disclosure obligations immediately upon returning to India. Indian tax law provides a transitional 'Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident' (RNOR) status, generally available for a period after return depending on the number of years spent abroad, during which foreign income and, in relevant respects, foreign asset disclosure obligations are more limited than those applying to a fully resident and ordinarily resident taxpayer. Understanding exactly when RNOR status ends and full resident status (with full Schedule FA disclosure obligations) begins is important for returning NRIs, since the transition is not always obvious and getting the timing wrong can mean assets that should have been disclosed were not — our companion guide on RNOR status covers this transition in more detail.

Penalties and Prosecution Exposure

The Black Money Act's penalties are notably more severe than those under the ordinary Income Tax Act: a flat penalty of Rs. 10 lakh for failure to disclose a foreign asset (subject to a threshold de minimis exception for certain small foreign bank accounts, which is itself narrowly drawn and should not be assumed to apply without checking), separate tax and penalty on undisclosed foreign income at a flat rate, and, in more serious cases involving wilful evasion, potential criminal prosecution. Because these consequences are materially harsher than ordinary domestic non-disclosure penalties, taxpayers who have become resident (or RNOR transitioning to full resident status) and are unsure whether an asset they hold abroad needs to be disclosed should resolve that question conservatively, rather than assuming a small or dormant account is safely below anyone's notice.

Practical Guidance

Anyone whose residential status for Indian tax purposes has changed — a returning NRI, someone whose RNOR window has ended, or a long-term resident who has simply never previously had to think about a small foreign account inherited or opened years ago — should specifically review whether Schedule FA disclosure obligations now apply to them, and should treat every foreign bank account, investment, and property interest as presumptively disclosable once resident and ordinarily resident status attaches, rather than assuming immateriality or dormancy provides any protection.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Black Money Act apply to genuine NRIs living abroad?

Generally not directly — its disclosure obligations apply to persons who are resident and ordinarily resident in India for tax purposes. The Act becomes relevant mainly when an NRI returns and becomes resident, or when a resident Indian retains undisclosed foreign assets.

Does a small foreign bank account balance excuse non-disclosure?

No — courts have specifically rejected the argument that a low balance or immateriality excuses non-disclosure in Schedule FA; the obligation to disclose does not turn on the asset's materiality, subject only to a narrowly drawn de minimis exception for certain small accounts.

What is RNOR status and why does it matter for foreign asset disclosure?

Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR) is a transitional status available to a returning NRI for a period after return, during which foreign income and asset disclosure obligations are more limited than those applying to a fully resident and ordinarily resident taxpayer.

What are the penalties for failing to disclose a foreign asset under the Black Money Act?

A flat penalty of Rs. 10 lakh generally applies for failure to disclose a foreign asset (subject to a narrow de minimis exception for certain small accounts), along with separate tax and penalty on undisclosed foreign income, and potential criminal prosecution in serious cases of wilful evasion.

What foreign assets must be disclosed in Schedule FA?

Foreign bank accounts, foreign financial interests such as shares and ESOPs, foreign immovable property, interests in foreign trusts, and other specified categories of foreign asset or income, for anyone who is resident and ordinarily resident in India for that assessment year.
