Mining Lease Renewal After the MMDR Amendments: Deemed Extension and the Move to Auction

How the 2015 and 2021 MMDR amendments changed lease renewal, who gets a deemed extension, and why old leases now end in auction, not renewal.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

As the law stands today, a mining lease granted before 12 January 2015 is treated as having run for fifty years rather than its original shorter term, and once that fifty-year period ends, the law no longer provides for the lease to be renewed in the old sense at all; the area instead goes to auction, with the outgoing lessee in some cases holding a right of first refusal rather than an automatic continuation. For leases granted after that date, there was never a renewal clock to begin with, since every new grant since the 2015 amendment is itself for the fifty-year term and is followed by auction on expiry.

This single change ended decades of litigation over second and subsequent renewals, but it also created its own disputes over who actually qualifies for the deemed extension, what happens to a renewal application that was pending on the cutoff date, and whether a lease that had already lapsed before 2015 can claim any benefit from the amendment at all.

What Section 8A actually changed

The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2015 inserted Section 8A into the parent 1957 Act and, with it, moved away from the earlier system of repeated renewal applications. Leases granted before the amendment's commencement were deemed to have been granted for a period of fifty years from their original date of grant, counted as a single continuous term rather than a series of separate renewal periods. The practical effect was to collapse what had often been a first term plus one or more renewal terms into one deemed fifty-year grant, after which the mineral block becomes available only through a fresh auction rather than another renewal application by the existing lessee.

Section 8A(9) carries an important limitation: a lease whose renewal had already been rejected, or which had already been determined or had lapsed before the amendment took effect, does not get the benefit of the deemed fifty-year extension. Courts have read this narrowly against lessees whose leases were already dead in law before the cutoff, holding that the deemed extension revives nothing; it only continues a lease that was still validly subsisting at the relevant date. An Allahabad High Court ruling reported in 2026 took the same approach on a related point, holding that the deemed extension under Section 8A is available only where the lessee had filed a valid renewal application and had otherwise met the lease's conditions, not as a blanket entitlement regardless of compliance.

Captive and non-captive mines were not treated the same

The 2015 scheme drew a distinction, since partly altered by the 2021 amendment, between mines whose output is used for the lessee's own captive purpose and mines that sell their output on the open market. Where a lease's deemed fifty-year period, or its subsisting lease period if longer, was due to end before 31 March 2030, captive leases were deemed extended up to that date; non-captive leases received a shorter transitional extension running only to 31 March 2020. Captive lessees were also given a right of first refusal when the area was subsequently put up for auction, a benefit that non-captive lessees did not share under the original 2015 scheme, which the industry's own representations at the time argued was unfair given how close the two transitional dates were.

The 2021 amendment then removed the captive and non-captive distinction for most minerals (atomic minerals remain a separate category), allowing a mine originally licensed as captive to sell a portion of its annual output, generally up to half, once its own end-use requirement is met. This change altered the commercial character of many captive leases going forward but did not by itself reopen the earlier transitional extension dates that had already applied to leases under the 2015 scheme.

The 2021 amendment and what it added

The 2021 amendment, which took effect in March that year, made several further changes relevant to a lessee thinking about renewal or extension. It extended the mandatory auction requirement to composite licences as well as mining leases, introduced the composite licence itself as a combined prospecting-and-mining concession rather than the earlier two separate stages, and removed the general bar on transferring leases that had been granted other than through auction, so that such leases can now be transferred with the State Government's consent. It also did away with certain categories of preferential right that had previously given some applicants priority in grant, meaning pending claims resting on that preferential right became largely academic once the amendment commenced.

On what happens as a lease approaches the end of its term, the Act allows a lessee to apply to the State Government before the lease actually lapses, but it does not provide any route to revive a lease once it has already lapsed. This makes the timing of any application, whether for extension, transfer, or any other relief, a matter of real consequence: an application filed even shortly after the lease has lapsed is generally treated very differently from one filed while the lease was still alive, however strong the underlying case might otherwise be.

What a lessee approaching the end of a term should actually check

The first question is the lease's own grant date relative to 12 January 2015, since that single date decides whether the lease falls within the deemed fifty-year scheme at all or was granted directly under the post-2015 regime with its own fifty-year term and auction-on-expiry structure. The second is whether any renewal application, if one was ever required or filed, was validly made and whether the lease's conditions, royalty payments and statutory compliances were kept current; a lapse in compliance before the relevant cutoff can be fatal to a claim for deemed extension even where the paperwork otherwise looks complete. The third is whether the mineral and the specific transitional date that applied to the lease's category, captive or non-captive as the position stood before 2021, have already passed, in which case the live question is no longer renewal at all but whether the lessee holds a right of first refusal at the auction that follows.

A lessee who assumes a renewal application will simply be processed in the way it would have been under the pre-2015 law is usually disappointed; the entire premise of a discretionary renewal by the State Government, assessed afresh each time, has been replaced for most leases by the deemed extension and auction structure, and the State's role is now largely confined to administering that structure rather than exercising the older renewal discretion.

Disputes that actually reach court

Litigation under Section 8A tends to fall into a small number of recurring patterns. One is a lessee arguing that a renewal rejected or left pending by the State before 2015 should still count as subsisting for deemed-extension purposes, which courts have generally rejected where the rejection or lapse genuinely predates the cutoff. Another is a dispute over whether a particular lease was captive or non-captive as of the relevant date, since that classification decided which transitional extension date applied under the original 2015 scheme. A third, increasingly common pattern concerns the right of first refusal at auction, where an outgoing lessee argues the auction process did not properly preserve that right, or a competing bidder argues it should not apply at all on the facts of that lease.

Because so much turns on dates, whether of grant, of a renewal application, of a rejection order, or of a lapse, the practical starting point in any dispute of this kind is to build a precise timeline from the lease's own file before arguing the legal consequence of any single event in isolation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a mining lease still be renewed in the way it used to be, with repeated renewal applications?

No, not for leases covered by the deemed fifty-year extension under Section 8A, or for leases granted after the 2015 amendment, which are themselves granted for a fifty-year term. On expiry, the area generally goes to auction rather than a further renewal application by the sitting lessee, subject to any right of first refusal the lessee may hold.

What happens if a lease's renewal application had already been rejected before 2015?

Section 8A(9) excludes such leases from the deemed fifty-year extension. Courts have read this strictly, holding that the amendment continues a lease that was still validly subsisting at the cutoff date and does not revive one that had already been rejected, determined or lapsed.

Does the 2021 amendment mean captive and non-captive mines are now treated identically?

For most minerals, the 2021 amendment removed the distinction going forward and allows captive mines to sell part of their output once their own end-use need is met. It did not retroactively reopen the different transitional extension dates that had already applied to captive and non-captive leases under the earlier 2015 scheme.

Can a lessee apply for anything once the mining lease has already lapsed?

The Act allows an application to be made before a lease lapses, but does not provide for reviving a lease after it has lapsed. Timing an application well before expiry, rather than treating the deadline loosely, is accordingly important to preserving any rights the lessee may have.

Can a non-auction mining lease now be sold or transferred to another company?

The 2021 amendment removed the earlier general bar on transferring leases granted otherwise than through auction, so such leases can now be transferred, but only with the concerned State Government's consent; it is not an unrestricted right to assign the lease.
