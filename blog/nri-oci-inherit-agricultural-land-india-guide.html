Can an NRI or OCI inherit or buy agricultural land in India?

An NRI or OCI cannot buy farmland, plantation property or a farmhouse in India, but can inherit it. Here is what FEMA allows, and what happens on resale.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An NRI or an OCI cardholder cannot purchase agricultural land, plantation property or a farmhouse in India. That bar sits in the foreign exchange rules, not in any state tenancy law, and it applies however the money is paid and wherever the buyer lives. The one real exception is inheritance: an NRI or OCI can inherit such land from someone who held it lawfully, without needing fresh permission for that step.

This comes up constantly in families where one sibling moved abroad decades ago and the parents still hold ancestral farmland. The sibling assumes citizenship status alone decides the answer. It does not. What decides it is whether the land is reaching them by purchase, gift or inheritance, and what they plan to do with it afterward. As on this date, the rule has not changed for several years, though the paperwork around it keeps shifting.

Where this rule actually comes from

The restriction is not in the Transfer of Property Act or in any state revenue code. It sits in India's capital account regime under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, now expressed through the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, and consolidated by the Reserve Bank of India in its Master Direction on acquisition and transfer of immovable property in India. That direction draws a sharp line: a person resident outside India who is a citizen of India, or an OCI cardholder, may acquire most immovable property here, but agricultural land, plantation property and farmhouses are carved out of that general permission.

The practical effect is that an NRI cannot simply sign a sale deed for a farm plot the way a resident buyer would, even if the seller is willing and the price is fair. A sub-registrar who is alert to this will refuse to register the sale, or a bank handling the remittance may decline to process the funds once it sees the land classification in the revenue record. The classification that matters is not what the land looks like on the ground, but what it is recorded as in the state's revenue register, which is why two plots that look identical can be treated very differently.

What an NRI or OCI can still do with such land

Inheritance is treated differently from purchase. The rule allows an NRI or OCI to acquire agricultural land, plantation property or a farmhouse by inheritance from a person resident in India who held it lawfully. No separate purchase permission is needed for that step because the ownership change is by operation of succession law, not by a sale contract. In day-to-day terms, if a parent living in India dies owning ancestral farmland and leaves it to a child who has since become an NRI or taken up OCI status, the child's name can go onto the land records as heir.

Gifting works the opposite way to inheritance. A resident relative cannot gift such land to an NRI or OCI while alive, because a gift is treated as an acquisition, not a succession, and the same purchase-style carve-out applies to it. Families sometimes try to get around this by having the resident parent execute a gift deed in favour of an NRI child instead of waiting for a will to operate, and that route runs straight into the same wall that blocks a sale.

Selling inherited farmland later

Having inherited the land lawfully does not give the NRI or OCI owner a free hand to sell it to whoever offers the best price. The land can be transferred onward, but only to a person resident in India who is an Indian citizen. It cannot be sold to another NRI or to an OCI cardholder without separate approval, because that onward sale would recreate the exact acquisition the rule is designed to prevent, just one step removed. This matters in practice because many diaspora buyers with money to spend are themselves NRIs or OCIs, and a seller who is not alert to the rule can end up negotiating a deal that no sub-registrar will complete.

Repatriation of the sale proceeds is also constrained. Even where the NRI or OCI owner lawfully inherited the land and lawfully sold it to an eligible resident buyer, taking the full proceeds out of India is not automatic in the way it is for, say, the sale of a flat bought with foreign exchange. Anyone planning this route should check the current repatriation limits with their authorised dealer bank before assuming the money can simply be wired abroad.

Why the land record, not the deed, decides the outcome

Buyers and heirs often look only at the sale deed or the will and miss the revenue record, which is what actually fixes whether land counts as agricultural. In Uttar Pradesh this shows up in the khatauni, in West Bengal in the record of rights maintained under the land reforms machinery, and in Delhi NCR in the authority's own land-use classification for colonies built on what was once farmland. A plot can carry a residential-sounding colony name while the underlying revenue entry still shows it as agricultural, and that entry is what a bank or registering officer checks.

Where a family genuinely wants to convert agricultural land to non-agricultural use before an NRI family member takes it over, that conversion has to happen through the state's own land-use change process, well before anyone tries to register a transfer. Attempting the conversion after the fact, once a dispute has already started over whether an NRI could lawfully hold the land, puts the family in a much weaker position than doing it in the right order.

This is also why a lawyer checking a property for an NRI client should always ask for a current copy of the revenue record rather than relying on an older one the family happens to have. Land classifications do get updated over time, whether through a formal conversion, a government scheme, or a correction of an old entry, and a record pulled even a year or two ago may no longer match what the land office will show today. Relying on a stale copy is one of the more avoidable ways this issue surfaces only after money has already changed hands.

What goes wrong in practice

The most common mistake is treating OCI status as equivalent to full Indian ownership rights. An OCI card gives broad parity with NRIs on property matters, but it does not erase the agricultural land carve-out, and cardholders are sometimes surprised that the same restriction that applies to a foreign passport holder also applies to them. A second common mistake is assuming that once land has passed by inheritance, every later dealing with it is equally free. The inheritance exception covers the one event of acquiring it from the deceased; it does not extend blanket freedom to every later sale, mortgage or further gift.

A third mistake, seen in disputed families, is one heir quietly getting the land mutated into their name while siblings abroad are unaware that an NRI co-heir's share needs separate handling. Mutation records who is shown as the owner for revenue purposes; it does not by itself settle whether that owner's further dealings with the land are permitted under the foreign exchange rules, and ignoring that gap tends to surface only when the next sale is attempted.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an NRI buy a farmhouse in India even if it has a residential-style building on it?

If the revenue record still classifies the land as agricultural or as a farmhouse, the same restriction applies regardless of what structure stands on it. The building's appearance does not override the land classification, and a buyer should check the record of rights, not just the sale brochure, before relying on a builder's description of the property.

Does an NRI need RBI approval to inherit agricultural land from a parent in India?

Inheritance from a person resident in India who held the land lawfully does not need the kind of fresh acquisition permission that a purchase would. The heir's name can be recorded through the normal succession and mutation process, though the local revenue office may still ask for the usual heirship documents regardless of the heir's residency status.

Can two NRI siblings jointly inherit and later divide agricultural land between themselves?

A partition between co-heirs who already own the land by inheritance is generally treated as dividing an existing holding rather than a fresh acquisition, but the point is contested enough in practice that each sibling should get this checked against the current rules and the specific state's partition procedure before relying on it.

Can an NRI lease out inherited agricultural land to a resident farmer?

Leasing arrangements over agricultural land are governed by the state's own tenancy law, and several states restrict how such land can be leased regardless of the owner's residency. An NRI landowner should check the specific state tenancy act that applies to the land, since the rules differ sharply between states and some discourage long leases altogether.
