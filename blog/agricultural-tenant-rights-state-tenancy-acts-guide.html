Tenancy Rights of Agricultural Tenants Under State Tenancy Acts: A Practical Guide

A practical guide for agricultural tenants and landowners on how state tenancy legislation protects cultivating tenants, when tenancy rights arise even without a written lease, and the statutory process for resuming tenanted land.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 29 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice. See our disclaimer

Overview

A practical guide for agricultural tenants and landowners on how state tenancy legislation protects cultivating tenants, when tenancy rights arise even without a written lease, and the statutory process for resuming tenanted land.

What You Need to Know

  • Most Indian states have their own tenancy legislation (variously titled tenancy acts, land reforms acts, or abolition-of-intermediaries acts) that confers specific statutory protection on a person who cultivates another's agricultural land, going well beyond the protection an ordinary contractual tenant would enjoy under general property law
  • A recognised feature across many of these statutes is that continuous, unbroken cultivating possession of agricultural land for a qualifying period can itself give rise to statutory tenancy rights: even where there is no written lease and the arrangement was informally described by the landowner as seasonal or permissive
  • Because of this, a landowner who wishes to allow cultivation only on a genuinely temporary, non-recurring basis should document each season's arrangement separately and avoid an unbroken multi-season pattern of possession by the same cultivator, if statutory tenancy protection is to be avoided
  • Terminating a recognised statutory tenancy is generally not done through an ordinary civil suit for ejectment: most tenancy statutes prescribe a specific resumption procedure, often requiring the landowner to establish a permitted ground for resumption (such as personal cultivation) before the designated revenue authority, not a civil court
  • Tenancy statutes commonly also give a protected tenant certain purchase or first-refusal rights over the land under specified conditions, and rights of succession that can pass the tenancy to the tenant's heirs on death, subject to the statute's own conditions
  • Given how significantly the qualifying period, protections and resumption grounds differ between states, both landowners permitting cultivation and cultivators seeking to establish tenancy rights should identify and work from the specific state statute applicable to the land in question

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a tenancy right arise without a written lease?

Yes, in many states: continuous, unbroken cultivating possession for the statutory qualifying period can itself confer tenancy protection, regardless of the absence of a written agreement.

Can a landowner evict a protected agricultural tenant through an ordinary civil suit?

Generally no: most state tenancy statutes require the landowner to pursue a specific statutory resumption procedure before the designated revenue authority, establishing a permitted ground for resumption.

Do agricultural tenancy rights pass to a tenant's heirs?

Under many state tenancy statutes, yes, subject to the specific conditions and qualifying relationships the statute recognises: this should be checked against the applicable state law.
