Stamp Duty on a Mining Lease: Why It Follows Anticipated Royalty, Not Dead Rent

The Supreme Court says mining lease stamp duty is valued on anticipated royalty, not dead rent. What that costs a lessee and how to budget for it.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A mining company negotiating a limestone or coal lease usually budgets stamp duty off the dead rent figure in the draft deed, the fixed minimum payment due whether or not any mineral comes out of the ground that year. That number is often a fraction of what the state's stamp authority actually demands at registration, and the gap can run into crores on a mid-sized lease.

The Supreme Court confirmed in July 2026 why that gap exists, and why dead rent was never the right reference point to begin with.

Dead Rent and Royalty Are Not the Same Thing

A mining lease typically fixes two separate payment obligations that get conflated by anyone reading the deed quickly. Dead rent is a minimum guaranteed payment the lessee owes regardless of whether any mineral is actually extracted in a given year, meant to stop a lessee sitting on a lease without working it. Royalty is a payment calculated per tonne, or other unit, of mineral actually removed, and once a mine is in full production, royalty income to the state routinely dwarfs dead rent many times over.

The two figures sit under different provisions and schedules, and a lessee is generally liable to pay whichever of the two is higher in a given year, not both cumulatively. The confusion that leads lessees astray is assuming that because dead rent is the only fixed annual number written plainly in the lease, it is also the number stamp authorities will use to value the instrument. That assumption has now been tested, and rejected, by the Supreme Court.

What the Supreme Court Decided in Birla Corporation

In Birla Corporation Ltd. v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2026 INSC 738, decided 23 July 2026), a bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and Augustine George Masih dealt with a limestone mining lease over roughly 56 hectares at Village Birhauli in Satna district. The District Collector had, back in 2004, demanded stamp duty of about Rs 4.32 crore calculated on the anticipated royalty the state expected the lease to generate over its term, rather than on the lease's much lower dead rent figure, a gap the lessee had not budgeted for when the deed was signed.

The lessee argued that duty should follow Section 9A of the MMDR Act and the Mineral Concession Rules, which fix dead rent, and that computing duty on an estimate of future royalty was inconsistent with the main body of Section 26 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899. The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal and upheld the Collector's approach in full, agreeing with the view the Madhya Pradesh High Court had already taken below it, and leaving the lessee liable for the full demand raised more than two decades earlier.

Why Section 26's Proviso Does the Work

The Court's reasoning rests on the proviso to Section 26 of the Stamp Act, which lets an instrument be valued, for stamp purposes, by reference to an estimate where the actual rent or value cannot be ascertained at the time of execution. A mining lease is, at the moment it is signed, exactly that kind of uncertain instrument: nobody can say with precision how many tonnes will actually be extracted over a ten or twenty year term, so the royalty that will actually accrue is unknowable on day one.

The Court treated this uncertainty as the trigger for the proviso, and held that estimating future royalty yield, rather than relying on the comparatively modest fixed dead rent figure, was a legitimate way to value the lease instrument. The Court also rejected the argument that the proviso conflicts with or is overridden by the main part of Section 26, treating the two as operating together, with the Collector carrying out the estimation where the state itself is the lessor.

The Form K Point: Why the Deed's Own Wording Matters

A detail worth attention appears in the judgment's treatment of Form K, the statutory lease deed format under the Mineral Concession Rules. According to the reported headnote, Part IX, Clause 9 of Form K itself specifies that anticipated royalty from the land under lease is the yardstick for valuing the lease for stamp purposes, so once a lessee signs a deed in that standard form, it has effectively agreed to that valuation basis in the document itself, not merely had it imposed externally by the Collector some years later once production figures became clearer.

That matters for any lessee tempted to argue the valuation basis was sprung on them after signing, since courts tend to be unsympathetic to a party disowning the plain wording of a deed it executed with full access to legal advice. If the executed deed uses the standard Form K language, the anticipated-royalty basis was written into the instrument from the start, and a challenge built on surprise, rather than on the arithmetic of the estimate itself, is unlikely to get very far in front of a Collector or a court.

Budgeting Before You Sign, Not After

The practical lesson for a company negotiating a new mining lease, or a lender financing one, is to get an estimate of likely stamp duty from the state's own valuation practice before execution, not after the Collector's demand arrives. Ask the state mining department or a stamp valuation consultant what basis that state currently uses, since several states have their own notifications on anticipated-royalty valuation, and Madhya Pradesh's 1993 notification was the one actually tested and upheld in Birla Corporation.

Build a realistic royalty projection for the lease term into the stamp duty estimate rather than defaulting to the dead rent figure because it is the only fixed number visible in the draft deed. Underestimating this cost by an order of magnitude, as happened here, creates a funding gap that surfaces only at registration, by which point the deal timeline rarely allows for renegotiation, and a lender who has already disbursed against a lower cost estimate ends up exposed to exactly the same shortfall.

Does This Apply Outside Madhya Pradesh?

The Supreme Court's ruling upholds Madhya Pradesh's specific 1993 notification computing mining lease stamp duty on anticipated royalty. It does not, by itself, establish that every state values mining lease instruments the same way, since stamp duty remains a state subject administered through each state's own amendments to the Indian Stamp Act and its own valuation notifications. A lessee negotiating a lease in Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand or elsewhere should check that state's specific rule rather than assume the Madhya Pradesh position transfers automatically to a lease signed under a different state's law.

What does transfer, as a matter of principle the Supreme Court has now endorsed, is that a state is entitled to value an inherently uncertain mining lease instrument by reference to anticipated royalty under the Section 26 proviso, rather than being confined to the dead rent figure merely because that is the easier number to point to. A similar notification in another state is, after this ruling, very unlikely to be struck down as ultra vires on the specific ground Birla Corporation raised and lost before the Supreme Court.

If a Collector's Demand Arrives Years Later

Where a lessee receives a fresh stamp duty demand years after execution, as happened in this case, the first question is whether the demand follows a state notification that was actually in force and applicable at the time the lease was executed, since a lessee cannot be made to pay under a valuation basis introduced after signing. The second is whether the Form K language used in the executed deed itself referenced anticipated royalty, since that removes most of the argument that the valuation was sprung on the lessee.

Challenging such a demand purely on the ground that duty should track dead rent is, after Birla Corporation, a weak position in any state with a similar notification in place. A lessee is better served checking the arithmetic of the Collector's own royalty estimate, whether the projected tonnage or royalty rate used is actually realistic for the lease, than challenging the valuation method in principle.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is dead rent and how does it differ from royalty?

Dead rent is a fixed minimum annual payment due regardless of production. Royalty is a per-unit payment on mineral actually extracted. A lessee typically pays whichever is higher in a given year, and royalty usually exceeds dead rent once a mine is in full operation.

Does the Birla Corporation ruling apply to all minerals or only limestone?

The case itself concerned a limestone lease, but the Court's reasoning turns on the general uncertainty of royalty yield at the time any mining lease is executed. That logic applies to mineral leases generally, not to limestone specifically, so a coal, iron ore or bauxite lessee faces the same exposure.

Can I challenge a stamp duty demand based on anticipated royalty?

You can challenge the state's specific royalty estimate as unrealistic, or argue that the state's valuation notification did not exist or did not apply when your lease was signed. Arguing that duty must follow dead rent instead is unlikely to succeed where a valid anticipated-royalty notification exists.

Does every state value mining leases on anticipated royalty for stamp duty?

No single uniform rule applies across India. Madhya Pradesh's specific notification was the one tested and upheld in this case. Other states should be checked individually, since each administers its own stamp valuation practice for mining leases under its own amendments to the Stamp Act.

Why does the lease deed's own wording in Form K matter so much?

If the executed deed uses the standard Form K clause referencing anticipated royalty as the valuation yardstick, the lessee has effectively agreed to that basis in the document itself, which weakens any later argument that the valuation method was imposed without consent.
