Renewing a Trademark Registration in India: Deadlines, Fees and What Happens If You Miss Them

How to renew an Indian trademark on Form TM-R under Section 25, the six-month late surcharge window, and the one-year route to restore a removed mark.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An Indian trademark registration lasts ten years from the date of registration, and it does not renew itself. Under Section 25 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999, the registration lapses and the mark can be removed from the Register if the renewal fee is not paid in time, and once that happens the business loses the exclusive statutory right that made the registration worth having in the first place, even though the underlying business and its reputation in the mark continue unaffected.

The renewal process itself is simple on paper, filed on a single form, but the deadlines around it are layered: an in-time window, a late window with a surcharge, and a final restoration window with a much higher cost and a real risk of refusal. As on November 2026, here is how each of those windows works.

The Normal Renewal Window

Section 25(2) read with Rule 57 of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017 allows the registered proprietor to apply for renewal at any time within one year before the date on which the current ten-year registration expires. The application is filed on Form TM-R, and if it is filed and the fee paid within this window, the registration is renewed for a further period of ten years running from the date the previous registration expired, not from the date the renewal application was filed.

Filing early inside this one-year window is worth doing as a matter of practice rather than waiting until the last month. A renewal filed close to the deadline leaves no room for the Registry to raise an objection, a mismatch in the proprietor's recorded name or address, or a discrepancy between the renewal application and the original registration certificate, without the proprietor running out of time to fix it before the mark lapses.

This is where a change of corporate name, a merger, or an assignment of the mark that was never recorded on the Register tends to surface. If the proprietor named on Form TM-R does not match the name on the existing registration, the Registry will usually ask for the assignment or the name-change documents to be filed and recorded first, which takes its own time, so a business that has changed hands or been renamed since the last renewal should start that reconciliation well before the renewal deadline, not alongside it.

The Registrar's Notice, and Why Waiting For It Is a Mistake

Section 25(3) requires the Registrar to send the proprietor notice, in the prescribed manner under Rule 58, before the expiration of the last registration, informing them of the date of expiration and the conditions for renewal. In practice this notice is often sent to the address recorded on the Register, which may be outdated if the business has moved, changed its registered agent, or not updated its address for service, and delivery failures are common enough that courts have had to deal with disputes over whether a particular notice was actually received.

The statutory scheme does not make renewal conditional on the proprietor actually receiving this notice. The obligation to renew runs from the fixed expiry date regardless of whether the Registrar's reminder reaches the proprietor, so a business that treats the notice as its trigger to act, rather than tracking its own renewal calendar independently, risks finding out about a problem only after the in-time window has already closed.

Missing the Deadline: The Six-Month Late Renewal Window

If the one-year in-time window passes without a renewal application, the proviso to Section 25(3) read with Rule 59 gives a further six months from the date of expiration to apply for renewal, on payment of the prescribed renewal fee together with a surcharge. This is still a renewal, not a restoration, and the mark is treated as having been continuously registered provided the late renewal is completed within this six-month grace period.

The cost difference is meaningful. Sources describing current Registry fee practice put the normal e-filing renewal fee at roughly nine thousand rupees per class, against a late renewal figure in the range of thirteen to fourteen thousand rupees per class once the surcharge is added, so the six-month grace period comes at a real but manageable premium over filing on time. A business with a large portfolio of marks approaching expiry should treat this surcharge as the cost of the deadline it missed, not as a sign that the deadline itself has moved.

After Six Months: Restoration of a Removed Mark

Once the six-month late renewal window also lapses without action, the Registrar removes the mark from the Register for non-payment of the renewal fee. Section 25(4) read with Rule 60 then provides a restoration route, allowing an application to restore the mark and renew the registration, filed within one year from the date of expiration of the original registration. This is the outer limit; a 2024 Delhi High Court ruling applied this one-year cut-off against a proprietor whose mark had expired in 2022 and who approached the Registry only after that window had closed, with the Court finding that the statutory grace period under the restoration provision had already lapsed.

Restoration is also discretionary and more expensive than either ordinary or late renewal, with the fee for the restoration route reported at close to double the late-renewal figure per class. The Registrar can refuse restoration, and an advertisement inviting opposition to the restoration may be required in some cases, which means a proprietor relying on the restoration route should not assume the mark will simply reappear on the Register once the fee is paid; it is a discretionary revival, not an automatic one.

What Happens to the Mark's Priority and Earlier Use If It Lapses

A trademark that lapses and is then renewed or restored within the statutory windows above does not lose its original priority date or continuity of registration for most purposes; the Register treats it as continuously registered through the process, which matters for infringement actions and for defeating a later applicant who tried to register a similar mark during the gap. A mark that is removed and never renewed or restored at all is a different matter, since the proprietor then has to rely on passing off and actual use in the market to protect the brand, without the evidentiary and procedural advantages a registration provides, such as the statutory presumption of validity and the ability to use the infringement remedy rather than only passing off.

A third party who spots a competitor's mark lapsing during the gap period, whether through a missed renewal or a removal, sometimes tries to file an identical or deceptively similar mark during that window. Whether that attempt succeeds depends heavily on the facts, including whether the original proprietor's use of the mark continued in the market throughout the gap, so a lapse is a genuine commercial risk even where the proprietor intends to renew eventually.

A Practical Renewal Calendar for a Portfolio of Marks

A business holding more than a handful of registered marks should maintain its own renewal calendar independent of the Registry's notice, built around each mark's actual expiry date rather than its registration certificate date, since the two are not always the same once an earlier renewal has already occurred. Filing inside the one-year in-time window, ideally well before the final few months, avoids both the late-renewal surcharge and the considerably more expensive and uncertain restoration route.

Where a mark has already lapsed, the first step is to establish exactly which window applies, since the fee and the procedure differ sharply between the six-month late renewal route and the one-year restoration route, and the second step is to check the Register for any third-party application filed in the interim that might need to be opposed once the proprietor's own mark is back on the Register.

A foreign company holding an Indian registration through a local subsidiary or agent faces the same deadlines as any domestic proprietor, with the added risk that renewal reminders sent to an Indian address of service may not reach the right person at the parent company if that local arrangement has since changed. Keeping the recorded address of service current, and not just the proprietor's own corporate records, is as much a part of avoiding a missed renewal as tracking the expiry date itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long before expiry can I renew a trademark in India?

Up to one year before the registration's expiry date, under Section 25(2) read with Rule 57 of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017, filed on Form TM-R. Renewing early inside this window avoids both the late-renewal surcharge and any risk of running out of time to fix an objection.

What happens if I miss the renewal deadline?

You get a further six months from the expiry date to renew on payment of the normal fee plus a surcharge, under the proviso to Section 25(3) read with Rule 59. The mark is treated as continuously registered if this late renewal is completed within that six-month window.

Can a trademark be restored after it is removed from the Register?

Yes, within one year from the original expiry date, under Section 25(4) read with Rule 60, on payment of a higher restoration fee. Courts have enforced this one-year outer limit strictly, and restoration is discretionary rather than automatic even within that period.

Do I need to wait for the Registrar's renewal notice before acting?

No. The Registrar is required to send a reminder under Rule 58, but the renewal deadline runs from the fixed expiry date regardless of whether that notice is actually received. Relying on the notice instead of tracking the expiry date independently is a common cause of missed deadlines.

Does a lapsed and later renewed trademark lose its original priority date?

Generally no, if it is renewed or restored within the statutory windows, since the Register treats it as continuously registered through the process. A mark that is never renewed or restored loses that registered status and the proprietor is left relying on passing off and actual market use instead.
