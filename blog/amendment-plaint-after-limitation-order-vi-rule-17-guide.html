Can You Amend Your Plaint After Limitation Has Run? What Order VI Rule 17 Actually Allows

When Indian courts allow a plaint amended after limitation has run on the original claim, and when they refuse, under the Supreme Court's test.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 16 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Litigants discover the problem with their plaint at the worst possible moment, usually midway through trial or at the first appeal stage, when going back to redraft the original suit is no longer an option and filing a fresh one is barred by time. The instinct is to amend. Add a relief, fix a prayer that was drafted too narrowly, or plead a fact that was always true but never spelled out. The opposing side's instinct, just as predictably, is to object that limitation has already run on whatever is being added, so the amendment should be refused outright.

Neither instinct is wrong by itself, which is exactly why this fight recurs in almost every long-running commercial suit. Indian courts have built a fairly workable set of principles over seven decades of litigation on Order VI Rule 17 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, and they turn less on whether limitation has technically expired on the new plea and more on whether the amendment is really a new case in disguise or simply a clearer statement of the one the plaintiff always had.

The basic rule: elaboration is allowed, a new case usually isn't

Order VI Rule 17 lets a court allow either party to amend pleadings at any stage of proceedings in a manner that is just, and it directs that necessary amendments for determining the real controversy between the parties be allowed. The 2002 amendment to the Code added a proviso restricting amendments after the trial has begun, permitting them only where the party shows that despite due diligence the matter could not have been raised earlier. That proviso bites hardest on an amendment application filed late in a suit's life, which is precisely when most limitation objections surface.

The oldest and still-cited authority on the limitation point is Pirgonda Hongonda Patil v. Kalgonda Shidgonda Patil, where the Supreme Court permitted an amendment even though limitation had run on the amended plea, because the amendment only elaborated the existing claim and caused no real prejudice to the defendant. The principle that survives from it is that an amendment which merely restates or sharpens the original cause of action can be allowed after limitation has expired on that restated version, because in substance nothing new is being brought into the suit.

Sampath Kumar: converting the suit itself

Sampath Kumar v. Ayyakannu, (2002) 7 SCC 559, tested the rule at its hardest edge. The plaintiff had filed a suit for permanent injunction and, after being dispossessed during the pendency of that very suit, sought to amend it into a suit for declaration of title and recovery of possession. This was not a cosmetic change. It altered the nature of the relief sought and the cause of action the defendant had to meet, and limitation had arguably run on the possession claim by the time the amendment was moved.

The Supreme Court still allowed the amendment, taking the view that a change in the nature of the relief sought, or the introduction of a cause of action arising after the original suit was filed, does not by itself bar an amendment, particularly where refusing it would force the plaintiff into a second suit over what is really the same underlying dispute about the same property. The Court permitted the amendment subject to costs and the payment of additional court fee, which is the standard way courts balance a plaintiff's need for a fair hearing against a defendant's legitimate complaint about a changed battlefield.

Revajeetu Builders: the structured test courts now actually apply

Revajeetu Builders & Developers v. Narayanaswamy & Sons, (2009) 10 SCC 84, decided by Justices Dalveer Bhandari and Harjit Singh Bedi, is the decision practitioners cite for the working checklist. The Court held that the touchstone is whether the amendment is necessary for deciding the real controversy between the parties, and that amendments should ordinarily be allowed unless they cause the other side an injustice or prejudice that cannot be compensated by costs, change the fundamental character of the suit or defence, or are sought mala fide after inordinate and unexplained delay.

What Revajeetu adds to Sampath Kumar is a clearer statement of what cannot be done by amendment: a plaintiff cannot use Order VI Rule 17 to withdraw an admission already made on record, to set up an entirely new case unrelated to the original pleading, or to resurrect a claim that was already time-barred on the date the original suit was filed, as opposed to a claim that only became time-barred afterward because the suit took years to reach the amendment stage. That distinction, between a plea that was always dead and one that has simply aged during litigation that the plaintiff did not control, is doing most of the real work in how judges rule on these applications today.

Mallavva v. Kalsammanavara Kalamma: the Supreme Court's most recent word

The Supreme Court revisited this exact tension in Mallavva v. Kalsammanavara Kalamma, Civil Appeal No. 14803 of 2024, 2024 INSC 1021, decided on 20 December 2024 by a bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan. The plaintiffs had amended their plaint at the first appellate stage to seek possession in addition to the declaration of title originally pleaded, and the defendants argued the possession relief was now time-barred.

The Court held that a suit seeking both declaration of title and possession is governed by Article 65 of the Limitation Act, 1963, which allows twelve years, rather than the three-year period under Article 58 that applies to a bare declaratory suit, and that the amendment at the appellate stage related back to the same underlying cause of action rather than introducing a fresh one. The ruling is a reminder that the limitation objection to an amendment often cannot be decided in isolation. It depends on correctly identifying which limitation article actually governs the relief being added, and courts citing Mallavva now routinely check that threshold question before even reaching the Revajeetu factors.

What this means when you are drafting the amendment application

Frame the amendment, wherever honestly possible, as clarifying or elaborating facts and reliefs that were always implicit in the original plaint, rather than as introducing a materially new claim, because that framing is what let Pirgonda and Sampath Kumar succeed. Address the delay head-on in the application itself. Courts are far more forgiving of a late amendment where the applicant explains why the need to amend arose only recently, such as a fact coming to light during discovery or an event occurring during the pendency of the suit, than where the application reads like an attempt to patch a drafting mistake that existed from day one.

Expect to pay costs and, where a relief is being added, additional court fee, since this is the standard price of a mid-stream amendment and objecting to it rarely helps. Where trial has already begun, be ready to meet the due diligence threshold under the Order VI Rule 17 proviso directly, with a specific explanation of what could not have been pleaded earlier despite reasonable effort, since a bare assertion of diligence without facts to back it tends not to survive scrutiny.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I add a completely new relief to my suit if limitation has already run on it?

Generally no, if the relief was already time-barred on the date the original suit was filed. Courts distinguish this from a relief that has only become arguably time-barred because the suit itself has run for years, which is treated far more sympathetically, especially where the new relief arises from the same facts already pleaded.

Does amending my suit to add a new relief mean I have to pay fresh court fees?

Usually yes. Where an amendment adds a relief, such as converting an injunction suit into one for declaration and possession, courts routinely allow it only on condition that the applicant pays the additional court fee the new relief attracts, along with costs to the other side for the inconvenience caused.

What happens if I want to amend my pleadings after the trial has already started?

The 2002 proviso to Order VI Rule 17 requires you to show that, despite due diligence, the matter could not have been raised before the trial began. Courts apply this threshold strictly, so the application needs to explain concretely why the point was not and could not have been pleaded earlier.

Can an amendment be refused even if limitation has not actually run on it?

Yes. Revajeetu Builders makes clear that limitation is only one factor. An amendment can still be refused if it would change the fundamental nature of the suit, cause prejudice that costs cannot cure, or appears to have been sought mala fide after an unexplained and inordinate delay.

Which limitation period applies if I want to add a claim for possession to an existing suit for declaration of title?

Mallavva v. Kalsammanavara Kalamma confirms that a suit combining declaration of title with possession is governed by the twelve-year period under Article 65 of the Limitation Act, not the three-year period under Article 58 that applies to a pure declaratory suit, which usually gives more room than parties assume.
