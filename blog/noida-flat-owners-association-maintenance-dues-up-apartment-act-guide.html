Noida Flat Maintenance Disputes: Can the AOA Recover Dues, and What Can Owners Do About Overcharging

How a Noida apartment owners' association can recover maintenance dues under the UP Apartment Act, and what a flat owner can do about overcharging.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A Noida housing society or Association of Allottees cannot simply cut off water or electricity to a flat because maintenance is unpaid, and an individual flat owner cannot simply stop paying because the charges feel inflated; both sides have statutory routes they are supposed to use instead, built mainly around the Uttar Pradesh Apartment (Promotion of Construction, Ownership and Maintenance) Act, 2010. In practice, the Act has a real gap at exactly the point most disputes arise, which forum actually decides a maintenance dispute, and the Allahabad High Court has had to tell the state government, more than once, that this gap needs fixing by notification rather than by litigants guessing which forum to approach.

This guide sets out how an Association of Allottees is meant to be formed and takes over from the builder, what a recovery claim for unpaid dues actually looks like in the absence of a dedicated tribunal, and what an overcharged owner can realistically do. Our guides on A Stalled Builder Project in Noida or Greater Noida: What Homebuyers Can Actually Do and on Red Flags in a Builder-Buyer Agreement cover related ground on builder obligations before and around handover.

RWA, AOA and the deed of declaration: getting the vocabulary right

People in Noida use Resident Welfare Association and Association of Allottees loosely as if they were the same thing, and in many buildings the same set of residents does end up running both, but they are not legally identical. The Association of Allottees, or AOA, is the body the UP Apartment Act, 2010 contemplates as the entity to which the builder executes a deed of declaration and eventually hands over the common areas and facilities, and it is this body, not an informally constituted RWA, that holds the statutory standing to deal with the builder and the Authority on maintenance and common-area questions under the Act.

The deed of declaration is the document that actually creates the apartment as a legal unit distinct from the larger plot, sets out each owner's undivided share in the common areas, and records the builder's obligations on handover. A Noida buyer should check, at the time of purchase, whether a deed of declaration has actually been executed and registered for the project, since an AOA that is trying to enforce maintenance obligations or take over common-area management without this foundational document in place is operating on considerably weaker legal footing than one that has it.

Formation of the AOA and the builder's handover obligation

Read together with the central Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016, the Act's scheme expects the promoter to enable formation of an association once a sufficient proportion of units have been booked, and to then hand over the common areas, facilities and relevant documents to that association within a defined period after the project receives its occupancy certificate. Under Sections 11(4) and 17 of the central RERA Act, the promoter's duty includes conveying the undivided proportionate title in the common areas to the association, not merely allowing residents to use them, and failure to do either, form the association in time or complete the handover after the occupancy certificate, is something a complaint to UP RERA can be built around in addition to, or instead of, action under the state Apartment Act.

Until the handover is properly completed, the builder generally remains responsible for maintaining the common areas and facilities, including paying for their upkeep out of whatever maintenance has been collected for that purpose, and a builder who has handed over physical possession of individual flats while continuing to hold on to common-area control and the maintenance corpus is a frequent source of dispute in Noida projects. Checking exactly what stage the handover is at, builder-controlled, interim facility-management arrangement, or fully transferred to the AOA, is usually the first thing worth establishing before deciding how to pursue a maintenance complaint.

Recovering unpaid maintenance: the Act's gap, and where claims actually go

The UP Apartment Act, 2010 does not set out a dedicated tribunal or summary recovery mechanism for an AOA to recover unpaid maintenance from a defaulting owner in the way some other states' apartment or cooperative society laws do, and the Allahabad High Court has specifically noted this legislative vacuum, holding that a writ petition is not the right route for what are essentially private disputes between an AOA and its members or the builder, and directing the state government to consider setting up a dedicated grievance mechanism. Until or unless that mechanism exists and is notified, an AOA seeking to recover arrears from a defaulting owner is generally left to file an ordinary civil suit for recovery of money, which is slower and more expensive than a summary mechanism would be, but remains available regardless of the gap in the Act.

Where maintenance charges are bundled with services that amount to a service under the Consumer Protection Act, an owner's dispute about deficient service, as opposed to a simple non-payment dispute, may also be pursued before the consumer commission in appropriate cases, though an AOA trying to recover money from a non-paying member is a different situation from an owner complaining about the AOA's service, and the two should not be conflated when deciding which forum to approach. Civil courts in Noida and Gautam Buddh Nagar have in practice continued to entertain and decide straightforward maintenance recovery suits, so the absence of a dedicated tribunal has not left AOAs without any remedy at all, only without the faster, cheaper one that exists elsewhere.

What an owner can do about charges that feel inflated or arbitrary

An owner who believes maintenance charges are being levied unfairly, equally across very differently sized flats rather than in proportion to area, or inflated without a transparent account of what the money is actually spent on, has a few practical options before escalating to litigation. The deed of declaration and the AOA's own bye-laws, where adopted, usually set out how charges are meant to be apportioned, and a demand for accounts, minutes of the body that approved the charge, and a breakdown of expenses is a reasonable first step that can resolve a genuine billing error without a dispute at all.

Where the AOA refuses to provide accounts or persists with a charge an owner believes is arbitrary, the owner's practical choices are broadly the same ones available to the AOA in reverse: a civil suit, given the gap in the state Act's own dispute mechanism, or a consumer complaint where the facts support treating the dispute as one about deficient service rather than a private billing disagreement among co-owners. Refusing to pay anything at all while the dispute is pending is rarely the safer course, since an owner who pays the undisputed base amount while formally contesting only the disputed increase is in a considerably stronger position if the matter does end up before a court.

Builder-controlled facility management before and after handover

A common arrangement in Noida projects is for the builder, or a facility management company the builder has appointed, to continue running maintenance for a period after flats are occupied but before the AOA is formally handed common-area control, and the charges and service standards during this interim period are frequently the subject of complaint. Because this arrangement usually rests on terms set out in the builder-buyer agreement, checking what that agreement actually promised about interim maintenance, its duration, and the basis for charging, is the starting point for any dispute about this phase, and our guide on red flags in a builder-buyer agreement covers the kind of maintenance clauses worth scrutinising before signing in the first place.

Once the deed of declaration is executed and the AOA takes over, continuing to pay a builder-appointed agency that has not actually been authorised by the AOA can itself become a point of confusion, particularly in larger Noida townships with phased handovers across different towers. A clear, dated handover notice from the builder to the AOA, specifying exactly what has been transferred and from what date the AOA's own billing applies, is worth insisting on in writing rather than allowing the transition to happen informally.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my Noida AOA disconnect water or electricity if I do not pay maintenance?

This is a frequent practical dispute, and such action is generally open to challenge where it is not clearly authorised by the bye-laws or the underlying agreements and is used to coerce payment rather than as a last resort after due process. An owner facing this is better placed contesting it if they have been paying the undisputed base charge while disputing only the increase.

What is the difference between an RWA and an AOA in a Noida project?

An RWA is typically a voluntary residents' body, while the Association of Allottees is the entity specifically contemplated under the UP Apartment Act, 2010 to receive the deed of declaration and take over common areas from the builder. The AOA carries the statutory standing for handover and common-area questions, even where the same residents also run an informal RWA.

Can our AOA take the builder to UP RERA for not handing over common areas?

Yes. Sections 11(4) and 17 of the central RERA Act require the promoter to enable formation of the association and convey common areas and relevant documents within the period the Act contemplates after the occupancy certificate, and a complaint to UP RERA can be built around a failure to do either.

Is there a special tribunal in Uttar Pradesh for AOA maintenance disputes?

No dedicated tribunal exists under the UP Apartment Act, 2010 at present, and the Allahabad High Court has specifically flagged this gap and asked the state government to consider a grievance mechanism. Until one is notified, recovery of unpaid maintenance generally proceeds through an ordinary civil suit.

Can maintenance be charged equally per flat instead of by area in a Noida society?

Charging strictly equally regardless of flat size, where the deed of declaration or bye-laws provide for apportionment by area or another defined basis, is open to challenge by an owner of a smaller unit. The specific apportionment basis actually adopted in the project's own documents is what governs, so it needs to be checked rather than assumed.
