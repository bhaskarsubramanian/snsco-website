Does Getting Married Cancel Your Will? What Section 69 Actually Says, and Who It Doesn't Cover

Why marriage automatically revokes a will for some Indian communities under Section 69 but not others, and what the Kerala High Court recently upheld.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 17 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A person executes a will in their twenties, marries years later, and assumes the will simply continues to operate until they get around to writing a new one. For a large share of Indian testators, that assumption is wrong, not because of anything they did, but because of a rule buried in Section 69 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 that revokes a will automatically the moment its maker marries. The rule has nothing to do with the content of the will or any intention to cancel it. It operates by itself, regardless of what the testator actually wanted.

What makes this rule genuinely confusing in practice is that it does not apply to everyone. Whether a client's old will survived their marriage, or quietly stopped having any effect the day the marriage happened, depends entirely on which personal law governs their succession, and the Kerala High Court's recent confirmation that the rule is constitutionally valid means this is not a dated technicality that courts are likely to abandon.

What Section 69 Actually Provides

Section 69 of the Indian Succession Act states that a will is revoked by the marriage of its maker, male or female, with one narrow exception for a will made in exercise of a power of appointment in certain limited circumstances. The revocation happens automatically, by operation of law, the moment the marriage takes place. It requires no act by anyone, no court order, and no awareness on the testator's part that this is even happening.

A companion provision, Section 70, works alongside this, setting out the various ways an unprivileged will can be revoked, marriage being one of several listed grounds alongside execution of a new will, a formal declaration of intention to revoke, or destruction of the document. Schedule III of the Act applies Section 70 with a specific modification stating that revocation of a will by marriage does not apply to Hindus, which is where the real complication for Indian testators begins.

Who the Rule Actually Covers

This automatic revocation bites on testators who are Christian, Parsi, or Jewish by religion. For Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains, the proviso to Section 57 of the Act expressly carves them out, stating that marriage does not revoke a will made by a person belonging to these communities. A Hindu testator who marries after making a will can rest easy on this specific point; the will remains valid and effective exactly as it was before the marriage, at least as far as this particular rule is concerned. This single distinction between personal-law communities is the detail most often missed by testators who read a general, non-specialist article about wills and assume the same rule applies to them regardless of their own religion.

Muslim testamentary succession sits outside the Indian Succession Act's general scheme in any event, being governed by personal law principles under which the concept of revocation by marriage does not arise in the same form. The practical takeaway for a lawyer advising a client is that the client's religion, not just the fact that they are getting married, is the first question that needs answering before saying anything about whether an existing will survives.

The Kerala High Court's Recent Ruling

A Division Bench of the Kerala High Court recently upheld the constitutional validity of Section 69 against a challenge arguing that applying the rule only to Christians, Parsis and Jews, while exempting Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains, violated Articles 14 and 15 of the Constitution. The Court rejected that argument, and also held that Section 69 does not conflict with Section 20 of the Act, which deals with a testator's general capacity to make a will.

The Court's underlying reasoning was that the provision does not actually curtail a testator's right to bequeath property, since a person affected by the automatic revocation remains entirely free to execute a fresh will at any time after marrying. On this reading, Section 69 is not really taking away testamentary freedom; it is simply resetting the position and leaving it to the newly married testator to decide afresh how their estate should be distributed, which the Court treated as a meaningfully different thing from an outright restriction on making a will at all.

The Unsettled Question: Does Divorce Do the Same Thing?

Marriage is addressed explicitly in the statute. Divorce is not. Section 69 revokes a will on marriage, but there is no equivalent provision stating what happens, if anything, to a will after its maker divorces. This gap matters practically in a fairly common scenario: a testator makes a will favouring a spouse, the marriage later ends in divorce, and the testator dies without having gotten around to updating the will, leaving an ex-spouse named as a beneficiary in a document that technically still appears to be in force.

Indian law does not currently have a clear, settled answer parallel to what some other jurisdictions provide, where a divorce is treated by statute as if the former spouse had predeceased the testator for the purposes of any gift in the will. Because this remains genuinely unsettled under Indian law, a testator going through a divorce should not assume any legal mechanism will automatically remove an ex-spouse from an existing will, and should treat executing a fresh will, or at minimum a codicil removing the relevant bequest, as something to do promptly rather than something the law will quietly handle on its own.

What a Testator Who Has Just Married Should Actually Do

For a Christian, Parsi or Jewish testator who married after making an earlier will, the practical position is straightforward even if the legal mechanics are not: that earlier will has no effect, and until a new one is executed, the estate will be distributed as though no will exists at all, under the ordinary rules of intestate succession applicable to that testator's personal law. This can produce results the testator never intended, particularly where the earlier will left specific bequests to siblings, parents or charities that intestate succession rules would simply bypass in favour of the surviving spouse and children.

The fix is simple in principle: execute a fresh will after the marriage, restating whatever intentions the testator actually has at that point, rather than relying on the pre-marriage document. A Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain testator does not face this particular problem, but should still treat a marriage, or any other major life event, as a natural occasion to review an existing will rather than assuming that because the document survives the marriage legally, it still reflects what the testator currently wants. For any testator whose family includes members of different religions through inter-faith marriage, it is also worth confirming which personal law actually governs their own succession before relying on either position, since that underlying classification, not simply the fact of the wedding, is what Section 69 and its exceptions actually turn on.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my will automatically become invalid if I get married after making it?

It depends on your religion. Under Section 69 of the Indian Succession Act, marriage automatically revokes a will made by a Christian, Parsi or Jewish testator. For Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains, the proviso to Section 57 expressly exempts them, so the earlier will remains valid after marriage.

Is Section 69's rule on revocation by marriage still valid law?

Yes. The Kerala High Court recently upheld its constitutional validity, rejecting an argument that applying the rule to some communities but not others violated Articles 14 and 15. The Court reasoned that the rule does not curtail testamentary freedom, since a testator remains free to make a new will after marrying.

Does getting divorced cancel my will the way marriage does?

Indian law does not clearly say so. Section 69 addresses marriage specifically, but there is no equivalent statutory provision for divorce, and this remains an unsettled question. A testator going through a divorce should not assume the law will automatically remove an ex-spouse from an existing will.

What happens to my property if my will is revoked by marriage and I don't make a new one?

The estate is distributed as if no will exists, under the ordinary intestate succession rules applicable to your personal law, rather than according to the wishes stated in the revoked document. This can bypass bequests the testator originally intended for people outside the immediate family.
