A Daughter's Share in Her Father's Property Under Hindu Law: What the Supreme Court Has Settled

Whether a Hindu daughter inherits equally with her brothers today, how ancestral and self-acquired property differ, and what a will changes.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

As the law stands today, a Hindu daughter inherits her father's property on the same footing as a son, whether the property is ancestral family property or something the father bought or earned himself, and whether he died with a will or without one. That answer looks simple, but it took two separate lines of Supreme Court decisions, roughly fifteen years apart, to arrive at, and the reasoning differs depending on whether the property in question was ancestral or self-acquired. Getting that distinction right is usually the first thing that matters in a real dispute, because it decides which statute and which line of authority actually governs the claim.

This guide deals only with Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains governed by the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, and only with intestate succession unless a will is specifically discussed. A different and simpler analysis applies where a will exists, and a different statute applies to Muslim, Christian and Parsi families, touched on briefly toward the end.

Ancestral property: the 2005 amendment and Vineeta Sharma

Before 2005, a daughter had no birthright in ancestral, or coparcenary, property; only sons acquired that right by birth, while a daughter's claim was limited to maintenance and a share if a partition actually took place during her father's lifetime. The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 rewrote Section 6 to make a daughter a coparcener by birth in exactly the same way as a son, with the same rights and the same liabilities.

What remained unsettled for years was whether this applied only where the father was still alive on the date the amendment came into force, or whether it reached back to daughters whose fathers had already died. The Supreme Court resolved this in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020) 9 SCC 1, holding that Section 6 as amended is retroactive, meaning it operates from the daughter's birth itself and does not depend on her father surviving until 2005. A daughter's coparcenary right is treated as having always existed by virtue of her birth, so it is not undone by the fact that the father died, or that a suit was already pending, before the amendment was enacted.

This specific question, how far the 2005 amendment reaches into coparcenary shares and partition claims that were already underway, is covered in more depth in our separate guide on daughters' coparcenary rights in a Hindu Undivided Family, which focuses on Section 6 itself rather than the broader inheritance picture addressed here.

Self-acquired property: intestate succession and Arunachala Gounder

Where the property is not ancestral but was earned or purchased by the father in his own right, a different part of the Act applies. On an intestate death, Section 8 lists Class I heirs, which includes sons, daughters, the widow and the mother, all of whom take simultaneously and, among themselves, in equal shares as tenants in common rather than in any order of priority. A daughter's claim to a share of her father's self-acquired property, where he left no will, has not needed the 2005 amendment at all; it flows directly from Section 8 and has applied since the Act was passed in 1956.

The harder question was what happened to a male Hindu's self-acquired property where he died before the 1956 Act even existed. In Arunachala Gounder (Dead) by LRs v. Ponnusamy (2022), the Supreme Court held that such property devolves by inheritance, not by survivorship, so a daughter could inherit it even though the death predated the Act, and even though earlier practice in some communities had treated such property as passing only to male heirs. The ruling drew heavily on the idea that a daughter's exclusion from her father's own earnings, as opposed to joint family property, was never actually justified by the logic that supports excluding her from coparcenary shares, and the Court read the statute accordingly.

Does the date of the father's death actually change anything?

For self-acquired property, the answer after Arunachala Gounder is largely no: inheritance by a daughter is recognised regardless of whether the death occurred before or after 1956, so long as the facts otherwise fit the inheritance analysis rather than a survivorship one. For ancestral property, the position after Vineeta Sharma is that the daughter's coparcenary right exists from her birth, so the date of the father's death does not defeat it either, provided the daughter herself was alive when the 2005 amendment came into force.

What can still matter is whether a partition of the ancestral property was already completed, by a registered deed or by a final court decree, before December 2004. The amendment expressly does not reopen a partition that was validly and finally concluded before that date, so a family that genuinely divided its property years earlier is not automatically exposed to a fresh claim simply because a daughter was excluded at the time. Disputes in practice tend to turn on whether the earlier partition was truly final and properly documented, rather than an informal family arrangement dressed up as one after the fact.

What changes if the father left a will

Everything above assumes intestate succession, meaning no valid will. A Hindu is generally free to will away self-acquired property to anyone, including excluding a daughter, or a son, entirely, and a will that does so is not automatically invalid merely because it treats children unequally. A daughter's statutory share under Section 8 only arises where there is no will, or where the will fails to dispose of the property validly, whether for want of proper execution, lack of testamentary capacity, or because it is successfully challenged on grounds such as fraud or undue influence.

A will generally cannot be used, however, to defeat a coparcener's birthright in ancestral property to the extent that right has already vested, since a person can only will away what is actually theirs to give. A father who tries to will away the whole of a jointly held ancestral property, ignoring his children's existing coparcenary shares, is generally only disposing validly of his own undivided interest in it, not theirs. Challenges to a will on these grounds usually turn on whether the asset was genuinely ancestral or the father's own, which brings the dispute straight back to the same distinction this guide opens with.

A brief comparison with other personal laws

Succession for Muslims in India is not governed by the Hindu Succession Act at all, but by uncodified personal law applied through the courts, under which a daughter typically takes half the share a son takes in the same inheritance, subject to variations between schools of Islamic law and the full set of surviving relatives. Christians, Parsis and Jews are governed by the Indian Succession Act, 1925, under which sons and daughters generally inherit equally, with a surviving spouse also taking a defined share of the estate before the remainder is divided among the children.

These comparisons are stated only in general terms. A family that has converted, that includes an interfaith marriage, or that married under the Special Marriage Act rather than personal law, may find a different statute governing succession entirely, and the governing law in such cases should be confirmed on its own facts rather than assumed from the family's religion at birth.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a father simply write a will excluding his daughter from his property?

For his self-acquired property, generally yes. A Hindu's testamentary freedom over property that is genuinely his own is wide, and unequal treatment of children in a will is not by itself a ground to invalidate it. The daughter's statutory right under Section 8 applies only where there is no valid will or it fails to dispose of the property.

My father died in 1990, before the 2005 amendment. Can I still claim a share of the ancestral property?

Under Vineeta Sharma, yes, provided you were alive when the 2005 amendment came into force, since the coparcenary right is treated as arising from your birth rather than from your father surviving until 2005. This does not revive a partition of that ancestral property that was already validly and finally completed before December 2004.

Does a married daughter lose her right to inherit her father's property?

No. Marriage does not affect a daughter's coparcenary right in ancestral property or her Class I heir status for self-acquired property under Section 8. Both rights are personal to her by birth and are not tied to her marital status or whether she has moved into another family.

What is the difference between ancestral and self-acquired property in this context?

Ancestral property is inherited joint family property that a daughter now takes as a coparcener by birth, under Section 6 as amended. Self-acquired property is what the father earned or purchased himself, which a daughter inherits as a Class I heir under Section 8 if he dies intestate, a right that does not depend on the 2005 amendment at all.

Does this guide apply to Muslim or Christian families?

No. It covers Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains under the Hindu Succession Act. Muslim succession follows separate personal law with different shares, broadly half for a daughter against a son's share, while Christians, Parsis and Jews fall under the Indian Succession Act, 1925, which generally treats sons and daughters equally.
