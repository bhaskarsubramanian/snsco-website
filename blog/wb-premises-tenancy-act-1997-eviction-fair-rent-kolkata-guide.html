Evicting a Tenant in Kolkata Under the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act and Fixing Fair Rent

How a landlord in Kolkata evicts a tenant under the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997: grounds, notice, deposit of rent, and how fair rent is fixed.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A landlord in Kolkata cannot end a tenancy simply by giving notice and expecting the tenant to leave. Once a person qualifies as a tenant under the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997, the landlord has to plead one of the grounds the Act allows, terminate the tenancy by a notice under Section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, and then file a regular eviction suit in the civil court. Fair rent runs on a separate track: either side can apply to the Rent Controller to have it fixed under Sections 17 to 19 of the 1997 Act, and that proceeding does not by itself decide whether the tenant can be evicted.

The 1997 Act replaced the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1956, and today governs most let-out residential and commercial premises in Kolkata and across the state, subject to a list of exclusions in the Act. This is a different scheme from Delhi and large parts of Uttar Pradesh, where our guide on Tenant and Landlord Rights in Delhi and Uttar Pradesh sets out a parallel but distinct structure of rent control and ordinary civil law. West Bengal keeps eviction and fair rent under one statute, and a Kolkata landlord or tenant should not assume the Delhi or UP position carries over.

Which tenancies the Act covers, and which it does not

Protection under the Act extends to any person who holds premises as a tenant, whether under an oral agreement, an expired written lease that has not been renewed, or a tenancy that continues by holding over. It covers residential premises and most commercial premises let out in Kolkata and the rest of West Bengal. A tenant who has paid rent regularly and occupies with the landlord's consent ordinarily gets the Act's protection even without a registered lease deed, because the statute is built around the fact of tenancy rather than the form of the document that created it.

Government premises and a short list of specific categories sit outside the Act, and premises let to certain institutional occupiers may fall outside it too, depending on the terms of the letting. A caretaker, a licensee under a leave-and-licence arrangement, or a person in permissive possession without the character of a tenancy gets no protection under the Act at all, and a landlord dealing with such an occupant can usually proceed by an ordinary suit for possession rather than an eviction suit built around the Act's grounds. Getting this threshold question right at the start decides which procedure applies and how long the case is likely to run.

The grounds a landlord can actually plead

Section 6 of the Act protects a tenant against eviction except on grounds the section itself sets out, so a landlord's suit has to be built around one or more of them. Default in payment of rent after it has fallen due is one ground, though a tenant who deposits the arrears and interest in time under Section 7 can often cure that default before the suit succeeds. Subletting the whole or part of the premises without the landlord's consent, where the tenancy terms do not permit it, is another, and reasonable requirement of the premises by the landlord for personal occupation or that of a dependant is a third, heavily litigated ground.

Other grounds include substantial damage to the premises caused by the tenant, use of the premises in a way that amounts to a nuisance to neighbours or is inconsistent with the purpose for which it was let, and the tenant having acquired alternative suitable accommodation elsewhere. Courts expect a landlord to plead and prove the specific ground relied on with particulars, not a general assertion that the tenant is undesirable. A suit built loosely on several grounds without tying evidence to each tends to fail on whichever ground is weakest, so the sounder approach is to pick the strongest basis and build the pleading and evidence around it.

The notice before the suit

Before an eviction suit under the Act can be filed, the landlord ordinarily has to terminate the tenancy by a notice under Section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, since the 1997 Act layers its own grounds on top of this requirement rather than dispensing with it. For a monthly tenancy the notice period is fifteen days and for other periodic tenancies it runs longer, and the notice has to be in writing, signed, and framed to end the tenancy with effect from the end of a period of the tenancy, not an arbitrary date chosen by the landlord. Courts treat a Section 106 notice as a condition precedent to the eviction suit, so a defective notice, wrong period, wrong date, or served on the wrong person, can sink the whole case before the Act's grounds are even reached.

Section 6(4) of the 1997 Act also deals with what happens once a notice has run its course and the tenancy has in that sense ended but the tenant has not vacated. The tenant does not become a trespasser overnight; the Act continues to regulate the occupation until a decree for eviction is passed and executed. This is why Kolkata eviction litigation often runs for years even where the landlord's case looks straightforward on paper: terminating the tenancy and recovering actual possession are two different things under this Act, and the second follows only after the suit is decided.

Deposit of rent and how a tenant can resist a default plea

A tenant who disputes the rate of rent, or who simply wants to avoid being treated as a defaulter, can deposit the admitted rent with the Rent Controller under Section 7 rather than pay the landlord directly, and courts read the deposit provisions strictly. Three things have to be shown for an application under Section 7(2) to be entertained: a genuine dispute about the rate of rent, deposit of the admitted arrears together with statutory interest, and a specific prayer asking the Controller to determine the rent payable. A tenant who deposits short, or late, or without actually disputing the rent, gets no protection from this provision and can still be found in default in the eviction suit.

The Supreme Court has examined deposits of this kind under Section 7(1) and treated compliance as something a tenant has to get right procedurally, not merely in substance, and Calcutta High Court decisions in the same vein have held that failure to comply with the deposit requirements in a pending suit can result in the tenant's defence being struck off altogether. A landlord facing a tenant who claims the protection of Section 7 should check the deposit record carefully rather than assume the claim is good, since a technical defect there often decides the case faster than arguing the merits of default.

How the Rent Controller fixes fair rent

Either the landlord or the tenant can apply to the Rent Controller to have fair rent fixed under Sections 17 to 19 of the Act, and this is a separate proceeding from the eviction suit even where both run over the same premises at the same time. The Controller looks at factors the Act sets out, broadly comparable rents in the locality, the size and condition of the premises, and any amount the landlord has spent on repairs or improvement, and fixes a figure that becomes the rent payable going forward. A 2002 amendment to Section 17 is understood to have made fixation automatic in certain conditions, and parties relying on that provision should check its exact scope against the current text of the Act and recent Calcutta High Court or Supreme Court decisions rather than assume older commentary still applies without qualification.

Once the Controller fixes fair rent, the order generally takes effect from the point the Act specifies rather than from the date of the original tenancy, so a landlord cannot use a favourable order to claim years of back rent at the new rate. Either party dissatisfied with the Controller's order has a right of revision or appeal within the scheme of the Act, and that further proceeding has to be filed within the period the Act allows, which makes it worth getting advice quickly rather than letting the clock run while deciding whether to challenge the order.

Appeal, execution, and what actually gets a landlord possession

A decree for eviction from a civil court is not the end of the matter if the tenant does not vacate voluntarily. The landlord has to apply for execution of the decree, and a tenant can resist execution on limited grounds, typically that the decree was obtained by fraud, that the tenant was not properly represented, or that circumstances the Act recognises, such as a fresh deposit curing default before execution in certain default-based decrees, apply. Execution in Kolkata civil courts can itself take months even after the decree becomes final, particularly where the tenant raises objections or seeks time, so a landlord should budget for this stage as a distinct piece of litigation rather than a formality.

An appeal against the trial court's decree lies to the appellate court in the ordinary civil hierarchy, and from there a second appeal is possible only on questions of law, not a fresh assessment of facts the trial and first appellate courts have already decided. Recent Calcutta High Court decisions in second appeals under the Act have turned on whether the first appellate court's finding of fact on a ground like reasonable requirement was properly arrived at, rather than on the High Court substituting its own view of the evidence. That tells a party resisting eviction that the first appeal, not a later second appeal, is usually where the real contest over facts has to be won.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a landlord evict a tenant in Kolkata simply because the written lease period has ended?

No. Once a person is a tenant under the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997, the expiry of a fixed lease term is not by itself a ground for eviction. The landlord still has to terminate the tenancy by a valid notice and then prove one of the specific grounds the Act allows, such as default in rent or reasonable requirement, in a civil suit.

How long does an eviction suit under the Act usually take in Kolkata?

There is no fixed timeline, and contested suits involving grounds like reasonable requirement commonly run for several years across the trial court, execution, and any appeal. Cases built on a clear default in rent with a weak Section 7 deposit defence tend to move faster than ones turning on disputed facts about the landlord's need or alleged subletting.

Can a tenant be evicted for subletting part of the premises?

Yes, subletting the premises or part of it without the landlord's consent, where the tenancy does not permit it, is one of the grounds under Section 6. The landlord has to prove an actual subletting arrangement, not mere sharing of space with a family member or a paying-guest arrangement that courts may not treat as subletting at all.

What does a landlord have to show to prove reasonable requirement?

The landlord has to show a genuine, present need for the premises for personal occupation or that of a dependant, supported by facts such as the size of the family and the condition of any alternative accommodation already available. Courts treat this as a question of fact decided on the evidence led in each case, so general assertions without supporting detail rarely succeed.

Does the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act apply to shops and commercial premises as well as homes?

Yes, the Act generally covers commercial premises let out in the same way as residential ones, subject to the exclusions the Act itself lists. A shop tenant gets the same statutory protection against eviction except on the specified grounds, and the same Section 7 deposit route is available where the dispute concerns the rate of rent.
