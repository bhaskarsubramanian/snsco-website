Unhappy With Your Lawyer's Work? Why You Can No Longer File a Consumer Complaint Against Them

What the Supreme Court decided in Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi on whether advocates can be sued for deficient service under consumer law.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

For years, a client unhappy with an advocate's handling of a matter had a route that felt faster and cheaper than a formal professional misconduct complaint: file a consumer complaint alleging deficiency in service, and let a District or State Consumer Commission award compensation. That route is now closed. In May 2024, the Supreme Court held in Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi that advocates cannot be proceeded against under the Consumer Protection Act for alleged deficiency in professional service, overturning a long-standing National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission position that had allowed exactly this kind of complaint.

This changes where a dissatisfied client's grievance against a lawyer actually belongs, and it is worth understanding precisely what the Court decided, because the ruling is narrower in one respect than some commentary has suggested, and it leaves open questions about other professionals that clients and practitioners should both be aware of.

How the dispute reached the Supreme Court

The case arose from a fairly ordinary fee dispute. A client had engaged an advocate to pursue a cheque-dishonour complaint under the Negotiable Instruments Act, and the matter was settled with the opposite party paying an amount to the advocate to be passed on to the client. The advocate allegedly withheld part of that amount, treating it as fees owed, without the client's agreement. The client filed a consumer complaint alleging deficiency in service.

This sat on top of an older, more fundamental dispute in Indian consumer law: in 2007, the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, in a case also involving a D.K. Gandhi, had held that legal services fall within the definition of service under the Consumer Protection Act, meaning advocates could in principle be proceeded against for deficiency in service like any other service provider. That 2007 position stood for roughly a decade and a half, with mixed reception from the legal profession, before the Bar Council and bar associations challenged it in the Supreme Court.

The appeal eventually reached a two-judge bench of Justices Bela M. Trivedi and Pankaj Mithal, which delivered separate, concurring reportable judgments disposing of the matter on 14 May 2024.

What the Court actually decided

The bench held that the legal profession is sui generis, meaning it stands in a category of its own and cannot simply be treated as an ordinary service rendered to a consumer in the way a shop or a contractor renders a service. The judgment drew on several features that distinguish a lawyer's relationship with a client from a typical commercial service relationship, including the degree of control the client retains over the advocate's conduct of the case, the advocate's overriding duty as an officer of the court rather than purely as the client's agent, and the fact that several other jurisdictions similarly exclude legal services from consumer protection regimes.

On this reasoning, the Court held that advocates cannot be made liable under the Consumer Protection Act framework for an alleged deficiency in professional service. It specifically overruled the NCDRC's 2007 decision in D.K. Gandhi v. M. Mathai that had held otherwise, meaning that line of consumer commission rulings against advocates no longer represents good law.

The judgment does not mean an advocate who genuinely withholds a client's money, or who is negligent in a way that causes loss, faces no consequence at all. It means the forum for the client's grievance is not a consumer commission. The client's remedies instead lie through the professional disciplinary machinery under the Advocates Act, 1961, before the State Bar Council and, on appeal, the Bar Council of India, or through an ordinary civil suit for damages for professional negligence or breach of a fiduciary obligation, or, where money has genuinely been misappropriated, a criminal complaint for breach of trust.

Why the Court drew this distinction for lawyers specifically

A central thread in the reasoning is that a client exercises significant, ongoing control over how an advocate conducts a matter, in a way that is different from, say, handing a car to a mechanic and waiting for it back repaired. The client instructs the advocate on strategy, decides whether to settle, decides whether to appeal, and can withdraw instructions at any point. This degree of client control, the Court reasoned, sits uneasily with treating the relationship as a standard service contract where the service provider alone decides how the job gets done.

The Court also placed weight on the advocate's duties to the court itself, which can at times require the advocate to act in a way that does not purely serve the client's immediate wishes, for instance by declining to pursue a frivolous argument or by being candid with the court about adverse authority. A standard consumer-service framework, built around a straightforward buyer-seller relationship, does not easily accommodate a professional whose duties sometimes run contrary to the client's preferences by design.

Commentary on the ruling has pointed out an apparent gap in how the judgment is framed: it refers throughout to advocates, a term with a specific statutory meaning under the Advocates Act, rather than to lawyers more broadly, which raises a question about whether law graduates or legal consultants who are not enrolled advocates, and who provide legal advice without appearing in court, might still fall within the Consumer Protection Act's reach. This is not a question the judgment resolves directly, and it is the kind of boundary a practitioner should flag rather than assume one way or the other.

The open question about other professionals

The judgment does something else that matters for anyone in healthcare or other professional services: it specifically records the Court's doubt about the correctness of a much older three-judge bench ruling, Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha, which had held that medical practitioners and hospitals do fall within the Consumer Protection Act's definition of service and can be proceeded against for deficiency in medical treatment.

Rather than overruling that medical-profession ruling outright, the bench referred the question of its correctness to a larger bench for reconsideration, since a decision of a smaller bench cannot overrule a larger bench's ruling. On 7 November 2024, a three-judge bench led by Justice B.R. Gavai declined to revisit Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha, holding that the reference was unnecessary. That ruling continues to apply, and consumer complaints against doctors and hospitals for medical negligence remain maintainable as they were before the Bar of Indian Lawyers ruling.

This means the current legal position, for now, treats advocates and doctors differently under consumer law, even though both are often described loosely as professionals rendering a personal service with significant client or patient trust involved. A three-judge bench has already confirmed this distinction, declining on 7 November 2024 to revisit the medical-profession ruling, so the difference between the two professions under consumer law should be treated as settled rather than pending.

What a dissatisfied client should actually do now

A client with a genuine grievance against an advocate, whether about withheld money, missed deadlines that caused the case to be lost, or outright negligence, should direct the complaint to the correct forum rather than file in a consumer commission, which is now likely to dismiss the complaint on maintainability grounds following this ruling, wasting time and the complaint fee.

Where the grievance is about professional misconduct, including withholding client funds without justification, a complaint before the State Bar Council under the Advocates Act is the appropriate route, and such proceedings can result in penalties against the advocate ranging from reprimand to suspension or removal from the roll of advocates in serious cases. Where the grievance is about financial loss caused by negligence, a civil suit for damages remains available and is unaffected by this ruling, since that is an ordinary civil remedy, not a consumer law remedy.

Clients should also keep in mind that a professional misconduct complaint before the Bar Council and a civil suit for damages are not mutually exclusive, and pursuing one does not prevent pursuing the other where the facts justify both, though each has its own procedure, timeline and standard of proof that a client should understand before choosing which to pursue first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I still file a complaint against my advocate for keeping money that belonged to me?

Not as a consumer complaint under the Consumer Protection Act, after this ruling. You can file a professional misconduct complaint before the State Bar Council under the Advocates Act, and depending on the facts, you may also have a civil remedy for recovery of the money or a criminal complaint if the facts amount to criminal breach of trust.

Does this ruling mean advocates face no accountability at all for bad work?

No. It only removes consumer commissions as a forum. Advocates remain accountable through Bar Council disciplinary proceedings for professional misconduct, through civil suits for professional negligence causing loss, and through criminal law where facts justify it. The ruling changes the forum, not the existence of accountability.

Does this same exclusion apply to doctors and hospitals under consumer law?

No. The Court expressed doubt about the correctness of the earlier ruling covering medical practitioners and referred that question to a larger bench, but a three-judge bench declined to revisit it on 7 November 2024, holding the reference was unnecessary. Consumer complaints against doctors and hospitals for medical negligence remain maintainable as before.

What if my consumer complaint against an advocate was filed and is still pending from before this ruling?

The ruling would generally be a ground for the advocate to seek dismissal of the pending complaint on maintainability grounds, since the Supreme Court has now held such complaints do not lie under the Consumer Protection Act. You would need to pursue your grievance through a Bar Council complaint or a civil suit instead.
