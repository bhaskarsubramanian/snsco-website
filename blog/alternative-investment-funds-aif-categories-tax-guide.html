Alternative Investment Funds in India: Categories, Accredited Investors and the Tax Pass-Through Rule

What Category I, II and III Alternative Investment Funds actually invest in, who qualifies as an accredited investor, and how each category is taxed.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An Alternative Investment Fund, or AIF, is a privately pooled investment vehicle registered with the Securities and Exchange Board of India that is not a mutual fund, a collective investment scheme or an insurance product, and it is sold mainly to wealthy individuals and institutions rather than to the retail public. As on 20 November 2026, India has three categories of AIF under the SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012, and each is taxed differently depending on which category it falls into.

High-net-worth individuals and family offices are told to put money into an AIF more often each year, usually as part of a pitch for a venture fund, a private credit fund or a long-short equity strategy, and the term gets used loosely to cover all three. The category actually chosen changes the fund's investment restrictions, its minimum tenure, and critically, whether the investor is taxed as if the income were their own or the fund itself pays tax before anything reaches the investor.

What an AIF Is, and What It Is Not

The SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012 were notified on 21 May 2012 and replaced the earlier, narrower SEBI (Venture Capital Funds) Regulations, 1996. An AIF under these regulations is a fund set up in India as a trust, a company, a limited liability partnership or a body corporate, which pools money from investors, Indian or foreign, for investment according to a defined policy for the benefit of its investors, and which is not otherwise covered by SEBI's mutual fund or portfolio management regulations.

This definition is what separates an AIF from a mutual fund or a portfolio management service even though all three involve professionally managed pooled money. A mutual fund is built for retail distribution with daily or periodic liquidity; an AIF is built for a smaller number of sophisticated investors who commit capital for a defined period and generally cannot redeem on demand the way a mutual fund unit holder can.

Category I: What the Regulator Considers Worth Encouraging

Category I covers funds that invest in sectors or activities the government or the regulator considers socially or economically desirable, a list that typically includes venture capital funds backing start-ups, infrastructure funds, social venture funds and funds supporting small and medium enterprises. These funds often get incentives, such as being permitted to call themselves venture capital funds for certain other regulatory purposes, precisely because the activity they fund is treated as a priority.

A Category I fund is required to be close-ended, meaning investors commit capital for a fixed term rather than being able to exit whenever they choose, and the minimum tenure for this category is three years. Leverage and borrowing are tightly restricted, consistent with the idea that these funds are meant to provide patient capital to the sectors they target rather than trade actively. For an investor, this category is usually the least liquid of the three, and anyone signing up should plan on the capital being unavailable for the full committed term rather than treating the three-year figure as a loose estimate.

Category II: the Residual, and Largest, Category

Category II is defined mostly by exclusion: it covers funds that do not fall within Category I or Category III and that do not undertake leverage or borrowing other than what is needed to meet day-to-day operational requirements, within limits the regulations set. Most private equity funds, debt funds and real estate funds that Indian investors encounter fall into this category, because they do not target the specific priority sectors Category I is built around, and they do not run the leveraged trading strategies that define Category III.

Like Category I, a Category II fund must be close-ended with the same minimum three-year tenure. For an investor comparing two funds pitched as similar strategies, the category label is often the quickest way to work out how much operational leverage the manager is actually permitted to use, which matters for risk even before looking at the underlying portfolio. A private equity fund buying stakes in unlisted companies and a real estate fund lending against under-construction projects can both sit in Category II despite having almost nothing else in common, so the category tells an investor about regulatory treatment, not about the strategy itself.

Category III: Where the Complex Strategies Live

Category III covers funds that employ diverse or complex trading strategies, including through the use of leverage, whether through investment in listed or unlisted derivatives or otherwise. Hedge funds and long-short equity funds are the clearest examples, and this is also the category where an investor is most likely to find a strategy that trades frequently rather than holding positions for years.

Category III is the only one of the three that may be either close-ended or open-ended, which lets some of these funds offer periodic redemption windows that Category I and II investors simply do not have. That flexibility on the investment side comes with a tax position that is, for most such funds, considerably less favourable than what applies to the other two categories, which is usually the first thing a prospective investor's own tax adviser will flag. The marketing material for a Category III fund tends to lead with the strategy and the track record, and an investor who does not separately ask about the fund's own trust structure can easily miss how differently its returns will be taxed compared with a Category I or II fund offering a broadly similar headline return.

Who Qualifies as an Accredited Investor

SEBI's accredited investor framework lets individuals, Hindu Undivided Families, family trusts and sole proprietorships qualify in one of a few ways: an annual income of at least two crore rupees, or a net worth of at least seven and a half crore rupees with at least three and three-quarter crore rupees of that held in financial assets, or a mixed test combining a lower income threshold with a lower net-worth threshold. Trusts other than family trusts and body corporates qualify on a net worth of at least fifty crore rupees, or on securities market exposure of at least twenty crore rupees for a trust.

Qualifying as an accredited investor is not just a badge; it is a certificate issued by a SEBI-recognised accreditation agency after verification of income tax returns, a chartered accountant's certificate or audited financial statements, and it unlocks relaxations that an AIF can build into its own structure, including reduced minimum investment thresholds for funds set up specifically for accredited investors.

Large Value Funds and the Point of Accreditation

SEBI has built a separate construct, a Large Value Fund for Accredited Investors, that still sits within whichever of the three categories the fund's strategy otherwise falls into, but with relaxed operational conditions available only because every investor in it has been accredited. A fund amendment in 2025 extended this further by allowing existing AIFs to convert into this status where their investor base already qualifies.

For a high-net-worth investor, the practical reason accreditation matters is this: a manager who wants to run a strategy with fewer of the regulatory guard rails that protect a broader investor base can only do so once the investor base itself has been certified as sophisticated enough to understand what it is buying. An investor who has not gone through accreditation is simply not eligible for some of the structures now being marketed under this route, whatever the return projections in the pitch deck look like.

How Each Category Is Actually Taxed

Category I and Category II AIFs get pass-through tax treatment under Section 115UB of the Income Tax Act. Most categories of income earned by the fund, other than business income, are exempt at the fund level and taxed instead in the hands of the investors at the rate that applies to each investor, which is usually the same rate that would have applied had the investor earned that income directly. Business income, where the fund earns any, is taxed at the fund level instead, and is then exempt when it reaches the investor, so the two income types are kept on separate tracks rather than mixed together.

Category III AIFs sit outside this pass-through scheme entirely, and are typically structured as trusts whose own tax treatment turns on whether the trust is determinate, meaning the beneficiaries and their shares are identifiable, or indeterminate. An indeterminate trust is taxed at the maximum marginal rate on its entire income, which is markedly higher than the capital gains and other rates an individual investor would otherwise pay, while a determinate trust is taxed at the rates applicable to each beneficiary. A High Court ruling reported in August 2025 held that a trust is not automatically treated as indeterminate merely because the original trust deed does not name every investor, reading down an earlier administrative circular that had required investor names to appear in the deed itself; this narrowed, but did not remove, the circumstances in which a Category III fund gets pushed to the higher rate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which AIF category is taxed most favourably for an individual investor?

Category I and Category II AIFs are generally more favourable, because Section 115UB gives them pass-through treatment: most income is taxed in the investor's own hands at the investor's applicable rate rather than at the fund level first. Category III funds sit outside this scheme and, where structured as an indeterminate trust, can be taxed at the maximum marginal rate on the fund's entire income before anything reaches the investor.

What net worth or income do I need to qualify as an accredited investor in India?

For an individual, a Hindu Undivided Family, a family trust or a sole proprietorship, the common routes are an annual income of at least two crore rupees, or a net worth of at least seven and a half crore rupees with a minimum financial-asset component, or a combined lower income and net-worth test. Trusts other than family trusts and body corporates generally need a higher net worth or securities market exposure instead.

Can an AIF offer redemptions whenever an investor wants, like a mutual fund?

Category I and Category II AIFs must be close-ended with a minimum three-year tenure, so investors commit capital for a fixed period rather than redeeming on demand. Category III AIFs may be either close-ended or open-ended, so some funds in that category do offer periodic redemption windows, but this depends on the specific fund's own structure rather than being guaranteed across the category.

Does being an accredited investor let me invest in any AIF with a lower minimum commitment?

Accreditation unlocks relaxations that specific funds, including those structured as a Large Value Fund for Accredited Investors, can build into their own terms, such as a reduced minimum investment. It does not automatically reduce the minimum commitment for every AIF; each fund's offer documents set out what, if anything, changes for an accredited investor in that particular fund.

Is a Category III AIF always taxed at the maximum marginal rate?

No. A Category III fund structured as a determinate trust, where the beneficiaries and their entitlements are identifiable, is generally taxed at the rates applicable to each beneficiary rather than at a flat maximum rate. The higher maximum-marginal-rate treatment applies mainly where the trust is indeterminate, and recent High Court authority has narrowed when a trust will be treated that way merely for omitting investor names from the original deed.
