Court Fees and Valuation in Property Suits: What to Expect Before You File

A practical guide explaining how a suit involving immovable property is valued for court-fee and jurisdiction purposes, why valuation matters, and common mistakes that lead to delay.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice. See our disclaimer

Why Valuation Matters

Every civil suit must state a valuation, both for the purpose of computing the court fee payable and for determining which court has pecuniary jurisdiction to try the suit. In property litigation, valuation is rarely a formality: the correct basis of valuation depends on the specific relief claimed, and getting it wrong can result in a plaint being returned or rejected well after substantial time and cost have already been incurred.

Different Reliefs, Different Valuation Rules

The applicable court-fee legislation (which varies somewhat by state) typically prescribes different valuation methods for different kinds of property suits. A suit for possession of immovable property is generally valued according to the market value of the property. A suit for a mere declaration of title, without a consequential relief such as possession, may in some states be valued at a notional or fixed figure: though where declaration is coupled with possession or injunction, the higher, ad valorem basis typically applies. A partition suit is often valued differently again, sometimes based on the value of the share claimed rather than the entire property's value.

The Temptation and Risk of Undervaluation

Because court fee in most Indian states is calculated as a percentage of the suit's valuation, there is an obvious incentive to value a suit as low as legitimately possible. Courts have repeatedly cautioned against deliberate or artificial undervaluation, particularly valuing a suit for possession at a nominal or outdated figure rather than current market value. Where a court finds a suit has been undervalued, it will typically direct the plaintiff to correct the valuation and pay the deficit court fee, but this correction itself consumes time, and persistent refusal to correct a valuation despite direction can result in rejection of the plaint.

Jurisdiction Follows Valuation

Because pecuniary jurisdiction (which level of court can hear a suit of a given value) is tied to valuation, undervaluing a suit can also mean it has been filed in the wrong court altogether: a defect that, if raised late, can require the matter to be refiled or transferred, causing further delay.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is a suit for possession of property valued for court fee?

Generally at the market value of the property, not an outdated revenue or notional figure: though the specific rules vary by state court-fee legislation.

Can I value my suit low to save on court fee?

Deliberately undervaluing a suit is not advisable: courts routinely direct correction and payment of deficit court fee, and persistent undervaluation can even result in rejection of the plaint, adding delay rather than saving cost.

Does a partition suit have different valuation rules?

Often yes: many state court-fee statutes value a partition suit based on the share being claimed rather than the full value of the property, but this varies and should be checked against the applicable state legislation.

What happens if my suit is found to be undervalued after filing?

The court will typically direct you to correct the valuation and pay the deficit court fee within a specified time; failing to comply can ultimately result in the plaint being rejected.
