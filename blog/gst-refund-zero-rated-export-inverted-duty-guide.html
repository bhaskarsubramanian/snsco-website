GST Refunds for Exporters: Zero-Rated Supply, LUT and the Inverted Duty Structure

How an exporter claims a GST refund in India: the LUT route against the IGST-paid route, the 2-year time limit, the Rule 89(5) formula and recent relief.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An exporter of goods or services from India can get back the GST tied up in its inputs through one of two routes: paying IGST on the export and claiming a refund of that tax under Rule 96 of the CGST Rules, or exporting under a Letter of Undertaking without paying IGST at all and claiming a refund of the accumulated input tax credit under Rule 89. Both routes exist because exports are treated as zero-rated supply under Section 16 of the IGST Act, meaning the export itself is taxed at a zero rate but the exporter is still entitled to the credit on what it bought to make or provide what it sold.

A separate and often confused problem is the inverted duty structure, where a manufacturer's inputs attract a higher GST rate than the finished product it sells, so credit piles up with no output tax to absorb it even though nothing is exported. Section 54(3) of the CGST Act and Rule 89(5) give a formula for recovering that stuck credit too, and the two situations, zero-rated export and inverted duty, are handled by different parts of the same refund application even though exporters sometimes face both problems on the same set of purchases.

Route one: export under LUT without paying IGST

Most exporters use this route because it avoids tying up cash in IGST that then has to be claimed back later. The exporter files a Letter of Undertaking in Form GST RFD-11 on the GST portal, undertaking to export within the time allowed and to pay IGST with interest if that undertaking is not met, and the LUT is generally valid for the financial year in which it is furnished. Exports then go out on invoices marked as made under LUT without IGST, and the accumulated input tax credit relating to those exports is claimed back through a refund application in Form GST RFD-01.

The standard formula for this refund, under Rule 89(4), is the turnover of zero-rated supply multiplied by net input tax credit, divided by the adjusted total turnover for the period, which spreads the available credit across the export turnover for that tax period rather than tracing every rupee of credit to a specific export invoice. Up to ninety per cent of the claim can be sanctioned provisionally in Form GST RFD-04 ahead of final scrutiny, which is meant to get cash back to the exporter quickly rather than waiting for the officer to complete a full verification first.

A current LUT matters more than exporters sometimes realise. If the LUT for the relevant year has lapsed or was never filed, exporting without paying IGST on the strength of an old LUT can put the zero-rated treatment itself in question for that shipment, even though at least one reported view treats the requirement to furnish an LUT before export as a procedural step rather than one that by itself invalidates an otherwise genuine export. Renewing the LUT at the start of each financial year, before the first export of that year goes out, avoids the argument altogether.

Route two: pay IGST, then claim it back

The alternative is to pay IGST on the export invoice as if it were a normal taxable supply, and treat the shipping bill filed with customs as itself amounting to an application for refund once the goods have actually left India and the export general manifest or export report is filed, a mechanism built into Rule 96 precisely to avoid a separate paper-heavy refund filing for exporters who go this way. The system is meant to process this refund largely on its own once customs data confirms the export, without the exporter filing the same kind of standalone application that an LUT exporter has to file.

This route suits an exporter who would rather pay the tax upfront and get a largely automatic refund than deal with LUT renewal and a manual refund application, or one that has a genuine commercial reason to invoice with IGST, for instance where a foreign buyer's import process expects to see tax charged on the face of the invoice. It costs more in working capital while the refund is pending, which is usually the deciding factor against it for a business running on thin margins or frequent shipments.

The two-year clock, and what happens if you miss it

A refund claim under either route has to be filed within two years of the relevant date, which for most export refunds is tied to the date the goods left India or the date payment was received for an export of services, with the exact starting point defined differently depending on the kind of supply. Several practitioner sources are consistent that once that two-year window closes without a claim being filed, the refund is treated as time-barred and is not ordinarily recoverable afterwards, which makes tracking the relevant date for each shipment, rather than assuming the deadline runs from the invoice date, worth building into a business's compliance calendar from the outset.

Because the claim is filed period by period rather than invoice by invoice in most cases, a business that misses a quarter's filing window for one set of exports can sometimes still fold that turnover into a later period's claim as long as the overall two-year limit from the relevant date for those specific exports has not expired. Waiting until the limit is close before checking unreconciled invoices is the most common way exporters lose a claim that would otherwise have been straightforward.

The inverted duty structure: a different formula, and real limits

Where input goods are taxed at a higher rate than the output, Rule 89(5) gives a formula built around the turnover of inverted-rated supply of goods and services, the net input tax credit on inputs, and the tax payable on the inverted-rated supply, with the formula amended by Notification 14/2022-Central Tax with effect from 5 July 2022 for applications filed on or after that date; refunds for earlier periods are worked out under the formula as it stood before that amendment. The formula as framed looks only at credit on input goods for this purpose, not credit on input services, a restriction the Supreme Court in Union of India v. VKC Footsteps India Pvt Ltd (2021 INSC 469, decided 13 September 2021) upheld as valid, rejecting the argument that excluding input services from the Rule 89(5) formula made it inconsistent with Section 54(3) of the Act.

The refund is also not available in every case where output tax is lower than input tax. CBIC's Circular 135/2020 and Circular 173/2022 both treat a case where the inversion arises only because the rate on the very same product was reduced, rather than because the inputs and outputs are genuinely different goods taxed at different rates, as outside the refund's purpose, and a claim resting on that kind of same-product rate cut is likely to be resisted at the assessment stage. A manufacturer in a sector that saw its own output rate cut under the GST rationalisation exercise in September 2025 should check whether its particular inversion falls on the right side of this line before assuming the full accumulated credit is recoverable.

The GST Council's 56th meeting on 3 September 2025 recommended extending the provisional refund mechanism, already used for zero-rated exports, to inverted duty structure refunds as well, sanctioning ninety per cent of the claimed amount on a risk-based basis ahead of full verification, with that mechanism operationalised from 1 November 2025. For a textile or similar manufacturer that has been waiting out a slow final-refund process, this provisional route is worth checking with the jurisdictional officer, since it is being rolled out administratively ahead of the formal legislative amendment catching up with it.

What to have ready before filing either refund

The common paperwork across both routes includes the GST returns for the relevant period, a statement of the relevant invoices in the format the portal requires, a copy of the current LUT where that route is used, bank realisation certificates or foreign inward remittance certificates showing payment was received for an export of services, and shipping bills or export general manifest data for export of goods, since the refund application is cross-checked against what customs has already recorded for the same shipments.

For the inverted duty claim specifically, a working showing input and output rates product by product, and a reconciliation that separates a genuine rate inversion between different goods from a same-product rate cut, makes the difference between a claim sanctioned on the first pass and one that gets stuck in a show cause notice asking the exporter to justify the formula applied. Keeping that working current every tax period, instead of reconstructing it only when a refund is finally filed months later, is the single biggest time saver in this part of GST compliance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an exporter claim a GST refund without an LUT on file?

Not under the LUT route, which legally requires a current Letter of Undertaking in Form GST RFD-11 before exporting without IGST. An exporter who missed renewing the LUT can usually still export by paying IGST on the invoice and claiming it back under Rule 96, or regularise the LUT and argue the lapse was procedural, but the safer course is to renew it before the first export of each financial year.

What is the time limit to claim a GST export refund?

Two years from the relevant date, which for goods is generally tied to the date they left India and for services to the date payment was received, with variations depending on the type of supply. Once that two-year period lapses for a given export, the claim is ordinarily treated as time-barred, so tracking the relevant date for each shipment rather than relying on the invoice date matters.

Why can't I get a refund for inverted duty when my output rate was simply cut?

CBIC's circulars treat an inversion caused only by a rate reduction on the same product, rather than by genuinely different inputs and outputs, as outside the inverted duty refund's purpose. A claim built on that fact pattern is likely to be questioned even though the credit has genuinely accumulated, so the underlying cause of the inversion needs checking before relying on the refund.

Does the inverted duty refund cover credit on input services?

No. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. VKC Footsteps India Pvt Ltd upheld Rule 89(5) as valid even though it confines the refund formula to credit on input goods and leaves out credit on input services, so that portion of accumulated credit on services used in production is not recoverable through this particular refund route.
