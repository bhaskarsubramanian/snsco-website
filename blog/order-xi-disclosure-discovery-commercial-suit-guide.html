Order XI Disclosure in a Commercial Suit: Getting the Other Side's Documents Without a Full Trial

How disclosure and discovery work under Order XI CPC in a commercial suit, and why courts are strict about documents filed late.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 21 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Most commercial disputes turn less on who is right in principle than on who can actually produce the invoice, the email, or the board minute that proves it. Before the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, a party chasing the other side's documents through Order XI of the Code of Civil Procedure dealt with a leisurely, interrogatory-heavy procedure that suited an ordinary civil suit but did very little for a business that needed its dispute resolved in months rather than years. The Act rewrote Order XI specifically for suits before a Commercial Division or a Commercial Court, built around disclosure at the pleading stage rather than discovery requested later, and courts have been notably unforgiving of parties who treat the new timeline casually.

This guide sets out what the rewritten Order XI actually requires, what happens if a party wants to bring in a document it did not disclose on time, and how two recent Delhi High Court rulings on exactly that question illustrate where courts are prepared to bend and where they are not.

Disclosure now starts with the plaint and written statement, not after them

The Commercial Courts Act substituted a new Order XI for suits that fall within its jurisdiction, built around the idea that a party should show its hand on documents at the earliest possible stage rather than holding cards back for a later discovery request. A plaintiff filing a commercial suit must file, along with the plaint, a list of all documents relevant to the case that are in its power, possession, control or custody, together with photocopies of each of them, and a statement, on affidavit, disclosing whether it is aware of or has in its power, possession, control or custody any document that it does not rely on but that might be relevant.

The same obligation falls on the defendant at the written statement stage. A party cannot simply attach a few convenient documents and leave the rest for later. The declaration about documents the party is not relying on, but is still aware of, is meant to flush out inconvenient material early, before either side has had the chance to shape its pleadings around a selective version of the paper trail.

What Order XI does not carry over from the ordinary CPC procedure

The rewritten Order XI for commercial suits keeps discovery by interrogatories, now in a leaner Rule 2 that still requires the court's leave, but deliberately leaves out several other mechanisms that exist under the general Order XI applicable to an ordinary civil suit, including formal premature-discovery applications, orders for inspection in the older style, verified copies, and some of the detailed machinery around using a party's answers to interrogatories at trial. The drafting choice reflects the Act's broader purpose: a commercial suit is meant to move through pleadings, disclosure, admission and denial of documents, and a case-management hearing at a pace an ordinary suit rarely achieves, and a long interrogatories process cuts directly against that purpose.

What survives, and matters most in practice, is the structured process for admitting and denying the documents each side has disclosed, feeding into the case management hearing where the court settles exactly which documents are in dispute and which are treated as admitted for the purposes of trial. Getting the admission and denial exercise right early tends to narrow a commercial trial considerably, since a document the other side has formally admitted rarely needs to be proved again through a witness.

Bringing in a document after the pleading stage: Order XI Rule 1(5)

The provision that generates the most litigation in practice is Order XI Rule 1(5), which allows a party to rely on a document not disclosed along with its pleadings only with the leave of the court, and only on showing a reasonable cause for the delay. This is a meaningfully higher bar than the more permissive approach a court might take under the ordinary, unamended CPC, where late documents were often let in with comparatively little resistance as long as no real prejudice was shown.

The Delhi High Court's decision in Eicore Technologies Private Limited v. Eexpedise Technologies Private Limited, decided on 29 October 2024, illustrates how this plays out. The plaintiffs sought leave under Rule 1(5), read with Section 151 of the CPC, to bring additional documents onto the record in a long-pending commercial suit. The court worked through whether the plaintiffs had shown a genuine, non-tactical reason for not producing the documents earlier, reflecting the now-settled approach that Rule 1(5) applications are decided on the specific explanation offered for the delay, not treated as a routine formality.

A later decision, Tulika Engineers v. Allied Tubes, decided by a single judge on 7 July 2025, concerned an application to bring a bank statement, delivery challans and e-way bills onto the record under the same provision. These cases, taken together, show that Rule 1(5) applications are neither automatically refused nor automatically allowed. What decides them is whether the document could genuinely not have been produced earlier with reasonable diligence, as opposed to a party holding a useful document back and producing it only once the case's weaknesses became apparent.

Why courts treat a late Rule 1(5) application with suspicion

Commercial courts have repeatedly refused Rule 1(5) applications where the circumstances suggest the application is a dilatory tactic rather than a genuine oversight, particularly where the document surfaces only after issues have already been framed, or only after the other side's evidence has exposed a gap in the applicant's own case. A court weighing such an application typically looks at how long after the original pleading deadline the application comes, whether the document was reasonably available to the party at the time of the original disclosure, and whether allowing it in now would force a reopening of evidence already led.

This is a markedly different posture from the generosity ordinary civil courts have historically shown toward late document production, and parties used to the older practice are sometimes caught out by just how firmly a commercial court will hold them to the original disclosure deadline. The safer practice, and the one courts clearly expect, is to treat the disclosure obligation at the pleading stage as the one real opportunity to get every relevant document on record, rather than as a formality to be supplemented later.

Practical steps before you file the plaint or written statement

Given how little room Rule 1(5) leaves for a change of mind later, the real discipline in a commercial suit happens before the first pleading is filed. That means assembling every document that supports the case, and every document the client is aware of that cuts against it, well before the drafting stage, rather than treating document collection as something that can run alongside drafting. It also means pressing the client specifically on documents in the possession of related entities, past employees, or external advisers, since the disclosure obligation extends to documents in a party's power or control even where the party does not physically hold them.

Where a genuinely new document surfaces later, for instance one that comes from a third party through a subsequent subpoena or an unrelated proceeding, the Rule 1(5) application should be filed immediately on discovering it, with an affidavit that explains precisely when and how it was found and why it could not have been produced earlier. Waiting even a few weeks to file that application, while the document sits in a file somewhere, materially weakens the explanation a court will eventually be asked to accept.

How this plays into the case management hearing

Once the disclosure exercise and the admission-and-denial process are done, the commercial court holds a case management hearing to frame the issues the suit will actually be tried on, settle the list of witnesses, and fix a timetable for the remaining stages. A party that has disclosed documents carelessly, or left the admission-and-denial process incomplete, walks into this hearing at a real disadvantage, since the court is entitled to treat an undenied document as admitted and proceed on that footing even if the party now regrets not having formally disputed it.

This is also the stage where the practical value of disclosing inconvenient documents early becomes clear. A document that is disclosed voluntarily, explained in the pleadings, and addressed head-on tends to do far less damage at trial than the same document surfacing for the first time under cross-examination, where it reads as something the party tried to hide. Clients are often reluctant to accept this in the drafting stage, but the Act's disclosure architecture is built precisely to take that choice away from them and force the issue into the open before trial begins.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to disclose documents that hurt my own case under Order XI?

Yes, in substance. The disclosure affidavit required with the plaint or written statement requires a party to state whether it is aware of or holds documents it does not rely on, which is intended to surface material a party might otherwise prefer to keep quiet. Deliberately omitting a known, relevant document from this declaration risks serious consequences later in the suit.

Can I still send interrogatories to the other side in a commercial suit?

Yes, in a leaner form. Rule 2 of the commercial-suit version of Order XI retains discovery by interrogatories, subject to the court's leave, running alongside the mandatory disclosure, inspection, and admission-and-denial exercise managed at the case management hearing.

What do I need to show to bring in a document I missed disclosing earlier?

An application under Order XI Rule 1(5) needs the court's leave and a reasonable explanation for why the document was not disclosed with the original pleading. Courts examine whether the document was genuinely unavailable earlier with reasonable diligence, and are markedly less forgiving of a late application than an ordinary civil court might be.

Does admitting a document under Order XI mean I accept everything it says?

No. Admitting a document generally means accepting that it is what it purports to be, such as a genuine copy of a particular invoice or letter, which usually avoids the need to formally prove it through a witness. It does not, by itself, mean admitting the truth of every statement contained within that document.
