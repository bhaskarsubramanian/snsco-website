Breaking a 50:50 Deadlock: Deadlock Resolution Mechanisms in Indian Joint Venture Agreements

A drafting guide to deadlock resolution mechanisms commonly used in Indian joint venture and shareholders' agreements (escalation clauses, buy-sell (shotgun) provisions, and third-party determination) and how courts and the NCLT have approached them.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 25 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice. See our disclaimer

Why Deadlock Provisions Matter Most in 50:50 Structures

A joint venture structured on an equal 50:50 shareholding, with matching Board representation, has no built-in majority to resolve a disagreement on a matter requiring Board or shareholder approval. Without a contractually agreed deadlock resolution mechanism, an equal-shareholding JV that reaches a genuine impasse on a significant matter has few clean options besides approaching the NCLT under Sections 241-242, which is a slow and often blunt instrument for what may be a commercially resolvable disagreement.

Escalation Clauses

The most common first layer is an escalation clause, requiring the parties' senior management, or a designated executive from each shareholder, to meet and attempt to resolve the disputed matter within a specified period before any further mechanism is triggered. Escalation clauses are low-cost and preserve the relationship, but are only effective where the underlying commercial disagreement is genuinely capable of compromise: they do little for a true strategic deadlock over the JV's fundamental direction.

Buy-Sell (Shotgun) Clauses

A buy-sell, or 'shotgun,' clause allows one shareholder to offer to buy the other's shares at a price it names, with the other shareholder then having the option either to sell at that price or to instead buy the offering shareholder's shares at the same price. Because the offering party does not know in advance whether it will end up buying or selling, the mechanism is designed to incentivise a genuinely fair price. Indian courts and tribunals have generally recognised the enforceability of contractually agreed shotgun clauses, though careful drafting is needed around funding timelines and what happens if the responding party cannot finance a purchase.

Third-Party Determination and Forced Sale to an Outsider

Other mechanisms include referring the specific deadlocked issue (as opposed to the whole JV) to an independent expert or arbitrator for binding determination, or, for deadlocks that go to the JV's continued viability rather than a single decision, providing for a structured sale of the entire company to a third party with the proceeds distributed between the shareholders. Which combination of mechanisms is appropriate depends heavily on the JV's specific commercial context, and is best negotiated and documented at the outset, before a live disagreement makes negotiating a fair mechanism far harder.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do 50:50 joint ventures need a deadlock clause more than other structures?

An equal shareholding and matching Board representation means there is no built-in majority to resolve disagreements on matters requiring approval, so without a contractual mechanism a genuine impasse can leave the JV without a clean path forward.

How does a shotgun (buy-sell) clause work?

One shareholder names a price at which it offers to buy the other's shares; the responding shareholder can either sell at that price or instead buy the offering shareholder's shares at the same price, which incentivises the offering party to name a fair price since it may end up on either side of the trade.

Are shotgun clauses enforceable in India?

Indian courts and tribunals have generally recognised the enforceability of properly drafted, contractually agreed shotgun clauses, though the specific drafting around funding timelines and default consequences matters significantly.

What if the parties cannot agree even with a deadlock clause in place?

Depending on how severe and persistent the deadlock is, an aggrieved shareholder in an appropriate case may still approach the NCLT under Sections 241-242, though this is generally a slower and more disruptive route than resolving the matter through the agreed contractual mechanism.
