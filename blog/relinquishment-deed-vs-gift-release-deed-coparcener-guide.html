Relinquishment Deed, Release Deed or Gift Deed: Which One Actually Fits Your Transaction

Why a relinquishment deed only works between co-owners, when it turns into a gift deed by law, and the registration and challenge rules that apply to each.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 24 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Families giving up a share in jointly held or ancestral property often default to calling the document a relinquishment deed without checking whether it actually qualifies as one. The label on the paper does not decide its legal character; the structure of the transaction does, and getting this wrong can mean paying the wrong stamp duty, or worse, executing a document that a court later treats as something other than what everyone intended.

This guide draws the line between a relinquishment deed, a release deed, and a gift deed, explains the test courts actually apply, and sets out what registration and challenge look like for each.

What a relinquishment deed is for

A relinquishment deed is used when a co-owner of jointly held or ancestral property gives up their undivided share so that it merges into the shares of the remaining co-owners. It applies squarely to situations such as a sibling giving up a share in inherited property so that the family home stays with the sibling who lives in it, or a coparcener releasing a claim in ancestral land in favour of the other coparceners. The defining feature is that the relinquishing person is already a co-owner, and the share passes only to the other existing co-owners, in proportion to their existing shares, unless the deed specifically directs otherwise.

Because the relinquishment enlarges the shares of people who already hold an interest in the property rather than bringing in someone new, it is treated differently from a transfer to an outsider for stamp duty purposes in several states, and some states extend a concessional rate where the parties are close blood relatives. Those concessions are state-specific and change from time to time, so the applicable rate needs to be checked with the Sub-Registrar or the state stamp authority at the time of execution rather than assumed from an old notification.

A relinquishment deed, once validly executed and registered, is treated as irrevocable in the same way a completed gift is, subject to the limited grounds discussed below.

Where it crosses into a gift deed

Delhi High Court authority on this point has shifted and is not settled. A 2020 ruling suggested that a relinquishment made in favour of only one among several co-owners, rather than all the remaining co-owners together, functions as a gift to that one person. A later Division Bench ruling (Ramesh Sharma v. Government of NCT of Delhi, 2025, following Anita Kumar v. Ajay Kumar) moved away from that automatic rule, holding that not every relinquishment in favour of one or more co-owners can be treated as a gift deed for stamp duty purposes; in October 2025 the same court held that sisters relinquishing their shares to their brother, a co-owner, was not a gift under the Stamp Act. The more recent position should be checked against the latest ruling before assuming either rule applies.

This distinction has real consequences. A release deed, confined to existing co-owners, is commonly treated more favourably for stamp duty in several states, particularly where it stays within a family. A gift to a single beneficiary, by contrast, attracts gift-deed stamp duty wherever that concession does not apply, and if the facts show that only one person benefited while the document was styled as a relinquishment, a tax authority or a party later disputing the transaction can press for the gift-deed treatment instead.

The practical lesson is to match the document to the actual intention from the start: if the share is meant to go to every other co-owner in proportion to their existing holding, a relinquishment deed is the right instrument; if it is meant to benefit one person specifically, or anyone who is not already a co-owner, a gift deed is the correct one, and trying to dress up the second as the first invites exactly the scrutiny the Delhi High Court described.

Release deed: broader than relinquishment, sometimes used as a synonym

In practice, release deed and relinquishment deed are often used interchangeably, and in many transactions they describe the same instrument. Where a distinction is drawn, a release deed is treated as the broader category, capable of releasing any legal claim or right in property, not necessarily confined to co-ownership, and usable between parties who are not co-owners of the same property at all, for example where one party releases a claim or encumbrance it holds against another's property.

A relinquishment deed, on that narrower reading, is really a particular kind of release deed limited to the co-ownership scenario described above. For most domestic transactions within a family, the practical difference rarely matters because the document is drafted to do exactly what the parties intend regardless of which label appears at the top; what matters legally is the substance, not the heading.

Neither a release deed nor a relinquishment deed can be used to route property to someone outside the existing ownership or claim relationship in the way a sale deed or gift deed can. If the intention is to bring in a new owner who held no prior interest in the property, a release or relinquishment deed is simply the wrong instrument, and a gift deed or a sale deed, as appropriate, should be used instead.

Registration and stamp duty

A relinquishment, release, or gift deed affecting rights in immovable property falls within the category of non-testamentary instruments that create, transfer, or extinguish an interest in immovable property, and registration is treated as mandatory under the Registration Act, 1908. An unregistered instrument of this kind cannot be relied on as evidence of the transaction and does not by itself affect the property, which means an unregistered relinquishment or gift is of little practical use if the transaction is later challenged.

Authority on this point conflicts. The Supreme Court held in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020) that relinquishment of a coparcener's share in joint family property must be by a registered instrument, and that an unregistered or oral relinquishment cannot be relied on to defeat another coparcener's share. A 2026 Bombay High Court second-appeal ruling took a different view on its specific facts, holding a coparcener could orally relinquish a share without a registered instrument, but that view has not been tested before the Supreme Court. The safer course is always to execute and register a written relinquishment deed rather than rely on an oral arrangement.

Stamp duty on both release and gift deeds is governed by the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 as adapted by each state, and rates differ sharply by state and sometimes by the relationship between the parties or by gender in some states' notifications. A figure quoted for one state, or for a transaction a few years old, should not be assumed to hold for a transaction today; the current schedule needs to be checked against the state's own stamp duty notification before the deed is drafted, since getting the duty wrong can delay or complicate registration.

Can a relinquishment or gift be challenged later

Once validly executed and registered, both a relinquishment deed and a gift deed are generally treated as irrevocable, which is one reason families should not sign either under pressure or without understanding what is being given up permanently. That said, irrevocability is not absolute. A person who relinquished or gifted a share can still challenge the deed on recognised grounds such as fraud, undue influence, coercion, or a fundamental mistake about what was being signed, though any such challenge is subject to the ordinary limitation period for setting aside a document and gets harder to make out the longer the challenger delays.

A separate statutory protection exists for senior citizens: where an elderly parent transfers property by gift on the condition that the recipient will provide maintenance and care, and that condition is not honoured, the transfer can be challenged and set aside under the senior citizens welfare legislation, even though an ordinary gift would otherwise be treated as final. This is a narrower route than a general fraud or undue influence claim and depends on the maintenance condition actually appearing in, or being provable alongside, the deed.

Before signing any relinquishment, release, or gift deed, the person giving up the share is well advised to obtain independent valuation of what is being relinquished and keep a copy of the executed, registered deed; the absence of either makes it noticeably harder to establish fraud or undue influence years later if the family relationship sours.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a relinquishment deed be used to transfer property to someone who is not a co-owner?

No. A relinquishment or release deed works within an existing ownership or claim relationship. To transfer a share to someone with no prior interest in the property, a gift deed or a sale deed is the correct instrument, not a relinquishment deed.

Is a relinquishment deed compulsorily registered?

Yes, where it affects rights in immovable property. It falls within the Registration Act's category of instruments that create, transfer or extinguish an interest in immovable property. An unregistered relinquishment cannot be used as evidence of the transaction and does not affect the property.

How is stamp duty on a relinquishment deed different from a gift deed?

Several states charge a lower rate, sometimes a flat nominal amount, where the relinquishment stays within a family and benefits the other co-owners proportionately, compared to the duty on a gift. Rates and concessions are state-specific and change over time, so they should be confirmed with the local registration authority before drafting.

Can a signed relinquishment deed be cancelled later?

Not easily. Both relinquishment and gift deeds are treated as irrevocable once validly executed and registered, but they can still be challenged on grounds such as fraud, coercion or undue influence, subject to the limitation period for such a challenge, and a gift to an elderly parent's benefit can separately be undone if a maintenance condition attached to it is not honoured.
