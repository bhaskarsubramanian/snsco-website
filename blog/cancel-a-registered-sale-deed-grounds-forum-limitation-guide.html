How to Cancel a Registered Sale Deed: Grounds, Forum and Limitation

A practical guide to cancelling a registered sale deed: the grounds courts accept, the right forum, and the limitation period that applies.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A registered sale deed cannot be undone by simply going back to the Sub-Registrar and asking for the registration to be cancelled. As the law stands today, only a civil court can set aside a registered sale deed, and which procedure applies depends on whether the deed is void or merely voidable. A deed that is void, because it was forged or because the person who signed it had no title to sell, never operated in law at all, and the true owner can simply ignore it and sue for possession on their own title. A deed that is voidable, obtained by fraud, coercion or undue influence against a person who did execute it, continues to operate until a court sets it aside under Section 31 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963. That distinction decides the forum, the limitation period and what has to be pleaded, and getting it wrong at the drafting stage can cost a case before any evidence is led.

This guide sets out when a sale deed can actually be cancelled, who is entitled to bring that suit, what a so-called cancellation deed signed by one party alone is worth, and the two different limitation clocks the Supreme Court has kept apart even in its most recent rulings on the point.

Why You Cannot Simply Cancel a Deed at the Registry

Clients often assume a sale deed can be reversed the same way it was made, by going to the registration office. It cannot. Courts treat the cancellation of a sale deed as a rescission of contract, and rescission under Section 62 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 requires the agreement of both parties. The Madras High Court has held in terms that a Sub-Registrar has no power under the Registration Act, 1908 to recall a completed registration, and that a party who wants a deed undone must sue under Section 31 of the Specific Relief Act rather than approach the registry directly.

A deed of cancellation is itself a document affecting rights in immovable property, so it is compulsorily registrable under Section 17(1)(b) of the Registration Act. Where only one party signs it, Section 32A requires the signature, photograph and fingerprints of the person presenting the document, and registering officers have been directed to refuse a cancellation deed executed by one side alone without the other's consent. Some states have gone further and declared unilateral cancellation deeds of specific instruments, such as development agreements, contrary to public policy and not registrable at all. Where both sides genuinely agree a transaction should be undone, a fresh registered reconveyance is often the safer and cheaper route rather than a one-sided cancellation document.

Cancellation Under Section 31: Who Can Sue, and How

Section 31(1) of the Specific Relief Act lets a person against whom a written instrument is void or voidable file a suit asking the court to adjudge it so and order it delivered up and cancelled. The person suing must also show a reasonable apprehension that the deed, left standing, could cause them serious injury. The relief is discretionary. A court may cancel an instrument in part, leaving the rest standing, and where the instrument was registered, Section 31(2) makes it mandatory for the court to send a copy of its decree to the officer where the document was registered, so the public record reflects the cancellation.

The provision is sometimes described as having been reshaped by the Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018, but that amendment was aimed at the law of specific performance, rewriting Sections 10, 14 and 20 and adding provisions on substituted performance. It left Section 31 itself untouched, and the Supreme Court has separately held the 2018 changes do not apply retrospectively in any event. One point that remains genuinely unsettled is whether a person who was never a party to the deed, such as another co-owner whose share was wrongly included in someone else's sale, can sue under Section 31 at all, or must instead seek only a declaration that the deed does not bind their share. Authority runs both ways, so the safer pleading, where it is available, asks for a declaration in the alternative.

Grounds That Actually Succeed, and Ones That Usually Do Not

Courts set aside sale deeds on a narrow set of grounds: the deed was forged or the signature on it is not genuine, the person signing was a minor or of unsound mind at the time, the deed was obtained by fraud, coercion or undue influence, or there was a total absence of consideration rather than simply an unequal price. The Supreme Court held in Prem Singh v Birbal that where the executant was a minor, the resulting deed is void, not merely voidable, which matters later for limitation.

What usually fails is an attempt to undo a deed because the seller now regrets the price, because of a side understanding that was never put in writing, or because the buyer has not paid later instalments due under a separate arrangement. In that last situation the seller's remedy ordinarily lies in a suit for the unpaid balance and a charge on the property under Section 55(4)(b) of the Transfer of Property Act, not in cancelling a deed that was validly executed and registered. A separate guide on this site looks specifically at the limitation question in fraud cases and is worth reading alongside this one for that narrower point.

Void or Voidable: Why the Label Changes Your Limitation Period

This is the point that decides most of these cases before the facts are even argued. Article 59 of the Limitation Act, 1963 gives three years to cancel or set aside an instrument, running from when the plaintiff first came to know the facts entitling them to have it cancelled. The Supreme Court held in Prem Singh v Birbal, (2006) 5 SCC 353, that Article 59 catches only voidable transactions, those induced by fraud, coercion or undue influence against a person who did execute the document, and does not apply to a transaction that is void from the start.

A more recent decision, Shanti Devi v Jagan Devi (2025 INSC 1105), applied that distinction squarely to a suit for possession founded on a sale deed the plaintiff said was void. The Court held that a person who never executed the deed, and whose title it could never have affected, does not need to sue separately for its cancellation at all; they can sue for possession based on their own title, and that suit runs under the twelve-year period in Article 65, not the three-year period in Article 59. The practical consequence is that a true owner whose property is sold away by someone with no title to sell has far longer to act than a person who signed a deed and later discovers it was procured by fraud.

Choosing the Forum and What the Suit Must Plead

A suit to cancel a sale deed or to recover possession based on title is filed in the civil court within whose jurisdiction the property is situated, under Section 16 of the Code of Civil Procedure, regardless of where the parties themselves live. Pecuniary jurisdiction, which level of civil court can hear a suit of that value, is fixed by each state's own civil courts act and varies, so the same dispute might go before a civil judge, junior division, in one state and directly before a district judge in another depending on the property's value. Court fee in a cancellation suit is typically calculated on the market value of the property or the deed's stated consideration, whichever the state's court fees act prescribes, and an undervalued plaint invites an early objection that can delay the case for months.

The plaint itself has to do more than assert that the deed is wrong. It should plead the specific fraud, forgery or incapacity relied on with dates and particulars, state precisely when the plaintiff came to know of it if limitation is likely to be contested, and ask for cancellation or declaration together with consequential relief such as recovery of possession, mesne profits, or a permanent injunction. Where the buyer appears to be dealing further with the property, an interim injunction restraining alienation or creation of third-party rights pending the suit is usually sought alongside the main plaint rather than later.

What a Buyer Should Do If Their Deed Is Challenged

A buyer sued to cancel the deed they relied on should start by checking whether the registration itself was procedurally regular, since registered documents carry a presumption of validity that the person challenging them has to rebut. Whether a defence of being a bona fide purchaser for value without notice of the defect is even available depends on the ground of challenge; it tends to help against claims of undue influence between family members but offers little protection against a deed that turns out to be forged, since a forged document transfers nothing regardless of how innocent the buyer was.

In practice, these suits rarely move quickly, and where possession has already changed hands and years have passed, a negotiated settlement, whether a partial refund, a fresh conveyance correcting the defect, or an agreed partition of the property, resolves more of these disputes than a judgment does. Budgeting for that reality, rather than assuming a quick win on either side, is the realistic starting point for advising a client in this position.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I cancel a sale deed simply because I regret the price I agreed to?

No. Regret over a low price, or a bad bargain, is not a ground on which an Indian court will cancel a registered sale deed. Courts look for forgery, fraud, coercion, undue influence, incapacity of the person who signed, or a total absence of consideration, not an unequal or unwise price freely agreed to.

Can two parties cancel a sale deed just by mutual agreement?

They can agree to undo the transaction, but the cancellation deed itself must still be registered if it affects rights in immovable property, and most registering officers require both parties' signatures, photographs and fingerprints. Many practitioners prefer a fresh reconveyance deed transferring the property back, since it avoids the uncertainty around purely unilateral cancellation documents.

Does limitation run differently for a void deed than for a voidable one?

Yes. A voidable deed, obtained by fraud or undue influence against the person who signed it, must be challenged within three years of the plaintiff learning the relevant facts, under Article 59 of the Limitation Act. A void deed, such as one forged or executed by someone with no title at all, can be ignored, and a suit for possession based on the true owner's own title has twelve years under Article 65.

Can the Sub-Registrar refuse to register a cancellation deed signed by only one party?

Generally yes. Several High Courts have held that a Sub-Registrar has no power to undo a completed registration on a one-sided request, and that cancelling a sale amounts to rescission of contract requiring both parties' consent. Some states also have specific rules barring registration of unilateral cancellation deeds for particular categories of documents.

Who has to prove what in a suit to cancel a sale deed?

The person seeking cancellation generally has to prove the specific ground relied on, since a registered document is presumed valid until that presumption is rebutted. Bare assertions that a deed is false or fraudulent, without particulars of how and when the fraud occurred, do not meet that burden and are a common reason these suits fail at an early stage.
