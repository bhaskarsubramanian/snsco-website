Escheat in India: What Happens to Property When Someone Dies With No Heirs at All

Escheat lets the government take a deceased person's estate, but only on a genuine total failure of heirs. How the rule actually works in India.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Families assume there is always somebody waiting in the wings when a relative dies, however distant, however estranged. Occasionally there genuinely is nobody: no spouse, no children, no parents, no siblings, no traceable relative under the applicable law of succession, and no will. Indian law has an answer for exactly that situation, a doctrine most people have heard of only as a word on a crossword puzzle, escheat.

It matters more in practice than its obscurity suggests, especially for banks, housing societies and tenants sitting on a deceased person's assets with genuinely nobody in sight to hand them to.

What Escheat Actually Means Under Indian Law

Section 29 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 provides that where an intestate Hindu leaves no heir qualified to succeed under the Act, the property devolves on the government, which takes it subject to all the same obligations and liabilities an actual heir would have taken it subject to, including any debts of the deceased that remain outstanding at the time of death. This is the statutory face of escheat for Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs governed by the Act, and it sits alongside, rather than instead of, the ordinary rules of intestate succession that apply whenever even one qualified heir can be found.

The Constitution separately anchors the concept more generally through Article 296, which vests property that would otherwise be bona vacantia, meaning ownerless goods, in the Union or the relevant state government depending on where the property is situated and what kind of asset it is. The doctrine exists for an unglamorous but real reason: property cannot simply sit in legal limbo with no owner capable of dealing with it, paying its taxes, maintaining it, or being sued over it, so the state becomes an owner of last resort rather than a first resort, stepping in only once every other possibility has genuinely been exhausted.

'Failure of Heirs' Is a High Bar

The phrase that matters in Section 29 is failure of heirs, and the Supreme Court has been clear that this means a complete, total absence of any heir to the person who died intestate, not merely that no heir has come forward or that tracing one proved difficult. In Kutchi Lal Rameshwar Ashram Trust v. Collector, Haridwar, decided by the Supreme Court in 2017, the Court held that Section 29 embodies this principle but does not itself supply a procedural mechanism for adjudicating disputed questions of fact about who the heirs actually are.

A Collector's administrative order asserting failure of heirs cannot substitute for a proper adjudication where the facts are genuinely contested. The burden of proving a complete failure of heirs rests heavily on whoever asserts it, typically the state or an administrative authority trying to take the property, not on anyone who might later turn up claiming to be an heir. A government functionary moving to take possession of a heirless estate without a proper civil adjudication, where the facts are actually disputed, is acting beyond what Section 29 allows.

A Valid, Probated Will Beats Escheat

Escheat is a remedy triggered only by intestacy, meaning it has no application once a person has left a valid will disposing of the property, whatever the state's appetite for the asset. The Supreme Court made this point squarely in State of Rajasthan v. Ajit Singh & Ors., decided in September 2025, where a testator's 1985 will leaving his estate to a trust had already been probated by a competent court decades after his 1987 death, and the state nonetheless tried to challenge the probate on the footing that the property should escheat to it.

The Court held the state had no standing to mount that challenge at all, because the matter was one of testamentary succession and probate had already been granted by a competent court, so Section 29 of the Hindu Succession Act was simply inapplicable. The Court added that merely initiating separate escheat proceedings under a state escheat law does not give the state standing to attack a probate grant, and that only persons who would actually inherit under intestate succession have standing to seek revocation of a probate under Section 263 of the Indian Succession Act.

Escheat Does Not Execute Itself

A recurring misunderstanding is that once it becomes clear nobody is managing a deceased person's estate, ownership simply transfers to the government by operation of law without anything more being required. That is not how Section 29 or Article 296 function in practice. Taking the property in the government's name still generally requires an administrative or revenue process, recording the change in land records, notifying the relevant authority, or obtaining possession where someone else is in occupation, and, where any person disputes the claim of heirlessness, a civil court is the only forum competent to decide that dispute rather than an officer acting on a file.

This is exactly the gap the Kutchi Lal ruling identified: a Collector adjudicating on his own that an intestate left no heirs, where the facts were actually contested between rival claimants, strayed outside the limited role Section 29 gives the state and encroached on territory that belongs to a civil court. A family that finds itself facing such an administrative claim should treat it as an invitation to litigate the heirship question properly, not as a final determination that forecloses the matter.

Does This Apply the Same Way Outside Hindu Law?

Section 29's text binds only intestate successions governed by the Hindu Succession Act. For a Christian, Parsi or person governed by the Indian Succession Act's general intestacy rules, or a Muslim governed by personal law principles of inheritance, the underlying constitutional basis for escheat shifts to Article 296 and general property law principles instead of a dedicated Hindu Succession Act provision, and the precise list of heirs that must be shown to have failed is naturally different, since it tracks each personal law's own scheme rather than the Hindu Succession Act's Class I and Class II categories of heirs.

The core idea, that the state becomes owner only once there is a genuine total failure of any person entitled under the applicable law, carries across every personal law even where the specific statutory hook differs. A family governed by a personal law other than Hindu law should not assume Section 29 itself has any direct bearing on their situation; the right question is what that personal law's own intestacy rules say about who counts as an heir, and only after that list is exhausted does the constitutional escheat principle under Article 296 come into play at all.

What a Bank, Society or Occupant Should Actually Do

An institution holding a deceased customer's deposit, or a society managing a flat whose owner has died with no apparent heir, should resist the temptation either to hand the asset to whoever first shows up claiming to be a relative, or to treat the asset as automatically escheated and available for disposal. The safer course is a formal process: public notice inviting claimants, a reasonable waiting period, verification of whatever documents are produced, and, where a genuine dispute or a total absence of claimants persists, letting the matter go to a civil court or the government's own escheat machinery rather than resolving it informally on the strength of an affidavit alone.

A nominee on a bank account or demat holding is a separate question from legal heirship entirely, and neither a bank's own nominee process nor an informal family consensus substitutes for the adjudication Section 29 actually contemplates where a real dispute exists. A nominee generally receives the asset only as a trustee for the actual legal heirs under most banking and company law practice, so paying out to a nominee does not itself resolve who is actually entitled to keep the money, and an institution that pays out too quickly on thin documentation can find itself facing a second claim later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the government simply take over my relative's property because nobody has claimed it yet?

No. The government can only step in once there is a genuine, total failure of heirs, and where that fact is disputed, a civil court, not a revenue official, has to decide it. Difficulty locating an heir is not the same as there being none at all.

Does a decades-old will that was never challenged protect an estate from escheat?

Yes, if it has been probated or is otherwise valid, since escheat applies only to intestate successions. The Supreme Court has held the state cannot even challenge a probate grant on escheat grounds unless it falls within the narrow class entitled to seek revocation.

What happens if a Christian or Parsi person dies with no will and no traceable heirs?

The same underlying principle applies through Article 296 of the Constitution and that personal law's own intestacy framework, though the specific statutory provision and the list of heirs that must be shown to have failed differ from the Hindu Succession Act's scheme.

If a bank account has no nominee and the holder has died, does the money escheat automatically?

Not automatically. A missing nominee only means the funds go through the legal heir determination process, typically a succession certificate or legal heir certificate, which is entirely distinct from escheat and is resorted to long before any failure-of-heirs question arises.

Who has to prove that there are no heirs at all?

Whoever asserts the failure of heirs, generally the state or an administrative authority seeking to take the property, carries that burden. It does not fall on a potential heir to prove their own existence before the state can be stopped from taking it.
