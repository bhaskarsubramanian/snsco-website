Specific Performance of an Agreement to Sell Immovable Property: A Practical Guide

A practical guide for a buyer or seller involved in an agreement to sell immovable property, explaining when specific performance is available, what 'ready and willing' means in practice, and what to expect procedurally.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

What Specific Performance Means

Specific performance is a remedy under the Specific Relief Act, 1963 by which a court directs a defaulting party to an agreement to sell immovable property to actually complete the transaction — executing and registering the sale deed on receipt of the balance consideration — rather than simply paying damages for breach. Since the 2018 amendments to the Act, specific performance is the rule rather than an exceptional remedy for most categories of contract, making it a significantly more accessible and commonly sought relief than it was previously.

Who Can Sue, and For What

A buyer under an agreement to sell who has been ready and willing to pay the balance consideration, but whose seller has refused or failed to complete the sale, can sue for specific performance, seeking a decree directing the seller to execute the sale deed. A seller, equally, can sue a buyer who has failed to complete the purchase, though sellers more commonly seek to forfeit any earnest money paid and treat the agreement as at an end rather than pursue specific performance against a reluctant buyer.

The Central Requirement: Readiness and Willingness

The single most litigated element in a specific performance suit is whether the plaintiff was 'ready and willing' to perform their own obligations under the agreement throughout the relevant period, not merely at the time of filing the suit. For a buyer, this generally means having the funds available (or being able to arrange them promptly) and having taken reasonable steps to complete the transaction — sending communications, attending for execution, or depositing funds where required. Courts examine the plaintiff's conduct as a whole, but a plaintiff should be prepared to demonstrate this readiness with a clear, contemporaneous, dated record.

Limitation and Timing

A suit for specific performance of a contract must generally be filed within three years from the date fixed for performance, or, if no such date is fixed, from when the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused. This is a comparatively short limitation period, and a party contemplating such a suit should not delay in seeking legal advice once it becomes apparent the other side does not intend to perform.

What the Court Considers

Even where the technical requirements are met, a court retains some discretion in specific performance matters — for instance, examining whether the contract itself is fair, whether there has been undue delay or acquiescence by the plaintiff, and the conduct of both parties generally. Where specific performance is refused, or is not practicable (for instance, if the property has since been sold to a bona fide third party without notice of the earlier agreement), the court may instead award compensation in lieu of, or in addition to, specific performance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get specific performance if I don't have the full balance amount ready right now?

You do not need to have the entire amount in hand at every moment, but you must be able to demonstrate a genuine, continuing ability and willingness to pay — including being ready to deposit the amount in court if directed. A demonstrated inability to arrange the funds at all will significantly weaken a specific performance claim.

What happens if the seller has already sold the property to someone else?

If the subsequent buyer purchased with notice of the earlier agreement, specific performance may still be granted against that subsequent buyer as well. If the subsequent buyer is a genuine bona fide purchaser without notice, specific performance may no longer be practicable, and the original buyer's remedy may be limited to compensation.

How long do I have to file a suit for specific performance?

Generally three years from the date fixed for performance, or from when you have notice that performance is refused, under the Limitation Act, 1963 — do not delay in seeking advice once it is clear the other party will not perform.

Is specific performance guaranteed if I prove the agreement and my readiness to perform?

It is considerably more likely to be granted following the 2018 amendments, but a court retains some residual discretion and will still examine the fairness of the contract and the conduct of both parties before granting the decree.
