Privileged Wills: Why a Soldier, Airman or Mariner Can Make a Will That Would Not Hold Up for Anyone Else

What a privileged will is under Sections 65 and 66 of the Indian Succession Act, who can make one, how it can be oral or handwritten, and when it stops being valid.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Most people who try to write a will without a lawyer run into the same problem: the Indian Succession Act, 1925 insists on two witnesses signing in the testator's presence, and a will that skips this formality is vulnerable to challenge the moment it matters. The Act carves out one narrow exception to this rule for a specific category of person in a specific kind of situation, called a privileged will, where the ordinary formalities of Sections 63 and 64 do not apply at all.

Sections 65 and 66 of the Act let a soldier employed in an expedition or engaged in actual warfare, an airman similarly employed or engaged, or a mariner at sea, dispose of property by a will made in a far more relaxed way than the Act otherwise permits, including, in some circumstances, by spoken word alone. The relaxation exists because the Act's drafters recognised that a person in these specific situations may have no time, no stationery and no two available witnesses to comply with the ordinary rules, yet may urgently need to record how property should pass.

Who actually qualifies as privileged

Section 65 limits the privilege to three categories of person, and only while each is in a specific situation, not merely by virtue of belonging to a service. A soldier qualifies only while employed in an expedition or engaged in actual warfare, so a soldier serving in a peacetime posting or sitting in barracks does not get the privilege merely by holding the rank. An airman is treated the same way, qualifying only while similarly employed or engaged, and a mariner qualifies only while at sea.

The word mariner is read broadly in commentary on the section, covering everyone serving on the vessel from the captain down to the ship's cook, as long as the person is at sea when the will is made, rather than being limited to officers or to any particular rank. The person making a privileged will also has to have completed eighteen years of age, the same minimum age the Act generally requires for testamentary capacity, so the relaxed formalities loosen how the will is made, not who is old enough to make one.

How a privileged will can be made

Section 66 allows a privileged will to be made either in writing or by word of mouth, a choice the Act does not give anyone making an ordinary will. A written privileged will that is wholly in the testator's own handwriting does not need to be signed or attested at all, a considerable departure from the two-witness attestation that Sections 63 and 64 otherwise insist on for every other will made in India.

Where the will is not entirely handwritten, lesser formalities still apply compared with an ordinary will, though the Act expects some signature or completion by the testator where the document is not wholly autographed. An oral will is made simply by the testator declaring an intention in the presence of two witnesses present together at the same time, without any requirement for a written record at the moment it is made, though what the witnesses heard becomes the only evidence of what was actually willed if the matter is later disputed.

The one-month rule for an oral will

An oral privileged will does not last indefinitely once the emergency that created the privilege has passed. The Act provides that such a will lapses one month after the testator ceases to be entitled to make a privileged will in the first place, meaning one month after the soldier returns from the expedition, the airman is no longer so employed, or the mariner comes ashore and is no longer at sea.

This lapse is the reason a privileged will is better understood as an emergency measure than as a substitute for ordinary estate planning. A soldier who survives the expedition and returns to barracks has a month to convert any oral wishes into a properly attested will under Sections 63 and 64, or to make a fresh written privileged will while circumstances allow, failing which the oral declaration stops having legal effect and the estate falls back to whatever will, if any, was made in the ordinary way, or to intestate succession if none exists.

Why these sections were written the way they were

Commentary on the Act describes Sections 65 and 66 as intended specifically to relax the otherwise strict formal requirements that Sections 63 and 64 impose for an ordinary will, rather than to create a separate and permanent category of easier will-making generally. The underlying concern was always the practical one of a person facing death in active warfare, in the air, or at sea, with no realistic access to the stationery, the privacy or the two independent witnesses an ordinary attested will needs.

There is also an older complication worth flagging rather than glossing over: commentary on the Act notes that the Third Schedule historically excluded Hindus from the operation of Sections 65 and 66, leaving Hindu members of the armed forces to be governed by customary, largely unenacted law on this point rather than by these two sections directly. Anyone relying on a privileged will made by a Hindu testator, or drafting one, should treat this as a point requiring specific legal advice on which personal law actually governs the will, rather than assuming Sections 65 and 66 apply without qualification to every testator regardless of personal law.

What this means for a family today

A privileged will is rare in ordinary civilian practice but comes up more often than expected in succession disputes involving defence personnel, merchant navy crew and, increasingly, families with a member deployed abroad in circumstances that could arguably fit the Section 65 categories. When a family produces a handwritten note from a deceased soldier or seafarer that looks informal, the first legal question is not whether it looks like a proper will, but whether the person met the Section 65 conditions, employed in an expedition, engaged in actual warfare, or at sea, at the exact time the document or declaration was made.

A family that includes someone serving in circumstances that might qualify should not treat a privileged will as a long-term substitute for ordinary estate planning. The one-month lapse rule for an oral will, and the narrower protection a written privileged will gets once the emergency passes, both point the same way: use the privilege only for the emergency it was built for, and convert to an ordinary attested will, or revisit the estate plan through probate, a succession certificate or letters of administration as the case requires, once the person is safely back to ordinary life.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can any member of the armed forces make a privileged will at any time?

No. The privilege under Section 65 applies only while a soldier is employed in an expedition or engaged in actual warfare, while an airman is similarly employed or engaged, or while a mariner is at sea. A person in the same service but not in that specific situation, such as a soldier in barracks, must make an ordinary will under Sections 63 and 64.

Does a privileged will need to be signed and witnessed?

A privileged will that is written wholly in the testator's own hand does not need to be signed or attested at all under Section 66. A privileged will can also be made orally, before two witnesses present together, without any written document at the time it is made.

How long does an oral privileged will stay valid?

Section 66 provides that an oral privileged will lapses one month after the testator ceases to be entitled to make a privileged will, for example one month after returning from the expedition or coming ashore. The testator should convert it into an ordinary attested will, or a fresh written privileged will, within that window.

Who counts as a mariner for this purpose?

Commentary on the Act treats the term mariner broadly, covering everyone serving on a vessel from the captain to the cook, provided the person is at sea at the time the will is made. The privilege is tied to being at sea, not to rank or role on board.

Does this privilege apply the same way to a Hindu member of the armed forces?

Commentary on the Act notes a historical exclusion of Hindus from Sections 65 and 66 through the Third Schedule, leaving the point governed by other, largely customary law. This is a genuinely unsettled area, and a family dealing with a Hindu testator's privileged will should get specific advice on which rules actually apply.
