Right of Way Disputes for Linear Infrastructure in India: Pipelines, Transmission Lines and Corridors

A guide to the distinct statutory frameworks governing right of way for pipelines, power transmission lines and highway corridors in India, and the compensation and dispute issues that typically arise.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 April 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Why Linear Infrastructure Needs Its Own Framework

Linear infrastructure, pipelines, transmission lines, optical fibre corridors and similar utilities, crosses hundreds or thousands of individually owned land parcels, making outright acquisition of title impractical. Indian law instead provides several sector-specific statutory rights of user or way, which grant the infrastructure operator a right to lay and maintain the asset without transferring ownership of the underlying land.

Pipelines: The 1962 Act Framework

The Petroleum and Minerals Pipelines (Acquisition of Right of User in Land) Act, 1962 allows the Central Government to acquire a right of user in land for laying pipelines for the transport of petroleum, minerals or related products, with compensation payable to the landowner for the right of user, and separately for any damage caused during the laying of the pipeline. Landowners retain the underlying title and most surface rights, subject to the operator's right to access and maintain the pipeline.

Power Transmission Lines: The Electricity Act Framework

The Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 (as applied to power lines through the Indian Electricity Act framework) and specific provisions under the Electricity Act, 2003 empower transmission licensees to place lines over, under or along any land, subject to payment of compensation for damage sustained, and to the landowner's right to object during a defined notice period before the line is placed. Disputes here commonly concern whether the compensation offered adequately reflects continuing restrictions on land use beneath high-tension corridors, distinct from one-time damage compensation.

Compensation Structuring: Three Distinct Heads

Across these frameworks, right of way compensation for linear infrastructure is best understood as potentially three distinct heads rather than a single payment: (i) compensation for the grant of the right of user or way itself; (ii) compensation for actual damage caused during construction (crop loss, ground disturbance); and (iii) compensation, where applicable, for continuing restriction on the landowner's future use of the affected strip. Not every statutory framework or project provides for all three, and courts have increasingly scrutinised whether the framework used actually captures a continuing-restriction component or leaves the landowner under-compensated for the corridor's ongoing presence.

Common Practical Disputes

Recurring disputes include: landowners disputing the adequacy of standing-crop compensation assessed at the time of laying; disagreement over whether a particular structure or plantation was validly restricted by the corridor's safety clearance requirements; and, for projects spanning multiple states, inconsistency in the compensation rates or assessment methodology applied by different local authorities implementing the same central statutory framework.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a statutory right of way for a pipeline or transmission line transfer ownership of the land?

No: these frameworks grant a right of user or right of way, not ownership; the landowner retains title, subject to the operator's defined rights of access, construction and maintenance.

Can a landowner object to a proposed transmission line route before it is finalised?

Yes: the applicable framework typically provides a notice period during which affected landowners can raise objections to the proposed alignment, before the authority finalises the route and compensation is assessed.

Is compensation for a right of way a one-time payment or can it include a recurring component?

This depends on the specific statutory framework and project structure: some frameworks contemplate only a one-time payment for the right of user plus damage, while landowners in some disputes have successfully argued for additional compensation reflecting a continuing restriction on land use, so the position is not uniform across sectors.
