You Inherited Agricultural Land in India as an NRI: Who Can You Actually Sell It To?

An NRI can inherit agricultural land in India even though they cannot buy it, but FEMA restricts who they can later sell it to.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

It surprises a lot of NRIs to learn that they cannot buy agricultural land, plantation property, or a farmhouse in India, yet can perfectly well end up owning exactly that kind of property if a parent or relative leaves it to them. Inheritance and purchase are treated completely differently under India's foreign exchange rules, and the gap between the two creates a real practical problem for an NRI who inherits a family farm or agricultural plot and later wants to sell it, whether because they live abroad permanently, have no use for the land, or simply need to liquidate the asset.

The short version is that an NRI can hold inherited agricultural land indefinitely, but cannot sell it to just anyone. The buyer has to meet a specific residency and citizenship requirement that does not apply to an ordinary sale of, say, inherited residential or commercial property. Getting this wrong at the sale stage can leave a transaction unable to close, or worse, exposed to challenge after the fact.

Why Inheritance Is Treated Differently From Purchase

The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 and the regulations issued under it restrict a person resident outside India, which includes most NRIs and persons of Indian origin living abroad, from acquiring agricultural land, plantation property, or a farmhouse by purchase. The underlying policy concern is straightforward: Indian agricultural land policy has historically aimed to keep farmland ownership within the hands of those actually resident in, and tied to, the country, and successive governments have been cautious about opening that category of land to open-market purchase by non-residents.

Inheritance, however, is not a purchase, and the regulations carve it out from this restriction. An NRI who inherits agricultural land, whether under a will or through intestate succession from a resident Indian relative, becomes the legal owner of that land just as any other heir would, and does not need Reserve Bank of India permission to hold it in the way that a purchase would typically require. The acquisition happens by operation of law or testamentary disposition, not by the NRI entering the market to buy farmland, and the restriction on purchase was never meant to reach that situation.

Holding the Land Is Easy; Selling It Is Where the Restriction Bites

The regulatory scheme does not leave an NRI free to deal with inherited agricultural land exactly as a resident owner could. Transferring that land onward, by sale or gift, is where the rules bite: they generally restrict such a transfer to a person resident in India who is also a citizen of India. This is a narrower category than it might first sound: a buyer must satisfy both conditions, actual residence in India and Indian citizenship, not just one or the other.

This means an NRI cannot sell inherited agricultural land to another NRI or to a foreign national, even one of Indian origin, regardless of how willing that buyer might be or how good a price is on offer. It also means that practical due diligence on a sale has to include confirming the buyer's residential and citizenship status, not merely checking that a sale price and documentation are in order. A sale concluded with a buyer who does not meet both conditions risks being treated as a transaction outside what FEMA permits, with the attendant regulatory exposure that follows.

What About Persons of Indian Origin?

Persons of Indian origin living abroad occupy broadly the same position as NRIs for this purpose: they may hold agricultural land, plantation property, or a farmhouse acquired by inheritance, but when transferring it onward, the transfer, whether by gift or sale, has to go to a person resident in India. The citizenship and residency requirement on the receiving side functions the same way regardless of whether the person making the transfer is classified as an NRI or a PIO, since the policy concern, keeping agricultural land within resident Indian hands, does not change based on that distinction.

Where an NRI or PIO is uncertain which category they fall into, or where the land in question sits in a border area or other region subject to additional state-level restrictions on agricultural land transactions, it is worth getting this confirmed before entering into any sale negotiation, since state tenancy and land reform laws can add further restrictions on top of the FEMA position.

Tax and Repatriation Once the Sale Goes Through

Selling inherited agricultural land to an eligible buyer triggers capital gains tax under the Income Tax Act in the ordinary way; the fact that the property was agricultural land does not automatically exempt the seller from tax, since the exemption for agricultural land under Indian tax law is itself narrower than many sellers assume, and turns on factors like the land's location relative to municipal limits and its actual use, not simply its classification in revenue records. An NRI selling such land should get this checked specifically rather than assuming agricultural status alone keeps the gain out of tax.

Once tax obligations on the sale are settled, the proceeds can generally be repatriated abroad, subject to the usual foreign exchange compliance that applies to repatriation of sale proceeds by NRIs, including documentation of the source of funds and compliance with whatever overall repatriation limits and reporting the Reserve Bank's framework requires at the time of the transfer. This is a procedural step that should be planned for before the sale closes, not treated as an afterthought, since delays in arranging the authorised dealer bank's documentation can hold up getting the money out even after the sale itself is complete.

Practical Steps Before Putting Inherited Farmland on the Market

An NRI planning to sell inherited agricultural land should start by confirming their own clear title, which usually means obtaining a succession certificate, legal heir certificate, or probate depending on how the land was inherited and whether a will was involved, and getting the land mutated into their name in the relevant revenue records. Buyers and their lenders will expect this before proceeding, and sorting it out after identifying a buyer only slows the transaction down.

Next, the NRI seller should specifically verify, and document, that the intended buyer is both a resident of India and an Indian citizen, since this is the condition the transfer actually turns on. Relying on the buyer's own assurance without some documentary basis for it is a weak position if the transaction is later questioned. Finally, it is worth checking the relevant state's own tenancy and land ceiling laws, since several states impose their own restrictions on who may hold or acquire agricultural land beyond what FEMA separately requires, and a transaction that clears the FEMA test can still run into a state-law obstacle if this step is skipped.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I, as an NRI, inherit my father's agricultural land in India even though I could not have bought it myself?

Yes. The restriction on NRIs acquiring agricultural land, plantation property, or a farmhouse applies to acquisition by purchase. Acquisition by inheritance, whether through a will or intestate succession, is treated differently and is not blocked by that restriction.

Can I sell inherited agricultural land to my NRI sibling who also wants to keep it in the family?

Generally, no. The transfer of such land is restricted to a person who is both resident in India and a citizen of India. An NRI sibling, even a close relative, would not meet that test purely by virtue of the family relationship, since residency and citizenship are assessed independently of who the buyer is related to.

Does selling inherited agricultural land qualify for any special tax exemption because it is agricultural land?

Not automatically. Capital gains tax exemptions tied to agricultural land depend on specific conditions, including the land's location relative to municipal limits and how it has actually been used, not simply its classification as agricultural in revenue records. This should be checked against the specific facts of the land before assuming any exemption applies.

Can I repatriate the sale proceeds abroad once I sell inherited agricultural land to an eligible buyer?

Generally yes, once applicable tax obligations are settled, subject to the usual foreign exchange compliance for repatriation of sale proceeds by NRIs, including documentation through an authorised dealer bank. It is worth arranging this documentation before the sale closes rather than after.

Do these FEMA rules apply the same way to overseas citizens of India as to NRIs?

Persons of Indian origin living abroad, including OCI cardholders, are generally in a broadly similar position to NRIs for this purpose: inherited agricultural land can be held, but an onward transfer by sale or gift has to go to a person resident in India. The specific classification can still matter for other purposes, so it is worth confirming your exact status before relying on this.
