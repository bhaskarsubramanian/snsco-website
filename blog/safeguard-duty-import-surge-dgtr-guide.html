A Flood of Cheap Imports Is Hurting Your Business: How Safeguard Duty Actually Works

Safeguard duty needs no proof of unfair pricing, only a genuine import surge and injury; the 2025 steel case shows how provisional becomes final.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A domestic manufacturer watching an unfamiliar flood of imports eat into its market share often assumes it has to prove the foreign exporters were doing something improper, underpricing their goods or receiving a government subsidy, before it can get any relief. Safeguard duty under Section 8B of the Customs Tariff Act, 1975 does not require that proof at all; it responds to the sheer surge in import volume and the injury that surge is causing, regardless of how fairly any individual exporter priced its goods.

The Directorate General of Trade Remedies' 2025 investigation into certain flat steel products, which moved from a provisional duty through to a multi-year final measure, is a useful illustration of how the safeguard process actually runs from application to a notified duty. This guide uses that case to walk through the mechanics.

What triggers a safeguard investigation

An application is typically brought by, or on behalf of, the domestic industry producing the like product, asking the Directorate General of Trade Remedies to investigate whether imports of that product have increased so as to cause or threaten serious injury to that industry. In the 2025 steel case, the investigation began in December 2024 after an application from the Indian Steel Association, raised on behalf of several of the country's major steel producers.

The Directorate examines an investigation period looking back over recent trade data to establish whether a genuine surge actually occurred, rather than relying on anecdotal impressions of rising imports; in the steel case, the investigation period ran from October 2023 to September 2024, giving the Directorate a defined window of import and injury data to analyse before reaching even a provisional conclusion.

The provisional duty: relief before the investigation finishes

Because waiting for a full investigation to conclude could mean months or years of continuing injury to the domestic industry, the Directorate can recommend, and the Ministry of Finance can notify, a provisional safeguard duty while the fuller investigation continues. In the steel case, the Directorate recommended a provisional duty in March 2025, and the Ministry of Finance notified a twelve percent provisional duty in the Gazette of India on 21 April 2025, running for two hundred days from that notification.

A provisional duty is exactly that: provisional. It can be confirmed, revised, or allowed to lapse once the final investigation is complete, and an importer or exporter affected by a provisional measure retains the ability to make submissions during the remainder of the investigation that could change the final outcome, rather than treating the provisional rate as the end of the matter.

From provisional to final: the steel case's outcome

The Directorate's final findings, issued in August 2025, concluded that the import surge continued to pose a serious threat to the domestic industry and recommended a longer safeguard measure rather than simply confirming the provisional rate as a one-off figure. The recommendation took the form of a multi-year, step-down duty: twelve percent in the first year, falling to eleven and a half percent in the second year, and eleven percent in the third year.

The Ministry of Finance then issued a notification at the end of December 2025 giving effect to this schedule, with the duty set to run at eleven and a half percent from 21 April 2026 and at eleven percent from 21 April 2027 through 20 April 2028. Because the provisional duty had lapsed before this final notification was issued, there was a gap period during which no safeguard duty applied to the product at all, something an importer tracking the case closely would have been positioned to use, and something a domestic industry relying on continuous protection needs to plan around in any future case with a similar procedural gap.

What a foreign exporter or importer can actually argue

Because a safeguard case does not turn on whether any particular exporter behaved unfairly, arguing that a specific company's pricing was reasonable or that it received no subsidy is not a defence to a safeguard measure the way it would be in an anti-dumping or countervailing duty case; those arguments simply do not engage with what a safeguard investigation is examining. What is open to challenge is whether the import data genuinely shows a surge, whether the domestic industry's claimed injury is actually linked to that surge rather than to other causes such as its own cost structure or unrelated market conditions, and whether the proposed duty's scope and duration are proportionate to the injury actually found.

Because the measure, once imposed, applies to all sources of the import rather than to specific exporters found to have acted unfairly, an exporter from a country that had little or nothing to do with the surge in question can still end up paying the same duty as every other exporter of the product, which is both the practical strength of safeguard duty for a domestic industry and the strongest point of grievance for an exporter caught by a measure aimed at a surge it individually did not contribute much to.

Why this differs from anti-dumping and countervailing duty

Anti-dumping duty, under Sections 9A and 9B of the Customs Tariff Act, and countervailing duty, under Section 9, both require the Directorate to find specific unfair conduct, underpricing in the case of dumping, a government subsidy in the case of countervailing duty, by identified exporters, and the resulting duty is generally calculated and applied exporter by exporter or country by country based on that conduct.

Safeguard duty dispenses with the unfairness requirement entirely and asks only whether the volume of imports has increased and whether that increase is hurting the domestic industry, which is why the same Directorate General of Trade Remedies can run all three kinds of investigations using broadly similar fact-finding tools while applying genuinely different legal tests to reach three different kinds of duty.

Practical takeaways from how the steel case actually unfolded

A domestic industry considering a safeguard application should treat the steel case's timeline as a realistic planning benchmark rather than an outlier: roughly a year from an industry body's application to a provisional measure, and several further months from the provisional measure to a final, multi-year duty. A business expecting overnight relief from a sudden competitive shock is likely to be disappointed by how long even an efficiently run safeguard investigation actually takes, though the provisional stage does provide meaningful interim protection well before the final finding.

An importer or foreign exporter should treat the period immediately after a provisional duty is notified as the critical window for making submissions that could affect the final outcome, rather than waiting passively for the final findings to be published; by the time a final measure is notified, as the steel case shows, the Directorate has already reached its conclusions on injury and causation, and the realistic opportunity to shape those conclusions was during the investigation that preceded them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a domestic industry have to prove an exporter was dumping to get safeguard duty?

No. Safeguard duty under Section 8B of the Customs Tariff Act responds only to a genuine surge in import volume causing or threatening serious injury, regardless of whether any exporter underpriced its goods or received a subsidy, which is the core difference from anti-dumping and countervailing duty.

Can a provisional safeguard duty be imposed before the investigation finishes?

Yes. In the 2025 steel case, a twelve percent provisional duty was notified in April 2025 for two hundred days while the Directorate General of Trade Remedies completed its full investigation, with the final, longer-duration measure notified only once that investigation concluded in August 2025.

Does a safeguard duty apply only to the exporters found to have caused the surge?

No. Once imposed, a safeguard duty generally applies to all sources of the imported product rather than just the specific exporters whose conduct was examined, which is different from anti-dumping and countervailing duty, where the duty is usually calculated and applied exporter by exporter.

What can an importer actually argue against a proposed safeguard duty?

Whether the import data genuinely shows a surge, whether the domestic industry's claimed injury is actually linked to that surge rather than other causes, and whether the proposed duty's scope and duration are proportionate, since arguing that a specific exporter's pricing was fair is not a relevant defence in a safeguard case.

Can there be a gap where no safeguard duty applies at all?

Yes, if the provisional duty lapses before the final notification is issued. In the 2025 steel case, the provisional duty's two-hundred-day period ended before the final notification took effect in December 2025, leaving a window with no safeguard duty in force on that product.
