Enforcing a Foreign Arbitral Award in India

How a foreign award holder enforces a New York Convention award in an Indian High Court under Sections 44 to 49, and the narrow Section 48 defences.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company holding an arbitral award against an Indian party does not need to sue on the award in India. Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 lets the award holder apply directly to a High Court to have the award treated as a decree of that court, provided the award was made in a country India recognises under the New York Convention. The Indian party resisting enforcement gets one narrow window to object, under Section 48, and courts have been told repeatedly in the last decade to keep that window narrow.

This guide sets out who can apply, where, on what proof, and what an award debtor can and cannot argue to stop enforcement. It assumes the award was made outside India in a New York Convention country; a different, older regime under the Geneva Convention applies to a shrinking set of countries and is not covered here.

Which awards qualify as a foreign award

Section 44 defines a foreign award for this purpose: it must arise from a written arbitration agreement, be made in the territory of a country that is a party to the New York Convention, and be made in a territory the Central Government has notified by order in the Official Gazette as one to which the Convention applies. India enforces the Convention from 11 October 1960, and only the notified list of reciprocating territories counts; an award from a Convention country that India has not separately notified sits in a grey zone and should be checked against the current gazette notification before anything else is filed.

Roughly fifty countries have been notified so far, Singapore among them, and the list has grown slowly over the decades, China and Mauritius were added only in recent years. The seat of the arbitration, not the nationality of the parties or the place of the underlying contract, decides whether Part II applies, and the gap can catch parties by surprise: the United Arab Emirates, a major trading partner with heavy Indian commercial links, has not been notified as a reciprocating territory under Section 44, even though it is notified for a different purpose under the Code of Civil Procedure for enforcing court decrees. A party choosing a seat for a future contract, or checking whether an award already in hand qualifies, should confirm the current Gazette notification rather than assume a major commercial centre is automatically on the list.

Where to file and what to prove

The application for enforcement goes to a High Court, not a district court; which High Court has jurisdiction depends on where the respondent's assets or the respondent itself is located. Section 47 sets out what the applicant must produce along with the application: the original award or an authenticated copy, the original arbitration agreement or a certified copy, and, if the award is not in English, a certified translation. Courts in India have in practice treated the single application under Sections 47 to 49 as the entire enforcement proceeding; there is no separate suit to first convert the award into a decree and then execute it, a point the Supreme Court settled in Fuerst Day Lawson Ltd. v. Jindal Exports Ltd., (2001) 6 SCC 356, reading the enforcement and execution stages as one composite proceeding rather than two.

Once the court is satisfied the award is enforceable, it is treated as a decree of that court for execution purposes under Section 49, and the usual machinery for executing a money decree, attachment of bank accounts, movable and immovable property, follows. Delay in filing does matter in practice even though Part II does not prescribe its own limitation period; the Limitation Act's residual period for applications is generally treated as the relevant yardstick, and an award holder who sits on an award for years without explanation should expect the delay itself to become a contested issue.

Applicants should also keep the Section 44 gap in mind at this stage: if the seat chosen for the underlying arbitration turns out not to be on India's notified list, Part II is simply unavailable, however straightforward the award itself is, and the enforcement strategy has to shift entirely to whatever remedy exists outside this framework. Checking the notification before filing, not after a High Court raises the question, avoids losing time on an application that cannot succeed as framed.

The Section 48 grounds and how narrowly they are read

Section 48 gives the award debtor a closed, exhaustive list of grounds to resist enforcement: incapacity of a party or an invalid arbitration agreement, lack of proper notice of the arbitrator's appointment or the proceedings, an award that goes beyond the submission to arbitration, an improperly constituted tribunal or procedure, an award not yet binding or set aside at the seat, or a subject matter not capable of settlement by arbitration or an award whose enforcement would be contrary to the public policy of India. There is no ground for reviewing the award on its merits, and that absence is deliberate; a party that lost on the facts or the law before the tribunal cannot relitigate those findings at the enforcement stage.

In Vijay Karia v. Prysmian Cavi E Sistemi SRL, the Supreme Court read the word 'may' in Section 48 as giving the enforcing court residual discretion to enforce an award even where a debtor technically makes out a ground, and it sorted the grounds into those going to jurisdiction, those protecting the resisting party's own interest (which that party can choose not to press), and the public policy ground, which the court held leaves no discretion once genuinely established. The Court also held that a breach of the Foreign Exchange Management Act does not, by itself, offend the fundamental policy of Indian law, closing off a line of resistance that had been tried in several enforcement proceedings. Treat any claim that an award is 'inconvenient' commercially, or that the tribunal reasoned poorly, as almost certain to fail; the public policy ground after Vijay Karia is read as covering only the gravest defects, not ordinary disagreement with the outcome.

Interim relief while enforcement is pending

An award holder worried about the Indian respondent dissipating assets before enforcement is decided is not required to wait. Indian courts have entertained applications for interim measures, including orders restraining disposal of assets, in aid of a foreign award even before the formal enforcement application is fully adjudicated, treating the pendency of a genuine enforcement claim as sufficient to invoke the court's protective jurisdiction. This is fact-specific and the threshold for showing a real risk of dissipation is not low; a bare apprehension, without some concrete indication that assets are being moved or secreted, is unlikely to persuade a court to freeze a respondent's business operations.

Parties who anticipate this fight are better served building the evidentiary record, bank statements, corporate filings, prior conduct showing asset transfers, before they need the order, rather than assembling it under time pressure once the respondent has had notice of the enforcement application.

Timeline and practical expectations

There is no official published average, and timelines vary sharply by High Court and by how hard the debtor contests the Section 48 grounds. Practitioners commonly describe an uncontested or lightly contested application moving through a commercial division in something like a year to eighteen months, with contested Section 48 defences, especially public policy arguments, pushing the matter out to two or three years including any appeal. These are informal estimates from practice commentary rather than statutory timelines, and an award holder should plan around the possibility of an extended fight rather than assume a quick conversion to a decree.

An appeal from an order refusing enforcement, or from certain orders made during the enforcement proceeding, lies to a Division Bench of the same High Court and potentially to the Supreme Court thereafter; a bare order allowing enforcement is generally not separately appealable in the same way, which is part of why the law is described as structurally weighted toward enforcement rather than resistance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an Indian party resist enforcement by arguing the foreign tribunal got the facts wrong?

No. Section 48 does not include merits review as a ground. Courts enforcing a foreign award will not reweigh the evidence or reconsider findings the tribunal already made; the grounds are confined to due process failures, jurisdictional defects, and the public policy exception as narrowed by the Supreme Court.

Does the award have to be in English to be enforced?

No, but Section 47 requires a certified translation into English where the award is in another language, filed along with the original or an authenticated copy of the award and the arbitration agreement. Without this, the enforcement application is incomplete.

Is a separate suit needed to convert the foreign award into an Indian decree?

No. Since Fuerst Day Lawson v. Jindal Exports, the enforcement application under Sections 47 to 49 is treated as a single proceeding; once the court finds the award enforceable, it is directly executable as a decree without a prior separate suit.

What if the respondent has already started proceedings to set aside the award at the seat?

Section 48 allows the Indian court to adjourn the enforcement application if a competent authority at the seat is seized of an application to set aside or suspend the award, and may require security as a condition of the adjournment. It does not automatically defeat enforcement.

Can interim relief be obtained in India before the enforcement application is decided?

Indian courts have granted interim protection, such as restraining disposal of assets, in aid of pending foreign award enforcement where a genuine risk of dissipation is shown. It requires more than a bare apprehension and is decided on the facts of each application.
