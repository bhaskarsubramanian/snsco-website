Can You Register a Sound as a Trademark in India?

How India registers sound marks under Rule 26(5) of the Trade Marks Rules 2017, what the MP3 and notation requirements mean, and where the limits are.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Yes, a sound can be registered as a trademark in India, and has been since the Yahoo yodel became one of the country's first registered sound marks, generally reported as granted around 2008. The rule governing these applications, Rule 26(5) of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017, asks for an MP3 recording no longer than thirty seconds along with a graphical representation of the sound, usually through musical notation, and that second requirement is where most of the real difficulty in filing one actually sits.

This guide sets out what a sound mark is, how the filing rule works, what tends to get refused, and where a business should be careful before assuming a jingle it has used for years is automatically protectable.

What a sound mark actually is

A sound mark is a trademark where the thing that identifies the source of goods or services to a consumer is an audio clip rather than a word, logo or shape. India's early and still most cited example is Yahoo's yodel, a human voice yodelling the word 'Yahoo', reported to have been filed around 2004 and registered around 2008 as one of the country's first sound trademarks.

ICICI Bank's four-beat 'dhin chik dhin chik' jingle is commonly described as the first sound mark registered by an Indian company, with the registration dated to around 2011 in commentary, though sources are not fully consistent on the exact year. Other sound marks reported as registered in India include Britannia's four-note chime, Nokia's start-up guitar notes, United Breweries' Kingfisher jingle, and Netflix's 'ta-dum' sound, which one source says claimed prior use in India going back to 2018.

What these examples share is that each sound was already in heavy, repeated commercial use, often for years, before or around the time registration was sought. A business asking whether its own jingle or notification tone could qualify should look at these as the realistic comparison group rather than assuming any sound used in advertising automatically clears the bar; a sound that consumers already associate strongly with one brand has a much easier path through the distinctiveness enquiry than a sound adopted only recently.

The filing rule: Rule 26(5) of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017

Rule 26(5) requires an application for a sound mark to be accompanied by the sound reproduced in MP3 format, not exceeding thirty seconds in length, recorded on a medium that allows it to be played back easily and audibly. Alongside the audio file, the application must include a graphical representation of the mark, which in practice means notating the sound so that an examiner, and later the public through the register, can see what it represents on paper rather than relying only on the audio file.

Commentary on the rule has pointed out that the word 'notations' is not itself defined in the Rules, which leaves some uncertainty over exactly what form of graphical representation the Registry will accept. Some practitioners describe a strict practice centred on musical notation, while at least one source describes a looser practice that has sometimes accepted verbal description or other representations. Because this is a point on which the Registry's actual day-to-day practice matters more than the bare rule text, anyone filing a sound mark should confirm current practice with the Registry or through a recently filed comparable application rather than relying on a single commentary piece.

How the filing and examination process works

A sound mark is filed on the same core form used for other trademarks, with the application specifying that the mark is a sound mark and attaching the MP3 file and the notation as Rule 26(5) requires. From there, examination follows the same broad path as any other mark: the Registry checks for distinctiveness and for conflicts with earlier marks, and the application is published for opposition before it proceeds to registration if unopposed.

What is different in substance, not procedure, is the distinctiveness enquiry itself. An examiner has to assess whether a sound, heard without any visual cue, is capable of pointing a consumer to one particular business rather than being a generic or commonly used sound in that trade. This is a harder question to answer on paper than it is for a word or logo mark, which is part of why sound mark filings remain a small fraction of India's trademark applications even though the route has existed since 2017.

What generally will not qualify

A sound that is functional or generic to the goods themselves is unlikely to pass the distinctiveness test, in the same way a plain descriptive word mark would be refused. A standard doorbell chime used by many manufacturers, or a sound that a product naturally makes in use, is a weak candidate for registration because consumers are unlikely to associate it with one specific source.

A jingle or sound effect that has been used widely across an industry, or that simply mimics a well-known tune without adding anything distinctive, faces the same objection any derivative mark would face elsewhere in trademark law. Businesses considering a sound mark filing should treat the distinctiveness requirement as seriously as they would for a word mark, rather than assuming that registering an audio clip is a lighter-touch process just because it is less common.

A sound that is primarily decorative, played purely for atmosphere rather than to signal a brand, such as generic background music in an advertisement, is also a weak candidate, because the whole premise of trademark protection is that the sign functions to identify a single commercial source. An applicant should be able to point to specific, consistent and repeated use of the exact same sound clip in connection with its goods or services, not merely a general association between the business and music or sound in its marketing.

Other non-traditional marks sit on shakier ground

Colour marks, shape marks and motion marks are conceptually recognised as capable of protection in India, in the sense that nothing in the Trade Marks Act rules them out by category. What is thin is reported Indian case law actually testing these categories in contested proceedings, compared to the handful of sound mark examples discussed above.

A business considering a colour or motion mark in India should expect more uncertainty in outcome than a conventional word or logo filing, and should not assume that the comparatively relaxed approach some other jurisdictions, including the European Union, have taken toward graphical representation requirements carries over to Indian practice, which has kept the stricter requirement that a mark be represented graphically on the application.

This gap matters for a foreign company bringing a brand into India that already holds a registered colour, shape or motion mark at home. The home registration is not automatically recognised in India, and the applicant should expect the Indian Registry to apply its own, comparatively stricter, graphical representation standard afresh, rather than treating an existing foreign registration certificate as sufficient proof that the mark is inherently registrable here.

Enforcement is harder than registration

Once registered, a sound mark still has to be enforced, and proving infringement or passing off for a sound is not the same exercise as for a logo. Visual comparison, which courts are used to doing side by side, does not work the same way for an audio clip; the comparison has to rely on aural recall and the overall impression a consumer retains, which is inherently less precise.

A business that owns a registered sound mark should keep contemporaneous evidence of how, where and how long the sound has actually been used commercially, because that kind of use evidence tends to matter as much in an infringement dispute as the bare registration certificate does, particularly where the defendant argues that the sound was already common in the trade before the claimant's use began.

An infringement claim will usually need an audio comparison placed before the court in a form it can actually assess, rather than relying on a written description alone, and expert or survey evidence on how consumers actually perceive and recall the two sounds can carry real weight given how much harder aural comparison is than placing two logos side by side on paper.

Practical points before filing

File early rather than waiting until a dispute arises, since registration gives a presumption of validity that is otherwise absent. Keep a clean, continuous usage record for the sound, including dated advertisements or broadcasts, especially where the sound itself is not obviously distinctive on its own and the claim to protection will partly rest on acquired distinctiveness through use.

Treat the notation requirement as a drafting exercise worth getting right the first time. An application that is rejected or objected to for an inadequate graphical representation loses the priority date advantage that a clean first filing would have preserved, and refiling simply starts the clock again.

Where a business already uses several distinct sounds, such as a start-up chime for one product and a different notification tone for another, each one that genuinely functions as a separate source identifier is better filed as its own application rather than bundled together, since a single application is assessed against a single sound clip, and an examiner or a court is not going to treat two different sounds as one mark simply because the applicant intended them to work together.

Frequently Asked Questions

What file format does India require for a sound mark application?

Rule 26(5) of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017 requires the sound to be submitted in MP3 format, not exceeding thirty seconds, recorded in a way that allows it to be played back clearly, along with a graphical representation such as musical notation.

Can a smell or a taste be registered as a trademark in India?

The same graphical representation requirement that applies to sound marks applies generally across non-traditional marks, and it is far harder to satisfy for a smell or taste than for an audio clip. No well-established Indian examples of registered smell or taste marks appear in current commentary.

Does a sound mark go through a different examination process from a word mark?

No, the procedural steps, including publication and opposition, are the same. What differs is the substance of the distinctiveness enquiry, which has to assess whether an audio clip alone can point a consumer to one specific business, a judgment call examiners make less often than for conventional word or logo marks.

Do I need to have already used the sound commercially before I can file?

Prior use is not a strict precondition for filing, but a documented history of consistent commercial use strengthens a claim to distinctiveness, particularly for a sound that is not inherently unusual, and it matters again later if the mark is ever challenged or needs to be enforced.

Which companies hold sound marks in India?

Commentary commonly cites Yahoo's yodel, ICICI Bank's jingle, Britannia's chime, Nokia's start-up tone, United Breweries' Kingfisher jingle and Netflix's 'ta-dum' sound as examples of sound marks associated with India, though exact registration dates vary across sources and should be checked against the Trade Marks Registry's own record before being relied on in any filing or dispute.
