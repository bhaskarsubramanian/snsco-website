Does the Hindu Succession Act Even Apply to Your Family's Farmland?

Why inheriting agricultural land in India can turn on state tenancy law, not the Hindu Succession Act, and what a 2026 Supreme Court ruling adds.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family dividing an urban flat or a bank deposit after a death usually applies the Hindu Succession Act without much argument about which law governs. Agricultural land is different, and has been different for longer than most people realise. For decades, a separate provision of the Act effectively handed inheritance of farmland over to state tenancy and land reform laws, and even though that provision was removed in 2005, the uncertainty it created has not fully gone away in every state.

This matters in very practical terms to an ordinary family. Whether a daughter inherits an equal share of her father's agricultural holding, or a smaller share, or none at all, can depend on which state the land sits in and on how that state's tenancy law was written decades ago, not simply on what the Hindu Succession Act says on its face. This guide explains how that gap arose, what changed in 2005, and what a recent Supreme Court ruling on a related provision adds to the picture.

Why Was Agricultural Land Ever Treated Differently?

The unamended Section 4(2) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 stated that nothing in the Act was to affect the provisions of any other law preventing fragmentation of agricultural holdings, fixing ceilings on land holdings, or providing for the devolution of tenancy rights in such holdings. In effect, Parliament carved agricultural land out of the Act's own succession rules and left its devolution to whatever tenancy or land reform law the relevant state had enacted.

Most state tenancy laws of that era were written with a male cultivator in mind and gave sons, and sometimes only sons, preference over daughters and widows in inheriting tenancy rights to agricultural holdings. So for close to fifty years, two Hindu siblings inheriting their father's estate could find that the non-agricultural assets, the house, the bank balance, the jewellery, split equally between them under the Hindu Succession Act, while the family's farmland followed an entirely different, often less equal, rule under the state's own tenancy legislation.

What Actually Changed With the 2005 Amendment?

The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 removed Section 4(2) from the Act altogether. The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Law and Justice had recommended the deletion, and its removal was framed as part of the same 2005 reform that gave daughters coparcenary rights in joint Hindu family property, both changes aimed at closing long-standing gaps in how Hindu women inherited property.

On a plain reading, removing Section 4(2) should mean the Hindu Succession Act now applies to agricultural land exactly as it applies to any other property, with no special carve-out for state tenancy law. Several commentators and at least one High Court decision have read the amendment that way. But the deletion did not come with any accompanying statement spelling out exactly how it was meant to interact with tenancy laws state legislatures had already enacted under their own constitutional authority over land, and that gap is where the continuing confusion sits.

Why Do Some States Still Apply the Old Rule?

Land, and rights in or over land, is a subject on which state legislatures have primary law-making power, while succession sits on the concurrent list, available to both Parliament and the states. Several states, including Punjab, Himachal Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh, have continued applying their own tenurial laws in a way that still denies women an equal right to inherit agricultural land, treating their state tenancy legislation as the governing law notwithstanding the 2005 deletion of Section 4(2) from the central Act.

Legal commentators are genuinely split on whether this is defensible. One view holds that once Section 4(2) is gone, any state tenancy provision inconsistent with the Hindu Succession Act's equal-inheritance rule should give way to the central Act, since succession is a concurrent subject and a validly enacted later central law would prevail over an inconsistent earlier state law on the same subject. The opposing view treats the state tenancy laws as falling within the state's exclusive competence over land itself, rather than succession, and therefore as unaffected by what Parliament did to the Hindu Succession Act. Neither position has been conclusively settled by the Supreme Court specifically on this point, which is why the practical answer still depends heavily on which state the land is in and what that state's highest court has said.

Does an Earlier Death Lock In the Old Rule Even Now?

Yes, and this is a separate point families frequently get wrong. Where succession to agricultural land opened, meaning the person owning the land died, before the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 came into force on 9 September 2005, the rights of heirs crystallised under the law as it stood on the date of death, including under the then-existing Section 4(2). Courts have treated the 2005 amendment as not disturbing inheritances that had already taken legal effect before that date.

So a family dealing with agricultural land inherited from a relative who died, say, in 1998, cannot simply apply today's equal-inheritance understanding retroactively to that earlier succession. The governing law is the law as it stood at the moment of death, which for pre-2005 deaths may well mean the old Section 4(2) regime and whatever the applicable state tenancy law provided at that time, even though the same family's later inheritances, from a death occurring after September 2005, are governed by the amended Act without that carve-out.

What Does the 2026 Supreme Court Ruling on Section 22 Add to This?

In Mahinder and Others v. Puran Singh, decided on 14 July 2026, the Supreme Court dealt with a related but distinct provision, Section 22 of the Hindu Succession Act, which gives a Class-I heir a preferential right to buy out a co-heir's share before that share is transferred to an outsider. The Court held that this preferential right, as applied to inherited agricultural land, is an incident of succession rather than a restriction on transfer of land as such, and on that basis upheld Section 22's application to agricultural holdings, rejecting an argument that an earlier Constitution Bench ruling striking down a different pre-emption law meant Section 22 could not survive either.

The ruling does not resolve the broader Section 4(2) debate directly, since it concerns a different provision and a narrower category of heirs, Class-I heirs specifically, rather than the general question of whether daughters inherit agricultural land equally in every state. But it is a useful recent signal that the Supreme Court treats provisions of the Hindu Succession Act touching agricultural land as ordinary incidents of succession law, within Parliament's competence, rather than as matters automatically beyond the Act's reach merely because the property happens to be agricultural. Families and practitioners watching how courts eventually resolve the wider state-tenancy-law question have reason to read this ruling as leaning toward the view that the central Act's succession rules, not state tenancy carve-outs, should ordinarily govern.

What Should a Family Actually Do When Dividing Agricultural Holdings Today?

A family facing this question in practice is better served starting with the revenue record for the land, the mutation entries and the record of rights maintained by the local revenue authority, rather than starting from an assumption about which inheritance law applies. These records often reflect how the land has actually been treated for decades, who has been shown as the recorded tenant or owner, and whether any earlier partition or settlement already addressed the holding, all of which matters practically even before the legal question of which statute governs is fully resolved.

Where family members disagree about shares, particularly where a daughter's claim to an equal share is being resisted on the basis of state tenancy law, a partition suit in the civil court, rather than an informal family settlement, forces the question onto the record and gets a reasoned decision applying whichever law the court finds governs. An informal settlement signed without addressing this question directly can leave a disputed share unresolved for another generation, since it does not actually decide which law applied, it simply papers over the disagreement for the people who signed it. Checking the specific state's tenancy legislation and any High Court rulings interpreting it, before assuming either the old or the amended position applies by default, is the step families and their lawyers most often skip, and the one that actually decides the outcome.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Hindu Succession Act now apply equally to agricultural land in every state?

In principle, after the 2005 deletion of Section 4(2), it should. In practice, several states, including Punjab, Himachal Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh, continue applying their own tenancy laws in ways that still restrict women's inheritance rights, so the answer can depend heavily on where the land is located.

My grandfather died in 1995, before the 2005 amendment. Does the new equal-inheritance rule apply to his land?

Generally not. Courts have treated succession as crystallising under the law in force on the date of death, so a pre-2005 death to agricultural land is typically still governed by the then-existing Section 4(2) regime and whatever state tenancy law applied at that time.

What is Section 22 of the Hindu Succession Act, and how is it different from Section 4(2)?

Section 22 gives a Class-I heir a preferential right to buy out a co-heir's share in inherited property before it passes to an outsider. It is a narrower, specific provision, distinct from Section 4(2), which was the general carve-out for agricultural land that Parliament deleted in 2005.

Did the Supreme Court's 2026 ruling settle whether daughters inherit agricultural land equally everywhere?

No. Mahinder v. Puran Singh dealt specifically with Section 22's preferential purchase right for Class-I heirs on agricultural land. It did not directly resolve the separate, still-unsettled question of how far state tenancy laws can continue overriding the Hindu Succession Act's general inheritance rules.

How do I actually find out which rule applies to my family's farmland?

Start with the date of the relevant death and the state where the land is situated, then check whether that state's tenancy or land reform legislation contains its own succession rule and how the state's High Court has treated its interaction with the 2005 amendment. This is genuinely state-specific and worth confirming before assuming either rule applies by default.
