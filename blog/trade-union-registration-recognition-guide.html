How Trade Unions Are Registered and Recognised Under Indian Labour Law

An explainer on how a trade union becomes a registered union under the Trade Unions Act, 1926, the difference between registration and recognition, and what recognition typically entitles a union to at the workplace.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 25 May 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Registration Under the Trade Unions Act

The Trade Unions Act, 1926 allows any seven or more members of a trade union to apply for registration with the Registrar of Trade Unions of the relevant state, by submitting the union's rules, a list of office-bearers, and other prescribed particulars. Registration confers a distinct legal identity on the union, immunity from certain civil and criminal liability for legitimate trade union activity, and the ability to hold property and enter into contracts in the union's own name: registration alone, however, does not automatically entitle the union to be recognised by a particular employer for collective bargaining purposes.

Registration Is Not the Same as Recognition

Recognition, the employer's formal acknowledgment of a union as the representative body for collective bargaining at a given establishment, is a separate concept from registration, and is not uniformly mandated by a single central statute in the way registration is. Recognition has historically developed through state-level legislation, industry codes of discipline, and negotiated practice, meaning the precise mechanism and threshold for recognition (such as a minimum membership percentage) can vary depending on the state and sector.

What Recognition Typically Enables

Where a union achieves recognition, it typically gains standing to represent workers in collective bargaining over wages and conditions of service, to be consulted on matters affecting the workforce, and often to access facilities such as notice board space, meeting space, or a 'check-off' arrangement for payroll deduction of union subscription. Employers cannot arbitrarily withdraw facilities already extended to a verified or recognised union without a justifiable, non-discriminatory basis, as this can be challenged as an unfair labour practice.

Multiple Unions at a Single Establishment

It is common in India for more than one trade union to operate at a single establishment, and disputes frequently arise over which union should be treated as the principal representative for collective bargaining, or whether facilities extended to one union must be extended equally to others. Employers navigating multiple unions should apply consistent, transparent, and objectively verifiable criteria, such as membership verification, to any decision affecting one union but not another.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many members are needed to register a trade union?

A minimum of seven members of a trade union can apply for registration under the Trade Unions Act, 1926, by submitting the prescribed application, rules, and particulars to the Registrar of Trade Unions.

Does registering a union automatically mean the employer must bargain with it?

Not automatically: registration confers legal status on the union itself, but formal recognition for collective bargaining purposes is a separate step that depends on applicable state legislation, industry practice, or negotiated arrangements, and often on a demonstrated membership threshold.

Can an employer refuse to deal with a registered union altogether?

An employer is not always statutorily compelled to formally 'recognise' a union in every state, but arbitrarily refusing to engage with a union whose membership is verified, or withdrawing facilities already extended to it without justification, can expose the employer to an unfair labour practice challenge.

What happens when multiple unions exist at the same workplace?

This is common and lawful: employers should apply consistent, objective and transparent criteria across all unions when it comes to facilities, consultation, or any differential treatment, to avoid claims of favouritism or an unfair labour practice.
