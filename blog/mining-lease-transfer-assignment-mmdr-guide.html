Transferring a Mining Lease in India: Why the State's Approval Comes First

How a mining lease can be transferred under Section 12A of the MMDR Act, the State's approval process, what it can refuse, and why an unapproved transfer is void.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A mining lease is not an ordinary piece of property that changes hands by private agreement between a seller and a buyer. Section 12A of the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 treats it as a concession granted by the State, so any transfer of that concession, whether dressed up as an assignment, a sale of the lease, or a restructuring inside a corporate group, needs the State Government's previous approval before it takes legal effect.

This guide sets out, as on today's date, what Section 12A allows after the 2021 amendment widened it, how the approval process actually runs, what a State can and cannot refuse on, and what happens to a transfer carried out without that approval. A recent 2026 Madras High Court ruling on exactly this point, and an older but still controlling Supreme Court judgment, both make the same point from different angles: skipping the State's consent does not just risk a penalty, it can mean the transfer never happened in law.

Why a mining lease cannot simply be sold like other property

Mineral rights vest in the State, and a mining lease is the instrument by which the State lets a private party extract and use a mineral for a fixed term, subject to conditions the lease itself and the Act impose. Because the relationship is between the State and the lessee personally, the law does not treat a change of lessee as a private matter between the outgoing and incoming party. Section 12A makes this explicit: the lessee of a mining lease or composite licence may transfer it only with the previous approval of the State Government.

The practical effect is that a transfer agreement signed between two companies, however carefully drafted, does not by itself move the lease. Until the State Government approves the transfer, the named lessee in the original grant remains the only party legally entitled to work the mine, and the buyer has no standing against the State or third parties on the strength of the private deed alone. This is the point most negotiating parties underestimate when they treat the State's approval as a formality to be picked up after signing, rather than a condition that has to be satisfied before the transfer can be said to exist in law at all.

What Section 12A permits after the 2021 amendment

Before the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2021, a mining lease granted otherwise than through auction could be transferred with the State's approval only where the mineral was used captively, meaning consumed in the transferee's own manufacturing unit rather than sold in the open market. The 2021 amendment removed that end-use limitation, so a non-auction lease can now be transferred with the previous approval of the State Government regardless of what the transferee intends to do with the mineral, alongside leases that were granted through auction, which Section 12A also covers.

This change was part of a broader liberalisation of the mining sector that also did away with the earlier practice of reserving some mines for a particular end use at the time of auction. For a buyer evaluating an acquisition that includes a mining asset, the 2021 amendment is the reason a captive-only restriction that would have blocked the deal a few years earlier may no longer apply, but the core requirement of prior State approval for the transfer itself has not gone away.

How the approval process runs

The transferor and the transferee apply jointly to the State Government for approval, in the form the Mineral Concession Rules prescribe for the relevant category of lease, whether the original grant was through auction or otherwise. The transferee has to accept all the conditions and liabilities attached to the lease as part of this process, since the State is not simply approving a change of name but approving a party it is satisfied can carry those obligations forward.

The Act builds in a timeline that protects the parties from indefinite delay: if the State Government does not communicate its decision on the transfer within ninety days of receiving the application, the approval is deemed to have been given. This deeming provision is useful leverage in a transaction where the State is slow to respond, but it only helps once a complete and properly made application is actually on file, since a defective or incomplete application does not start the ninety-day clock running in any meaningful sense.

What the State can refuse, and on what ground

The State's power to refuse a transfer is not open-ended. Section 12A confines a refusal to the ground that the transferee is not eligible under the Act, and the State has to communicate its reasons for refusal in writing. This matters in practice because it stops a State from sitting on an application indefinitely or refusing it for reasons that have nothing to do with the transferee's eligibility to hold a mining lease, such as a dispute about valuation or royalty arrears that properly belongs in a separate recovery proceeding.

Eligibility itself tracks back to the same conditions a fresh grantee would have to meet, so a transferee that could not itself have been granted the lease in the first place, whether for want of the necessary approvals, a disqualification under the Act, or a failure to meet the financial or technical capacity the mineral and the scale of operation call for, is not going to clear this bar simply by inheriting an existing lease from someone else. A buyer doing due diligence on a mining asset should treat its own eligibility as a genuine condition precedent to closing, not a box-ticking exercise to be sorted out after the transfer deed is signed.

What changes once the transfer is approved

From the date the transfer takes effect, the transferee is bound by the Act and the rules exactly as the original lessee was, and becomes liable to both the Central and State Governments for the liabilities relating to the mining lease, including obligations that arose before the transfer was approved unless the lease and the approval order say otherwise. This is a meaningful point for a buyer to price into a transaction: the mine's compliance history, including pending royalty demands, environmental obligations and any show-cause notices, travels with the lease, not with the seller.

A separate but related requirement operators sometimes overlook is that the transfer, once approved, still has to be properly executed and, where the lease deed itself provides for it, registered, since an approval from the State Government is not a substitute for the formalities the Registration Act requires of the instrument transferring the leasehold interest. Treating the government approval as the only box to tick, while leaving the transfer deed unregistered or improperly executed, leaves the transferee's title open to challenge on a separate footing entirely.

What goes wrong: disguised transfers and the courts' response

The recurring mistake is trying to achieve the economic effect of a transfer without going through Section 12A, usually by structuring the deal as a sale of shares in the lease-holding company, a merger, or some other corporate restructuring that leaves the named lessee technically unchanged on paper while control of the mine passes to a new group. The Supreme Court closed this route firmly in State of Rajasthan v. Gotan Lime Stone Khanij Udyog Pvt. Ltd., holding that the State's previous approval for a transfer of mining rights cannot be treated as a mere formality or bypassed through a restructuring that amounts, in substance, to a transfer of the lease, and upholding the State's cancellation of a lease where this had been attempted.

A Madras High Court ruling reported in April 2026, in a matter involving the assignment of leasehold rights over a refractory mineral lease to a Steel Authority of India subsidiary, reinforced the same principle from the registration side: the court held that an assignment of a mining lease made without the State Government's prior written consent is impermissible in law and void, and that a defect of this kind cannot be cured by getting the State's consent afterwards. Read together, the two rulings leave very little room for the view that State approval under Section 12A is something a transaction can work around or complete later; it has to be in hand, in the correct form, before the transfer is treated as valid at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a mining lease be transferred by a private sale agreement alone?

No. Section 12A of the MMDR Act requires the previous approval of the State Government before a mining lease or composite licence can be transferred. A private sale or assignment deed signed without that approval does not legally transfer the lease, whatever the parties intended.

Does the 2021 amendment allow all mining leases to be transferred?

It removed the earlier captive-use restriction, so a non-auction lease can now be transferred with the State's approval regardless of end use. Leases granted through auction were already transferable subject to approval. The requirement of prior State approval itself was not removed.

What happens if the State does not respond to a transfer application?

If the State Government does not communicate a decision within ninety days of receiving a complete application, the approval is deemed to have been given. This deeming provision only protects an application that was properly and completely filed.

Can a transfer be achieved by selling shares in the company that holds the lease instead?

Courts have treated a corporate restructuring that amounts in substance to a change of control over the mining lease as a transfer requiring Section 12A approval, and have upheld cancellation of leases where this route was used to avoid the State's consent. Structuring around the section carries real cancellation risk.

Does approval of the transfer wipe out liabilities that arose before the transfer?

Generally no. Once the transfer takes effect, the transferee is bound by the Act and rules and becomes liable for the liabilities relating to the mining lease, which typically includes obligations that arose before the transfer unless the lease and the approval order provide otherwise. A buyer should verify the lease's compliance history before closing, not after.
