A Foreign Court Awarded Damages Against Your Company: Can FEMA or RBI Rules Cap What You Actually Pay?

Delhi High Court enforced a full English decree against an Indian borrower despite FEMA and RBI objections. What ECB and FCCB borrowers should know.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 24 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An Indian company that raises foreign-currency debt under RBI's External Commercial Borrowing or FCCB framework tends to assume that framework also sets the outer limit on what it can ever be made to pay if things go wrong. That assumption took a direct hit from the Delhi High Court in February 2026. In Peter Beck und Partner Vermogensverwaltung GmbH v. Prakash Industries Ltd., the court enforced an English Commercial Court decree at its full value, rejecting the borrower's argument that FEMA and RBI pricing or remittance ceilings should cap the payout because the underlying instrument had itself been RBI-approved at issuance.

The case is a useful corrective for any treasury or legal team negotiating foreign-law-governed debt. The regulatory approval that lets a company raise the money in the first place does not travel with the instrument into a damages claim for breaching it. Once a competent court, Indian or foreign, awards damages for breach, the FEMA architecture that governed the original borrowing has largely left the room.

What the Prakash Industries case actually decided

Prakash Industries had issued Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds subscribed to by Peter Beck. When the company missed coupon payments and delayed conversion, Peter Beck sued in England. The English Commercial Court's judgment, dated 1 April 2022, with an ancillary order on 20 May 2022, held Prakash Industries liable for an Early Redemption Amount above USD 11 million, interest at 7.95 percent, and additional damages for the conversion delay. No appeal was filed against that judgment in England.

Peter Beck then sought to execute the decree in India. Justice Amit Bansal, in the order reported as 2026:DHC:1547 and decided on 23 February 2026, held the decree executable under Section 44A of the Code of Civil Procedure and found that Prakash Industries' objections did not fall within the narrow exceptions to a foreign judgment's conclusiveness under Section 13(c) or Section 13(f) of the CPC. The court called the objections devoid of merit and imposed costs of Rs. 1 lakh on the company for raising them.

Why Section 13 CPC did not help the borrower

Section 13 of the CPC treats a foreign judgment as conclusive on matters directly decided between the same parties, subject to six narrow exceptions: lack of jurisdiction in the foreign court, absence of a decision on the merits, an incorrect view of international law or a refusal to recognise applicable Indian law, proceedings opposed to natural justice, a judgment founded on fraud, and a judgment sustaining a claim founded on a breach of a law in force in India. Prakash Industries' resistance was pressed under the international-law and breach-of-Indian-law limbs, Section 13(c) and 13(f), effectively arguing that enforcing the full award would offend FEMA's regulatory scheme for this kind of borrowing.

The court did not accept that framing. A damages award for breach of a contractual obligation is not the same thing as enforcing a claim that itself breaches Indian law merely because the contract happened to be FEMA-regulated at the point the money was borrowed. The breach being compensated was the company's own default, not a transaction Indian law prohibits, and Section 13(f) was never built to let a defaulting party invoke the regulatory framework that let it borrow in the first place as a shield against the consequences of defaulting.

RBI's own position undercut the FEMA objection

A detail in the court's record makes the ruling harder to dislodge on appeal: the Reserve Bank of India itself had told the court, in November 2024, that there was no bar to remitting the amount abroad since the payment would satisfy a judgment. A borrower arguing that its own regulator's ceilings should cap a judgment debt has a considerably weaker case once that regulator has already told the court it sees no obstacle to the payment going out.

This also answers a question treasury teams often ask before resisting enforcement on a FEMA ground: whether RBI needs to be heard, or will object, before a judgment debt denominated in foreign currency can be remitted. Here RBI had already been consulted, and did not object, which removed one of the stronger practical obstacles a borrower might otherwise have relied on. A company in a similar position would do well to find out early what its own regulator actually thinks, rather than assuming the regulator's silence or absence from the proceedings means it would object if asked.

The ruling is not yet final

This is a single-judge decision, and reporting from AZB & Partners indicates an appeal against it is pending before a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court. The detail of that appeal, its grounds and hearing dates, was not available in the sources reviewed for this guide, so the position should be treated as settled at the execution-stage, single-judge level for now, not as a final, unappealable statement of Indian law on this question.

A company facing a similar enforcement action should factor that into its own litigation strategy. Citing Prakash Industries as persuasive authority for the proposition that FEMA ceilings do not cap a foreign damages decree is reasonable; treating it as a closed question beyond further challenge is not, until the appeal is resolved one way or the other. Counsel on either side of a similar dispute should keep an eye on the Division Bench docket before advising a client on how much weight the ruling can bear.

What a borrower should check before raising a FEMA defence

Before a company resisting enforcement leans on a FEMA argument, it is worth asking whether the regulator itself has taken, or is likely to take, a position on the remittance. Where RBI has no objection to the payment, as in Prakash Industries, the borrower is essentially asking a court to enforce a ceiling the regulator does not think applies, which is a considerably weaker position than arguing the point in the abstract.

It is also worth separating two different questions that tend to get run together: whether the underlying debt instrument complied with FEMA when it was issued, and whether a later damages award for breaching that instrument is itself subject to the same regulatory ceiling. The first question can be answered yes without the second following automatically, and conflating them is part of what went wrong for the borrower here. A litigation team that keeps these two questions apart from the outset is in a better position to tell a client realistically what an enforcement objection is likely to achieve.

What this means for ECB and FCCB default scenarios generally

The practical consequence for a lender holding RBI-approved foreign-currency debt is that it is not limited to the contractually agreed redemption premium or coupon if a foreign court, applying the governing law of the instrument, calculates a different, often higher, damages figure for breach. The FEMA pricing framework that applied when the instrument was issued governs the borrowing itself; it does not rewrite what a court can award once the borrower has defaulted and the dispute has moved to litigation under the instrument's own governing law.

For a borrower negotiating this kind of debt in the first place, the lesson points the other way: if a FEMA-ceiling-style limit on downside exposure is commercially important, it has to be built into the loan or bond documentation itself, through a negotiated cap or a liquidated-damages clause, rather than assumed to be implied by the regulatory framework that approved the borrowing. An Indian court at the enforcement stage will not read that ceiling into the contract on the borrower's behalf.

The costs lesson for resisting enforcement on a weak FEMA ground

The Rs. 1 lakh costs order is a smaller detail but a useful one for any company weighing whether to resist enforcement at all. Courts have shown limited patience for a FEMA or RBI objection that does not fit within one of Section 13's actual exceptions, and resisting enforcement on that basis purely to delay payment carries a real risk of costs being imposed on top of the underlying liability, rather than a straightforward downside-free way to buy time.

A company genuinely unsure whether a FEMA ground applies to its situation is better served getting that assessed early and precisely, against the specific Section 13 exception it would need to fit within, rather than raising the objection broadly and hoping the court reads the regulatory framework as expansively as the borrower would like. A narrow, well-targeted objection that actually engages with one of the six statutory exceptions stands a meaningfully better chance than a general appeal to FEMA's existence as a reason the decree should be scaled back.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can FEMA or RBI pricing ceilings limit a foreign court's damages award against an Indian borrower?

The Delhi High Court held no, in a case involving a defaulted FCCB. Once a competent foreign court awards damages for breach of contract, FEMA and RBI ceilings that governed the original borrowing do not cap what the Indian court will enforce, unless the borrower can fit its objection within one of Section 13 CPC's specific exceptions.

What exceptions did Prakash Industries try to rely on, and why did they fail?

The company argued its case fell within Section 13(c) and Section 13(f) of the CPC, the international-law and breach-of-Indian-law exceptions to a foreign judgment's conclusiveness. The court held that enforcing a damages award for the company's own contractual default was not the same as sustaining a claim that itself breaches Indian law.

Is the Prakash Industries ruling final and binding on other courts?

No. It is a single-judge decision, and an appeal is reported to be pending before a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court. Treat it as persuasive and currently operative at the execution stage, not as a final or unappealable statement of law.

Did the Reserve Bank of India object to the payment being remitted abroad?

No. RBI told the court in November 2024 that there was no bar to remitting the amount since it would satisfy a judgment. That statement from the regulator significantly weakened the borrower's own argument that its FEMA framework should be read to limit what the company had to pay.

How should a borrower protect itself against open-ended foreign-court damages exposure on ECB or FCCB debt?

Negotiate a cap or liquidated-damages clause directly into the loan or bond documentation at the time of borrowing. An Indian court will not read a FEMA-style ceiling into the contract at the enforcement stage merely because the instrument was originally RBI-approved.
