Mandatory Product Recall Under Section 20 of the Consumer Protection Act: When the CCPA Can Order It

What lets the CCPA order a company to recall a product, how that differs from an FSSAI recall, and what a business must then do.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 21 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Most businesses first hear about a product recall from a food regulator, or from their own quality team spotting a defect before anyone else does. Fewer realise that the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 gave an entirely separate regulator, the Central Consumer Protection Authority, its own power to order a recall of any unsafe good or service, not just food, once it decides consumers are at risk. That power sits beside the Authority's better-known fining role in dark pattern and misleading advertisement cases, and businesses selling anything from electrical appliances to children's toys need to understand it as a distinct exposure of its own.

This guide works through where the CCPA's recall power actually comes from, how an inquiry gets to the point of a recall order, and what a business served with one actually has to do, as distinct from the sector-specific recall regimes, such as FSSAI's, that some readers may already be familiar with.

Where the power comes from, and who else can recall

Section 20 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 gives the Central Authority the power to recall goods or withdraw services that are hazardous, unsafe, or risk being hazardous or unsafe to consumers, direct that the price paid be reimbursed to affected consumers, and discontinue the practice that caused the harm. This sits within a broader scheme where Section 18 sets out the Central Authority's general protective functions and Section 19 lets it investigate complaints and demand documents and evidence from a business as part of that process, so a recall order rarely arrives without an investigation preceding it.

The Central Authority is not the only body with this power. A District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, hearing an individual consumer's complaint about a specific defective or unsafe product, can also direct a recall as part of the relief it grants in that case. The practical difference is scale and reach: a District Commission's order addresses the complainant's own grievance and whatever relief that complaint supports, while a Central Authority recall under Section 20 is a market-wide direction that can require a business to pull a product nationally, regardless of whether any individual consumer has yet complained about it.

How a Section 20 inquiry actually gets started

A Section 20 recall does not require a court finding of liability first. The Central Authority can act on its own motion, on a reference from the central or a state government, on a complaint received through the National Consumer Helpline or otherwise, or on information from a recognised consumer protection council. Once it has reason to believe a good or service is unsafe, Section 19 lets it direct an inquiry, which can include calling for records, samples, and explanations from the business, while Section 22 separately gives the Authority search-and-seizure powers, applying the safeguards of the Code of Criminal Procedure, where the investigation justifies it.

Because the trigger for an inquiry can be as informal as a pattern of consumer complaints or a referral from another agency, a business is often better served treating an information request from the Authority as the first real warning sign, rather than waiting to see whether a formal show-cause notice for a recall actually follows. Businesses that engage substantively and transparently at the inquiry stage, including volunteering a batch-specific root cause analysis where one genuinely points to a narrower problem than the whole product line, are in a materially stronger position than ones that stay silent and are then met with a recall order addressed to the entire product category.

How this differs from an FSSAI recall

Food businesses already operate under FSSAI's own recall procedure, which is triggered under the Food Safety and Standards Act and its regulations whenever a food product is found unsafe. A CCPA recall under the Consumer Protection Act is not limited to food at all, and covers any good or service, from a faulty pressure cooker to a defective electrical fitting to a misdescribed financial product bundled with a consumer good. Where a food product is involved, the two regimes can, in principle, both be engaged, since FSSAI's jurisdiction over food safety and the CCPA's general consumer protection jurisdiction are not mutually exclusive, though in practice a food-specific recall is more likely to proceed primarily through FSSAI given its specialised expertise and dedicated recall machinery.

For a non-food consumer product, FSSAI has no role at all, and the CCPA's Section 20 power, together with the Bureau of Indian Standards' own compliance regime for products under mandatory quality control orders, becomes the relevant regulatory exposure. A business manufacturing or importing a consumer product that is not food should map which of these regimes could plausibly apply to it well before any safety issue arises, rather than discovering the answer for the first time during an actual inquiry.

What a recall order actually requires a business to do

A recall direction under Section 20 is not simply a public announcement that a product has a defect. It typically requires the business to withdraw the specific goods from the market, arrange for reimbursement of the price paid by affected consumers, and stop whatever practice produced the unsafe product in the first place, which could mean a manufacturing process change, a supplier substitution, or a packaging and warning label correction depending on what the inquiry found was actually wrong. The order can be structured to cover the whole product line or a narrower batch or production run, depending on the scope of the defect the Authority's inquiry actually established.

A business facing a wide recall order where it believes the defect is genuinely confined to a specific batch or production run has an interest in making that case clearly and with supporting technical evidence at the inquiry stage, since a recall addressed to an entire product line carries far greater commercial cost and reputational damage than one confined to the batches actually shown to be defective. Once an order is passed, compliance needs to be documented carefully, including proof of the actual withdrawal from retail and online channels and proof that affected consumers were in fact reimbursed, since a business that claims compliance without being able to show it invites further enforcement action rather than closing the matter.

Penalties for non-compliance and what else can follow

Failing to comply with a direction of the Central Authority, including a recall order, is treated as a separate offence under the Act, carrying its own penalty exposure distinct from whatever liability triggered the original recall. A business that treats a recall order as optional guidance rather than a binding direction therefore compounds its own exposure considerably, turning a product safety problem into a regulatory non-compliance problem as well.

A recall order also does not extinguish an individual consumer's right to pursue their own complaint before a District, State or National Commission for compensation specific to their own loss, including for injury caused by the unsafe product before the recall took effect. Businesses sometimes assume that complying with a Central Authority recall closes out their exposure entirely. It does not, and a pattern of individual complaints can continue, and indeed often accelerates, once a recall has publicly confirmed that a defect existed.

Building a recall response before you need one

Treating recall readiness as something to work out after the first inquiry letter arrives is a common and expensive mistake. A manufacturer or importer of a consumer product benefits from having a recall protocol already written down before any issue surfaces: who within the business authorises a voluntary withdrawal, which distributors and retailers need to be notified and how quickly, how refunds are actually processed at scale rather than one complaint at a time, and what records are kept to prove the recall was carried out rather than merely announced.

A business that moves first with a voluntary, well-documented withdrawal once it identifies a genuine safety issue, rather than waiting for the Central Authority to order one, is in a far better position when the inquiry does come, since a prompt voluntary response is routinely treated as a mitigating factor in how the regulator frames the eventual order and in whether further penalty proceedings follow. Waiting to be told, by contrast, reads as exactly the kind of indifference to consumer safety that Section 20 was designed to address, and tends to invite the broader, more commercially damaging version of the recall rather than the narrower one the facts might otherwise have supported.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the CCPA order a recall before any consumer has actually complained?

Yes. The Central Authority can act on its own motion or on a government reference once it has reason to believe a product is unsafe, and does not need to wait for an individual consumer complaint to begin an inquiry under Section 19 that could lead to a Section 20 recall direction.

Does an FSSAI recall mean the CCPA cannot also act on the same product?

The two regimes are not mutually exclusive in principle, though a food safety issue typically proceeds primarily through FSSAI given its specialised jurisdiction and dedicated machinery. For a non-food consumer product, FSSAI has no role, and the CCPA's Section 20 power is the relevant exposure.

Can a recall be limited to a specific batch instead of the entire product line?

Yes, where the inquiry's findings support a narrower defect. A business with technical evidence showing the problem is confined to a particular batch or production run should present that evidence during the inquiry, since it materially affects how broad the eventual recall direction is likely to be.

What happens if a business simply ignores a CCPA recall order?

Non-compliance with a direction of the Central Authority is a separate offence under the Consumer Protection Act, carrying its own penalty exposure in addition to whatever liability the underlying product defect created, so ignoring the order compounds rather than avoids the business's legal risk.
