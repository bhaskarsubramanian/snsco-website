Wasiyat Under Muslim Law: Why You Generally Can't Will Away More Than a Third of Your Estate

Muslim personal law caps how much of an estate can be bequeathed by Will — this guide explains the one-third rule, when consent of other heirs can override it, and how it interacts with fixed succession shares.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

A Fundamentally Different Starting Point From Hindu or Christian Wills

Where the Indian Succession Act generally permits a Hindu, Christian or Parsi testator to bequeath their entire estate as they see fit, subject only to challenges based on capacity or genuine suspicious circumstances, Muslim personal law starts from a materially different premise: a Muslim testator's freedom to dispose of property by Will (wasiyat) is deliberately and structurally limited, so that the bulk of the estate continues to pass according to the fixed shares Islamic law prescribes for specific heirs, regardless of what the testator might otherwise have wanted to do with it.

The One-Third Rule

The core limitation is that a Muslim testator can ordinarily bequeath, by Will, no more than one-third of their net estate (after payment of funeral expenses and debts) without the consent of their other legal heirs. This is not a default that can simply be overridden by clearly stating a contrary intention in the Will itself — a bequest purporting to exceed one-third is not void outright, but it does not take effect beyond the one-third limit unless the other heirs, with full knowledge of their own rights, actually consent to the excess after the testator's death. The remaining two-thirds (or more, where the excess bequest is not ratified) passes according to the fixed personal-law shares applicable under either Sunni (Hanafi) or Shia (Ithna Ashari) succession rules, depending on the testator's school.

Why the Limit Exists

The one-third rule reflects a specific principle within Islamic inheritance law: the fixed shares prescribed for specific classes of heirs (spouse, children, parents, and others, in defined proportions depending on the family composition) are treated as a matter of religious obligation, not merely a default the deceased could have overridden through ordinary testamentary freedom. Allowing unlimited testamentary freedom would let a testator effectively rewrite those prescribed shares through a Will, defeating the underlying scheme; capping bequests at one-third preserves the core of the prescribed distribution for the remaining heirs while still giving the testator meaningful, though bounded, freedom to make specific provision — commonly for a person who is not otherwise a Quranic heir at all, such as a daughter-in-law, a friend, a charity, or an heir the testator wants to benefit beyond their fixed share.

A Bequest to an Existing Heir Faces an Additional Hurdle

Beyond the one-third cap, Muslim law imposes a further, separate restriction: a bequest to a person who is already an heir entitled to a fixed share under the intestate succession rules (for instance, a bequest to one of the testator's own children, who would inherit a share regardless) generally requires the consent of the other heirs to take effect at all, even if the bequest itself falls within the one-third limit — the concern being that a testator should not be able to favour one heir over the others who have an equal religious claim, without those other heirs' agreement. A bequest to someone who is not otherwise an heir at all (a stranger to the succession, a charity, or a more distant relative with no fixed statutory share) does not face this additional consent requirement, and only needs to satisfy the one-third overall limit.

Sunni and Shia Variations

While the general one-third principle is broadly shared, the precise mechanics — including exactly when and how heir consent can be given (before or only after the testator's death, for instance) and some of the specific fixed shares themselves — differ in detail between Sunni (Hanafi) and Shia (Ithna Ashari) jurisprudence, both of which Indian courts apply according to the testator's own school. A Muslim testator, or a family administering a Muslim testator's estate, should identify the applicable school clearly, since assuming one set of rules applies when the other actually governs can lead to real errors in working out what the Will can validly achieve.

Practical Drafting Guidance

A Muslim testator wanting to make a bequest exceeding one-third of the estate, or a bequest to an existing heir, should understand from the outset that such a bequest's actual effect depends on the other heirs' consent after death — a consent that cannot be guaranteed in advance and should not be assumed. Where a testator has a specific reason to want to benefit a particular heir more generously, discussing that intention with the family during the testator's lifetime, so that consent is a realistic prospect rather than a surprise imposed on the other heirs after death, is generally the more effective approach than relying solely on the Will's own terms to achieve an outcome the one-third rule does not, by itself, permit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much of their estate can a Muslim testator bequeath by Will?

Ordinarily no more than one-third of the net estate (after funeral expenses and debts), without the consent of the other legal heirs. A bequest exceeding this limit does not take effect beyond one-third unless the other heirs consent after the testator's death.

Can the other heirs' consent be obtained before the testator dies?

The precise mechanics of when and how consent can be validly given differ between Sunni (Hanafi) and Shia (Ithna Ashari) jurisprudence, and should be confirmed against the applicable school rather than assumed.

Can a Muslim testator leave a larger share to one of their own children by Will?

Only with the consent of the other heirs — a bequest to a person who is already entitled to a fixed intestate share, such as a child, generally requires the other heirs' consent to take effect, even if it falls within the one-third limit.

Does the one-third rule apply to a bequest to a charity or a non-heir?

The one-third overall limit still applies, but the additional heir-consent requirement does not — a bequest to someone who is not otherwise a fixed-share heir (a charity, a friend, a more distant relative) only needs to satisfy the one-third cap, not separate heir consent.

Why does Muslim law limit testamentary freedom this way?

Because the fixed shares prescribed for specific heirs under Islamic inheritance law are treated as a matter of religious obligation rather than a default the testator can simply override, and the one-third cap preserves the core of that prescribed distribution while still allowing bounded testamentary freedom.
