Will Contests: Proving (or Disproving) Testamentary Capacity and Sound Mind

A Will can be challenged on the basis that the testator lacked capacity when it was made: this guide explains the 'sound disposing mind' standard and the kind of evidence courts actually look for.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A Focused Look at One Specific Ground

Our companion guide on contesting a Will covers the full range of recognised grounds, standing requirements, and the caveat/probate procedure generally. This guide goes deeper into one specific, frequently litigated ground: the claim that the testator lacked testamentary capacity, a 'sound disposing mind', at the time the Will was executed. Capacity challenges are among the most fact-intensive and evidence-heavy disputes in succession litigation, and understanding what courts actually require to prove or disprove capacity matters both for someone contemplating such a challenge and for a family defending a Will against one.

What 'Sound Disposing Mind' Actually Means

Section 59 of the Indian Succession Act requires a testator to be 'of sound mind' to make a valid Will, and courts have developed a specific, functional test for what this requires in the testamentary context, rather than a generic assessment of overall mental health. A testator is considered to have testamentary capacity if, at the time of execution, they understood the nature of the act they were undertaking (that they were making a Will disposing of their property on death); understood, at least in general terms, the extent of the property they were disposing of; and understood and appreciated the claims of the persons who might ordinarily be expected to benefit from their estate, even if the testator ultimately chose to exclude or under-provide for some of them, provided that choice reflected an actual, comprehending decision rather than a failure to even recognise those claims existed.

Capacity Is Not the Same as Perfect Health

A recurring misconception in capacity disputes is that any diagnosis of a cognitive condition, early-stage dementia, a stroke's after-effects, general old-age frailty, automatically defeats testamentary capacity. It does not. Courts have consistently held that testamentary capacity is decision-specific and time-specific: a person can have a diagnosed medical condition and still have a lucid interval, or a generally sufficient level of comprehension for the specific, relatively simple decision a Will involves, even where that same person might lack capacity for a more complex decision. The relevant question is always whether the testator had sufficient capacity at the specific time the Will was executed, not their general medical status at some other point before or after.

What Evidence Actually Matters

Because capacity is assessed at the moment of execution, the most persuasive evidence tends to be evidence closest in time to that moment: the testimony of the attesting witnesses (who were legally required to be present and can describe the testator's demeanour and apparent understanding at signing); contemporaneous medical records, particularly any medical assessment specifically obtained around the time of execution (a practice increasingly recommended for elderly or ill testators precisely to protect against a later capacity challenge); the testator's own conduct and communications around that time, including any correspondence or witness accounts of conversations showing the testator understood their affairs; and, where available, the drafting lawyer's own file notes and recollection of the testator's demeanour and responses during the drafting and execution process, which is one reason experienced drafters make a deliberate practice of noting their own capacity impressions contemporaneously.

The Burden of Proof

The propounder of a Will (the person seeking to have it upheld and given effect) generally carries the initial burden of proving the Will was validly executed, including that the testator had testamentary capacity, though where a Will's due execution and attestation are duly proved through the ordinary process, courts generally presume capacity absent some evidence raising a genuine doubt, at which point the burden effectively shifts to the party asserting incapacity to substantiate that specific claim with real evidence, not mere suspicion or a bare assertion that the testator was old or unwell.

Practical Guidance

Testators who are elderly, seriously ill, or otherwise have reason to anticipate a future capacity challenge should consider obtaining a contemporaneous medical capacity assessment at the time of execution, and should ensure the drafting process itself, including private conversations with the testator away from any beneficiary who might otherwise be present and later accused of influencing the outcome, is properly documented. Families anticipating or defending against a capacity challenge should gather contemporaneous evidence as early as possible, since witnesses' memories fade and medical records can be harder to obtain the longer a dispute is delayed.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does 'sound disposing mind' actually require under Indian law?

Under Section 59 of the Indian Succession Act, a testator must understand the nature of making a Will, understand the extent of the property they are disposing of, and understand and appreciate the claims of persons who might ordinarily expect to benefit, even if they choose not to provide for some of them.

Does a dementia diagnosis automatically invalidate a Will?

No: capacity is assessed at the specific time of execution, not by general medical status. A person with a diagnosed cognitive condition can still have sufficient capacity for the relatively simple decision a Will involves, particularly during a lucid interval.

What evidence is most useful in a testamentary capacity dispute?

Evidence closest in time to execution: attesting witnesses' testimony, contemporaneous medical assessments, the testator's own conduct and communications around that time, and the drafting lawyer's file notes and recollections.

Who has the burden of proving testamentary capacity?

The propounder of the Will generally carries the initial burden, but once due execution and attestation are proved, courts generally presume capacity unless the challenger produces genuine evidence raising real doubt, not mere suspicion.

How can a testator protect a Will from a future capacity challenge?

By obtaining a contemporaneous medical capacity assessment at the time of execution, particularly if elderly or seriously ill, and by ensuring the drafting process is properly documented, including private conversations with the testator away from any potentially influencing beneficiary.

References & Further Reading

This guide provides a general overview of testamentary capacity disputes. Outcomes are highly fact-specific and depend on the quality and timing of available evidence, and both testators and families anticipating a dispute should seek tailored advice.
