An Income Tax Search Team Has Arrived: What Section 132 Actually Lets Them Do

What an income tax search team can and cannot do under Section 132 (Section 247 from 1 April 2026), and what happens to seized cash and jewellery.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A search under the income tax law is not a sudden event that arrives without any legal basis, even though it often feels that way to the person standing at the door. The power is Section 132 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, and from 1 April 2026 the same power sits in Section 247 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, which the government has described as carrying the existing framework forward with updated language for digital records rather than creating a new power. A search can only be authorised when a competent officer has information and has recorded a genuine, reasoned belief that one of the statutory conditions exists, such as a person being unlikely to produce documents if asked, or holding undisclosed income or assets.

What follows below is written as on 28 November 2026, after the new Act has come into force and after the Supreme Court declined, in March 2026, to interfere with the basic design of this power. It covers what the search party must show you, what they can take, what happens to cash and jewellery that gets seized, and where an aggrieved person can actually push back.

Who can order a search, and on what basis

A search is authorised in writing by an officer of at least the rank of Joint Director, Joint Commissioner, Director or Commissioner, acting on information in their possession. The authorising officer must have reason to believe, not merely reason to suspect, that one of the specified situations exists, for instance that a person summoned to produce documents has not done so or is unlikely to, or that undisclosed money, bullion, jewellery or other valuable article representing income not disclosed for tax is kept at a particular place. This belief has to rest on tangible material already on record, not a hunch, though in practice the reasons recorded are rarely shown to the person searched at the time.

The warrant of authorisation names the premises and, generally, the authorised officers who will conduct the search. The officers executing the search are required to identify themselves, show their official identity cards, and produce the warrant before beginning. A person at the premises is entitled to see that the warrant exists and covers that address, even if the full reasons behind it are not disclosed immediately.

Courts have in the past struck down searches where the recorded reasons showed no live link between the information available and the conclusion drawn, but challenging the validity of the authorisation itself, rather than what was found during the search, is a narrow and fact-heavy argument that usually has to wait until the resulting assessment is challenged in appeal.

What happens during the search, practically

The search team can enter and search the building, place, vessel, vehicle or aircraft named in the warrant, break open a door, box, locker or safe if the keys are not produced, and search any person who has left or is about to enter the premises if there is reason to suspect concealment. Every item found and every item seized has to be recorded in an inventory, usually called a panchnama, prepared in the presence of witnesses and signed by those present; a copy is given to the person from whose custody the material is taken.

The occupant or a representative is entitled to remain present through the search and may call a chartered accountant or an advocate to be present, though the search does not have to pause and wait for that person to arrive. Statements may be recorded during the search, and while staying silent can invite an adverse inference being drawn later, nobody can be coerced into signing a confession of undisclosed income or assets, and any statement extracted under duress or at an unreasonable hour is open to challenge when the assessment is contested.

The law also allows the authorised officer to seal a room, almirah or container that cannot be searched immediately, and in a digital search to require technical assistance to access a computer, server or cloud account so that data stored there can be examined, rather than only what is physically present on the device.

What can be seized, and what cannot

Cash, bullion, jewellery and other valuable articles found during the search can be seized if they represent undisclosed income, along with books of account, documents and, since the law's digital-era expansion, electronically stored information. Stock-in-trade of a running business is ordinarily not meant to be seized outright, though it can be inventoried.

Once seized, the law lets the department apply this cash, bullion or jewellery against an existing tax liability of the person searched, under the provision now generally referred to as Section 132B. This is where a common misunderstanding arises: an explanation inserted into the law with effect from 1 June 2013 makes clear that an existing liability for this purpose does not include advance tax payable under the ordinary instalment scheme. Before that amendment the point was contested, with at least one tribunal taking the opposite view, but the position since 2013 is that seized assets cannot simply be treated as having discharged an advance tax instalment that had not yet fallen due.

If the value of what is seized exceeds the liability it is applied against, the balance has to be released, and where money is retained beyond what the law allows, interest runs in the taxpayer's favour on the excess. Where jewellery or valuables are retained pending a fuller assessment, the explanation given for ownership and source is examined at that stage, and parts of a claim can be accepted while other parts are not.

The Supreme Court's March 2026 ruling on search powers

In March 2026 the Supreme Court declined to entertain a challenge to the constitutional validity of the search provisions, including the extension of search powers to digital devices and cloud accounts without prior notice to the person concerned. The petitioner had argued that proceeding without advance notice, and without disclosing the reasons recorded for a search, made meaningful judicial review impossible and breached principles of natural justice.

The bench's reasoning, reported consistently across legal news outlets, was that giving advance notice before a search would allow the very evidence the search is meant to uncover to be destroyed or moved, defeating the purpose of the power. The Court is reported to have allowed the petitioner to make a representation to the government on the digital-search questions rather than deciding those points itself. The effect, at least for now, is that the basic structure of search without prior notice, including digital search, stands; anyone wanting to challenge a specific search still has to do so on the facts of that search, such as whether the recorded reasons actually support it or whether the search exceeded what the warrant authorised, rather than by attacking the power as such.

This matters for anyone advising a business or a family that has been searched: a frontal challenge to the law itself is very unlikely to succeed on the current precedent, so the realistic battleground is the facts of the particular search, the statements recorded, and the assessment that follows.

What a person searched should actually do

During the search itself, verify the warrant covers the correct premises, note the names and designations of the officers present, insist that a panchnama is actually prepared and that a copy is handed over, and keep a copy of any statement recorded along with a note of when it was taken. If documents are needed to keep a business running, Section 132(9) allows the person searched to apply for and obtain copies of seized documents for that purpose, a right that is sometimes overlooked in the stress of the moment.

After the search, the assessment that follows a search is typically a block assessment covering the relevant years, and the person searched has the ordinary right to object to additions made, to explain the source of cash or jewellery found, and to carry the matter in appeal to the Commissioner and then the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal if the explanation is rejected. An order passed under the provision dealing with retention of seized assets can itself be challenged before the Commissioner.

None of this is a reason to resist or obstruct a search in the moment; resistance only adds separate legal trouble on top of whatever the search itself uncovers. The leverage available to the person searched lies almost entirely in the paperwork, in what gets recorded contemporaneously, and in the assessment and appeal that follow once the dust has settled.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an income tax search happen without any notice at all?

Yes. The law does not require advance notice before a search, and the Supreme Court in March 2026 declined to strike down that design, reasoning that advance notice would let evidence be moved or destroyed before officers arrive. What the law does require is a written warrant, recorded reasons held by the authorising officer, and a search actually conducted within the scope that warrant allows.

Can the department keep seized cash permanently?

No. Seized cash, bullion or jewellery can be applied only against an existing tax liability, not an advance tax instalment that was not yet due at the time of seizure, a position settled by a 2013 amendment to the law. Anything in excess of what can lawfully be adjusted has to be released, with interest running on amounts retained beyond what the law permits.

Is a statement recorded during a search binding?

A statement recorded during a search is evidence that can be used, and silence can invite an adverse inference, but a person cannot lawfully be coerced into confessing to undisclosed income. A statement taken under pressure, at an unreasonable hour, or without being allowed a reasonable opportunity to consult a representative is open to being challenged when the resulting assessment is contested.

Did Section 132 disappear when the new Income-tax Act, 2025 came into force?

The numbering changed but the power did not disappear. From 1 April 2026 the Income-tax Act, 2025 governs, and its Section 247 carries forward the search and seizure framework that sat in Section 132 of the 1961 Act, with the government stating that it did not create new powers beyond updated handling of digital records. A search that began before 1 April 2026 is generally dealt with under the 1961 Act.

Can a business get its seized stock or documents back to keep operating?

The law allows a person searched to apply for copies of seized documents needed to continue business, so day-to-day operations are not meant to grind to a halt for want of paperwork. Stock-in-trade is ordinarily inventoried rather than seized outright, though this depends heavily on what the search team concludes at the time.
