Guardianship of a Minor's Property: The Guardians and Wards Act Explained

Managing property inherited by or belonging to a minor isn't always as simple as a parent stepping in: this guide explains when a natural guardian's authority is enough and when court appointment is required.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Natural Guardianship Has Real Limits

A minor's parents are, in the ordinary course, their natural guardians: for a Hindu minor, this follows from Section 6 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, which recognises the father, and in certain circumstances the mother, as natural guardian of both the minor's person and property (subject to constitutional and judicial developments giving mothers a stronger and more equal footing than the bare text of the older provision might suggest). What surprises many parents is that natural guardianship, while real, is not unlimited when it comes to the minor's property: a natural guardian generally cannot mortgage, charge, or transfer by sale, gift, exchange or otherwise any part of the minor's immovable property, nor lease it for a term exceeding five years, without the previous permission of the court.

When Court Permission Is Required

This restriction, set out in Section 8 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act (and reflected in analogous form for guardianship generally under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890), exists to protect a minor's property from being dissipated or mismanaged, even by a well-meaning parent, without independent court oversight for the more significant transactions. A parent wanting to sell a minor's inherited flat to fund the minor's education, for instance, cannot simply do so as natural guardian; they must apply to the court for permission, satisfy the court that the transaction is genuinely for the minor's benefit, and obtain an order before the sale can proceed. A transaction completed without the required permission is voidable at the instance of the minor, who can challenge it after reaching majority.

When a Formal Guardian Appointment Is Needed

Beyond the natural guardian's own limited authority, a formal application under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 for appointment of a guardian of the minor's property becomes necessary in several common situations: where both parents have died or are otherwise unavailable to act, requiring the court to appoint someone else (often a relative) as guardian; where a testator's Will names a testamentary guardian for a minor beneficiary's property, whose appointment still generally requires confirmation or at least registration with the court in the relevant circumstances; or where a dispute arises among family members about who should manage a minor's property, requiring the court to decide. The Guardians and Wards Act sets out the court's power to appoint, and to define the scope of, a guardian's authority over a minor's person, property, or both, guided throughout by the minor's welfare as the paramount consideration.

What a Court-Appointed Guardian Can and Cannot Do

A guardian appointed under the Guardians and Wards Act manages the minor's property subject to the court's continuing oversight: the guardian is typically required to file periodic accounts with the court, and significant transactions involving the minor's immovable property generally still require specific court permission, similar to the restriction that applies to a natural guardian. This continuing oversight is a deliberate feature, not a bureaucratic inconvenience: it exists because a guardian, however trusted, is managing property that legally belongs to someone else who cannot yet look after their own interests, and the court's role is to stand in for that absent oversight until the minor reaches majority.

Practical Guidance for Parents and Relatives

A parent or relative managing everyday matters for a minor, school fees, routine expenses, small bank transactions, generally does not need court intervention for that ordinary day-to-day management. The moment a transaction involves selling, mortgaging, or long-term leasing of the minor's immovable property, or managing a substantial inheritance or investment portfolio on the minor's behalf, the guardian should assume court involvement will likely be required, and plan the timeline for any transaction accordingly, since obtaining court permission is not always fast. Where a testator is drafting a Will for a minor beneficiary, naming a testamentary guardian explicitly, and considering whether a testamentary trust (discussed in our companion guide) might avoid some of these procedural steps for at least a portion of the estate, is worth discussing at the drafting stage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a parent sell a minor child's inherited property without court permission?

No, not for immovable property. Under Section 8 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, a natural guardian cannot sell, mortgage, gift or lease (beyond five years) a minor's immovable property without prior court permission, even acting in the minor's own interest.

What happens to a transaction of a minor's property made without required court permission?

It is voidable at the instance of the minor, who can challenge and potentially undo the transaction after reaching majority, even if the transaction appeared to be in the minor's interest at the time.

When does a formal guardian need to be appointed under the Guardians and Wards Act?

Commonly where both parents are unavailable to act as natural guardians, where a Will names a testamentary guardian requiring court confirmation, or where family members dispute who should manage a minor's property: the court then appoints a guardian guided by the minor's welfare as the paramount consideration.

Does a court-appointed guardian have unrestricted control over a minor's property?

No: a court-appointed guardian remains subject to continuing court oversight, generally including periodic accounting requirements and the need for specific court permission before significant transactions involving the minor's immovable property.

Does managing a minor's everyday expenses require court involvement?

No: routine, day-to-day management such as school fees and ordinary expenses generally does not require court intervention; the restrictions apply specifically to significant transactions in the minor's immovable property or substantial investment assets.

References & Further Reading

This guide provides a general overview of guardianship of a minor's property. The applicable rules can vary by the minor's personal law and specific family circumstances, and formal guardianship applications should be handled with tailored legal advice.

  1. Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, Sections 6 and 8: natural guardianship and its limits over a minor's property.
  2. Guardians and Wards Act, 1890: court's power to appoint and oversee a guardian of a minor's person or property.
