Right of First Refusal in a Shareholders' Agreement: Is It Actually Enforceable in India

Whether a right of first refusal clause in a shareholders' agreement holds up in India, what the Messer Holdings ruling settled, and the difference for private and public companies.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A right of first refusal clause lets an existing shareholder step in and buy another shareholder's stake on the same terms before that stake can be sold to an outsider, and almost every shareholders' agreement drafted for a joint venture, a family business bringing in outside capital, or a foreign investor's India entry contains one. For years Indian lawyers drafted these clauses while carrying real doubt about whether a court would actually enforce them, because of a long running tension with the idea that shares, especially in a public company, are supposed to be freely transferable.

That doubt was substantially settled by the Bombay High Court's Division Bench ruling in Messer Holdings Ltd v Shyam Madanmohan Ruia, decided on 1 September 2010, which held that a right of first refusal agreed between shareholders of a public company does not violate the statutory rule on free transferability. The ruling remains the anchor case cited whenever this question comes up, even though the position for a private company, and the exact scope of the statutory provision that now deals with it, still leave room for genuine argument.

Why the question arose in the first place

The old Section 111A of the Companies Act, 1956 declared shares of a public company freely transferable, a rule meant to protect the liquidity that public shareholding depends on. A contract between shareholders restricting who they could sell to, on its face, looks like it cuts against that free transferability, and that tension is exactly what produced years of conflicting commentary and litigation before Messer Holdings was decided.

The specific dispute in Messer Holdings arose out of a Share Purchase Agreement dated 23 June 1997, under which the controlling shareholders of Bombay Oxygen Ltd, a public listed company, had agreed to divest a majority shareholding to Messer Griesheim GmbH. When Messer Griesheim later tried to transfer its stake to Messer Holdings Limited, a joint venture vehicle it had set up with another partner, the other side resisted on the ground that the arrangement restricting the transfer was void against the free transferability rule.

What the Bombay High Court actually decided

The Division Bench held that a private arrangement between shareholders of a public limited company restricting share transfer, including a right of first refusal, is not rendered void by Section 111A, and that a restriction of this kind is enforceable between the parties who agreed to it unless the company's own articles bar it. The ruling drew a distinction between the company's shares being freely transferable as against the world at large, which Section 111A protects, and two shareholders privately agreeing between themselves how they will deal with their own shares, which the court treated as a separate question the statute was never meant to override.

Notably, the Division Bench reached this conclusion only months after the same High Court, in the Western Maharashtra Development Corporation line of reasoning commonly discussed alongside the Bajaj Auto dispute, had taken a narrower view of similar restrictions. Commentators describe Messer Holdings as effectively overruling that earlier, more restrictive position, which is part of why the ruling is treated as the decisive word on the question rather than just one view among several conflicting High Court decisions.

Where the law stands for a public company today

The Companies Act, 2013 carried this debate into its own text through Section 58(2), which now expressly deals with contracts or arrangements between two or more shareholders of a public company for transferring securities, including rights of pre-emption. Commentary treats this as having made explicit, in the statute itself, what Messer Holdings and the Western Maharashtra reasoning had already worked out through case law under the 1956 Act's Section 111A, so a right of first refusal between public company shareholders now has a clearer statutory anchor than it did when Messer Holdings was argued.

That said, legal commentary continues to flag the proviso to Section 58(2) as carrying genuine ambiguity about exactly what kinds of pre-emption arrangements it protects and how far it goes, and academic writing on the point describes the drafting as inviting both conflicting judicial interpretation and continued contractual experimentation by parties trying to draft around the uncertainty. A SEBI notification is also cited in commentary as clarifying, around 2013, that pre-emption contracts including rights of first refusal do not themselves need SEBI's separate permission, though this point was not independently confirmed here against the notification's own text.

Why private companies sit on different footing

A private company is in a structurally different position because the Companies Act itself contemplates, and indeed requires, that a private company's articles restrict the transfer of its shares, which is part of what makes it private rather than public in the first place. Commentary notes that the Act requires a private company to have a transfer restriction but is silent on what form that restriction has to take, and identifies a right of pre-emption and a right of first refusal as the two most commonly used mechanisms to satisfy that requirement in practice.

This structural difference is a large part of why a right of first refusal is rarely controversial for a private company's own articles, and why most of the hard litigation on enforceability has concerned public companies instead, where the statute's default position favours free transferability rather than requiring a restriction. Where disputes do arise for a private company, they tend to turn on drafting questions, such as whether the restriction sits in the articles themselves or only in a separate shareholders' agreement the articles do not incorporate, rather than on the basic question Messer Holdings settled for public companies.

What this means for drafting a shareholders' agreement

For a foreign investor or an Indian promoter negotiating a joint venture or a family settlement, Messer Holdings gives real comfort that a right of first refusal clause agreed between shareholders of a public company will not be struck down purely on free transferability grounds, provided the company's own articles do not independently prohibit the restriction. That comfort does not extend automatically to every drafting choice, and the continuing uncertainty around the exact scope of the Section 58(2) proviso means a clause that tries to do something unusual, rather than a standard pre-emption mechanism, still carries some litigation risk.

The practical lesson drawn from this history is to make sure a right of first refusal or similar pre-emption clause is reflected consistently between the shareholders' agreement and the company's articles of association, rather than left to sit only in the contract between the parties. A restriction that appears in both documents, drafted to track the statutory language rather than invent new mechanics, gives the clause the best chance of being read the way Messer Holdings read the Bombay Oxygen arrangement, as a private contractual bargain the company's shareholders are free to make rather than an attack on the company's own transferability rule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a right of first refusal clause enforceable in an Indian public company?

The Bombay High Court's Division Bench in Messer Holdings Ltd v Shyam Madanmohan Ruia, decided on 1 September 2010, held that such a clause between shareholders of a public company does not violate the statutory rule on free transferability and is enforceable between the parties who agreed to it, unless the company's own articles independently bar it.

Does the Companies Act, 2013 deal with this directly?

Section 58(2) of the Companies Act, 2013 expressly addresses contracts or arrangements between shareholders of a public company for transferring securities, including rights of pre-emption, treating what case law had already worked out as now reflected in the statute itself. Commentary still flags genuine ambiguity in how the proviso to this subsection is worded.

Why is this less of an issue for a private company?

The Companies Act itself requires a private company's articles to restrict share transfer, which is part of what defines it as private. A right of pre-emption or right of first refusal is one of the common ways that restriction is drafted, so the basic enforceability question Messer Holdings settled for public companies rarely arises the same way for a private company.

Does it matter whether the right of first refusal is only in the shareholders' agreement and not the articles?

It can. Disputes for private companies have tended to turn on exactly this kind of drafting question, including whether a restriction sitting only in a shareholders' agreement, without being reflected in the articles, binds the company and later transferees the same way a restriction written into the articles themselves would.

Should a foreign investor rely on Messer Holdings for every kind of pre-emption clause?

The ruling gives comfort for a standard right of first refusal between shareholders of a public company, but commentary continues to describe the scope of the related statutory proviso as unsettled for unusual or non-standard mechanics. A clause that departs from the standard pre-emption structure should be reviewed specifically rather than assumed to be covered by the same reasoning.
