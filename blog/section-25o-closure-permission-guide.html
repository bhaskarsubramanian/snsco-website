Government Sat on Your Closure Application? Why Silence Can Count as Approval Under Section 25-O

What Section 25-O requires to close a large establishment, and what the Harinagar Sugar Mills Supreme Court ruling changed on delay.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An employer running a factory or large establishment with 100 or more workmen cannot simply announce a closure and shut the gates. Chapter V-B of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 requires prior government permission before such a closure, and for decades this has been a genuine bottleneck, with applications sitting for months without a reasoned order from either a labour department or state government that has little incentive to approve a closure affecting local employment. The June 2025 Supreme Court ruling in Harinagar Sugar Mills Ltd. (Biscuit Division) v. State of Maharashtra addressed exactly this problem, and gave employers a concrete tool where the government simply does not respond.

This guide sets out what Section 25-O actually requires, what the Court decided about silence and delegation, and what an employer stuck waiting on a closure application should actually do.

What Section 25-O requires before you can close

Section 25-O applies to an industrial establishment covered by Chapter V-B, which broadly means one employing 100 or more workmen on average over the preceding twelve months, though some states have raised this threshold through their own amendments, so the applicable state position should be checked first. An employer intending to close such an establishment must apply for permission to the appropriate government, ordinarily 90 days before the intended date of closure, stating clearly and specifically the reasons for the intended closure.

A copy of the application has to be served on the workmen's representatives, and the appropriate government is required to make such inquiry as it thinks fit, give a reasonable opportunity of being heard to the employer, the workmen and any other persons interested, and then pass a reasoned order either granting or refusing permission, having regard to factors including the genuineness of the reasons for closure, the interests of the workmen, and the balance of interests between the employer, the workmen and the broader public interest.

The statute builds in a time limit on the government's own decision. If the government does not communicate its order within the prescribed period, running from the date the application was made, the law provides a deemed approval, meaning the permission applied for is treated as having been granted on the expiry of that period. This deemed-approval mechanism exists precisely because an employer cannot otherwise be held hostage to administrative delay indefinitely.

What Harinagar Sugar Mills actually decided

Harinagar Sugar Mills, running a biscuit division, applied for permission to close that unit and the government did not pass a timely, reasoned order granting or refusing it within the statutory window. The employer argued this triggered the deemed-approval provision, while the State resisted on the basis that some communication, through an official other than the designated decision-maker, had in fact been sent within time.

The Supreme Court, in a bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and P.K. Mishra, accepted the employer's position. It held that only the appropriate government, meaning the designated authority such as the state's Labour Minister or the officer specifically empowered to decide such applications, can grant or refuse permission under Section 25-O, and that this power cannot be informally sub-delegated to a lower officer who then communicates something purporting to be a decision. Where the decision-making power was not validly exercised by the correct authority within the statutory period, the Court treated this as equivalent to no order having been passed at all, triggering the deemed approval.

The Court also held that internal government file notings, even where they record some consideration of the application, cannot substitute for an actual, properly issued, reasoned order communicated to the employer. An employer cannot be left to establish what the government privately thought from its internal files; the law requires a formal order, from the right authority, communicated within the window, and anything short of that does not stop the deemed-approval clock from running out.

The constitutional dimension the Court also addressed

Beyond the specific statutory question, the Court reaffirmed that the right to close a business is an aspect of the freedom to carry on a trade, occupation or business under Article 19(1)(g) of the Constitution. This does not mean an employer has an unrestricted right to shut down regardless of consequences; Article 19(6) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of the general public, and Section 25-O's permission requirement is itself an example of such a restriction that the Court has previously upheld as constitutionally valid.

What the ruling does is calibrate how that restriction actually operates in practice: the government can scrutinise, inquire into, and finally refuse a closure application for good reason, but it cannot achieve a de facto refusal simply by sitting on the application past the statutory deadline, since that outcome defeats both the deemed-approval mechanism the legislature built in and the underlying constitutional freedom the restriction is supposed to balance rather than extinguish.

This framing matters for how employers argue future cases, since it gives a constitutional backing to the statutory argument about delay and non-communication, rather than leaving the point purely as a technical question of administrative procedure.

What this means for an employer with a pending application

An employer whose closure application has sat beyond the statutory period without a reasoned order from the correctly designated authority now has a stronger basis to treat the closure as deemed-approved and proceed, rather than continuing to wait indefinitely for a decision that may never formally come. This is a meaningful practical shift, because employers have historically been reluctant to act on a deemed approval without a court first confirming it, given the risk that workers would challenge the closure as unauthorised and claim reinstatement and back wages.

That said, acting on a deemed approval still carries litigation risk if the facts are disputed, for instance over exactly when the application was received, whether any communication from a validly empowered officer was in fact issued within time, or whether the application itself met the content requirements Section 25-O specifies. An employer relying on deemed approval should keep a clear, dated record of the application's filing, service on workmen's representatives, and the complete absence of any valid order within the statutory period, since this evidentiary record is exactly what a court will examine if the closure is challenged later.

Employers should also not assume this ruling removes the retrenchment compensation and notice obligations that accompany a lawful closure under Sections 25-N, 25-F and related provisions. Deemed approval under Section 25-O addresses only the permission requirement; it does not excuse the separate compensation and notice obligations owed to affected workmen on closure.

Why workmen's representatives should not assume silence means nothing happens

From the workmen's side, this ruling is a reminder that a closure application left unanswered by the government is not a safe, indefinite state of affairs; the statutory clock runs regardless of whether the workmen's union is actively pressing the government for a decision. A union or workmen's representative aware of a pending closure application should push the labour department for a timely, reasoned decision rather than assume delay favours the workers, since delay past the statutory window can now favour the employer through deemed approval.

Where a closure is later claimed to have been deemed-approved, workmen's representatives who believe the application itself was deficient, for instance because it did not state genuine reasons, or because service on the workmen's representatives was defective, should raise those specific deficiencies promptly, since the strength of a challenge to a deemed closure depends heavily on pointing to a concrete defect in the application or the process, not simply on arguing that a closure should not have been allowed as a matter of general fairness.

Both sides, in other words, have an interest in treating the Section 25-O timeline seriously rather than as a formality, since the consequence of the government's own delay can now cut decisively in the employer's favour.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the statutory period within which the government must decide a Section 25-O closure application?

Section 25-O prescribes a period running from the date of the application within which the appropriate government must communicate its order; if no valid order is communicated within that period, the permission applied for is deemed to have been granted. The exact number of days should be checked against the current text of Section 25-O and any state amendment, since some states have modified Chapter V-B provisions.

Can a junior labour department officer validly communicate the government's decision on a closure application?

Not according to the Harinagar Sugar Mills ruling, unless that officer has been properly and validly empowered as the appropriate government for this specific purpose. A decision communicated by an officer without that authority is not treated as a valid order, and does not stop the deemed-approval period from running out.

If my closure is deemed approved because the government missed the deadline, am I excused from paying retrenchment compensation to affected workers?

No. Deemed approval under Section 25-O only addresses the permission requirement for closure. The separate statutory obligations to pay compensation and give notice to affected workmen, under provisions like Sections 25-F and 25-N of the Industrial Disputes Act, continue to apply independently of how permission was obtained.

Does the Constitution give an employer an unrestricted right to close a business whenever it wants?

No. The Supreme Court has held that closing a business is part of the freedom to carry on an occupation under Article 19(1)(g), but this is subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(6), and Section 25-O's permission requirement is itself a valid restriction the government can use to scrutinise and, for good reason, refuse a closure application.
