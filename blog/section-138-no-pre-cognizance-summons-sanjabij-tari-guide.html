Cheque Bounce Complaints: Why the Accused No Longer Gets a Pre-Cognizance Hearing

The Supreme Court's September 2025 ruling in Sanjabij Tari removed the pre-cognizance notice step the BNSS otherwise requires in NI Act complaints.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Section 223 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 generally requires a magistrate to give the accused an opportunity to be heard before taking cognizance of a complaint on a private complaint case. Applied literally to a Section 138 Negotiable Instruments Act cheque dishonour complaint, this would have meant an accused drawer getting a hearing even before the magistrate formally decided to proceed with the case at all, adding an extra procedural step to litigation that Parliament has tried repeatedly to speed up.

The Supreme Court closed this question in Sanjabij Tari v. Kishore S. Borcar, decided on 25 September 2025, holding that no such pre-cognizance summons or hearing is required in a Section 138 complaint. This guide sets out what the Court actually held, why, and what it means for how a cheque dishonour complaint now proceeds from filing to the issue of process.

The procedural problem the ruling resolved

Section 223(1) of the BNSS, which replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure from 1 July 2024, carries a proviso requiring that the person complained against be given an opportunity of being heard before a magistrate takes cognizance of a complaint. Read on its own terms, this provision would appear to create a pre-cognizance hearing stage even for a routine Section 138 complaint, which traditionally proceeded straight from the complaint and the complainant's sworn statement to the magistrate's decision on cognizance and, if satisfied, the issue of summons to the accused.

Different High Courts had begun to take different views on whether this BNSS proviso genuinely applied to Section 138 proceedings, creating exactly the kind of procedural uncertainty and scope for delay that the interim compensation and appellate deposit provisions added to the Negotiable Instruments Act in 2018 were specifically meant to reduce. The Supreme Court took up the question in Sanjabij Tari to resolve this split and set a uniform national position.

The stakes in getting this wrong were not trivial for either side. A complainant facing an added pre-cognizance hearing stage would see the already-criticised slowness of cheque dishonour litigation extended further at the very first procedural step, before the case had even formally begun in the magistrate's eyes. An accused, on the other hand, stood to gain a genuine, if narrow, additional opportunity to argue against cognizance being taken at all, which is exactly the kind of extra procedural leverage that made the question worth litigating all the way to the Supreme Court rather than letting divergent High Court views simply persist.

What the Supreme Court actually held

The Court, in a judgment reported as 2025 INSC 1158, agreed with the view the Karnataka High Court had taken in Ashok v. Fayaz Aahmad, and directed that there is no requirement to issue summons to, or hear, the accused under Section 223 of the BNSS at the pre-cognizance stage of a Section 138 Negotiable Instruments Act complaint. The practical effect is that a magistrate dealing with a cheque dishonour complaint can proceed to decide on cognizance based on the complaint and the complainant's sworn statement, without first issuing a separate notice calling on the accused to be heard on whether cognizance should be taken at all.

The Court's stated rationale, as reported in commentary on the ruling, was to avoid building an unnecessary procedural step into the process before the magistrate has even formally taken cognizance, consistent with the broader object of keeping Section 138 litigation, which already moves through a private complaint, a summons or summary trial, and the specific interim compensation and appellate deposit provisions discussed elsewhere in cheque dishonour procedure, from accumulating further delay at its very earliest stage.

How later courts have applied the ruling

At least one High Court has since relied on Sanjabij Tari to hold that the pre-cognizance hearing an accused might otherwise expect under Section 223 BNSS is dispensed with specifically because Section 138 proceedings are governed by the Negotiable Instruments Act as a special statute, reinforcing that the ruling's effect is to carve Section 138 complaints out of the general BNSS procedure on this specific point rather than to read down Section 223 for criminal complaints generally.

The position is not entirely free of friction even after Sanjabij Tari: at least one later Delhi High Court judgment records an argument that the proviso to Section 223(1) BNSS still requires notice to the accused before cognizance, referencing an earlier Delhi ruling that had taken that view. Whether that argument was accepted, and how the Delhi High Court reconciled it with the Supreme Court's ruling in Sanjabij Tari, was not something that could be confirmed from the material available, so a practitioner relying on this point in a live Delhi matter should check the most current Delhi High Court position specifically rather than assume Sanjabij Tari has fully settled every variant of the argument.

What this means for drafting and timing a complaint

A complainant filing a Section 138 complaint no longer needs to build in, or worry about, a separate pre-cognizance hearing stage for the accused before the magistrate decides whether to take cognizance; the complaint can be presented, the complainant examined, and cognizance decided on that material in the ordinary course, consistent with how such complaints were handled before the BNSS provision raised the question in the first place.

An accused who has previously argued, or seen others argue, that a Section 138 complaint is procedurally defective for proceeding straight to cognizance without a prior hearing under Section 223 BNSS should not expect that argument to succeed following Sanjabij Tari, at least outside a jurisdiction where a specific, unresolved local ruling genuinely complicates the point, such as the open Delhi High Court argument noted above. The accused's substantive defences on the merits, including the presumption under Section 139 and any genuine dispute about the underlying debt, are entirely unaffected by this ruling and remain available at trial in the ordinary way.

Where this fits among the other post-BNSS questions in Section 138 litigation

The shift from the Code of Criminal Procedure to the BNSS from 1 July 2024 raised a number of transitional questions for Section 138 litigation beyond the pre-cognizance point, including how existing complaints filed under the old Code's numbering should be treated and how various procedural cross-references in the Negotiable Instruments Act, which still refer to the old Code in places, should be read against the new Sanhita. Sanjabij Tari resolves one specific, recurring piece of that broader transitional puzzle rather than the whole of it.

A litigant or practitioner dealing with a Section 138 matter that straddles the 1 July 2024 changeover should treat the pre-cognizance question as settled by this ruling, while still checking separately whether any other BNSS transitional issue specific to their own complaint, such as which procedural code applied to steps taken before that date, has been independently resolved for their jurisdiction, since a single Supreme Court ruling on one procedural point does not automatically settle every other transitional question the changeover raised.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does an accused in a cheque bounce case get a hearing before the magistrate takes cognizance?

No, following the Supreme Court's ruling in Sanjabij Tari v. Kishore S. Borcar (2025 INSC 1158, decided 25 September 2025). The Court held that the pre-cognizance hearing requirement under Section 223 of the BNSS does not apply to Section 138 Negotiable Instruments Act complaints.

Why did this issue arise in the first place?

Section 223(1) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, which replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure from 1 July 2024, has a proviso requiring an opportunity of being heard before cognizance on a complaint, which some courts read as applying even to routine Section 138 cheque dishonour complaints before Sanjabij Tari resolved the question.

Does this ruling affect an accused's defences at trial?

No. Sanjabij Tari addresses only the procedural question of whether a pre-cognizance hearing is required. Substantive defences, including challenging the presumption under Section 139 or disputing the underlying debt, remain fully available to the accused at trial regardless of this ruling.

Is the position settled in every High Court now?

Largely, but not without some residual friction. At least one later Delhi High Court judgment has recorded an argument that notice to the accused is still required under the Section 223(1) proviso, so a practitioner in a Delhi matter should check the current local position rather than assume the question is closed everywhere without qualification.

What is the practical effect for someone filing a Section 138 complaint today?

The complaint can proceed from filing and the complainant's sworn statement directly to the magistrate's decision on cognizance, without a separate step where the accused is given a pre-cognizance hearing, which keeps the complaint moving at the pace Section 138 procedure generally intends.
