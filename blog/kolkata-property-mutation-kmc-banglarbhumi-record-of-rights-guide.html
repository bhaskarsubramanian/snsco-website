Mutating a Property in Kolkata: KMC Assessment, Banglarbhumi and the Record of Rights

How property mutation works in Kolkata under the KMC Act and through banglarbhumi for the rest of West Bengal, and why mutation does not prove ownership.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Kolkata runs two separate mutation systems, and most buyers only discover this after they have already dealt with one and assumed it covers the other. Inside the Kolkata Municipal Corporation's jurisdiction, mutation is tied to the municipal assessment of the property for property tax under the Kolkata Municipal Corporation Act, 1980. Outside that area, across the rest of West Bengal, mutation of the record of rights runs through the Land and Land Reforms Department under the West Bengal Land Reforms Act, 1955, administered today through the banglarbhumi portal. A buyer dealing with land at the edge of Kolkata may need to engage with both systems, the KMC for tax assessment and name change, and the land revenue system for the underlying record of rights.

Neither system decides who owns the property. Mutation, on both tracks, is a revenue and fiscal exercise: it tells the municipal or revenue authority who to bill and who to treat as being in possession for administrative purposes. Courts have said this repeatedly and in blunt terms, and a buyer who treats a mutated name as proof of title is making a mistake this guide tries to prevent. Our guide on Mutation of Property Records sets out the general, state-neutral version of this point; what follows here is specific to how Kolkata and West Bengal actually run the process.

Mutation within Kolkata Municipal Corporation: assessment and the Section 183 route

Within KMC's area, mutation is tied closely to the municipal assessment of the property under the Kolkata Municipal Corporation Act, 1980. A buyer, legal heir, or anyone else acquiring an interest applies to have the assessment register updated so that future property tax bills and notices go out in the new name, commonly called municipal mutation locally even though the Act's own language is built around assessment and the valuation register. The application is usually supported by the deed or other document showing the transfer, proof of identity, and the property's existing assessment particulars.

Section 183 of the Act governs this process, and a proviso to sub-section (5) specifically bars the Corporation from withholding mutation purely because arrear dues are outstanding on the property, though the same provision preserves the Corporation's separate power to refuse mutation where arrears actually exist and remain unaddressed. Calcutta High Court rulings on this point are not uniform: some hold the Corporation cannot withhold mutation for non-payment of arrears at all, while others hold the Corporation can still refuse mutation where arrears attributable to the transferor or a predecessor-in-interest remain unpaid. A buyer should clear outstanding property tax before applying rather than rely on the bar in the proviso to force mutation through while dues remain open.

Revising the assessment after mutation

Mutation is often followed, sooner or later, by a revision of the annual valuation on which property tax is calculated, particularly where the transfer involved a change in use or where the Corporation's self-assessment scheme under Section 182A applies. A revised assessment under Section 180 takes effect only prospectively, from the beginning of the quarter following the one in which the revision is made, so a new owner cannot be billed retrospectively at a higher valuation merely because the name on the register has changed. The Commissioner can also assess suo motu under Section 185 where a required self-assessment return was not filed, a separate trigger worth watching for in older properties bought from an owner who may not have kept filings current.

Objections to a valuation, whether arrived at through self-assessment or a Corporation-led revision, go through the objection procedure the Act provides, and courts treat that procedure as something the Corporation has to actually follow rather than a formality it can skip before raising a demand. A new owner who disputes a valuation inherited along with the mutated assessment should use this objection route promptly rather than simply refusing to pay, since unpaid demands accumulate interest and can themselves become a ground to resist mutation on a later, different transaction.

Mutation outside KMC's area: the West Bengal Land Reforms Act and banglarbhumi

For land outside Kolkata Municipal Corporation's jurisdiction, and for the underlying record of rights more generally, mutation of names runs under Section 50 of the West Bengal Land Reforms Act, 1955. The application is decided by the Revenue Officer attached to the Block Land and Land Reforms Office, the prescribed authority for this purpose, and the triggering event is usually a transfer by sale, gift or hebanama, an exchange, or succession on the death of the recorded owner. The applicant files the relevant deed or succession document along with the existing record-of-rights particulars at the Block office or through the banglarbhumi portal, which today is the primary interface for tracking, and in many cases initiating, these applications online.

Once mutation is allowed, the updated record of rights can be downloaded from the banglarbhumi portal, and that document becomes the current record the applicant relies on for future dealings with the land. Timelines for a clean, undisputed application vary in practice, and a chain of inheritance with multiple legal heirs or an unclear earlier record typically takes considerably longer than a straightforward sale-based mutation. A buyer should ask the seller to show an already-mutated record wherever possible rather than relying on an application still pending at the time of purchase.

Correcting the record of rights, as opposed to a routine mutation

A straightforward mutation, changing the recorded name after a sale or inheritance, differs from correcting an error already in the record of rights, a wrong area, a misspelt name, an entry never updated after an earlier transaction. Section 51A of the Act empowers a revising officer to correct the record after hearing the persons interested, and this route is used where the dispute concerns the accuracy of an existing entry rather than simply recording a fresh transfer. An entry in a finally published record carries a presumption of correctness until it is properly revised, so a party seeking correction has to actually make out a case before the revising officer rather than assume the presumption will give way on its own.

Where the dispute is not a simple clerical correction but a genuine contest over who is entitled to be recorded, rival claimants under competing deeds or wills, for instance, the revenue authority's mutation proceeding is not the forum to finally settle that question. The proceeding is summary in character, and the Supreme Court has made clear in recent years that a person aggrieved by how a mutation turned out, including one based on a will, has to pursue a regular civil or revenue suit to have the underlying right actually adjudicated, since the mutation order itself settles nothing beyond who gets billed and who looks, on paper, like the person in possession.

Why mutation does not prove title, and what it is useful for

The Supreme Court has held for decades, and restated as recently as in a 2025 decision on mutation based on a will, that an entry in the revenue or municipal record confers no right, title or interest on the person named in it; the entry is fiscal in purpose, telling the authority whom to tax and whom to treat as being in possession, not deciding ownership. That 2025 decision allowed a mutation based on a will to stand procedurally while making clear that the actual question of entitlement under the will remained open to be decided in a proper suit, consistent with the older line of authority holding that a revenue entry is not a substitute for proving title through the chain of deeds, possession and, where relevant, succession.

None of this makes mutation pointless. A mutated record is usually required before the authority will accept further transactions on the property, such as a later sale or a mortgage, and it is one of the documents a buyer's lawyer checks as part of ordinary due diligence precisely because an unmutated or disputed entry is itself a sign of trouble in the property's history. The practical lesson for a Kolkata buyer is to treat mutation as a necessary step to complete after a purchase and as one thing to check before one, never as proof that the seller actually owns what they are selling.

What to do when a mutation application is refused or delayed

Where the Corporation or the Block Land and Land Reforms Office refuses an application, the order should state reasons, and the applicant's remedy is ordinarily a revision or appeal within the hierarchy the relevant Act provides, rather than an immediate writ petition, since courts generally expect the statutory remedy to be exhausted first. Where an application sits pending for an unreasonably long time without being decided one way or the other, the banglarbhumi portal's grievance section, or a written representation to the Block Land and Land Reforms Officer, is the first practical step, and only a sustained, undocumented failure to act typically justifies approaching the High Court.

Our guide on Mutation After a Father's Death deals with the inheritance-triggered version of this process in more detail, including how multiple legal heirs are expected to be recorded where the property has not been formally partitioned. A Kolkata family dealing with an inherited property should read that guide alongside this one, since the inheritance route and a straightforward sale-based mutation raise different documentary issues even though both end up at the same Block office or banglarbhumi application.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a mutated record of rights in my name mean I own the property?

No. Courts have repeatedly held that mutation serves fiscal and administrative purposes only and does not decide ownership. If someone disputes your title, the mutation entry does not protect you; the question has to be settled in a civil or revenue suit based on the actual chain of title.

Can the Kolkata Municipal Corporation refuse to mutate my property because of unpaid arrears?

Calcutta High Court decisions are divided on this: some hold the Corporation cannot refuse mutation purely for outstanding dues, while others hold it can still refuse where arrears genuinely exist and remain unpaid. Clearing outstanding property tax before applying avoids this becoming a point of dispute.

How is mutation outside Kolkata Municipal Corporation's area different from mutation inside it?

Outside KMC's jurisdiction, mutation of the record of rights is handled by the Revenue Officer at the Block Land and Land Reforms Office under the West Bengal Land Reforms Act, 1955, usually through the banglarbhumi portal. Inside KMC's area, it is tied to the municipal assessment register under the Kolkata Municipal Corporation Act, 1980, so a property near the municipal boundary should have this checked carefully.

What if the record of rights has an error that predates my purchase?

That is a correction of the record under Section 51A of the West Bengal Land Reforms Act, 1955, decided by a revising officer after hearing the interested parties, which differs from a routine mutation following a sale. If the error is really a dispute about entitlement rather than a clerical mistake, a civil or revenue suit may be needed instead.

I inherited land with other family members who have not yet divided it. Can mutation still go through?

Yes, mutation on inheritance can be recorded for the recognised legal heirs even before a formal partition, though the entries will usually show the property as held jointly. Our guide on mutation after a death in the family sets out the documentation this typically needs.

The provisions and rulings below were relied on for this guide. The position can change with amendments and later decisions, so check the current text and the latest case law before acting on any of it.

  2. West Bengal Land Reforms Act, 1955, Sections 50 and 51A (mutation and correction of the record of rights)
