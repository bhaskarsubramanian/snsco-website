Industrial Plot Allotment or Lease Cancelled by a Development Authority: Grounds, Notice and Remedies

What Noida, Greater Noida and YEIDA can cancel an industrial plot for, the notice owed, and whether to go by writ, suit or arbitration.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Industrial plots in Noida, Greater Noida and the Yamuna Expressway Industrial Development Authority area are allotted on terms that give the authority considerable room to cancel if the allottee defaults, chiefly on payment of the balance premium, lease rent or on executing the lease deed within the time fixed. Cancellation notices are common, and in some rounds the authorities have cancelled dozens of plots at once for missed lease deed deadlines, so an allottee who receives one is far from alone, but the path back from a cancellation is narrower than many assume and usually runs through paying the outstanding dues rather than through a legal challenge to the cancellation itself.

This guide covers the grounds authorities typically rely on, what notice an allottee is entitled to before cancellation, how restoration on payment of dues generally works, and the recurring question of forum: whether a cancelled allottee should move by writ petition, civil suit or arbitration, given that courts have taken different views depending on whether the dispute is genuinely a public-law matter or, in substance, a contractual one.

What a lease or allotment deed typically lets the authority cancel for

Allotment letters and lease deeds for industrial plots usually set a payment schedule for the premium, rent and any incidental charges, a time limit for taking possession, a further time limit for executing the lease deed once possession is handed over, and conditions tied to actually putting the plot to industrial use within a set period rather than holding it idle or using it for an unauthorised purpose. Default on any of these, most commonly non-payment of instalments with accumulated penal interest, or simply letting the lease deed execution deadline lapse without completing the formality, is the ground authorities rely on most often, and cancellation for this reason is treated as squarely within the terms the allottee accepted rather than an exercise of some separate punitive power.

Authorities have, in large batches, cancelled plots purely for missed lease deed execution deadlines, which is a reminder that even an allottee who has paid the bulk of the premium can still lose the allotment over a procedural step left incomplete. Change of land use without permission, subletting or transfer in breach of the allotment conditions, and prolonged non-construction beyond the permitted period are the other grounds that recur across reported cancellations, and each of these is usually spelled out as an express condition in the allotment letter itself rather than left to be implied.

The notice an allottee is owed before cancellation

Because these authorities are statutory bodies exercising powers under their respective development acts, cancellation is generally expected to follow a show cause notice identifying the specific default, rather than arrive as a surprise final order, and courts have been willing to interfere where an allottee can show the cancellation was passed without any real opportunity to respond or to cure the default within a reasonable time. That said, where the allotment letter itself specifies a notice period before cancellation for a payment default, meeting or missing that specific contractual notice period tends to be the first thing a court or tribunal looks at, more than any general public-law notice requirement.

An allottee who disputes the cancellation on procedural grounds should assemble the actual correspondence trail, demand letters, any part-payment receipts and the dates on which possession or the lease deed deadline fell due, since disputes in this area are frequently resolved on exactly when a default occurred and what notice, if any, followed it, rather than on any larger question of the authority's power to cancel in principle.

Restoration: usually available, usually on paying up

A recurring pattern in reported Noida and YEIDA matters is that cancellation for payment default is treated as reversible on the allottee clearing the outstanding balance along with penal interest, rather than as a final forfeiture that closes the file permanently. Courts and the authorities themselves have in several instances directed or accepted that cancellation stands set aside once the balance allotment money and penal interest are deposited, effectively treating the cancellation as a lever to recover dues rather than a decision the authority wants to hold to at any cost once payment is actually made.

This is not unconditional. Where years have passed, where the authority has already reallotted the plot to someone else, or where the default goes beyond a payment shortfall into unauthorised use or transfer, restoration becomes considerably harder to secure and the authority is far less likely to simply reverse the cancellation on payment alone. An allottee who intends to seek restoration should move promptly after the cancellation, before the authority takes any step toward reallotting the plot, since delay works squarely against the allottee in this kind of dispute.

Writ, civil suit, or arbitration: picking the right forum

Courts have repeatedly said that the mere presence of an arbitration clause in an allotment or lease deed does not automatically oust writ jurisdiction under Article 226, particularly where the authority is a statutory, public body and the challenge genuinely raises a question of arbitrary or unreasonable exercise of public power. At the same time, the Supreme Court has also held, in matters outside this specific sector but on comparable facts, that where the dispute really turns on contested facts, such as whether a building condition was actually breached or whether a payment was actually made on time, a writ court is not the right venue to resolve those factual disputes, and the parties should be left to a civil suit or to arbitration where the deed provides for it.

The practical rule that emerges is this: a challenge built on the authority's jurisdiction, a complete failure of the required notice, or patent unreasonableness is more likely to be entertained as a writ petition, while a dispute that genuinely turns on who breached what term and when is more likely to be pushed toward a civil suit or arbitration, especially once the deed itself contains an arbitration clause covering disputes arising from the lease. An allottee should read the specific dispute resolution clause in the allotment letter or lease deed before assuming a writ petition is available as a matter of course, since an authority facing a writ petition on an essentially contractual dispute will often raise exactly this objection at the threshold.

What tends to work, and what does not

Challenges have succeeded where the allottee can show the show cause notice never actually reached them, where the order recites consideration of a reply that was never in fact filed, or where the authority moved to cancel without giving the contractually specified notice period for the default in question. They have also succeeded, at least to the extent of securing restoration, where the allottee is able and willing to clear the outstanding dues promptly and the authority has not yet reallotted the plot, since this is the fact pattern courts and authorities have most consistently accommodated.

They tend to fail where the allottee simply disputes the authority's assessment of a long-standing default, where possession was never taken and the lease deed was never executed well beyond the permitted window, or where the allottee is really asking a writ court to decide a contested factual dispute dressed up as a public-law challenge. An allottee weighing whether to fight the cancellation or negotiate restoration should be realistic about which category the dispute actually falls into before committing to litigation that is more likely to end in a direction to approach the civil court or arbitrator in any event.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my industrial plot allotment be cancelled just for missing the lease deed execution deadline, even if I have paid most of the premium?

Yes. Authorities have cancelled allotments in bulk purely for missed lease deed execution deadlines, treating it as a default on the terms accepted at allotment regardless of how much of the premium has been paid, so this deadline should be tracked as carefully as payment instalments.

If my allotment is cancelled for non-payment, can I get it back by simply paying the dues?

Often, yes, at least where the default is a payment shortfall and the authority has not yet reallotted the plot. Courts and authorities have repeatedly treated payment of the balance amount with penal interest as grounds to set the cancellation aside, but this becomes much harder once significant time has passed or the plot has been given to someone else.

Should I file a writ petition or go to civil court over a cancelled industrial plot?

It depends on what the dispute is actually about. A challenge based on lack of notice or the authority exceeding its power is more suited to a writ petition, while a dispute over disputed facts, such as whether a payment was actually late, is more likely to be pushed toward a civil suit or arbitration, especially if the lease deed contains an arbitration clause.

Does having an arbitration clause in my lease deed stop me from filing a writ petition?

Not automatically. Courts have held that an arbitration clause does not by itself oust writ jurisdiction against a public authority, but a dispute that is genuinely contractual and fact-heavy is still likely to be directed toward arbitration or a civil suit rather than decided in the writ petition itself.
