Letters Rogatory and Order 26 CPC: Getting a Witness Abroad to Give Evidence in an Indian Civil Case

How an Indian court gets evidence from a witness abroad under Sections 77-78 CPC and Order 26, when a letter rogatory is needed, and what it covers.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 23 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A party to a civil suit in India sometimes needs testimony from someone who lives outside the country and will not come back to depose in person. The Code of Civil Procedure gives a court two formal routes to reach that evidence: it can ask a foreign court, through a letter of request often called a letter rogatory, to examine the witness there, or, where a witness is willing, the court can record the evidence directly by video link. This piece deals with the first route, the one used when a witness cannot or will not simply log on and testify.

The legal basis sits in Sections 77 and 78 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, read with Order 26 Rules 4 to 8 for a request going out from India, and Rules 19 to 22 for the reverse situation, where an Indian High Court executes a request coming in from a foreign court. Getting this right matters because a commission issued on the wrong rule, or routed through the wrong authority, can cost months before the evidence is even taken.

When does a court use a letter of request instead of simply summoning the witness?

An Indian court cannot compel a person outside its territorial reach to attend in person, and an ordinary summons has no force once the witness is abroad. Where the witness is cooperative and has access to a reliable video link, courts increasingly prefer recording that testimony directly, treating virtual presence as presence for the purpose of the proceeding. A letter of request remains necessary where the witness is unwilling, where no secure video facility is practically available, or where the foreign court's own procedure requires its participation before anyone there can be examined on oath.

Order 26 Rule 4 lets a court issue a commission to examine a witness who is beyond its local limits, and where the witness is outside India altogether, Rule 5 and the connected provisions route that commission through diplomatic or judicial channels as a formal request to the foreign court. The mechanism exists because an Indian court has no power to administer an oath or compel attendance on foreign soil; only a court sitting in that country, acting under its own law, can do that.

What has to be filed, and who decides whether to send the request

The party wanting the evidence applies to the court seized of the suit, setting out why the witness's testimony is necessary, why personal attendance cannot be secured, and the questions or the subject matter on which evidence is sought. Many High Courts require a list of interrogatories or a clear statement of the issues, because the foreign court executing the request will often confine the examination to what the letter specifies rather than allow free-ranging cross-examination.

The decision to issue the commission is discretionary. A court will usually ask whether the evidence is relevant and necessary to the issues framed, whether an alternative such as video recording has been tried or ruled out, and whether the delay inherent in a letter rogatory, which can run into several months once diplomatic transmission is involved, is justified by the importance of the testimony. Courts have also required the applicant to bear the practical costs, since the requesting party is asking the machinery of a foreign jurisdiction to be used for its case.

The reverse situation: an Indian High Court executing a foreign court's request

Rules 19 to 22 of Order 26 cover commissions issued at the instance of foreign tribunals, that is, a foreign court asking an Indian High Court to examine a witness located in India for use in proceedings pending abroad. The High Court within whose local limits the witness resides is the court that acts on the request, and Rule 19 sets out the conditions before that jurisdiction can be invoked at all, generally that there is a civil proceeding pending before the foreign court, that the person to be examined is in India, and that the foreign court has in fact made the request through the appropriate channel.

Courts reading Rule 19 together with Section 78 have treated the three conditions as a gateway rather than a formality: the commission will not issue merely because a foreign litigant wants an Indian witness examined. Rule 21 identifies the subordinate court to which the High Court may send the commission for actual execution, usually the court within whose jurisdiction the witness resides, and Rule 22 governs how the evidence so recorded is returned and transmitted back to the requesting foreign court.

What the commission can and cannot cover

Delhi High Court rulings interpreting Rule 19 have read the phrase to examine witness narrowly: a commission under this rule authorises recording oral testimony, not a general direction to produce documents. Where a foreign court's letter of request also asks for documents to be produced, courts have held that this falls outside Rule 19 and has to be pursued, if at all, through other provisions of the Order dealing with a commissioner's powers, such as Rule 16.

This distinction catches out applicants who draft a letter of request expecting it to double as a discovery tool. A foreign litigant who genuinely needs both testimony and documents from a person in India should expect to address the two separately, and should not assume that a single commission will deliver both without a specific basis for each.

There is a related limit worth planning around: once the commissioner records the oral evidence, the examination tends to stay within the questions or interrogatories the requesting court or party put forward. A commission is not a free-ranging deposition in the way common-law practitioners abroad sometimes expect; it is closer to an examination on specific points, and a party that wants a wide-ranging cross-examination should say so clearly in the application rather than assuming the commissioner will allow it as a matter of course.

Where the Hague Evidence Convention fits in

India is a party to the Hague Evidence Convention of 1970 on the Taking of Evidence Abroad in Civil or Commercial Matters, and the Hague Conference's own status table lists India's accession alongside a designated Central Authority and competent court for Article 8 purposes. A request from a court in another member state can, in principle, be sent through that Convention channel rather than only through the general diplomatic route the Code of Civil Procedure otherwise contemplates for non-member countries.

This still leaves Order 26 Rules 19 to 22 doing the domestic work once a request, whether it arrives through the Convention's Central Authority or through an ordinary diplomatic channel, reaches an Indian High Court: the High Court applies the same conditions under Rule 19 and the same limits on what a commission can cover, whichever channel brought the request in. Counsel abroad dealing with an Indian witness should confirm with the Ministry of Law and Justice's designated authority, or with the receiving High Court's registry, which channel applies to the requesting country before assuming either route automatically.

Timing, delay and whether it is worth doing at all

A letter rogatory is slow by design. Transmission between governments or courts, translation where needed, and the receiving court's own listing schedule add up, and a case can sit for a year or more before the evidence actually comes back. Litigants should weigh this against the alternative of recording evidence by video conferencing, which most High Courts now permit for a willing witness and which avoids the diplomatic chain entirely.

Where the witness is hostile, or where the evidence must be taken on oath before a court rather than informally, the letter rogatory stays the only route, and the better course is to apply early, frame the questions precisely, and avoid treating the commission as a vehicle for anything beyond the oral testimony it is designed to secure.

Counsel handling the application should also budget for translation, since a request going to or coming from a non-English-speaking jurisdiction will usually need the questions, the order and the eventual deposition translated at some stage, and for the practical step of confirming, before filing anything, which authority in the destination country actually receives and processes letters of request, since this differs between countries and changes from time to time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a letter rogatory be used to get documents from a person in India, not just testimony?

Generally not under Order 26 Rule 19 alone. Courts have read that rule as covering only the oral examination of a witness. A request for document production from a person in India is treated as falling outside Rule 19 and has to be grounded in some other provision of the Order.

Does India recognise requests under the Hague Evidence Convention?

India acceded to the Hague Evidence Convention of 1970 in 2007. A request from another Convention member country can be routed through India's designated Central Authority and competent court under the Convention, as an alternative to the general diplomatic channel the Code of Civil Procedure otherwise recognises for foreign commissions.

Which court actually examines the witness when a foreign court sends a request to India?

The High Court within whose territorial limits the witness resides receives the request and typically sends it on, under Order 26 Rule 21, to a subordinate court in the same area for the examination itself, after which the evidence is returned to the High Court for transmission abroad.

Is a video-recorded examination an alternative to a letter rogatory?

Where the witness is willing and a reliable link can be arranged, many High Courts now prefer recording evidence by video conferencing over sending a formal letter of request, since it avoids the delay of diplomatic transmission. A letter rogatory remains the route where the witness will not cooperate voluntarily.

How long does a letter rogatory usually take?

There is no fixed statutory period, and the time depends on the receiving country's own court process and the channel of transmission used. Parties should expect this to take considerably longer than recording evidence directly, often many months, and should plan the suit's timetable around that delay.
