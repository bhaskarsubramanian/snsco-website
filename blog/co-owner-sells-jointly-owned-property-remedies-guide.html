A Co-Owner Wants to Sell Jointly Owned Property: What Can You Do?

What happens when a co-owner sells jointly held property without consent, what the law allows, and the remedies available under Indian law.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A co-owner can sell only their own undivided share in jointly owned property, and cannot sell away the shares belonging to the other co-owners, whether or not those other co-owners agree. Section 44 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 lets a co-owner transfer their own interest without needing anyone else's permission, and that often surprises family members who assume a sale needs everyone's signature. What the Supreme Court has made clear, most recently in a 2024 ruling, is that a buyer who takes a sale deed describing the entire property, when it was actually sold by only one of several co-owners, acquires nothing beyond that seller's own undivided share until the property is formally divided by metes and bounds.

This guide sets out what a co-owner can and cannot sell, what the other co-owners can realistically do when they learn of such a sale, the special rule that lets family members buy out an outside purchaser of a share in a dwelling house, and when the dispute has to end up in a partition suit regardless.

What a Co-Owner Can Sell, and What They Cannot

Section 44 of the Transfer of Property Act lets any co-owner transfer, mortgage or lease their own share in jointly held property, and the transferee steps into that co-owner's shoes to the same extent, with the same rights of joint possession and joint enjoyment the seller had. No consent from the other co-owners is required for this limited sale, and it is not open to the other co-owners simply to veto it. What a selling co-owner cannot do is purport to convey the whole property, including shares they never owned, and a sale deed that says otherwise does not bind the shares of co-owners who were not parties to it and never authorised the sale.

There is one specific carve-out. The proviso to Section 44 says that where the property is a dwelling house belonging to an undivided family, a stranger who buys a share in it is not entitled to joint possession or common enjoyment of the house itself, even though the sale of that share is otherwise valid. That provision exists to keep outsiders from moving into a family home on the strength of one member's sale, and it is the foundation for the buy-out right discussed further below.

The Supreme Court's 2024 Ruling on Selling an Undivided Share

In Sk. Golam Lalchand v Nandu Lal Shaw, decided on 10 September 2024, the Supreme Court dealt with a sale deed by which one co-owner, whose own share in a Howrah property had never been separately determined, sold the entire property to a buyer. The other co-owners sued; the trial court dismissed that suit, but the first appellate court and the High Court held the property had never actually been partitioned between the family members, a finding the Supreme Court upheld. The Supreme Court held that a co-owner whose share remains undetermined cannot transfer the entire suit property to a buyer without the property first being partitioned by metes and bounds, and that the buyer in such a case acquires at most the seller's own undivided interest, not the property as a whole.

The buyer was left to pursue a suit for partition to carve out whatever share the seller actually had, or a claim for damages against the seller for misrepresenting what was being sold. For a family facing a similar sale by one relative, the case is useful precisely because it confirms that an overreaching sale deed does not, by itself, dispossess the other co-owners or convert the buyer into an owner of specific, identified portions of the property.

What the Other Co-Owners Can Actually Do

Because Section 44 does not require consent for the sale of a co-owner's own share, the other co-owners generally cannot stop that part of the transaction, and an injunction sought purely to prevent the sale itself is unlikely to succeed. What they can resist is any attempt by the buyer to step beyond that share, to take exclusive possession of a specific portion, to exclude the other co-owners from common areas, or to have revenue or municipal records changed to show the buyer as owner of the whole property rather than of an undivided interest.

A suit for declaration that the sale deed operates only to the extent of the seller's own share, combined with an injunction restraining the buyer from interfering with joint possession, is the usual first step. Objecting promptly to any mutation entry that overstates what was actually transferred matters just as much, since an unchallenged mutation record, while not itself proof of title, tends to be relied on later by both sides and by courts assessing who has been in possession of what.

Buying Out a Stranger Under Section 4 of the Partition Act

Where the property is a family dwelling house and a stranger has bought a share in it from one family member, Section 4 of the Partition Act, 1893 gives the other family members a right to buy out that stranger's share instead of having the outsider join in the house. The Supreme Court held in Gautam Paul v Debi Rani Paul, (2000) 8 SCC 330, that this right of pre-emption arises only once the stranger purchaser sues for partition, not before.

What is genuinely unsettled, and where High Courts have gone different ways, is whether a family co-sharer can invoke Section 4 by filing the partition suit themselves, or whether the section is available only once the outsider is the one suing. Some decisions have denied the buy-out right to a co-sharer who filed the suit rather than the stranger, while others have allowed it. Given that split, a family member relying on this provision should expect the point to be argued, and should not assume the right is available automatically just because an outsider has bought into the house.

When a Partition Suit Becomes Necessary Regardless

Where a co-owner has sold their share and the family wants a clean, final division rather than an ongoing joint holding complicated by an outside buyer, a partition suit is usually the only way to get specific portions allotted to each owner, including the buyer to the extent of the share actually purchased. The suit is filed in the civil court having jurisdiction over the property, and the court works out each party's share based on title, including whatever undivided interest a buyer like the one in the Sk. Golam Lalchand case actually holds, before dividing the property by metes and bounds or ordering a sale and division of proceeds where physical division is not practical.

These suits take time, often years where several parties and several claims to shares are involved, and interim orders protecting the status quo, such as restraining alienation of specific portions pending the suit, are commonly sought alongside the main partition claim rather than as an afterthought.

Practical Steps If You Learn of an Unauthorised Sale

The first step is usually to pull the current revenue or municipal records for the property and check exactly what the mutation entry says was transferred, since many disputes trace back to a mutation that was allowed to go through unchallenged showing the buyer as owner of the whole property rather than a share. A written objection to the revenue authority, followed where necessary by a formal legal notice to the buyer and the selling co-owner, puts the dispute on record early.

Where the buyer appears to be taking active steps toward possession, such as fencing a portion, starting construction, or attempting a further sale, an injunction suit should not be delayed, since courts weigh how promptly a co-owner acted once they learned of the sale. Waiting several years before objecting, even where no formal limitation bar applies, can still count against a co-owner when a court decides whether to grant interim relief.

What a Buyer of an Undivided Share Should Check First

Anyone buying a share from one co-owner out of several should start from the assumption that they are buying an undivided interest, not a specific, identifiable portion of the property, unless the shares have already been formally demarcated by a registered partition deed or a court decree. Checking revenue records for whether a partition has actually occurred, and asking directly whether other co-owners exist and have consented to or at least been informed of the transaction, avoids the position the buyer in Sk. Golam Lalchand ended up in, holding a deed for the whole property but a right to only a fraction of it.

Where the property is a family dwelling house, a buyer should also recognise that the family may have a statutory right to buy out their share once a partition suit is filed, so treating the purchase as a stepping stone toward eventual possession of a specific part of the house, rather than as the end of the matter, is the more realistic way to assess the risk before paying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my brother sell our jointly inherited property without telling me?

He can sell his own undivided share without your consent under Section 44 of the Transfer of Property Act, but he cannot sell your share, and a deed describing the whole property as sold does not bind your interest. The buyer only acquires what your brother actually owned.

If a co-owner sells the whole property to an outsider, is the sale completely invalid?

No. The Supreme Court has held the sale remains valid to the extent of the selling co-owner's own share. The buyer does not get the entire property and is generally left to seek partition or claim damages from the seller for the shortfall.

Can family members force an outside buyer to sell back their share in a family house?

They may, under Section 4 of the Partition Act, 1893, but only once the outside buyer files a suit for partition, according to the Supreme Court. Whether a family member can trigger this right by filing the partition suit themselves is unsettled and depends on which High Court's view applies.

What should I do first if I find out a co-owner has sold without telling me?

Check the current revenue or mutation records for what was actually recorded, file a written objection if the entry overstates what was sold, and consider an injunction suit promptly if the buyer is taking steps toward exclusive possession. Delay in objecting can weigh against you later.

Does a partition suit take the place of challenging the sale itself?

Often, yes. Rather than trying to invalidate a sale that may well be valid to the extent of the seller's own share, a partition suit lets the court work out and allot each owner's actual share, including the buyer's, which usually resolves the practical dispute more directly.
