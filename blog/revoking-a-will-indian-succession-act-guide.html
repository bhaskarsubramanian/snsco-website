How to Revoke a Will in India: What Actually Works and What Doesn't

Valid ways to revoke a will under the Succession Act, why marriage revokes a Christian will but not a Hindu one, and why crossing out a signature fails.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 24 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

People routinely assume that a will can be undone the same informal way it might feel like it should be undone: tear it up, scratch out a signature, write 'cancelled' across the first page. None of that is enough on its own under Indian law, and getting this wrong leaves a family arguing after death over which version of a will actually governs, with the original, uncancelled document sometimes still capable of being probated.

This guide sets out the recognised ways to revoke an unprivileged will under the Indian Succession Act, 1925, where the rule differs by religion, and the point most people get wrong about what counts as a valid act of revocation.

The valid ways to revoke a will

Section 70 of the Indian Succession Act governs revocation of an unprivileged will or codicil, which covers the great majority of wills made in India (a privileged will, made by certain members of the armed forces in actual service, follows a separate and more relaxed regime). The recognised routes are: executing another will or codicil that revokes the earlier one, executing some writing that declares an intention to revoke and is executed in the manner a will is required to be executed, or burning, tearing, or otherwise destroying the will with the clear intention of revoking it.

A later will does not need to say in so many words that it revokes the earlier one, though it is far better practice if it does. Where a later will is simply inconsistent with an earlier one, the later document is generally treated as revoking the earlier will to the extent of the inconsistency, but relying on inconsistency rather than an express revocation clause invites exactly the kind of dispute a careful will-maker should want to avoid.

Destruction has to be of the actual physical document, and it has to be done, or directed to be done in the maker's presence, with the intention of revoking it. A will that is accidentally destroyed, lost, or destroyed by someone without the maker's authority and clear intention is not revoked in law; its contents can, in suitable cases, still be proved by other evidence such as a copy or witnesses to its contents.

What does not count: the 'cancelled' trap

Writing the word 'cancelled' across a will, drawing a line through it, or scratching out the testator's signature is not, by itself, sufficient to revoke the will under Section 70. These acts fall short of the destruction the section contemplates unless they go far enough to actually destroy the document's character as a will, and courts have held that symbolic defacement of this kind does not automatically amount to legal revocation.

This creates a genuine trap: a person who marks up an old will intending to revoke it, but does not execute a fresh will or a properly executed revoking writing, and does not actually destroy the document, can die leaving a will that still looks, to a court, like the operative one. Anyone who has a probate dispute involving a marked-up but undestroyed will should expect the other side to argue, with real force, that the marking changes nothing because Section 70's requirements were never met.

The safer course, whenever a will needs to be revoked, is to execute a new will that expressly revokes all earlier wills and codicils, and then destroy the physical copies of the earlier document, rather than relying on any halfway measure.

Marriage: revokes a Christian's will, not a Hindu's

A separate provision, Section 69, provides that a will is revoked by the subsequent marriage of the maker. Taken on its own, this would mean that getting married after making a will automatically revokes it, regardless of what the will says.

That rule, however, does not apply uniformly across religions. Section 57 of the Act makes only the specific provisions of Part VI listed in Schedule III applicable to the wills of Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, and Jains, and Section 69 is one of the provisions excluded from that list for these communities. The practical result, confirmed by a Kerala High Court ruling on the point, is that marriage does not revoke a will made by a Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh, or Jain testator, while it does revoke the will of a testator to whom the unmodified Part VI applies, which in practice principally means Christian and Parsi testators (Muslims are excluded from Part VI of the Act entirely under Section 58, and testamentary succession for Muslims is governed by personal law rather than this Part).

Anyone remarrying, or marrying for the first time after making a will, should not assume their religion-specific position on this point; a Christian testator who marries after making a will should generally treat that will as revoked and make a fresh one, while a Hindu testator in the same position needs a fresh will only if they actually want to change their testamentary wishes, since marriage by itself will not have revoked the earlier one.

Partial revocation and alterations

A will can be partly revoked, leaving the rest standing, typically through a codicil that changes or deletes specific clauses while confirming the remainder of the will. A codicil has to be executed with the same formalities as a will itself, meaning signature and attestation by the required number of witnesses, for it to have legal effect.

Interlineations, erasures, or obliterations made on the face of an existing will after it has been executed are treated cautiously by probate courts, because an alteration made after execution, without being separately re-executed and attested, does not automatically form part of the will. Where the original wording can still be read despite the alteration, courts have tended to give effect to the original wording rather than the alteration, on the basis that an informal change made later does not meet the formalities the law requires.

Anyone wanting to make even a small change to an existing will is better served by a short, properly executed codicil than by writing in the margin or striking through a clause on the original document, precisely because the latter risks being disregarded altogether when the will eventually comes up for probate.

Practical steps when revoking a will

Execute the new will first, with an express clause revoking all previous wills and codicils, signed and attested in the presence of the required witnesses. Only once the new will is validly executed should the earlier document be destroyed, so that there is never a gap where no valid will exists if something were to happen in between.

Collect and destroy every copy of the earlier will that can be located, including copies held by a bank, a lawyer, or family members, since a surviving copy in someone else's hands can still be put forward as evidence of the earlier will's contents if a dispute arises later about whether destruction of the original was intended to revoke it.

Where a testator has married, or is about to marry, after executing a will, check whether their personal law brings Section 69's automatic revocation into play; if it does, treat the earlier will as revoked and execute a fresh one rather than assuming the old one still stands.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does writing 'cancelled' on a will revoke it?

No. Courts have held that writing 'cancelled', drawing a line through the text, or scratching out a signature does not by itself amount to revocation under Section 70. Revocation requires a later will, a properly executed revoking writing, or actual destruction of the document with intent to revoke.

Does getting married after making a will cancel it?

It depends on the testator's personal law. Section 69 revokes a will by the maker's subsequent marriage, but this provision is excluded for Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh, and Jain testators under Section 57 and Schedule III, so their wills are not automatically revoked by marriage. It generally still applies to Christian and Parsi testators.

Can a will be partly revoked?

Yes, through a codicil that changes or deletes specific clauses while leaving the rest standing. The codicil must be executed with the same formalities as a will, meaning signature and attestation by the required witnesses, to have legal effect.

If I destroy my will, is it automatically revoked?

Only if the destruction is intentional and is of the actual physical document, done or directed by the maker with the clear intention of revoking it. Accidental loss or destruction, or destruction by someone without the maker's authority, does not revoke the will.
