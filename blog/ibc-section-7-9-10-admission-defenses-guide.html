Admission of Section 7, 9 and 10 Applications Under the IBC: What Every Corporate Debtor and Creditor Should Know

A guide for a corporate debtor facing an insolvency application, and for a financial, operational or corporate applicant filing one, explaining the distinct admission tests under Sections 7, 9 and 10 of the IBC and the limited defenses available at the admission stage.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 29 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

Overview

A guide for a corporate debtor facing an insolvency application, and for a financial, operational or corporate applicant filing one, explaining the distinct admission tests under Sections 7, 9 and 10 of the IBC and the limited defenses available at the admission stage.

What You Need to Know

  • Section 7 applications (by a financial creditor) require the adjudicating authority to satisfy itself only that a debt and a default exist — it need not examine whether the debt is disputed on other grounds, and admission follows once the two elements are shown, subject to a limited opportunity for the corporate debtor to point to a complete absence of default.
  • Section 9 applications (by an operational creditor) require the corporate debtor to have received a demand notice under Section 8 and to have failed, within ten days, to either pay the debt or point to a pre-existing dispute — the existence of a plausible, pre-existing dispute (not merely a moonshine or spurious defence raised after the demand notice) is a valid ground to resist admission.
  • Section 10 applications (by the corporate debtor itself) require the applicant's own board to have authorised the filing and require the applicant to disclose the existence of any pending insolvency resolution or liquidation proceeding against it, along with the names of proposed resolution professionals.
  • The adjudicating authority's inquiry at the admission stage is deliberately summary — it is not the occasion to conduct a detailed trial of disputed facts, and courts have consistently held that the NCLT should not embark on a mini-trial where a genuine, prima facie dispute is shown to exist for an operational debt.
  • A corporate debtor's realistic defences narrow considerably once a demand notice or Section 7 petition is properly served — the emphasis should shift from resisting admission on technical grounds to preparing for participation in the CIRP, including timely claim submissions and engagement with the resolution process.
  • Where a genuine settlement is reached after an application is filed but before admission, the applicant may withdraw the application; once admitted, withdrawal instead requires a Section 12A application supported by the requisite CoC majority.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the key difference in the admission test for Section 7 versus Section 9 applications?

A Section 7 application is admitted once a debt and default are shown, without an inquiry into whether the debt is disputed on unrelated grounds. A Section 9 application, by contrast, can be resisted by showing a genuine pre-existing dispute that predates the demand notice.

Can a corporate debtor defeat a Section 9 application by raising a dispute after receiving the demand notice?

Generally no — courts look for a pre-existing dispute, meaning one that existed before the Section 8 demand notice was issued, not a dispute manufactured or raised for the first time in response to it.

What must a corporate debtor disclose in its own Section 10 application?

It must disclose board authorisation for the filing, its financial statements, and the existence of any other pending insolvency resolution or liquidation process against it, along with proposed resolution professionals for the adjudicating authority's consideration.
