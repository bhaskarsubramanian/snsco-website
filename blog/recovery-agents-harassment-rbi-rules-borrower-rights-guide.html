Recovery Agents and Harassment: What Lenders May and May Not Do Under RBI's Rules

What RBI's rules allow a bank's recovery agent to do, what counts as harassment, and the complaint routes available to a borrower facing coercive recovery.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A bank or NBFC is allowed to recover a genuine loan default, but it is not allowed to do so through intimidation, abusive calls, public humiliation or forcible repossession, and this is not a matter of courtesy; it rests on specific RBI directions and on Supreme Court rulings going back nearly two decades. As on today's date, a borrower facing aggressive recovery has three practical levers: the lender's own internal grievance mechanism, a complaint to the RBI's integrated ombudsman, and, where the conduct crosses into criminal intimidation or forcible seizure, a police complaint, alongside a civil claim for damages in an appropriate case.

The rules have also just been tightened. RBI issued a fresh set of directions on recovery practices on 6 August 2026, which, according to reported coverage, extends the existing bar on harassment to explicitly cover misuse of social media and sharing of a borrower's personal details, photographs or recordings to pressure repayment. The core obligations described below, however, are not new; they build on guidelines that go back to 2008 and on a line of Supreme Court decisions on forcible repossession.

The Starting Point: ICICI Bank Ltd. v. Prakash Kaur (2007)

The leading case on this subject is ICICI Bank Ltd. v. Prakash Kaur, (2007) 2 SCC 711, where the Supreme Court dealt with a bank's use of hired musclemen, described in reported commentary as goondas, to seize a financed vehicle, and held that recovery of a loan must happen through lawful means, not through force or intimidation. Secondary commentary on the case consistently describes it as the basis on which RBI issued its 2008 guidelines for loan recovery agents, covering due diligence before engaging an agent, training requirements and a grievance redressal mechanism for borrowers.

A more recent application of the same principle appears in reported 2026 coverage of a Supreme Court decision, described in multiple reports as Hari Dutta Sharma v. State of U.P., where the Court again relied on the 2007 ruling and directed RBI to ensure that banks and NBFCs actually comply with existing guidelines rather than treating them, as one headline put it, as rules that exist only on paper. Reports describe the Court holding that a forcible vehicle seizure in that case violated both RBI's fair practices guidelines and the lender's own contractual notice clause.

What RBI's Rules Actually Require of a Recovery Agent

RBI's instructions, built up over successive circulars and now consolidated into directions issued in August 2026, require a lender to ensure its recovery agents avoid any form of harassment, whether verbal or physical, and restrict the hours during which a borrower can be contacted, with earlier instructions specifically barring calls before 8 am and after 7 pm. Reported coverage of the August 2026 directions describes an expanded list of prohibited conduct: abusive language, excessive or anonymous calls, public humiliation of the borrower, and, newly, the use of social media to disclose a borrower's details or to circulate their personal photographs, videos or audio recordings as a means of pressuring repayment.

Agents are also required to carry a certification from the Indian Institute of Banking and Finance for debt recovery work, and the lender itself is required to maintain a recovery policy, carry out due diligence before engaging an agent, and ensure that recovery targets and incentive structures for agents do not themselves encourage harsh methods. Reported coverage gives differing effective dates for these August 2026 directions, so a borrower or lender checking compliance should confirm the applicable date directly from RBI's own notification rather than relying on any single press report.

Repossessing a Financed Vehicle or Asset: What Is and Is Not Allowed

A lender with a valid security interest, such as a hypothecation over a financed vehicle, is not acting unlawfully merely by seeking to repossess it after a genuine default; the issue is how that repossession happens. Reported commentary on the Prakash Kaur line of cases describes the settled position as requiring repossession to follow the loan agreement's own terms, including any notice period before seizure, and to be carried out without force, threats or intimidation, and not by stealth or in the dead of night, phrases that have been used in later reported rulings describing what courts will not tolerate.

A borrower whose vehicle or other secured asset has been forcibly repossessed without notice, or recovered through intimidation, has grounds to challenge the repossession itself, separately from challenging the underlying loan default, and reported 2026 commentary notes that courts have continued to scrutinise this conduct closely even as repossession through lawful means remains available to a lender.

Where to Actually Complain

The first step is the lender's own internal grievance redressal mechanism, which every regulated entity is required to maintain and publicise, including contact details for escalation if the first response is unsatisfactory. If the lender's own process does not resolve the complaint, or does not respond within the time the lender's own policy sets out, a borrower can take the complaint to the Reserve Bank - Integrated Ombudsman Scheme (RB-IOS), 2026, which took effect on 1 July 2026 and replaced the earlier 2021 scheme (itself a merger of RBI's separate ombudsman schemes for banks, NBFCs and digital transactions), covering deficiency in service by a regulated entity, including conduct by its recovery agents.

A complaint under the integrated scheme can be filed online through RBI's complaint management system, or by writing to RBI's designated email address, and the scheme does not charge the complainant anything to use it. Where recovery conduct amounts to criminal intimidation, trespass, or use of force, a borrower can also approach the police directly, and separately, a civil suit for damages remains available where the harassment has caused quantifiable loss or injury, though in practice most borrowers find the ombudsman route faster for stopping ongoing harassment.

What a Borrower Should Keep in Mind Before and During a Complaint

A complaint is far stronger when the borrower has kept a record: call logs and timestamps, screenshots of any messages or social media posts, names of agents who visited in person, and copies of any written communication from the lender. A borrower does not lose the right to complain about harassment simply because the underlying debt is genuinely owed; the two questions, whether the debt is due and whether the recovery method was lawful, are assessed separately, and a lender's compliance obligations under RBI's directions do not depend on whether the borrower is found to be at fault on the debt itself. Where a complaint under the integrated ombudsman scheme involves a credit card account specifically, the scheme allows compensation up to a stated cap for expenses, mental anguish and harassment, which is a useful marker of how seriously RBI's own framework treats this kind of conduct even outside a court case.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a bank's agent call me at any hour to recover a loan?

No. RBI's instructions restrict calling hours, with earlier guidance specifically barring calls before 8 am and after 7 pm, and the August 2026 directions continue to treat excessive or anonymous calls, along with abusive language, as a prohibited recovery practice regardless of the time of day.

Can a recovery agent post about my debt on social media or contact my relatives?

Reported coverage of RBI's August 2026 directions describes this as now expressly prohibited, covering both the disclosure of a borrower's personal details, photographs or recordings on social media and pressure tactics aimed at relatives, friends or colleagues to force repayment of the loan.

Can a lender forcibly take back my financed vehicle without notice?

Generally not. Courts, following ICICI Bank Ltd. v. Prakash Kaur (2007) 2 SCC 711, have required repossession to follow the loan agreement's own notice terms and to be carried out without force or intimidation, not through stealth or at night.

Where should I complain first if a recovery agent is harassing me?

Start with the lender's own internal grievance mechanism. If that does not resolve the issue, escalate to the Reserve Bank - Integrated Ombudsman Scheme (RB-IOS), 2026, which took effect on 1 July 2026 and replaced the 2021 scheme, covering deficiency in service by a bank or NBFC, including the conduct of its recovery agents, at no cost to you.

Does owing the debt genuinely mean I cannot complain about how it was recovered?

No. Whether the debt is actually due and whether the recovery method itself was lawful are treated as separate questions; a lender's obligation to recover through fair means under RBI's rules applies regardless of whether the borrower is at fault on the underlying debt.
