Related-Party Financial Creditors and the Committee of Creditors: How Section 21(2) Excludes Them

Not every financial creditor gets a seat at the CoC table. Here is how the Section 21(2) proviso excludes related parties, and the narrow exception that lets some back in.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 7 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice, see our disclaimer

The Section 21(2) Bar on Related-Party Financial Creditors

The proviso to Section 21(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 bars a financial creditor that is a 'related party' of the corporate debtor, as that term is defined in Section 5(24), from any right of representation, participation or voting in meetings of the Committee of Creditors. The exclusion is not confined to voting alone: a related-party creditor has no seat at the table at all.

Related-party status for this purpose can arise through equity or voting control, a common directorship or management link, or any of the other relationships listed in Section 5(24). Courts have read the exclusion purposively, so as to catch disguised or collusive related-party financing structured to influence CoC outcomes, rather than confining the test to the bare letter of a formal shareholding or directorship.

The Narrow Exception for Regulated Creditors Converting Debt to Equity

A second proviso to Section 21(2) carves out an exception: a financial creditor that is regulated by a financial sector regulator is not excluded from the CoC merely because it became a related party solely through the conversion of debt into equity, or into instruments convertible into equity, before the insolvency commencement date.

In most judicial readings, the test for exclusion looks at the relationship actually existing as of the insolvency commencement date, and courts have been alert to last-minute restructurings engineered either to artificially acquire related-party status so as to escape liability elsewhere, or to artificially shed it so as to gain a CoC vote.

What a Resolution Professional, and a Creditor, Should Do

A resolution professional who wrongly admits a related party to CoC voting, or who wrongly excludes a genuine arm's-length creditor by mischaracterising it as related, exposes the CoC's decisions, including approval of the resolution plan, to challenge before the NCLT or NCLAT.

The practical safeguard for creditors and resolution professionals alike is to document the actual ownership and control chain, and the transaction history, behind any financial debt claim, and to flag a related-party question early, before a contested CoC vote, rather than after the plan has already been approved.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a related-party financial creditor still attend CoC meetings as an observer?

No. The Section 21(2) proviso excludes a related party from representation, participation and voting alike, not merely from casting a vote.

Does becoming a related party only through converting debt to equity before insolvency always disqualify a creditor?

Not necessarily. A second proviso exempts a financial creditor regulated by a financial sector regulator that became a related party solely through such a pre-insolvency conversion.

What happens if a related party is wrongly allowed to vote in the CoC?

The CoC's decisions, including approval of the resolution plan, become vulnerable to challenge before the NCLT or NCLAT on the ground that the voting composition was itself unlawful.
