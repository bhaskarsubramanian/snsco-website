India's E-Commerce Rules and the Foreign Seller: What Changes From January 2027

What the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules require of a foreign online seller or platform, and what the 2026 amendment changes from 1 January 2027.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign business that sells into India through its own website, or through a seller account on an Indian marketplace, often assumes Indian consumer law stops applying once the transaction happens outside India's borders. The Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 were written specifically to close that gap, and a September 2026 amendment that takes effect on 1 January 2027 tightens several of the obligations that already applied.

This guide sets out who the Rules reach, the two routes a foreign entity can use to meet the compliance structure the Rules expect, the grievance and disclosure duties that apply regardless of where the seller is based, and what specifically changes once the 2026 amendment comes into force.

The Rules reach you even without an office in India

Rule 2(2) of the 2020 Rules extends the framework to an e-commerce entity that is not established in India but systematically offers goods or services to consumers located in India. There is no requirement of a registered office, a warehouse, or local staff for this test to bite; a foreign website that takes orders from and ships to Indian consumers on a regular basis, or a foreign seller operating through an account on an Indian marketplace, falls within scope on the same basis as a domestic platform or seller.

The Rules apply across both business models recognised under the framework: the inventory model, where the e-commerce entity owns the goods it sells directly to consumers, and the marketplace model, where the entity provides the electronic platform on which independent sellers transact with buyers. A foreign business can be caught either as the platform itself, if it runs its own site serving Indian consumers, or as a seller using someone else's marketplace, and the obligations that attach differ depending on which role it plays.

Two routes to being treated as compliant on structure

Rule 4(1)(a) gives a foreign e-commerce entity two ways to put itself on a recognised footing for compliance purposes: register as a company under Indian company law, or operate through an office, branch or agency outside India that is owned or controlled by a person resident in India. Several commentators have flagged that the second route borrows language from foreign exchange control concepts that are not worded identically in the Rules themselves, which leaves room for differing interpretations of exactly when an overseas branch counts as owned or controlled by a resident for this purpose.

In practice, the structure chosen tends to track the foreign business's broader India strategy rather than the compliance question alone. A foreign business planning a long-term India presence more often incorporates an Indian subsidiary, which also gives it a domestic entity for tax, FEMA and contractual purposes beyond the e-commerce rules. A business selling into India more opportunistically, without a current plan to incorporate locally, has to work through whether its existing overseas structure genuinely satisfies the ownership or control test, rather than assume that merely accepting Indian orders is itself enough.

Neither route removes the need to look at other Indian laws that apply separately from the e-commerce framework. A foreign seller shipping goods into India still has to clear customs valuation and classification requirements, and a foreign entity collecting payments from Indian consumers still has to work through the applicable foreign exchange rules on inward remittances. Treating Rule 4(1)(a) compliance as the whole of the India entry question, rather than one piece of it, is a common and avoidable mistake.

The grievance clock runs the same way for a foreign entity

Rule 4 requires every e-commerce entity in scope, foreign or domestic, to appoint a grievance officer and maintain a grievance redressal mechanism sized to the volume of complaints it ordinarily receives from India. Under Rule 4(5), that officer must acknowledge a consumer's complaint within forty-eight hours and redress it within one month of receipt. These are not aspirational service levels; they are the baseline the Rules set, and nothing in the framework relaxes them for a seller or platform operating from outside India.

The September 2026 amendment adds a further duty on top of the existing timelines, requiring the grievance officer to give the complainant a copy of the complaint as it was recorded. A foreign entity whose grievance handling currently sits with a regional or global customer-support team, rather than a function built around these specific Indian requirements, should check that the acknowledgement-and-copy step and the one-month redressal clock are actually being tracked against Indian timestamps, not against a different internal service-level target.

Marketplace fallback liability, and new limits on using seller data

A marketplace entity is not automatically liable for a seller's failure to deliver, but Rule 5 imposes a fallback liability where non-delivery by a seller on the platform causes loss to a consumer and the marketplace has not taken the steps the Rules expect of it, such as obtaining the seller's undertaking as to the accuracy of its listings. A foreign seller operating through an Indian marketplace should not assume the platform absorbs every failure; the platform's fallback exposure is conditional, not a blanket indemnity for the seller's own non-performance.

The 2026 amendment strengthens ranking transparency under Rule 5(3)(f), requiring a marketplace to explain, in descending order of importance and in plain language, the parameters that principally determine how goods and sellers are ranked in search results, along with their relative weight. It also adds a new Rule 5(6) restricting a marketplace entity from using information it collects from sellers and transactions to sell its own goods under a brand or name common with the marketplace itself. A foreign entity that operates an Indian marketplace and also has, or is considering, its own private-label line needs to review this restriction specifically, since it goes to how collected seller and transaction data can be used internally.

What a seller, as distinct from the platform, must disclose

Rule 6 sets disclosure duties that fall on the seller rather than the marketplace operating the platform. The 2026 amendment expands Rule 6(5)(d) to require disclosure of the country of origin of the goods, best-before or use-before information where relevant, return and payment terms, and the cost of return shipping, all before the consumer completes the purchase. A new Rule 6(5)(j) requires the seller to furnish a government-issued identification number as part of the same listing information.

Where the goods being sold are imported, the amendment adds a further requirement that the e-commerce entity importing the goods state the importer's name and details together with the full country of origin. A foreign seller shipping stock into India for sale through a marketplace, rather than drop-shipping directly from abroad, should treat this importer-identification duty as a distinct line item from the general country-of-origin disclosure, since the two serve different purposes on the listing page.

What actually changes on 1 January 2027

The Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) (Amendment) Rules, 2026 were notified on 9 September 2026 and take effect from 1 January 2027, giving affected entities a lead time of roughly four months from notification to enforcement. For a foreign seller or platform already complying with the 2020 Rules, the practical changes are incremental rather than a wholesale rewrite: the copy-of-complaint duty under Rule 4(5), the expanded disclosure fields under Rule 6(5), the ranking-transparency requirement under Rule 5(3)(f), and the private-label data-use restriction under Rule 5(6) are the items that need new process or new listing fields, not a different compliance structure altogether.

A foreign business that has treated its India e-commerce compliance as a one-time setup exercise completed around 2020 or 2021 should use this amendment as the occasion to re-check its grievance workflow, its product listing templates, and, if it operates a marketplace, its ranking-disclosure page and internal data-use policy, rather than wait for an enforcement action to find the gap.

The lead time between notification and the force date also matters for a business running a global product catalogue built around one template. Updating a listing template that feeds the same country-of-origin and importer fields into every market, rather than only the Indian storefront, is usually simpler to build once and apply everywhere than to carve out an India-specific exception close to the deadline.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do India's e-commerce rules apply to a foreign seller with no office in India?

Yes. Rule 2(2) extends the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 to any e-commerce entity, including one not established in India, that systematically offers goods or services to consumers located in India, whether it operates its own site or sells through an Indian marketplace.

What are the two ways a foreign e-commerce entity can meet the Rule 4(1)(a) structural requirement?

It can register as a company under Indian law, or operate through an office, branch or agency outside India that is owned or controlled by a person resident in India. Commentators note the second route uses ownership-and-control language that is not worded identically to the related foreign exchange rules, so it needs a careful, fact-specific check.

Is a marketplace automatically liable if a seller does not deliver the goods ordered?

Not automatically. Rule 5 imposes a fallback liability where non-delivery by a seller causes loss to a consumer and the marketplace has not taken steps the Rules expect, such as obtaining the seller's undertaking on the accuracy of listed information; it is a conditional liability, not a blanket guarantee of every seller's performance.

What changes for foreign sellers and platforms from 1 January 2027?

The Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) (Amendment) Rules, 2026, notified on 9 September 2026 and in force from 1 January 2027, add a duty to give complainants a copy of their recorded complaint, expand seller disclosures to cover country of origin and importer details, require marketplaces to explain ranking criteria, and restrict using collected seller data for a marketplace's own private-label sales.

Must a seller disclose the country of origin of goods sold through an Indian marketplace?

Yes, under the amended Rule 6(5)(d), and where the goods are imported, the e-commerce entity importing them must also state the importer's name and details together with the full country of origin, as separate disclosure items on the listing.
