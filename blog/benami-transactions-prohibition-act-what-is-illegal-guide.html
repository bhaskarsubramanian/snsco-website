The Benami Transactions Act: What Is Banned and What Is Not

What counts as a benami transaction under the 1988 Act, the family and fiduciary exceptions, and what the Ganpati Dealcom ruling settled.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A great many ordinary Indian family arrangements, a flat bought in a wife's name, a property held by one sibling for the benefit of all, a plot registered in a Karta's name but paid for from joint family funds, sit close enough to the statutory definition of a benami transaction that families with real property to protect need to know exactly where the line falls. The Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988, rewritten substantially by the 2016 amendment, bans holding property in one person's name while someone else provides the consideration and enjoys the benefit, and it backs the ban with confiscation and criminal liability, not just a civil declaration.

The Act also carves out specific, named exceptions, and a 2022 Supreme Court ruling narrowed when the harshest consequences, confiscation and prosecution, can even be applied. This guide sets out what counts as benami, what does not, and what the current state of enforcement actually allows.

What counts as a benami transaction

Section 2(9) of the amended Act defines a benami transaction broadly: a transaction where property is transferred to, or held by, one person, but the consideration for it has been provided, or is to be provided, by another person, and the property is held for the immediate or future benefit, direct or indirect, of the person who paid. The person named on the title is the benamidar; the person who actually paid and benefits is the beneficial owner. The definition also separately catches transactions carried out in a fictitious name, and transactions where the owner is not traceable or denies knowledge of the ownership, which is aimed at arrangements designed specifically to obscure who really owns an asset.

The consequences for a transaction caught by this definition are not limited to a civil fight over title. The property can be confiscated by the government through a dedicated adjudication process, and the Act separately creates criminal liability for entering into a benami transaction, with the beneficial owner, the benamidar, and anyone who abets or induces the transaction all potentially exposed. This combination, confiscation plus prosecution, is why the exceptions described below matter so much in practice: a family that genuinely falls within one of them has a complete defence, while a family that assumed it was exempt but was not can face losing the property altogether.

The family and fiduciary exceptions

Section 2(9)(A) of the amended Act lists exceptions to the general definition, and the two most commonly relied on by families are the Hindu undivided family exception and the spouse-or-child exception. Under the HUF exception, a transaction is not benami where a Karta, or another member of a Hindu undivided family, holds property in their own name but the consideration for it was paid out of funds belonging to the HUF, and the property was purchased for the benefit of the family as a whole. Under the spouse-or-child exception, property held in the name of a person's spouse or child is not benami where the consideration was paid out of that person's own known sources of income.

A third exception covers a person holding property in a fiduciary capacity, a trustee, an executor, a partner holding firm property, a director holding company property, and similar relationships, where the fiduciary relationship itself can be demonstrated. These exceptions are not a blanket license; each depends on the paying party's funds being traceable to a known, legitimate source, and on the relationship, HUF membership, marriage, parentage, or fiduciary role, being capable of proof if the arrangement is ever questioned. A family that cannot show where the money actually came from does not get the benefit of the exception merely because the relationship between the parties fits the description.

Why known source of funds is the real battleground

In practice, disputes over whether a family arrangement is genuinely exempt rarely turn on whether the relationship, spouse, child, HUF member, existed. They turn on whether the consideration can be traced to a source the paying party can actually document: salary income, inheritance, sale proceeds of another asset, a bank loan taken in that person's own name, or similar. A property bought in a daughter's name using funds that trace back to an unexplained cash deposit, rather than a documented, legitimate source, does not automatically qualify for the spouse-or-child exception just because the relationship is genuine.

This is where families with long-standing, informally structured property holdings are most exposed. Arrangements made decades ago, when documentation habits were looser and large cash transactions were more common, can look very different when examined against a modern documentary standard. Families holding significant property across generations, especially where titles were put in the names of various relatives for reasons that had nothing to do with concealment, benefit from reviewing the paper trail for each such holding well before any dispute or scrutiny arises, rather than assuming the family relationship alone will carry the day.

Disputes of this kind often surface years after the original purchase, typically when a family relationship sours, a sibling dies, or a lender or buyer's counsel asks awkward questions during a due diligence exercise on an unrelated sale. By that point the people who actually arranged the original funding may no longer be alive to explain it, and the only evidence available is whatever paper trail survives. Collecting and preserving bank statements, loan documents, gift deeds and family settlement records for older holdings, while the people who can explain them are still around to do so, is a far cheaper exercise than reconstructing the same facts under cross-examination decades later.

Ganpati Dealcom and the limits on retrospective enforcement

The 2016 amendment substantially rewrote the 1988 Act, adding the confiscation machinery and the criminal offence provisions in close to their current form. A question that reached the Supreme Court was whether these harsher 2016 provisions could be applied to transactions that took place before the amendment came into force. In Union of India v. Ganpati Dealcom Pvt. Ltd., decided in 2022, the Supreme Court held that the 2016 amendment operates prospectively: it cannot be used to prosecute or confiscate property over transactions that were completed before the amendment took effect. The Court also held the unamended Act's own confiscation provision was unconstitutional, and that the criminal offence under the amended Act applies only to transactions entered into after the amendment came into force.

The government sought review of this ruling, and the Supreme Court did not confirm it: on 18 October 2024 a Bench recalled the entire 2022 judgment (Review Petition (C) No. 359 of 2023, reported as 2024 INSC 799), holding that the constitutionality of the unamended Act should never have been decided in the absence of a live dispute between the parties on that specific question, and restored the appeal for fresh adjudication by a new Bench. The 2022 prospective-only position was therefore not the last word, and a later ruling has gone the other way on confiscation: in Manjula and Others v. D.A. Srinivas, 2026 INSC 465 (8 May 2026), the Supreme Court held that confiscation under the 2016 amendment is a civil consequence attached to the property, not a penal consequence, so it is not barred by Article 20(1) and can apply to transactions completed before the 2016 amendment, while criminal prosecution under the Act's penal provisions remains prospective only. The practical effect for families is the reverse of what the earlier, now-displaced position suggested: an old property arrangement completed well before November 2016 can still be confiscated under the amended Act's machinery, even though its participants cannot be criminally prosecuted for it, and families should not assume an old arrangement is safe from confiscation merely because it predates 2016.

What this means for structuring family property going forward

For any property arrangement entered into now, the safest course is to keep the relationship and the funding source aligned and documented from the outset, rather than relying on an exception to rescue an arrangement after the fact. If a property is genuinely being bought for an HUF's benefit with HUF funds, the documentation should show that clearly at the time of purchase. If parents are funding a property in a child's name, a paper trail showing the source of those funds, and ideally a contemporaneous record of the family's intention, protects the arrangement far better than an after-the-fact explanation offered once the transaction has already been questioned.

Families planning a larger restructuring, consolidating scattered holdings, transferring assets ahead of succession, or formalising arrangements that were historically informal, are better served treating each transfer as its own transaction to be tested against the current definition and its exceptions, rather than assuming that because a past arrangement in the family was never challenged, a new one structured the same way will be treated the same way under the current law.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it automatically benami if a father buys a flat in his son's name?

Not automatically. If the father can show the funds came from his own known, legitimate sources, the spouse-or-child exception under Section 2(9)(A) can apply. The exception depends on proving where the money actually came from, not merely on the family relationship between the person who paid and the person named on the title.

Can the government confiscate a property bought benami in, say, 2010?

Possibly, yes. The 2022 Ganpati Dealcom ruling that treated confiscation as prospective-only was recalled by the Supreme Court in October 2024, and a later ruling, Manjula and Others v. D.A. Srinivas, 2026 INSC 465, held that confiscation under the 2016 amendment is a civil consequence that can reach a transaction completed before the amendment. Criminal prosecution remains prospective only, so the participants cannot be charged over a pre-2016 transaction, but the property itself is not automatically safe from confiscation merely because it predates 2016.

Does holding property as a trustee count as benami?

No, provided the fiduciary relationship, trustee, executor, partner or director holding property for the entity, can actually be demonstrated with evidence. The fiduciary exception under Section 2(9)(A) exists specifically for this kind of arrangement, but the relationship still has to be proved if it is ever questioned.

What happens to the person whose name is on the title if a transaction is found benami?

The benamidar, the named title holder, faces exposure alongside the beneficial owner under the Act's criminal provisions, and the property itself is liable to confiscation through the adjudication process. Being only the named holder, rather than the person who paid, is not a defence by itself.

Is property bought with HUF funds in the Karta's own name always safe from the Act?

It falls within the HUF exception only if the consideration genuinely came from HUF funds and the property was purchased for the family's benefit. If the funding or purpose cannot be shown, the exception does not apply simply because the buyer happens to be the Karta.
