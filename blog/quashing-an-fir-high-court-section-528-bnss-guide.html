How an FIR Can Be Quashed in the High Court: Section 528 BNSS and the Settled Tests

The inherent power under Section 528 BNSS, the Bhajan Lal categories, how quashing differs from compounding, and what a petition actually needs to show.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 preserves the High Court's inherent power to prevent abuse of the process of any court or otherwise to secure the ends of justice, carrying forward what was previously Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure. It is a saving clause rather than a fresh grant of power, which is why decades of Supreme Court authority on when that power should be used, chiefly the categories laid down in State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal, continue to apply largely unchanged.

This guide sets out what those categories actually look like, how a quashing petition differs from compounding an offence, what a settlement between the parties does and does not achieve, and the practical mechanics of filing. It focuses on the general framework; where a business or contract dispute has been given a criminal colour specifically, our guide on breach of contract versus cheating goes into that fact pattern in more depth.

The source of the power, and why it survived the recodification

When the BNSS replaced the CrPC in 2024, there was real uncertainty over whether the High Court's inherent power would carry over in the same form. Section 528 answers that by using substantially the same language as the old Section 482, preserving the power to give effect to any order under the Sanhita, to prevent abuse of the process of any court, and to otherwise secure the ends of justice. Because the wording and the stated purpose are essentially unchanged, courts have continued to treat the pre-2024 body of Supreme Court precedent, including Bhajan Lal and its progeny, as directly applicable to petitions filed under the new section.

This point is not entirely free of debate. Some High Courts have raised questions about the precise scope of Section 528 at the investigation stage, and in 2025 the Allahabad High Court referred to a larger bench the specific question of whether an FIR and the investigation that follows it can be quashed under this inherent power before a chargesheet is filed, given that some earlier rulings had suggested investigation itself should not ordinarily be interfered with. Petitioners filing at an early stage, before any chargesheet, should be aware this question is not finally settled everywhere and check the current position in the relevant High Court.

The Bhajan Lal categories

In State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal, reported at 1992 Supp (1) SCC 335, the Supreme Court set out a set of situations in which the inherent power could properly be exercised to quash a complaint or investigation, while repeatedly cautioning that the power should be used sparingly and only in the rarest of cases, and that courts should not use it to assess the truth or falsity of the allegations as if conducting a mini-trial. The most commonly invoked category is where the allegations in the FIR or complaint, even if taken entirely at face value and accepted in their entirety, do not disclose the commission of any offence at all.

Other recognised categories include where the allegations are so absurd or inherently improbable that no prudent person could ever reach the conclusion that there is sufficient ground to proceed, where the matter is expressly barred by law such as one requiring a mandatory sanction that was never obtained, and where the criminal proceeding is shown to have been instituted with a mala fide intention, to wreak personal vengeance or to settle what is really a civil dispute through the pressure of a criminal case. Courts have treated the list as illustrative rather than a closed set of boxes to tick, which is why petitions are argued on the specific facts rather than by mechanically slotting the case into one labelled category.

Quashing is not the same as compounding

In Gian Singh v. State of Punjab, reported at (2012) 10 SCC 303, the Supreme Court drew a clear line between the trial court's power to compound certain offences under Section 320 of the former CrPC, now carried forward under the BNSS, and the High Court's separate inherent power to quash proceedings in light of a settlement. The two are not interchangeable: compounding is available only for offences the statute specifically lists as compoundable, and only in the manner the statute prescribes, whereas quashing on the strength of a settlement is an exercise of the High Court's inherent power and can, in an appropriate case, extend even to a non-compoundable offence.

The Court was careful to limit this. The inherent power to quash on settlement is generally exercised for offences that are predominantly civil or commercial in flavour, arising out of matrimonial, property or business disputes, where continuing the prosecution would serve no real purpose once the parties have voluntarily resolved their dispute. Offences that carry a serious public dimension, or that affect society rather than only the two private parties involved, are approached far more cautiously, and courts have been reluctant to quash merely because the complainant has agreed to withdraw, if the offence alleged is a grave one.

What a petition under Section 528 actually needs

The petition is filed before the High Court that has territorial jurisdiction over the police station or court where the case is registered, typically naming the State and the complainant as respondents, and annexing the FIR, the complaint if any, and any chargesheet already filed. There is no fixed limitation period for filing; a petition can in principle be filed at the FIR stage, during investigation, after a chargesheet, or even at a later stage of trial, though courts become more reluctant to interfere the further the case has progressed, and the standard for interference after a chargesheet requires the court to be satisfied on the FIR and chargesheet taken together, not merely the initial complaint in isolation.

Alongside the main petition, an application for stay of further proceedings in the trial court or of further investigation is almost always filed, since without it the underlying case can continue and even conclude while the quashing petition is pending. Courts do not grant this stay automatically; it generally requires showing a reasonably strong case on the merits of the quashing petition itself, not simply the inconvenience of defending two matters.

What tends to succeed, and what does not

Petitions tend to succeed where the complaint, on its own wording, reads like a civil claim with a criminal label attached: a specific and admitted sum due, no particularised allegation of deception or dishonest intention at the time the parties dealt with each other, and a complaint filed only after a demand for payment went unanswered. They are far less likely to succeed where the complainant points to a specific act of deception before the money or property changed hands, such as a forged document or a company shown never to have existed, since that kind of allegation, if it survives scrutiny on its face, is exactly what the offence is meant to cover and is ordinarily left for trial to decide on evidence.

The Supreme Court has also made clear in recent rulings that the mere existence of an available civil remedy does not by itself bar a criminal proceeding; a complaint cannot be quashed only because the complainant could also have filed a civil suit. At the same time, the Court has reaffirmed that the High Court has a duty to step in where continuing a prosecution would plainly amount to an abuse of process, particularly where the underlying dispute is genuinely one for a civil court to decide on disputed questions of fact. The two statements sit together rather than in tension: availability of a civil remedy is not itself decisive either way, and each petition still has to independently satisfy the Bhajan Lal test on its own facts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a time limit for filing a quashing petition under Section 528?

No fixed limitation period applies, and a petition can be filed at the FIR stage, during investigation, or after a chargesheet, though courts apply a more demanding standard of satisfaction the further the matter has progressed.

If the complainant and I have settled, is the FIR automatically quashed?

No. A settlement supports a quashing petition but is not self-executing; the High Court still examines whether the offence is one it considers appropriate to quash on that basis, and offences with a serious public dimension are approached far more cautiously even where the parties have settled.

Can an FIR be quashed before any investigation has even started?

In principle yes, but this specific point is currently unsettled in some High Courts; the Allahabad High Court referred the question of quashing an FIR and its consequential investigation to a larger bench in 2025, so check the current position in the relevant jurisdiction before assuming an early-stage petition will be entertained the same way everywhere.

Does filing a quashing petition stop the criminal case automatically?

No. The underlying proceedings continue unless the High Court specifically grants an interim stay, which is why a stay application is almost always filed alongside the main petition rather than left for later.

Will the court decide whether the allegations are actually true?

No. Bhajan Lal itself cautions that the High Court should not weigh the truth or falsity of the allegations at this stage; the question is whether the allegations, taken at face value, disclose an offence or otherwise fall within a recognised category for quashing, not who is right on the facts.
