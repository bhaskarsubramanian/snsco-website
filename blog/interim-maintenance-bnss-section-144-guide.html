Interim Maintenance Under the BNSS: How Courts Decide the Amount and the Date It Runs From

How interim maintenance works under BNSS Section 144 (formerly CrPC Section 125), the Rajnesh v. Neha disclosure rule and date of award, as on Nov 2026.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A spouse who has just filed for maintenance, and has no income of their own while the case runs its course, usually cannot wait a year or two for a final order. The law anticipates exactly this problem and lets the Magistrate order something to live on while the main application is still being decided. The provision that does this has a new number since 1 July 2024, but its working has not changed as much as the renumbering might suggest.

This guide explains interim maintenance under Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which replaced Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, the mandatory financial disclosure the Supreme Court built into the process in Rajnesh v. Neha, and the date from which an award of maintenance actually starts running.

A new number, largely the same provision

Section 144 of the BNSS replaced Section 125 of the CrPC with effect from 1 July 2024. A maintenance case already pending on that date continues to be governed by the old Section 125 under the savings provision in Section 531(2) of the BNSS, so a case filed in, say, 2023 is not automatically moved onto the new section's numbering, even though a case filed after 1 July 2024 is brought under Section 144 from the start.

The class of people who can claim under either version is the same: a wife who is unable to maintain herself, including a divorced wife who has not remarried, a minor child, a major child who cannot maintain themselves because of a physical or mental disability, and a father or mother who is unable to maintain themselves. A married daughter does not fall within this specific provision, which is a frequent point of confusion for parents trying to claim against a married daughter rather than a son.

The second proviso: maintenance while the case is still running

Section 144(1) carries a proviso dealing specifically with interim relief, empowering the Magistrate to order the person liable to pay a monthly interim maintenance allowance, and the expenses of the proceeding, during the pendency of the main application. This is the provision an applicant actually relies on in the first weeks or months of a maintenance case, since a final order can take considerably longer to reach.

The statute directs that an application for interim maintenance should, as far as practicable, be disposed of within a set period after notice is served on the other side. Different secondary sources describe how firmly that period is enforced in practice, so an applicant should treat it as a direction to the court to move quickly rather than a deadline that automatically invalidates a delayed order; what matters practically is pressing the Magistrate for an early hearing date rather than assuming a strict cut-off will do that work on its own.

The affidavit that decides most interim applications

In Rajnesh v. Neha, decided by the Supreme Court on 4 November 2020 and reported at (2021) 2 SCC 324, the Court required both parties in a maintenance proceeding to file a sworn affidavit of disclosure of assets and liabilities, in a standard format the judgment itself sets out, so that neither side can hide income or assets while the application is being decided. Both affidavits are meant to be filed around the same point in the case, precisely so that one party does not get a tactical advantage by seeing the other's figures first.

Where the respondent does not file the affidavit on time, the Court made clear that the Magistrate can decide the interim application on the strength of the applicant's affidavit alone, after giving no more than a couple of further opportunities to comply. This single guideline has done more to speed up interim orders in practice than almost any other part of the judgment, because it removes the respondent's ability to stall the interim hearing simply by not engaging with it.

How the amount actually gets fixed

Rajnesh v. Neha also set out criteria for the Magistrate to weigh in fixing the quantum of maintenance, interim or final, rather than leaving the figure to unguided discretion. The disclosed income and assets of both parties, the reasonable needs of the applicant and any dependants, the respondent's actual earning capacity rather than only current income, and the standard of living the parties were accustomed to are the kind of considerations the guidelines are built around, applied to the facts the two affidavits put before the court.

A figure fixed at the interim stage is not final and can be revisited once the main case is decided, or earlier if either side's financial position changes materially. An applicant should not treat a low interim figure as the end of the matter, nor should a respondent treat a generous interim figure as binding once the full evidence is in.

Courts also look past the headline salary figure on a payslip. A respondent who has recently taken a pay cut, changed jobs, or shows a sudden and unexplained drop in income around the time the claim is filed tends to attract closer scrutiny of the disclosed figures, including bank statements and past tax filings, rather than a figure accepted purely at face value.

From what date maintenance actually runs

Before Rajnesh v. Neha, courts differed on whether maintenance should run from the date the application was filed or only from the date the order was finally passed, which could be months or years later depending on how contested the case was. The Supreme Court settled this in favour of the applicant, holding that maintenance, including under Section 125 CrPC, is to be awarded from the date the application was made, not from the later date of the order.

This matters in practical terms because a long-delayed interim or final order does not shortchange the applicant for the delay; once granted, the arrears run back to the filing date. A respondent facing a maintenance claim should factor this backdated exposure into any settlement discussion rather than assume that dragging out the proceeding reduces the eventual liability.

It also changes how an applicant should think about filing early. Waiting several months after separation to file, in the hope of first gathering a stronger set of financial documents against the other side, carries a real cost, because the arrears clock only starts once the application is actually on record, not from the date the parties stopped living together or the date the cause of action first arose.

When more than one maintenance claim is running at once

It is common for a spouse to have more than one maintenance proceeding open at the same time, for instance a claim under Section 144 BNSS alongside an application under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 or under Section 24 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955. High Courts had taken different views on whether an amount awarded in one of these proceedings should be set off against an amount claimed in another, with some treating each statute as creating an independent right and others requiring adjustment to avoid double recovery.

Rajnesh v. Neha directs courts to consider adjusting or setting off an amount already awarded in an earlier proceeding when deciding how much further, if anything, is due in a later one under a different statute. An applicant running more than one claim, and a respondent facing more than one, should both disclose the other pending or decided proceedings to the court hearing the later claim, rather than let the point surface only if the other side happens to raise it.

Practical points before the first hearing

Prepare the affidavit of disclosure as carefully as the application itself, since it is frequently the single document the interim order turns on. Bank statements, salary slips, income tax returns for the last few years, and a realistic statement of monthly expenses carry more weight than a round, unsupported figure for income or need.

List every other maintenance-related proceeding, whether already decided, pending, or only contemplated, in the application or the affidavit itself, rather than leaving the court to discover a parallel claim later in the case. Doing this early makes the set-off guideline work in the applicant's or respondent's favour as intended, instead of becoming a fresh ground of dispute once the other proceeding comes to light.

Finally, treat the interim order as a working arrangement rather than a verdict on the merits of the whole case. Both sides sometimes read more into an interim figure than the Magistrate intended, when its real function is only to keep the applicant afloat while the facts are tested properly at the final hearing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Section 125 CrPC still used for maintenance applications?

Only for cases that were already pending on 1 July 2024, which continue under the old Section 125 CrPC by virtue of the savings clause in Section 531 of the BNSS. A maintenance application filed after that date is brought under the new Section 144 of the BNSS instead.

Can a spouse get maintenance while the main case is still pending?

Yes. The proviso to Section 144(1) of the BNSS lets the Magistrate order a monthly interim maintenance allowance and litigation expenses while the main application is being decided, and the statute directs this to be disposed of quickly, though the exact timeline is treated as a direction rather than a rigid cut-off.

What happens if the respondent does not file the financial disclosure affidavit required under Rajnesh v. Neha?

The Magistrate can decide the interim maintenance application on the basis of the applicant's own affidavit of assets and liabilities, after allowing the respondent no more than a couple of further opportunities to file theirs, rather than letting non-compliance stall the hearing indefinitely.

From what date does a maintenance order actually start running?

From the date the application was filed, not the later date the order is passed, following Rajnesh v. Neha. Arrears for the period between filing and the final order are payable once the order is made.

Can someone claim maintenance under more than one law at the same time?

Yes, for example under the BNSS and the Domestic Violence Act or the Hindu Marriage Act in parallel, but Rajnesh v. Neha directs courts to consider setting off an amount already awarded in one proceeding against what is claimed in another, to prevent double recovery of the same relief.
