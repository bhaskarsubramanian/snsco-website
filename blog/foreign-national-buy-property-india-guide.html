Can a Foreign National Buy Property in India?

Why a foreign national who is not an NRI or OCI generally cannot buy property in India, the narrow exceptions, and how the rules differ for NRIs and OCIs.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 16 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

The short answer is no, not in most cases. A foreign national who is not a Non-Resident Indian or an Overseas Citizen of India cannot acquire immovable property in India by purchase, as the law stands today. This comes as a surprise to many foreign executives and investors who assume that owning a home or an office here works the same way it does for a foreign buyer in the United Kingdom, Dubai or Singapore. It does not, and the restriction is a foreign exchange control rule, not a general property law rule, which is why it applies regardless of how the purchase is funded.

The position is different for a person of Indian origin who holds NRI or OCI status, who can buy most categories of property without special permission. The confusion usually arises because people conflate 'foreigner living in India' with 'NRI' or 'OCI', when these are legally distinct categories with different rights under India's foreign exchange rules.

The general rule: a foreign national cannot buy, only lease short term

Under the rules that govern how a person resident outside India may deal with immovable property here, a foreign national who is not of Indian origin, and who has not taken up residence in India, cannot acquire immovable property in India by purchase. The one broad carve-out that applies to any foreign national, Indian-origin or not, is that these restrictions do not apply to a lease of immovable property for a term not exceeding five years. That is why most foreign executives posted to India for a defined assignment take a residential lease rather than attempt a purchase, and it is also why a foreign company wanting office space either leases it or, if it wants to own, does so through an Indian subsidiary rather than in its own name.

There is also a narrower route for a foreign national who is not an NRI or OCI but is married to one. The rules allow such a spouse to acquire one immovable property in India, jointly with the NRI or OCI spouse, with funds that comply with the usual foreign exchange rules, but this excludes agricultural land, farm houses and plantation property, and the marriage must have subsisted for a continuous period of not less than two years, among other conditions that an authorised dealer bank will check before registering the transaction.

How NRIs and OCIs are treated differently

A Non-Resident Indian and an Overseas Citizen of India sit outside the general prohibition. Both can acquire immovable property in India other than agricultural land, plantation property and a farm house, without needing specific RBI permission for the purchase itself, because the general permission built into the rules already covers them. The exclusion of agricultural land, plantation property and farm houses applies equally to NRIs and OCIs, which is a point that catches out buyers who assume their NRI status removes every restriction; it does not, and this particular category of property stays closed to purchase by both NRIs and OCIs regardless of funds.

Inheritance is treated separately from purchase. A foreign national of non-Indian origin, resident outside India, can hold immovable property in India that was inherited from a person who was resident in India, and this applies even to agricultural land, farm houses and plantation property that would otherwise be out of reach on a purchase. The logic is that inheritance is not an acquisition the rules are trying to prevent; it is succession, which is governed by India's succession laws rather than by the foreign exchange restriction on purchase.

The country-specific layer that sits on top of the NRI and OCI rules

Separately from the NRI/OCI distinction, the rules carry a further restriction that applies to citizens of certain countries that India has notified for this purpose, regardless of whether that person happens to be of Indian origin or otherwise falls within an exception elsewhere in the rules. A citizen of one of these notified countries needs the Reserve Bank of India's prior permission to acquire or transfer immovable property in India, even in situations where a citizen of another country in a similar position would not. This permission requirement applies to citizens of the notified countries, though the RBI's own FAQ states the restriction does not apply to an OCI cardholder, so whether Indian-origin status affects this requirement should be checked against the current notification rather than assumed either way. Because the list of notified countries changes and is not reproduced in most commentary, anyone in this position should check the current notification directly with an authorised dealer bank or the RBI rather than rely on a general description.

This layered structure, a general prohibition for foreign nationals, exceptions for NRIs, OCIs, inheritance and NRI/OCI spouses, and then a further restriction for citizens of certain countries on top of all of it, is the single biggest source of mistaken assumptions in this area. A foreign national who is told generally that 'foreigners cannot buy property in India' is being given a simplification that misses the inheritance and spousal exceptions, while an NRI who is told 'NRIs can buy anything' is being given a simplification that misses the agricultural land exclusion and, if relevant, the country-specific layer.

Selling and remitting proceeds is a separate question from buying

Being allowed to hold property and being allowed to freely sell it and take the money out of India are not the same permission, and this trips up people who focus only on the purchase stage. An NRI or OCI who sells property they were permitted to acquire can generally repatriate the sale proceeds, but subject to conditions on the amount, the number of residential properties covered, and the route through which the original purchase was funded, and this usually needs supporting paperwork from a chartered accountant on the tax position before the bank will remit funds abroad. A foreign national of non-Indian origin who is holding inherited property faces its own set of conditions when selling and remitting, which are generally tighter than for an NRI or OCI, and typically require RBI's permission where the property falls outside the categories that have general permission.

Anyone planning a purchase, or a sale followed by remittance abroad, should work out the full chain before signing anything: residential status under the foreign exchange rules, Indian-origin status if relevant, the category of property, and whether the funding and eventual repatriation route is one that has general permission or needs a specific application. Getting the category wrong at the start is far more expensive to fix after a sale deed is registered than before, since the authorised dealer bank will ask for this chain to be documented at the time money is remitted abroad, not retrospectively once a dispute or a tax query has already arisen.

Buying through an Indian company instead of personally

A route many foreign nationals and foreign companies use instead of a personal purchase is to acquire or lease property through an Indian company, including a wholly owned subsidiary, which as an Indian resident entity is not subject to the same personal restrictions on foreign nationals. This is common for office premises, factory land and warehousing, and it is the normal structure once a foreign company has moved from a liaison or branch office to an Indian subsidiary. It does not, however, let a foreign national acquire a personal residence through a corporate wrapper for the purpose of living in it, since authorities and banks look at the real purpose and beneficial use of the property, and a mismatch between a company's stated business purpose and an individual's personal residential use invites scrutiny on exactly that basis.

The same logic applies to funding. Money brought in to buy property through an Indian company has to be routed as genuine foreign direct investment or an equivalent permitted inflow, reported to the RBI in the ordinary way, rather than structured to look like a round trip meant only to get around the personal purchase restriction. Banks and auditors involved in such a transaction typically ask for the underlying business rationale in writing before processing the funds, precisely because this structure is the one most often misused by individuals trying to buy a home for personal use under a corporate name.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a foreign national on a work visa in India buy an apartment to live in?

Generally no, unless that person qualifies as an NRI or OCI. A foreign national who is simply working in India on a visa, without Indian-origin status, is still treated as a person resident outside India for this purpose and falls under the general prohibition, so the usual route is a residential lease of up to five years instead of a purchase.

Can an OCI cardholder buy agricultural land in India?

No. The exclusion for agricultural land, plantation property and farm houses applies to OCI cardholders in the same way it applies to NRIs. An OCI can buy most other residential and commercial property without specific permission, but agricultural land stays outside the general permission for both categories.

What happens if a foreign national inherits a farmhouse from an Indian resident relative?

Inheritance is treated differently from purchase. A foreign national, including one with no Indian-origin status, can hold property inherited from a person resident in India, and this covers agricultural land, plantation property and farm houses that could not have been bought directly. Selling and repatriating the proceeds afterward is a separate step with its own conditions.

Does a foreign company need special permission to lease office space in India?

A lease of five years or less is outside the restrictions that apply to a purchase, so a foreign company or its liaison, branch or project office can generally take a short lease directly. Longer-term premises are usually held through an Indian subsidiary instead of leased personally or by the foreign entity itself.
