Correcting an Error in the Khatauni or Revenue Record in Uttar Pradesh

How to correct an error in a khatauni, khasra or map in UP under Sections 35 and 38 of the Revenue Code, and why the corrected entry is not proof of title.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A landowner in Noida, Greater Noida or anywhere else in rural Uttar Pradesh who finds their name misspelled in the khatauni, an old owner still showing against a plot that was sold years ago, or a wrong area or boundary recorded in the khasra, usually wants one thing: get the record fixed, fast. The Uttar Pradesh Revenue Code, 2006 gives two different routes for this depending on what actually went wrong, and using the wrong one wastes time in front of the wrong officer. Buyers run into the same confusion from the other side, assuming a clean record settles a question it was never designed to answer.

This guide separates mutation on succession or transfer under Section 35 from the correction of a genuine error or omission under Section 38, explains who decides each, and why neither proceeding, however successful, settles a real dispute about who owns the land.

Two different problems, two different sections

Most people describe both situations the same way, as the record needing to be corrected, but the Revenue Code treats them differently. Section 35 deals with mutation, which is the entry that follows a transfer of the holding, typically a sale, gift or inheritance, where the record simply has not caught up with a change in ownership that everyone accepts happened. Section 38 deals with an error or omission in the map, the khasra, or the khatauni itself, something that was recorded wrongly in the first place, such as a misspelled name, a wrong khasra number, an incorrect area, or an entry that was never updated when it should have been.

The practical difference matters because the application goes to different proceedings with different standards. A mutation application under Section 35 assumes the underlying transfer is not seriously in dispute and asks the Tehsildar to update the record to reflect it. A correction application under Section 38 is aimed at the record itself being wrong on its face, independent of any transfer, and the Tehsildar's power here extends to correcting old and existing entries, going well beyond entries made recently.

Mutation on succession or transfer: Section 35

Under Section 35(1), the Tehsildar records mutation in the khatauni once satisfied that a transfer or succession has taken place, usually on the strength of the sale deed, the registered gift deed, or the documents establishing inheritance, such as a succession certificate or the legal heirs' own affidavits where the law does not insist on a certificate. The Tehsildar's order under this sub-section is what actually changes the recorded owner's name.

An order passed under Section 35(1) is appealable under Section 35(2), and Allahabad High Court rulings have treated the Sub-Divisional Magistrate as the appellate authority for that appeal, reading Section 35(2) together with Section 207 and Schedule III of the Code. The courts have also held that, because no further appeal lies against an order passed in a Section 35(2) appeal itself, such an appellate order is open to revision under Section 210, which is available precisely where no appeal lies against the order being challenged.

Correcting a genuine error: Section 38

Section 38 covers errors or omissions in the map, the khasra, and the khatauni, and an application for correction is made to the Tahsildar in the manner prescribed. The Allahabad High Court has read this power as extending to non-Z.A. areas as well, and as reaching old and existing records, not just entries made after the 2006 Code came into force. The correcting authority is not limited to acting on an application either; the courts have confirmed the power can be exercised suo motu, on the authority's own initiative, once an error comes to notice.

Where the entry is plainly forged or manipulated rather than merely mistaken, Section 38 allows it to be expunged outright. Rules framed under the Code direct that an endeavour be made to conclude a Section 38 correction proceeding within forty-five days of the application being received, with reasons to be recorded where that target is missed; this is an administrative target rather than a hard deadline a court will treat as mandatory, so an applicant should follow up rather than assume the file is closed once forty-five days pass.

The Allahabad High Court has also described Section 38 proceedings as summary in nature, meaning the finding reached in them is not treated as a final adjudication of the underlying right. This is the single most important thing to understand before filing: a Section 38 order gets the record right on its face, but it does not finally decide a contested question of ownership between rival claimants.

Why none of this is proof of title

Revenue entries, whether a fresh mutation under Section 35 or a correction under Section 38, serve the State's own purposes: identifying who pays land revenue, who is in possession for administrative purposes, and who the record currently treats as the holder. None of that is the same as a judicial determination of title. Courts applying the Code have consistently treated these entries as not conferring or extinguishing ownership, and as remaining open to recall, appeal or revision precisely because they are administrative rather than adjudicatory in character.

For a buyer doing title due diligence on a Noida or rural UP property, this cuts both ways. A clean, up-to-date khatauni in the seller's name is a useful and often necessary administrative fact, and its absence is a real red flag, but it is not by itself conclusive proof that the seller actually owns the property free of competing claims. Where real doubt exists about who owns the land, rather than simply whose name the record currently shows, a civil suit for declaration of title, not a Section 35 or Section 38 application, is the proceeding that can actually settle the question.

What to file, and what to keep ready

For a mutation application, the sale deed, gift deed, will, or succession documents establishing the transfer are the core papers, together with identity proof and the existing khatauni extract showing the current entry. For a correction application under Section 38, the application should point precisely to the error, whether it is in the name, the area, the khasra number, or the map, and should attach whatever documentary proof shows what the entry should actually say, such as an earlier correct extract, the registered deed, or survey records.

Local practice in most UP tehsils involves the Lekhpal verifying the position on the ground before the Tehsildar passes an order, so an applicant should expect at least one site-level verification step in addition to the paper scrutiny. Keeping certified copies of every khatauni extract obtained over the years, rather than relying on memory of what the record used to say, makes both a mutation and a correction application considerably easier to support.

Where the record can be checked online through the State's land records portal, printing or saving a dated copy of the entry before applying is worth doing as a matter of course, since it gives a fixed reference point for exactly what was wrong and when it was still showing that way, which the officer handling the application will usually ask for in any event.

When correction turns into a dispute

An application that looks like a simple correction can turn contested the moment another party with an interest in the entry objects, for instance a co-owner who disputes the extent of someone else's share, or a person claiming under a competing document. Once that happens, the proceeding is no longer a straightforward administrative correction; the officer has to address the dispute before deciding whether, and how, to amend the record, and the matter effectively becomes adversarial even though it is still being heard by a revenue officer rather than a civil court.

An applicant facing this situation should not expect the revenue proceeding to resolve the deeper ownership question even if it results in an order in their favour, for the reasons explained above. Where the dispute is substantial, for instance where title itself, rather than a clerical fact, is genuinely contested, filing or preparing to file a civil suit alongside the revenue proceeding is usually the more durable route, rather than treating the revenue order as the final word.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between mutation and correcting a khatauni error in UP?

Mutation under Section 35 updates the record after an accepted transfer or succession, such as a sale or inheritance. Correction under Section 38 fixes an error or omission already present in the map, khasra or khatauni, such as a wrong name or area, and is not tied to a transfer at all.

Does a corrected khatauni entry prove that a person owns the land?

No. Courts applying the UP Revenue Code have treated mutation and correction entries as administrative records that neither confer nor extinguish title. A contested ownership question is decided by a civil court, not settled finally by a revenue entry.

Who hears an appeal against a Tehsildar's mutation order in Uttar Pradesh?

An order under Section 35(1) is appealable under Section 35(2), and Allahabad High Court rulings have treated the Sub-Divisional Magistrate as the appellate authority for that appeal, reading Section 35(2) with Section 207 and Schedule III of the Code together.

Can a revenue officer correct a khatauni error without anyone filing an application?

Yes. Courts have confirmed that the correction power under Section 38 can be exercised suo motu, on the authority's own initiative once an error comes to notice, in addition to being triggered by an application from an interested party.

How long does a Section 38 correction proceeding take in UP?

The Rules direct an endeavour to conclude the proceeding within forty-five days of the application being received, with reasons recorded if that is not met. This is an administrative target rather than a strict legal deadline, so an applicant should follow up actively rather than assume delay is unusual.
