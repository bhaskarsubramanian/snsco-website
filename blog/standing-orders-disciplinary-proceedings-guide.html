Standing Orders and Disciplinary Proceedings: The Process an Employer Must Follow Before Dismissing an Employee

A guide to what certified standing orders are, why they matter for disciplinary action, and the essential steps of a valid domestic inquiry before an employer can lawfully dismiss an employee for misconduct.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 18 June 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

What Are Standing Orders

Standing orders are the formal rules governing conditions of employment, classification of workers, hours of work, leave, termination, and disciplinary procedure, that certain establishments are required to draft and get certified by the labour authority under industrial employment (standing orders) legislation, or which are supplied through model standing orders where an establishment has not certified its own. Once certified, standing orders have statutory force and bind both the employer and its workmen, and disciplinary action inconsistent with the certified standing orders can itself be challenged as improper.

Misconduct Defined Under Standing Orders

Standing orders typically list specific categories of misconduct that can attract disciplinary action, ranging from habitual absence or late attendance to insubordination, fraud, theft, and conduct prejudicial to the employer's interest. An employer initiating disciplinary action should identify precisely which listed category of misconduct the employee's conduct falls under, since a charge that does not correspond to a recognised category of misconduct under the applicable standing orders is vulnerable to challenge.

The Essential Steps of a Domestic Inquiry

A lawful domestic inquiry generally requires: a clear charge-sheet specifying the alleged misconduct; a reasonable opportunity for the employee to respond in writing; appointment of an inquiry officer distinct from the person who framed the charges, wherever practicable; a fair hearing at which the employee can cross-examine witnesses and lead their own evidence; a reasoned inquiry report; and, critically, furnishing that report to the employee with an opportunity to represent against it before the disciplinary authority decides the punishment.

Consequences of a Procedurally Defective Inquiry

Where a domestic inquiry is procedurally defective, the resulting dismissal is vulnerable to being set aside by a Labour Court or Industrial Tribunal, which may then examine the merits of the alleged misconduct afresh, or in more serious procedural lapses, may order reinstatement with back wages. Getting the process right is therefore not a formality: it is frequently the deciding factor in whether a dismissal, even one grounded in genuine misconduct, is ultimately sustained.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does every employer need certified standing orders?

Employers above a specified employee-strength threshold under the applicable standing orders legislation are required to certify their own standing orders; smaller establishments and, in some cases, employers who have not certified their own, are governed by the applicable model standing orders.

Can an employer dismiss an employee without a domestic inquiry?

Only in narrow, exceptional circumstances recognised by law, such as where holding an inquiry is genuinely impracticable, and even then, the employer typically bears the burden of justifying the misconduct if the dismissal is later challenged. As a general rule, a fair domestic inquiry is required before dismissal for misconduct.

What happens if the inquiry officer is the same person who framed the charges?

This is not automatically fatal, but it raises a fairness concern that increases the risk of the inquiry being challenged: employers should appoint an independent inquiry officer wherever practicable to reduce this risk.

Can a Labour Court reinstate an employee even if the misconduct genuinely occurred?

Yes: where the inquiry process itself was fundamentally unfair, a Labour Court or Tribunal can set aside the dismissal on that basis, sometimes ordering reinstatement, even where the underlying facts might otherwise have supported disciplinary action, underscoring the importance of getting the process right.
