EPS-95 Higher Pension: Where the Supreme Court's 2022 Option Actually Stands

What EPFO v Sunil Kumar B (2022) actually allows on EPS-95 higher pension, who can still apply, and why many applications remain stuck as on November 2026.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An eligible EPF member who was in service on 1 September 2014 and contributed on wages above the statutory ceiling can still ask EPFO to compute pension on actual salary rather than the capped amount, by exercising the higher-pension joint option and depositing the contribution shortfall with interest. The right comes from the Supreme Court's ruling in EPFO v Sunil Kumar B & Ors, decided on 4 November 2022, and as on this date a large share of the applications filed under it are still stuck in verification rather than finally decided.

This piece sets out what the Court actually held, who the option realistically helps, how the application and payment process works, and where the backlog and the exempted-establishment dispute currently stand. It also flags where the scheme itself has moved under the new labour codes, because EPS-95 is no longer the scheme's current name even though the underlying entitlement for members who already opted in continues.

What the Supreme Court actually decided in 2022

The Employees' Pension Scheme, 1995 originally let a member whose salary exceeded the wage ceiling contribute, and draw pension, on the actual higher salary, subject to paying extra contribution, under the proviso to paragraph 11(3). A 2014 amendment deleted that proviso and replaced it with paragraph 11(4), tightening the route and adding a fresh 1.16 percent employee contribution on salary above the ceiling. Several High Courts struck the amendment down; EPFO appealed, and a three-judge bench decided the batch on 4 November 2022 in EPFO v Sunil Kumar B & Ors (Civil Appeal Nos 8143-8144 of 2022, reported variously by different reporters).

The Court did not strike the 2014 amendment down outright. It upheld the amendment's validity but read down two of its harsher features: it struck the requirement that a member must have exercised the option within a rigid cut-off window before the amendment took effect, and it held the additional 1.16 percent employee contribution unsustainable without a corresponding government contribution, directing that it not be recovered in that form. The practical effect was to reopen the option, for a further defined period, to members who were in service on 1 September 2014 but had not exercised it earlier, while leaving the scheme's basic 2014 structure intact for everyone else.

Who the option actually helps

The ruling is not a general pension increase for every EPF pensioner. It helps a narrower group: EPS members who were contributing on salary above the wage ceiling and who were in service, or whose employment continued, as on or after 1 September 2014. A member who retired well before that date without ever exercising an option under the old paragraph 11(3) sits outside the core group the judgment was written for, and EPFO's own 2025 circular (as reported in trade publications) treats pre-September 2014 retirees who had exercised the option differently from those who had not, which is a distinction worth checking against the actual circular text before assuming eligibility either way.

EPFO initially gave eligible members a short window counted from its first implementing circular, then extended the cut-off for filing the joint option; different trade and advisory sources describe the final extended date differently, so anyone relying on a specific calendar date should check it against EPFO's own current circular rather than a news report. What is consistent across sources is that the option had to be exercised jointly with the employer, since the employer's cooperation in depositing the contribution differential is not optional.

A member who switched employers during the relevant years also needs to check whether each employer, past and present, is willing to cooperate on its share of the back-payment, because the computation runs employer by employer rather than as one consolidated figure across a career. This is one of the quieter reasons applications stall: a member's current employer may be fully cooperative while a previous one, now under different management or simply uninterested in old liabilities, is not.

How the application and the contribution shortfall actually work

A member applies through EPFO's online higher-pension facility, identifying the period during which contributions were capped at the wage ceiling. EPFO computes the differential between what was actually contributed to the pension fund and what would have been contributed had contributions been on the real salary, and the employer is required to deposit that differential, along with interest at the rate applicable to EPF accumulations for the relevant years, before EPFO revises the pension.

In practice this step is where most delay happens. Employers that have since closed, been acquired, or simply resist making the back-payment leave the member's application stalled regardless of the member's own entitlement. Where an employer refuses or drags its feet, a member's practical remedy sits closer to pressing the regional EPFO office and, if the employer is in default on provident fund dues generally, to the enforcement route under Section 7A of the EPF Act rather than any separate pension-specific proceeding.

The interest component also deserves attention. Because the shortfall often spans several years, the interest added to the principal differential can end up being a substantial part of what the employer owes, and employers sometimes contest the interest rate applied or the period over which it runs. A member who receives a demand for an unusually large or unusually small shortfall figure should ask EPFO for the year-by-year computation rather than accepting a single consolidated number without seeing how it was built.

Where the backlog stands as on late 2026

Parliament was told, in a reply dated around 10 August 2026, that EPFO had received roughly 15.24 lakh applications for higher pension and that a five-figure number of cases remained pending at that point, a sharp fall from the much larger pending count reported a year or two earlier, though the exact pending figure moves with each parliamentary answer and should be checked against the latest one rather than this guide. A Rajya Sabha committee has separately sought a status report from the Labour Ministry, and media reports describe the Prime Minister's Office as examining the pending cases, which signals that the backlog is still a live political and administrative concern rather than a closed chapter.

Members who filed early and are still without a revised pension order are not unusual; the sensible course is a written follow-up to the regional EPFO office citing the application reference number, rather than assuming silence means rejection. Where a regional office gives no substantive response over an extended period, escalating in writing to the zonal or head office, with copies of every earlier follow-up, tends to move a file faster than repeated phone enquiries, which leave no paper trail an escalation can later point to.

The exempted-establishment dispute

A separate fight concerns members of exempted establishments, meaning employers that run their own private provident fund trust instead of routing contributions through EPFO directly. EPFO had issued a circular restricting higher-pension claims from such members, on the view that paragraph 39 of the EPS exempted them from the ordinary option. In September 2025 the Madras High Court set that circular aside and directed EPFO to accept joint-option applications from exempted-establishment members filed on or before 31 January 2025, rejecting the view that paragraph 39 operates as a blanket exemption.

Anyone working for an employer with its own PF trust who was earlier told they could not apply should treat that earlier refusal as open to challenge in light of this ruling, while confirming whether their own application was filed within the window the Madras High Court actually addressed. A member in this position should also check how their employer's own trust deed treats the higher-pension computation, since an exempted trust runs its own fund subject to standards at least equivalent to EPFO's, and the trust's internal rules can affect how quickly a revised calculation is actually carried out once the legal entitlement is no longer in dispute.

EPS-95 is no longer the scheme's current name

Under the Code on Social Security, 2020, the government has notified a consolidated Employees' Pension Scheme that replaces both EPS-95 and the older Employees' Family Pension Scheme, 1971. Members who already exercised the higher-pension joint option under EPS-95 continue to receive the same higher-pension benefit, now carried forward under the new scheme, rather than losing it on the changeover.

Separately, there has been recurring talk of raising the minimum monthly EPS pension above its long-standing one thousand rupee floor, with reported figures ranging as high as seven thousand five hundred rupees in some accounts and a flat denial of any immediate hike in others. As on this date no revised minimum pension figure has been confirmed from an official notification in the material available, and readers should treat any specific rupee figure circulating in the press as unconfirmed until EPFO notifies it.

For a member who already has a revised, higher pension order under the 2022 judgment, the rename and consolidation exercise should not require any fresh application; the expectation across the sources reviewed is that existing orders simply carry forward. Anyone whose pension payment changes unexpectedly around the time of this transition should ask EPFO for a written explanation referencing the specific order or circular responsible, rather than assuming a reduction is a routine administrative adjustment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is EPS-95 still the scheme that applies to me today?

The scheme has been consolidated into a new Employees' Pension Scheme under the Code on Social Security, 2020. If you already exercised the higher-pension option under EPS-95, that benefit continues under the new scheme; the underlying entitlement has not been taken away by the rename.

I retired before September 2014 and never exercised an option. Can I still apply for higher pension now?

This is the group the 2022 judgment was least focused on. EPFO's reported 2025 position treats pre-2014 retirees who had exercised an option under the old paragraph 11(3) differently from those who never did. Check your own facts against EPFO's current circular rather than assuming either way.

My employer won't deposit the pension contribution shortfall. What can I do?

Follow up in writing with the regional EPFO office quoting your application reference, and if the employer is generally in default on provident fund dues, EPFO's enforcement powers under Section 7A of the EPF Act are the relevant route, since there is no separate pension-only enforcement mechanism.

Does working for a company with its own private PF trust block my higher-pension claim?

Not any more, within limits. The Madras High Court's September 2025 ruling requires EPFO to accept joint-option applications from exempted-establishment members filed on or before 31 January 2025, rejecting EPFO's circular that had treated such members as excluded. If your application was filed within that window and still refused, that refusal is worth revisiting against this ruling.

Has the government confirmed a hike in the minimum EPS pension?

Not as on this date. Reports describe proposals ranging up to several thousand rupees a month, alongside statements that no immediate hike is planned. Treat any specific figure in the press as provisional until EPFO or the Labour Ministry issues an actual notification.
