Can an Employer Recover Training Costs When an Employee Quits Early? Training Bonds in India

Training bonds are enforceable in India within limits set by Sections 27 and 74 of the Contract Act. What employers can recover, and what they cannot.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An employer spends real money sending a new hire through a structured training programme, sometimes abroad, sometimes through a specialised course, and then watches the employee resign eight months later to join a competitor. The employment contract has a clause requiring two or three years of service or repayment of training costs. Whether that clause is actually worth anything depends on how it was drafted and what the employer can prove, not on the fact that a bond was signed.

This question comes up constantly in sectors that invest heavily in onboarding, from IT services firms sending engineers through vendor certification programmes to aviation and healthcare employers funding licences and specialised courses. The law on it has not changed dramatically in decades, but it is routinely misapplied because employers draft these clauses as if they were ordinary liquidated damages provisions, without the evidentiary record that actually makes them enforceable.

Why a training bond is not automatically a non-compete

A training bond and a non-compete clause get confused constantly, but they work on different principles. A non-compete tries to stop an employee from working for a competitor, or in a particular line of business, after leaving. A training bond does not stop the employee from leaving or from joining a competitor at all. It simply imposes a monetary obligation, to repay training costs or a defined sum, if the employee leaves before completing an agreed minimum period of service.

This distinction matters because Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 voids any agreement in restraint of trade, profession or business, and Indian courts have consistently struck down post-employment non-compete clauses on that ground, following the Supreme Court's holding in Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning & Mfg. Co. Ltd. that restraints taking effect after the employment relationship ends are hit by Section 27, while restraints operating during the term of employment are not, provided they are not unconscionable or unreasonable. A training bond that merely requires repayment of money, and does not restrict the employee's freedom to take up other work, falls outside that restraint-of-trade problem entirely.

Employers sometimes draft training bonds with an attached non-solicitation or confidentiality obligation extending beyond employment, and those additional obligations are judged on their own terms under Section 27. The repayment obligation itself, properly drafted, is a different animal and survives scrutiny that a non-compete would not.

The legal basis: Section 74 and genuine pre-estimated loss

Where the employment contract fixes a specific sum payable on early departure, Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act governs. It limits the employer to reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount named in the contract, rather than guaranteeing the employer the full sum regardless of circumstances. The named figure operates as a ceiling, not an entitlement.

The Supreme Court's long-standing authority on this, Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Dass, holds that Section 74 dispenses with the need to prove the precise amount of actual loss, but it does not justify awarding compensation where no legal injury has resulted from the breach at all. Applied to a training bond, this means an employer still has to show that leaving early caused some genuine loss connected to the training investment; a bond cannot be enforced as pure punishment for resigning if the employer suffered no real detriment.

Courts and arbitral tribunals assessing a training bond dispute typically ask two linked questions: how much of the employer's training investment remained unrecouped at the point the employee resigned, and what evidence supports that figure. An employer that can show the specific course fees, travel costs, salary paid during an unproductive training period, or a vendor certification cost, and can show the employee resigned well short of the minimum service period, is in a strong position. An employer that simply inserted a large round number into the contract without any underlying cost record is not.

What actually gets upheld in practice

Reported decisions on employment bonds consistently reward employers who can tie the repayment figure to documented expenditure. A bond requiring repayment of the actual cost of a specific certification programme, prorated for the unserved portion of the minimum period, is far more defensible than a bond demanding a flat penalty unconnected to any identifiable spend. Courts are comfortable treating a well-evidenced, reasonably proportionate sum as a genuine pre-estimate of loss rather than a penalty, which is the key distinction Section 74 draws.

The minimum service period itself needs to be reasonable in relation to the training actually given. A bond tying an employee to three years of service after a two-week orientation session is a weak candidate for enforcement, because the disproportion between the training given and the restriction imposed invites a finding that the clause is punitive rather than compensatory. A bond tying an employee to eighteen months after a genuinely expensive, months-long overseas training programme sits on much firmer ground.

Pro-rating the repayment amount by the unserved period, rather than demanding the full bond amount regardless of how much of the minimum term the employee actually completed, also strengthens an employer's position considerably. An employee who serves twenty of twenty-four required months and is asked to repay the full two years of training cost has a real argument that the sum bears no relation to any loss the employer actually suffered for those last four months.

Section 73 and the harder route when there is no fixed sum

Where the contract does not name a specific repayment figure, and the employer instead wants to sue separately for the cost of training after the employee leaves, the claim falls under Section 73 of the Contract Act rather than Section 74. Section 73 requires the employer to prove actual damage, meaning it has to establish, with evidence, that it suffered loss equal to or greater than the sum being claimed, rather than relying on a contractually pre-fixed figure that need not be separately proved up to the same degree.

This is a materially harder case to run. An employer without a liquidated-damages clause in the contract, trying to recover training costs after the fact through an ordinary breach-of-contract claim, needs to produce the underlying training expenditure records, show the direct connection between that expenditure and the specific employee, and establish the loss was actually caused by the early departure rather than being a cost the employer would have borne regardless.

The practical lesson is that a properly drafted Section 74 liquidated-damages clause in the employment contract, with a sum genuinely tied to training cost and a reasonable minimum period, saves an employer from having to run this harder Section 73 case later. Employers relying on a vague contractual reference to 'recovering training costs' without naming a figure are effectively choosing the harder route without realising it.

Drafting a training bond that actually holds up

The clause should itemise, or at least reference, the specific training cost it is meant to recover, rather than stating an unexplained lump sum. Attaching a schedule showing the components of the training cost, whether course fees, travel, accommodation, or the cost of an external certifying body, makes the figure far easier to defend later as a genuine pre-estimate rather than an arbitrary penalty.

The minimum service period should be set with reference to how long the training itself took and how specialised or transferable the skill is, not simply copied from a template used for every role in the company. A single bonded period applied uniformly across junior support staff and senior specialist engineers, where the training investment in each case is wildly different, invites exactly the disproportionality argument that defeats enforcement.

The repayment obligation should be expressed as pro-rated for the unserved balance of the minimum period, and the clause should be kept separate from any non-compete or non-solicitation obligation so that a court striking down the latter under Section 27 has no reason to treat the repayment obligation as tainted by the same defect. Employers should also retain the underlying cost documentation for as long as the bonded period runs, since a bond is only as strong as the evidence available to support the figure when the dispute actually arises.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a training bond the same thing as a non-compete clause?

No. A non-compete restricts where an employee can work after leaving and is generally void under Section 27 of the Contract Act once it extends beyond the employment term. A training bond simply requires repayment of training costs on early departure and does not restrict the employee's freedom to take another job, which is why it survives scrutiny that a non-compete usually does not.

Can an employer recover the full bond amount even if the employee served most of the required period?

Courts are more likely to enforce a figure that is pro-rated for the unserved portion of the minimum service period than a flat sum demanded regardless of how much time the employee actually completed. Demanding the full amount from someone who served nearly the entire period invites an argument that the sum is a penalty rather than genuine compensation.

What happens if the employment contract does not name a specific repayment amount?

The employer's claim then falls under Section 73 of the Contract Act rather than Section 74, which requires proving actual damage with evidence rather than relying on a pre-agreed figure. This is a harder case to win, which is why a properly drafted liquidated-damages clause naming a reasonable, cost-linked sum is worth getting right at the drafting stage.

Does the training bond need to show the actual cost incurred by the employer?

It is not legally required to itemise every cost in the contract itself, but being able to produce documented training expenditure when the dispute arises is what separates an enforceable bond from one struck down as an arbitrary penalty. Employers should keep the underlying cost records for as long as the bonded period runs.

References & Further Reading

The provisions and rulings below were relied on for this guide. The position can change with amendments and later decisions, so check the current text and the latest case law before acting on any of it.

  1. Indian Contract Act, 1872, Section 27 (agreements in restraint of trade are void)
  2. Indian Contract Act, 1872, Section 73 (compensation for loss caused by breach, requiring proof of actual damage)
  3. Indian Contract Act, 1872, Section 74 (compensation for breach where a sum is named in the contract, capped at reasonable compensation)
  4. Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning & Mfg. Co. Ltd., AIR 1967 SC 1098 (restraint operative during the employment term is valid if not unconscionable or unreasonable; post-termination restraint is void under Section 27)
  5. Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Dass (Supreme Court authority that Section 74 dispenses with proof of exact loss but requires a genuine legal injury from the breach)
