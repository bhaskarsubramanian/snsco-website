Authorised Economic Operator Status in India: What AEO Certification Actually Gets You

What the AEO-T1, T2, T3 and AEO-LO tiers under CBIC's programme require, which businesses qualify, and the customs facilitation benefits each tier unlocks.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign-owned manufacturer, importer or logistics operator moving goods through Indian customs regularly runs into the same friction every compliant business runs into: physical examination of shipments, bank guarantees against duty, and queues for refunds that sit with the department for months. The Authorised Economic Operator programme run by the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs exists to take a business with a demonstrated compliance record out of that queue, in exchange for a one-time accreditation exercise rather than repeated case-by-case clearance checks.

The programme is not a discount or a waiver of duty. It is a trust-based facilitation status, built on the World Customs Organisation's SAFE Framework, and an Indian subsidiary of a multinational, an export-oriented manufacturer, or a customs broker handling third-country trade can all apply for it once their compliance history meets the bar.

Who can apply, and for which tier

The AEO programme is built on CBIC Circular No. 33/2016-Customs, dated 22 July 2016, as modified by later circulars. It creates three tiers for importers and exporters, AEO-T1, AEO-T2 and AEO-T3, with T3 as the highest level of accreditation, and a separate single tier, AEO-LO, for other economic operators in the supply chain such as customs brokers, warehouse operators, custodians and logistics providers.

Eligibility rises with each tier. AEO-T1 is pitched at a business with a reasonably clean customs record over a defined recent period and basic internal controls; AEO-T2 expects stronger financial solvency, a more developed compliance and record-keeping system, and a longer track record; AEO-T3 is aimed at large, highly compliant entities, and applying for it requires having held T2 status continuously for a period before the T3 application can be made. A business that has never engaged with customs compliance at this level typically starts at T1 rather than attempting to leapfrog to T2 or T3.

CBIC separately simplified the process for MSMEs through Circular No. 54/2020-Customs, recognising that the standard documentation and audit burden for T1 and T2 accreditation was disproportionate for smaller manufacturers and exporters, including Indian subsidiaries of foreign groups that qualify as MSMEs under the applicable criteria. Circular No. 02/2022-Customs later aligned the AEO framework, including the MSME package, with the Customs (Administration of Rules of Origin under Trade Agreements) Rules, 2020, known as CAROTAR, which governs how importers claiming preferential tariff treatment must maintain origin-related records.

What accreditation actually changes at the port

The practical value of AEO status shows up at the point of clearance rather than in the duty rate itself. Accredited entities get facilitation such as a lower rate of physical examination and document checks, direct port delivery of containers without the intermediate steps a non-AEO importer goes through, and advance information or alerts on vessel arrival so cargo can be planned for rather than discovered. Higher tiers add a dedicated customs relationship manager as a single point of contact and a reduced frequency of audits, substituting periodic self-certification for repeated on-site verification.

Bank guarantee relief is one of the more commercially significant benefits. An AEO-accredited importer or exporter typically furnishes a lower-value bank guarantee, or in some cases none at all, where a non-accredited business would have to post full security against provisional assessments, warehousing or specific schemes. For a business moving high volumes, the capital otherwise locked up in guarantees is a real cost, and AEO status is one of the few levers available to bring that down without changing the underlying trade.

The scheme does not exempt a business from the substantive customs law. Classification disputes, valuation questions, and anti-dumping or safeguard duty still apply in the same way to an AEO-accredited importer as to anyone else. What changes is the administrative pathway: fewer routine interventions, faster release, and a presumption of good faith that the business still has to keep earning through its own record-keeping.

Renewal and the shift toward self-certification

Validity periods were originally fixed terms under the 2018 framework, commonly described as three years for T1 and T2 and five years for T3 and AEO-LO, after which the entity had to apply afresh for renewal. CBIC moved away from a hard expiry for the T1 tier through Circular No. 18/2021-Customs, dated 31 July 2021, which replaced fixed-term renewal for AEO-T1 with an auto-renewal mechanism built on annual self-certification, with the department conducting a full compliance review on that basis rather than requiring a fresh application cycle.

This matters for a foreign-owned entity planning its compliance calendar: an AEO-T1 business should not assume status lapses automatically after a fixed number of years in the way that was once true. It should still track its annual self-certification obligation, since missing that filing is itself a compliance lapse that can put the facilitation status at risk even without a formal expiry date arriving.

Why a foreign group's Indian arm applies for this rather than treating it as optional

A multinational's Indian manufacturing or distribution subsidiary that imports components or exports finished goods at scale has the clearest case for applying. The time saved at the port compounds across hundreds of consignments a year, and the reduced bank guarantee requirement frees up working capital that would otherwise sit against the department rather than funding operations. A foreign group evaluating India as a manufacturing base, alongside incentives such as production-linked schemes, often finds AEO accreditation referenced as part of the broader ease-of-doing-business pitch for the Indian entity, separate from any tax incentive the parent itself might rely on.

The accreditation process itself is not instantaneous. It involves a documented application addressing the eligibility criteria in the governing circular, verification by the jurisdictional customs authority, and in practice a period of preparation to put internal controls, record-keeping and a designated compliance function in place before the application is filed. A business should treat this as a project with its own timeline, not a form submitted alongside a routine customs filing.

Groups that already run AEO-equivalent accreditation abroad, such as the European Union's Authorised Economic Operator scheme or a comparable programme under another country's customs law, sometimes assume the Indian application will be a formality because the parent already clears a similar bar elsewhere. CBIC's framework does recognise the broader SAFE Framework philosophy behind these overseas schemes, but it runs its own verification of the Indian entity's own record, systems and premises, and foreign accreditation elsewhere is not treated as a substitute for that. The Indian subsidiary still has to make out its own case on its own compliance history.

Where applications commonly run into trouble

An application is often slowed less by the eligibility criteria themselves than by a mismatch between what the business believes it can show and what its own records actually contain. A company that has never consolidated its import-export data, its history of show-cause notices, or its internal process manual into a single file finds that assembling that record for the AEO application is where most of the delay sits, not the department's review once the file is complete.

A second common issue is a change in the entity's own structure, such as a merger, a change in directorship, or a shift to a new customs broker, during the pendency of the application or shortly after accreditation. The AEO status attaches to the specific legal entity and the facts it certified, so a material change that is not promptly reported to the jurisdictional customs authority can put the facilitation benefits, and in a serious case the accreditation itself, at risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does AEO status reduce the customs duty payable on imports?

No. AEO accreditation does not change the rate of duty or the classification and valuation rules that determine it. It changes the administrative treatment at clearance, such as reduced examination, faster release and lower bank guarantee requirements, not the amount of duty legally payable.

Can an Indian subsidiary of a foreign company apply directly for AEO-T2 or T3?

In practice a business without an existing compliance track record at this level applies for AEO-T1 first. AEO-T3 specifically requires a period of continuous T2 status before that application can be made, so a new entrant typically works through the tiers rather than starting at the top.

Does AEO-T1 status now expire after a fixed number of years?

For AEO-T1, CBIC Circular No. 18/2021-Customs replaced the earlier fixed renewal cycle with an auto-renewal process based on annual self-certification and periodic compliance review. The entity should still track its self-certification obligation rather than assuming the status runs indefinitely without any action.

Is AEO status the same thing as the MSME facilitation package?

No, though they overlap. CBIC's Circular No. 54/2020-Customs simplified AEO-T1 and T2 accreditation specifically for MSME importers and exporters. A qualifying MSME, including an Indian subsidiary that meets the MSME criteria, follows a lighter documentation path within the same AEO tiers, not a separate scheme.

What happens to customs disputes, such as a classification or valuation challenge, once a business holds AEO status?

AEO status does not insulate a business from substantive customs disputes. Classification, valuation and anti-dumping questions are decided on the same legal footing regardless of accreditation. The benefit is procedural facilitation at clearance, not a shield against the department raising a demand on the merits.
