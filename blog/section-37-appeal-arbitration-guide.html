Appealing an Arbitration-Related Order Under Section 37: What Can, and Cannot, Be Appealed

A guide to Section 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, explaining which categories of orders are appealable, the narrow scope of appellate interference, and the limitation period for filing.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 2 April 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Appeals in Arbitration Are the Exception, Not the Rule

The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 is built around minimal judicial intervention, and this philosophy extends to appeals. Unlike ordinary civil litigation, where most interlocutory and final orders can be appealed, Section 37 provides an exhaustive, closed list of the specific categories of arbitration-related orders that can be appealed: an order not falling within one of the listed categories generally cannot be appealed under Section 37 at all, even if a party feels aggrieved by it.

What Section 37 Actually Covers

Section 37(1) permits an appeal from an order (a) refusing to refer parties to arbitration under Section 8, (b) granting or refusing to grant an interim measure under Section 9, and (c) setting aside or refusing to set aside an arbitral award under Section 34. Section 37(2) separately permits an appeal from an order of the arbitral tribunal itself accepting a jurisdictional plea under Section 16, or granting or refusing an interim measure under Section 17.

Notably absent from this list is a straightforward right to appeal from an order appointing (or refusing to appoint) an arbitrator under Section 11, or from most other interlocutory directions a court may issue during the arbitration: these categories of orders are deliberately left outside the appellate structure, reflecting the legislative intent to keep the arbitral process moving without repeated appellate detours.

How Narrow Is Appellate Scrutiny?

Even for an order that does fall within one of the appealable categories, courts have repeatedly held that a Section 37 appellate court's scrutiny is narrower than even the scope available to the court whose order is under appeal. This is particularly pronounced for an appeal from a Section 34 order: the appellate court does not undertake yet another round of merits review of the underlying award, but examines only whether the Section 34 court itself exceeded or misapplied the limited grounds available to it.

Limitation and Procedure

Section 37 itself does not prescribe a specific limitation period, so the residual provisions of the Limitation Act, 1963 apply, generally requiring an appeal to be filed within ninety days (or a shorter period, depending on the specific forum and applicable High Court rules) from the date of the order appealed against, subject to the court's power to condone delay in appropriate cases under Section 5 of the Limitation Act. Parties should check the specific limitation period applicable in their jurisdiction, since High Court rules can vary the period for intra-court appeals.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I appeal an order appointing an arbitrator under Section 11?

No: an order under Section 11 is not among the categories of orders made appealable under Section 37, and is generally treated as final for the purpose of appeal (though not immune from a challenge to the underlying award later, on different grounds).

Can I appeal a tribunal's rejection of a Section 16 jurisdictional objection?

No: Section 37(2) permits an appeal only where the tribunal accepts a jurisdictional plea and declines to proceed; where the tribunal rejects the plea and proceeds with the arbitration, that decision is reviewable only later, if at all, in a challenge to the final award under Section 34.

Is there a further appeal beyond Section 37?

A second appeal is generally barred by Section 37(3), though the Supreme Court's discretionary jurisdiction under Article 136 of the Constitution remains available in appropriate cases.

What is the limitation period for filing a Section 37 appeal?

The Act itself does not prescribe one, so the residual provisions of the Limitation Act apply: commonly ninety days, though the applicable High Court's own rules can shorten this for intra-court appeals, so the specific rule should always be checked.
