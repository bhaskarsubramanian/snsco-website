Free Legal Aid in India: Who Is Eligible Under the Legal Services Authorities Act and How to Apply

Who qualifies for free legal aid under Section 12 of the Legal Services Authorities Act, how NALSA and DLSAs decide, and how to apply.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Free legal aid in India is not a general welfare scheme open to anyone who cannot afford a lawyer. It is a statutory entitlement under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, and Section 12 of that Act lists specific categories of people who qualify, only some of which turn on income. A woman or a child, for instance, can be eligible regardless of financial status, while a person who does not fall into any of the named categories still qualifies if their annual income is below the limit the relevant State has prescribed. Getting the right category right matters because it decides whether an applicant has to prove financial need at all.

This guide sets out who Section 12 actually covers, how the application reaches the District Legal Services Authority or a higher authority depending on the forum, what the Act requires before aid is actually granted, and the newer Tele-Law channel that has become a common first point of contact, particularly in rural areas, for the kind of basic legal advice that used to require a personal visit to the DLSA office.

Who Section 12 actually covers

Section 12 of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 sets out a list of categories entitled to free legal services, and the drafting deliberately goes beyond a pure income test. Women and children are covered without any income condition attached, reflecting a policy choice that these groups should not have to establish financial hardship before getting legal help. Members of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, victims of trafficking in human beings or of begar, persons with disabilities, and persons in custody, including in a protective home or a juvenile home, are also covered as distinct categories in their own right.

Beyond these named groups, Section 12 also covers victims of mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster, and industrial workmen, again without requiring proof of income for these specific categories. The residual, income-based category catches everyone else: a person whose annual income is below the limit the State Government has prescribed for that purpose is entitled to free legal services even if they do not fall within any of the other named groups. Because each State fixes its own income ceiling, and fixes it differently for the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee compared to State or District level aid, the applicable limit has to be checked for the specific forum and State involved rather than assumed from a figure quoted for a different authority.

Eligibility under a category is the start, not the end

Falling within a Section 12 category establishes entitlement to apply, but Section 13 of the Act adds a further condition: the concerned Legal Services Authority has to be satisfied that the person has a prima facie case to prosecute or defend before aid is actually extended. This is not meant to operate as a merits filter that screens out every difficult case, but it does mean an application can be examined for whether there is an arguable case at all, not accepted automatically the moment the applicant's category is established.

In practice this means an applicant should be ready to explain, even briefly, what the dispute or proceeding is about and what relief is being sought, since the authority processing the application needs enough to form a view on whether a case exists to prosecute or defend. This is also why applicants are generally asked to bring whatever documents they have relating to the matter, such as a notice received, a complaint filed against them, or papers from a pending case, rather than simply presenting an identity document and category proof.

Where to apply, and the structure behind the application

Legal aid in India runs through a layered structure: the National Legal Services Authority at the apex, State Legal Services Authorities in each State, District Legal Services Authorities at the district level, and Taluk Legal Services Committees below that, along with a separate Supreme Court Legal Services Committee and High Court Legal Services Committees for matters pending at those levels. An applicant generally approaches the authority or committee appropriate to where the case is, or is expected to be, filed, most commonly the District Legal Services Authority for proceedings in the local courts.

Applications can be made in person at the relevant office, and most authorities also accept an application made online through the legal aid application facility available on NALSA's website or the website of the concerned State or District Legal Services Authority. Once an application is accepted, the authority typically assigns a panel lawyer to represent the applicant, and the representation extends through the proceeding for which aid was sought, though an applicant whose financial or category eligibility materially changes during the proceeding can, in principle, have that aid reconsidered.

Lok Adalats and the paralegal volunteer network

Separately from representation in a contested case, Legal Services Authorities also organise Lok Adalats, informal forums for settling disputes by consent, and an award passed by a Lok Adalat has the same binding force as a decree of a civil court and is final, with no appeal lying against it except through a limited writ remedy in genuinely exceptional circumstances. A person eligible for legal aid can access this route through the same DLSA structure, and it is often faster than contested litigation for disputes where both sides are, in substance, willing to settle but need a formal and enforceable resolution.

At the grassroots level, DLSAs deploy paralegal volunteers in villages and local communities specifically to help people identify that they have a legal problem, understand that free aid may be available, and get connected to the local legal aid clinic or Tele-Law access point rather than a lawyer's private office. These volunteers are not themselves lawyers and do not give legal advice in a strict sense, but they are often the first and most accessible point of contact for someone unsure whether their problem is even the kind of thing legal aid covers.

Tele-Law: remote advice through Common Service Centres

Tele-Law is a scheme run through the Department of Justice that connects a person, usually through a Common Service Centre or the Tele-Law mobile application, with a panel lawyer for a scheduled advice session, generally a fixed short time slot, rather than in-person representation in a pending case. It is aimed particularly at rural and remote applicants who may not be able to travel easily to a DLSA office, and a paralegal volunteer or the Common Service Centre operator typically helps register the query and schedule the session.

Advice through this channel is intended to be free for those who meet the Section 12 eligibility criteria, and other applicants can generally still use the service even where they do not meet those criteria, though the free-of-cost basis for every category of user has been described differently across different periods, so an applicant should confirm the current cost position, if any, at the time of booking rather than assuming last year's terms still apply. The scheme has been through more than one phase of expansion, and the current programme structure and its stated end date should likewise be checked against the latest official notification rather than an older report.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to prove I am poor to get free legal aid?

Not always. Section 12 of the Legal Services Authorities Act covers several categories, including women and children, without any income condition. Income only becomes relevant if you do not fall within one of the other named categories, in which case the State's prescribed income limit applies.

Is every woman automatically entitled to free legal aid, regardless of how much she earns?

Yes, this is one of the categories under Section 12 that does not carry an income condition, so a woman's financial status is not a bar to eligibility under this specific ground. She still needs to show, as with any applicant, that there is a genuine case to prosecute or defend.

Does being eligible under Section 12 mean I will definitely get a free lawyer?

Eligibility is the first requirement, but Section 13 also requires the Legal Services Authority to be satisfied that you have a prima facie case to prosecute or defend. Most applicants who meet a Section 12 category and have an arguable case do get assistance, but the authority can examine whether a case actually exists.

What is the difference between going to the DLSA directly and using Tele-Law?

The DLSA structure provides a panel lawyer for representation in a pending or contemplated case. Tele-Law is generally a remote advice session, typically booked through a Common Service Centre, aimed at quick guidance rather than ongoing representation, and is often the first point of contact before a fuller legal aid application if one is needed.

Can I apply for legal aid online, or do I have to visit the DLSA office in person?

Both options are generally available. Most State and District Legal Services Authorities, along with NALSA, accept online applications through their websites, in addition to in-person applications at the local office. A paralegal volunteer at the local level can also help with filling in the application.
