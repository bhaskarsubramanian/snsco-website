Stages of a Civil Suit in India: From Plaint to Execution, and Why It Takes So Long

A walk through every stage of an Indian civil suit, from the plaint and summons to judgment, appeal and execution, and where commercial suits move faster.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Clients who have never been through litigation tend to imagine a lawsuit as one continuous event that ends in a single hearing. In reality a civil suit under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 moves through a fixed sequence of stages, each with its own filings, timelines and ways to go wrong, and a case can sit at any one of them for months. As of today, commercial disputes above a specified value have a materially faster, more structured version of this process under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, while an ordinary civil suit still runs on the older, more open-ended timetable.

Knowing the stages in advance does not make a suit move faster on its own, but it tells a client what is actually happening at each point, and which delays are routine procedure rather than something to worry about.

Before the plaint is even filed

For most civil claims there is no formal pre-filing step beyond checking the limitation period and, often, sending a notice. For a commercial dispute above the value threshold set under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, Section 12A makes pre-institution mediation compulsory before the suit can be filed at all, unless the plaintiff is seeking urgent interim relief. The Supreme Court held in Patil Automation Pvt Ltd v Rakheja Engineers Pvt Ltd (2022 SCC OnLine SC 1028) that this step is mandatory, and that a commercial suit filed without first attempting it is liable to rejection of the plaint.

This stage is easy to underestimate, because it feels like a formality standing between the client and the actual suit. In practice it fixes the record of what was claimed and when, and a plaintiff who skips it on the assumption that mediation will obviously fail can lose months later when the defendant raises the omission as a technical objection, quite apart from whether the underlying claim has any real force.

Filing the plaint and serving summons

The suit formally begins with the plaint, which sets out the parties, the facts, the cause of action and the relief claimed, filed along with the court fee calculated on the value of the claim. Drafting it carefully matters beyond style: a plaint that omits a necessary party, misstates the valuation, or fails to disclose a complete cause of action can be returned or rejected before the case even reaches the defendant. Once the court admits the plaint, Order V of the Code requires summons to be issued to the defendant, calling on them to appear and, in most suits, to file a written statement within a set period from service.

Service of summons, getting the document actually and verifiably delivered, is where a surprising number of suits stall early. A defendant who evades service, or whose address is wrong or outdated, can push this stage out by weeks or months, through repeated returns and substituted service applications. Courts have increasingly allowed service by courier, email or other electronic means alongside the traditional process server, specifically to cut down on this kind of delay, though a defendant determined to avoid being found can still draw the process out considerably.

The written statement, and the shrinking clock for commercial suits

Under Order VIII Rule 1, a defendant must file their written statement within thirty days of service of summons. For an ordinary civil suit, courts retain discretion to extend this, generally up to ninety days, on sufficient cause and usually on payment of costs.

For a commercial suit, that discretion is sharply narrower. The amended Order VIII Rule 1, inserted by the Commercial Courts Act, still gives thirty days as the default but caps any extension at a total of one hundred and twenty days from service, after which several High Courts have treated the right to file a written statement as simply gone, with no further extension possible however good the explanation. This is one of the most consequential procedural traps in commercial litigation, since a defendant who delays instructing counsel can lose the right to contest the claim on the merits at all, leaving the plaintiff able to seek judgment on an uncontested record.

Framing issues and the case management hearing

Once pleadings are complete, the court frames issues under Order XIV, essentially the specific questions of fact and law the trial needs to answer, drawn from the points on which the plaint and written statement disagree. A commercial suit layers a further requirement on top of this: Order XV-A requires a case management hearing, to be held within four weeks of the admission or denial of documents by all parties, at which the court sets a concrete timetable for the remaining stages, including the number of witnesses, the time allotted for evidence and arguments, and the date fixed for judgment.

A commercial suit also allows an early exit route that an ordinary suit does not: an application for summary judgment under Order XIII-A, where a party can ask the court to decide the suit, or part of it, without recording oral evidence, on the ground that the other side has no real prospect of succeeding on the claim or defence. This is used sparingly and only where the facts genuinely do not need a trial to be resolved, but where it applies it can shortcut what would otherwise be a multi-year process.

Evidence and arguments

Trial proper consists of each side leading evidence, typically affidavits of evidence followed by cross-examination, on the issues framed earlier, governed by Order XVIII. This is usually the longest stage in an ordinary suit, stretched out by witness availability, adjournments and, in busier courts, sheer docket pressure. Commercial suits are meant to compress this through the case management timetable fixed earlier, with courts directing day-to-day or block hearings for evidence rather than allowing it to be spread across scattered dates over years.

Final arguments follow once evidence closes, with each side presenting its case on the law and the facts as established by that evidence, often supported by written submissions. The gap between the end of arguments and the judgment itself is where a case can sit for an unpredictable period, since there is no fixed statutory deadline for an ordinary suit, though commercial suits again come with a degree of structure through the case management order.

Judgment, decree and the first appeal

The court's judgment under Order XX sets out its findings and reasoning, and the decree is the formal expression of what the judgment actually orders, the document that gets executed. The gap between the final hearing and the judgment itself varies widely from court to court, and a client's natural impatience during this wait is rarely a sign that anything has gone wrong. A party dissatisfied with the decree can file a first appeal, generally under Section 96, within the limitation period that applies to appeals, which runs separately from the limitation that governed the original suit and is usually shorter.

An appeal is not a fresh trial. The appellate court works from the record built in the trial court, re-examines the findings on the evidence already led, and can confirm, vary or set aside the decree, or occasionally remand the matter back to the trial court if it finds the record incomplete on a point that genuinely needs further evidence. New evidence is admitted only in narrow circumstances, so a party who did not lead a particular witness or document at trial generally cannot introduce it for the first time at the appeal stage.

Execution: winning the suit is not the end

A decree that the other side does not honour voluntarily has to be enforced through execution proceedings under Order XXI, filed before the court that passed the decree or, where the judgment debtor's assets sit elsewhere, transferred to a court with jurisdiction over those assets. Section 51 sets out the modes available to the executing court, including attachment and sale of property, arrest and detention in limited circumstances, and appointment of a receiver.

Execution is often where a plaintiff's real frustration begins, because a judgment debtor determined to avoid paying can raise objections, claim exemptions, or simply have no identifiable assets left to attach by the time the decree is finally ready for enforcement. A decree holder who waited years for the judgment can find the execution stage taking almost as long, chasing property that has since been transferred or hidden behind other names. Lok Adalats and mediated settlements sometimes work better at this stage than at any earlier one, precisely because both sides now know exactly what the decree says and what it will actually cost to keep fighting over enforcing it rather than settling.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do ordinary civil suits in India take so many years to finish?

No single stage is usually to blame; delay accumulates across service of summons, extensions for written statements, adjournments during evidence, and the gap between arguments and judgment, none of which carry a hard statutory deadline for an ordinary suit. Commercial suits are faster mainly because the Commercial Courts Act imposes firm caps and case management timetables that an ordinary suit does not have.

What happens if a defendant simply never files a written statement?

In an ordinary suit the court can proceed to pronounce judgment on the material before it once the extended time has lapsed. In a commercial suit, several High Courts treat the right to file a written statement as lost once the outer limit of one hundred and twenty days from service passes, after which the defendant generally cannot contest the claim on the merits at all.

Can a civil suit be decided without a full trial?

In a commercial suit, yes, through an application for summary judgment under Order XIII-A, where the court can decide the claim or defence without recording oral evidence if it finds no real prospect of success on one side. This route is unavailable in an ordinary civil suit, which generally must proceed through full evidence and arguments.

Is pre-institution mediation required for every civil suit, or only commercial ones?

It applies specifically to commercial suits covered by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, under Section 12A, and the Supreme Court has held it mandatory except where urgent interim relief is genuinely needed. An ordinary civil suit outside that Act's coverage does not carry this pre-filing mediation requirement.

Does winning the suit mean the money or property is automatically handed over?

No. A decree still has to be enforced through separate execution proceedings under Order XXI if the losing party does not comply voluntarily, and execution can itself take considerable time where the judgment debtor resists, raises objections, or has no easily attachable assets.
