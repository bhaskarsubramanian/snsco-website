A Muslim Dies Without a Will: Who Inherits, and in What Shares

Sharers get their fixed Quranic fraction first, residuaries split what is left, and a son's share is double a daughter's. Here is how the shares work.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

When a Muslim dies without a will, or without a will covering a particular asset, the estate does not pass under the Indian Succession Act the way it would for most other communities in India. The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937 directs that intestate succession among Muslims is governed by Muslim personal law itself, which for the great majority of Indian Muslims means the classical Hanafi (Sunni) scheme of fixed shares, with the Shia schools applying a somewhat different structure for Shia estates.

This guide works through how the Hanafi scheme actually divides an estate: who counts as a sharer entitled to a fixed fraction, who takes as a residuary, and why a son's share typically ends up double a daughter's even though both are blood relatives of the same degree. It does not cover the separate one-third limit on what a Muslim testator can bequeath by will, which is a different rule governing testate rather than intestate succession.

The three classes of heirs

Hanafi law sorts heirs into three groups. Sharers, or dhawul-furud, are a defined list of relatives, including the surviving spouse, parents, and daughters in certain combinations, who take a fixed Quranic fraction of the estate regardless of how much or little is left. Residuaries, or asabat, are generally male agnatic relatives, principally sons and, in their absence, more remote male descendants and collaterals, who divide whatever remains after the sharers have taken their fixed fractions.

A third category, distant kindred, inherits only where there is no sharer and no residuary available at all, which in practice is unusual once the first two categories are properly worked through. The basic sequence is always the same: pay funeral expenses and debts first, calculate and pay out each sharer's fixed fraction from what remains, and then divide whatever is left among the residuaries; distant kindred only come into the picture if that second step leaves nobody to take the residue.

The widow's and husband's shares

A surviving wife's share depends on whether the deceased husband left any children or grandchildren. Where there are no such descendants, a widow is entitled to one-fourth of the estate after funeral expenses and debts are paid; where the husband is survived by a child or grandchild, her share drops to one-eighth. The Supreme Court confirmed the one-fourth entitlement for a childless Muslim widow in a 2025 ruling, tracing it directly to the Quranic verse on which the Hanafi rule is based.

Where a husband leaves more than one widow, the applicable one-fourth or one-eighth share is divided among the widows collectively rather than each widow taking that full fraction separately. A surviving husband's position mirrors this structure with its own fixed fractions depending on whether the wife left descendants, though the husband's fraction under the Hanafi scheme is generally larger than the corresponding widow's fraction in the same circumstances.

Why a son typically gets double a daughter's share

A daughter's position under Hanafi law depends on whether she has a brother. Where a man leaves only daughters and no sons, a single daughter is a sharer entitled to one-half of the estate, and two or more daughters together are generally entitled to two-thirds, divided equally among them, subject to the other sharers' prior claims.

Where both sons and daughters survive, the daughters are no longer treated as sharers in the same way; instead, sons and daughters together take the residue left after the other sharers (such as a widow or parent) have been paid, and that residue is divided so that each son receives twice what each daughter receives. This two-to-one structure is one of the most commonly misunderstood aspects of Muslim inheritance law, often assumed to mean daughters get nothing, when the accurate position is that they inherit a defined but smaller share relative to a brother of the same degree.

Parents also have fixed positions in this structure: a mother is generally entitled to one-sixth of the estate where the deceased left children or more than one sibling, and to a larger share in their absence, while a father typically takes as a sharer, a residuary, or both depending on who else survives.

A worked illustration

Consider a woman who dies leaving a surviving mother and two sons, with no husband, father, or other sharers in the picture. The mother takes her fixed one-sixth share as a sharer. The remaining five-sixths of the estate then passes to the two sons as residuaries, who divide it equally between themselves, giving each son five-twelfths of the total estate.

Change the facts slightly, so the woman leaves a mother, one son, and one daughter instead of two sons. The mother still takes one-sixth. The remaining five-sixths then goes to the son and daughter together as residuaries in the two-to-one ratio described above, meaning the son receives roughly two-ninths of that five-sixths share, and the daughter roughly one-ninth of it, before the fractions are reduced to their final form. Working through an actual estate with several categories of surviving relatives at once is where this calculation gets genuinely complicated, which is why a formal statement of shares, sometimes informally called a shares certificate, prepared by someone familiar with the calculation is worth getting rather than dividing an estate on an assumed, round-number basis.

How this interacts with documentation and bank or property transfers

Because there is no will in an intestate Muslim estate, the heirs typically still need a succession certificate from a civil court to deal with the deceased's debts, securities, and bank balances, the same instrument described for other communities under the Indian Succession Act, since Part X of that Act (dealing with succession certificates) is not among the parts excluded for Muslims; what is excluded for Muslims under the Act is Part VI, which governs wills and intestate succession generally for other communities.

A family wanting to mutate immovable property in the names of all the heirs according to their Muslim-law shares will typically need a document, whether a court-recorded settlement, a registered deed among the heirs, or a formal calculation accepted by the revenue authority, that actually sets out the fractional shares correctly; getting this calculation wrong at the outset is a common source of family disputes later, particularly where daughters' shares are informally rounded down or where a sharer's claim is overlooked because the family's own sense of who inherits does not match the Hanafi calculation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a Muslim's estate pass under the Indian Succession Act if there is no will?

No. The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937 directs that intestate succession for Muslims is governed by Muslim personal law, principally the Hanafi scheme for Sunni Muslims, rather than the general intestacy rules in Part VI of the Indian Succession Act that apply to other communities.

How much does a childless widow inherit from her Muslim husband's estate?

One-fourth of the estate, after funeral expenses and debts are paid, where the husband left no children or grandchildren. This was confirmed by the Supreme Court in a 2025 ruling. Where the husband did leave children or grandchildren, the widow's share drops to one-eighth.

Does a daughter get half of what a son gets under Muslim inheritance law?

Where both sons and daughters survive, the residue left after other sharers are paid is divided so each son gets double what each daughter gets, which in a simple two-child case works out to the daughter receiving half the son's amount, though the precise fractions change once other sharers such as a widow or parent are also in the picture.

What if a man leaves only daughters and no sons?

A single daughter with no brother is a sharer in her own right, entitled to one-half of the estate; two or more daughters together, with no brother, are generally entitled to two-thirds collectively, divided equally among them, subject to the prior claims of any other sharers such as a surviving spouse or parent.

Is this the same as the one-third rule on how much a Muslim can leave by will?

No. The fixed-share scheme described here applies only where there is no valid will covering the asset, meaning intestate succession. The separate one-third rule limits how much of the estate a Muslim testator can give away by will at all, and the two rules operate on different parts of the same estate.
