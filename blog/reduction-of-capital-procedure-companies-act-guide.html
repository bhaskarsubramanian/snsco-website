Reducing Share Capital Under the Companies Act: The NCLT Procedure Explained

A procedural guide to reduction of share capital under Section 66 of the Companies Act, 2013: when a company can reduce capital, the NCLT approval process, and creditor safeguards built into the framework.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 25 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

What a Capital Reduction Achieves

A reduction of share capital under Section 66 of the Companies Act, 2013 allows a company to reduce its issued, subscribed or paid-up share capital in ways such as extinguishing or reducing liability on partly-paid shares, cancelling paid-up capital lost or unrepresented by available assets, or paying off paid-up capital in excess of what the company's business actually requires. Common commercial uses include restructuring a company's balance sheet, facilitating an internal group reorganisation, or enabling a targeted buy-out of a specific shareholder or class of shareholders.

Special Resolution and NCLT Confirmation

A capital reduction requires a special resolution of the company's shareholders, followed by confirmation from the National Company Law Tribunal: the company cannot implement the reduction on the strength of the shareholder resolution alone. The NCLT's role is not a rubber stamp: it examines whether the reduction is fair as between different classes of shareholders, whether the interests of creditors are adequately protected, and whether the reduction is being used for a genuine commercial purpose rather than to prejudice a particular group of stakeholders.

Creditor Safeguards Built Into the Process

Because a capital reduction can directly affect the cushion of capital available to meet creditor claims, the framework requires the company to give notice to the Central Government, Registrar, and, in specified circumstances, sectoral regulators, and to seek objections from creditors. The NCLT typically requires the company to either secure the debts of dissenting creditors or obtain their consent before confirming the reduction, unless it is satisfied that creditor interests are otherwise sufficiently protected.

A reduction confirmed without properly addressing legitimate creditor objections is vulnerable to challenge, so companies should map out their creditor base and likely objections well before filing the NCLT petition, rather than treating creditor engagement as a procedural afterthought.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a company reduce its share capital by a shareholder resolution alone?

No: a special resolution is necessary but not sufficient; the reduction also requires confirmation by the National Company Law Tribunal before it can be implemented.

What does the NCLT examine when confirming a capital reduction?

Whether the reduction is fair between different classes of shareholders, whether creditor interests are adequately protected, and whether the reduction serves a genuine commercial purpose.

Do creditors have a say in a capital reduction?

Yes: the process requires notice to be given and creditor objections to be addressed, typically through securing dissenting creditors' debts or obtaining their consent, before the NCLT will confirm the reduction.

What are common commercial reasons for a capital reduction?

Writing off accumulated losses no longer represented by available assets, returning capital surplus to the company's actual business needs, or facilitating an internal restructuring or targeted shareholder buy-out.
