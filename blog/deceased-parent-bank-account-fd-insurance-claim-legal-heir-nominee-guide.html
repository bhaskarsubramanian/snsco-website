A Deceased Parent's Bank Account, FD, Insurance and Shares

How legal heirs claim a deceased parent's bank account, FD, insurance payout and shares in India, with claims from abroad.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 16 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

When a parent dies, the family is rarely dealing with one asset. There is usually a savings account, one or two fixed deposits, a life insurance policy, and sometimes a demat account holding shares or mutual funds, each governed by a different rulebook and each asking for different paperwork before it releases money. The starting question for every one of these is the same: did the account, deposit, policy or holding carry a valid nomination, and if it did not, how much is involved, because that answer decides whether the family needs a death certificate and an identity proof or a court-issued certificate that can take months to obtain.

This guide walks through banks and fixed deposits, life insurance, and shares or mutual funds in demat form, and then through the position of heirs who live outside India and cannot appear in person at every step. None of it changes who actually owns the money under personal law; nomination and the legal heir's ultimate entitlement are two different questions, and the institution's job is only to pay the right person without becoming liable if it pays the wrong one.

Why a nominee is not automatically the owner

A nomination under banking law, insurance law or the depository system tells the institution who to pay; it does not by itself decide who is entitled to keep that money under the law of succession that applies to the deceased. The Reserve Bank of India's directions on settlement of claims of deceased bank customers, issued in September 2025, require a bank to treat payment to a nominee or a surviving joint holder as a full and valid discharge of the bank's liability, and a bank is barred from demanding a succession certificate, probate or an indemnity bond from a nominee or survivor regardless of the amount involved. That rule protects the bank, not the nominee against a sibling or other heir who later shows that the deceased's personal law entitled them to a share as well.

Where there is no nomination and no survivorship clause on the account, a bank is required to fix its own threshold limit for a simplified claim procedure, with a floor set by the RBI's 2025 directions at 15 lakh rupees for banks generally, and a lower floor reported for co-operative banks; a bank may set its own limit higher than the floor. Below that threshold, a legal heir certificate from a competent authority, or in some cases a declaration from a person well known to the family on the bank's prescribed form, is ordinarily accepted without the family having to go to court. Above the threshold, or where the claim is disputed among potential heirs, the bank will ask for a succession certificate, probate, or letters of administration, and nothing less moves the file. Confirm the exact threshold and accepted documents with the specific bank, since the RBI directions allow each bank some latitude and the figure is itself a floor, not a fixed number.

Fixed deposits and the same nomination logic

A fixed deposit is treated the same way as a savings or current account for this purpose: if a valid nomination was recorded when the FD was opened, the bank pays the nominee on production of the death certificate and the nominee's identification, without insisting on a succession document. Banks sometimes allow premature closure of an FD on death without the usual penalty that would apply to a living depositor who breaks the deposit early, though this is a matter of each bank's own policy rather than a statutory entitlement, so it is worth asking rather than assuming.

A common complication arises where an FD was opened jointly, naming two holders with instructions such as 'either or survivor' or 'former or survivor'. The surviving holder in an 'either or survivor' deposit can usually operate and close the account alone on production of the death certificate, again without a succession certificate, because the mandate itself already identified who could act. Families sometimes discover only after a death that an FD was opened as 'jointly' without a survivorship mandate, in which case the bank will treat it like an unnominated account and ask for heir documentation from everyone with a claim before releasing the funds.

Life insurance: Section 39 and the beneficial nominee

Section 39 of the Insurance Act, 1938 governs nomination on a life policy, and it was significantly amended by the Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015. Before that amendment, a nominee under a life policy was generally treated by courts as holding the payout as an agent or trustee for the legal heirs, meaning the nominee collected the money but did not necessarily keep all of it if other heirs asserted a claim under succession law. The 2015 amendment introduced the concept of a 'beneficial nominee' through a new sub-section: where the nomination is in favour of the policyholder's parents, spouse, children, or spouse and children together, that nominee is entitled to receive and keep the policy proceeds beneficially, and not merely as a collecting agent for the wider family, unless it can be shown from the nature of the policyholder's title that the policyholder could not have conferred that beneficial right in the first place.

A nomination in favour of someone outside that immediate family circle, such as a sibling, a more distant relative or a friend, still falls into the older pattern, where the insurer pays the nominee but the proceeds can remain open to a claim by the legal heirs under the succession law that applies to the deceased. Families sometimes assume a nomination settles the question of entitlement once and for all; for anyone outside the immediate family listed in the amended provision, it does not, and a dispute among heirs is better resolved by agreement or, failing that, by a civil court, rather than by treating the insurer's payment as the final word.

Shares and mutual funds held in demat form

Shares and mutual fund units held in a demat account pass to the nominee recorded with the depository participant on production of the death certificate, the nominee's KYC documents and a transmission request form, broadly mirroring the bank procedure, though depositories and registrars and transfer agents have tightened documentation requirements in recent years, including re-KYC steps for nominees that were not always required earlier. Where no nomination exists, or where the value crosses a threshold the depository participant sets for its own simplified procedure, a legal heir certificate or succession certificate is required before the holdings can be transmitted, exactly as with an unnominated bank account.

Shares and funds left dormant because no claim was made, and dividends that went unclaimed, eventually get transferred to the Investor Education and Protection Fund under Sections 124 and 125 of the Companies Act, 2013. Recovering them at that stage means filing Form IEPF-5 with the IEPF Authority, with the company's nodal officer verifying the claim, a separate and slower process than a straightforward transmission request made before the shares are swept into the fund. Heirs who discover a deceased parent's old demat holdings years later should expect this route, and should expect it to take longer than a fresh transmission claim would have.

When a succession certificate is actually required

Section 372 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 sets out the procedure for obtaining a succession certificate from the District Judge having jurisdiction, essentially where the deceased resided or where the relevant property is situated, and Section 381 gives the certificate its real force: a bank, insurer, company or other debtor that pays the amount specified in the certificate to the person it names is fully protected, even if it later turns out that the certificate holder was not in truth the only person entitled to the money. The certificate under this scheme covers only debts and securities, not immovable property or other assets, which is why a succession certificate does not by itself settle who inherits a house or land left by the deceased.

A succession certificate becomes necessary once an institution's nomination documents, or its simplified heir-certificate route, are not available or not accepted, typically because there was no nomination, the claim exceeds the bank's or depository's internal threshold, or more than one person is asserting a competing claim. The application names near relatives, as the law requires, and the court issues public notice before granting the certificate, so the process is slower than a direct nomination-based claim, usually running to several months rather than weeks, and it is wise to start it as soon as it becomes clear that a nomination-based route will not work.

Claiming from abroad

None of these claim routes requires an heir to be physically present in India throughout, but each one does require documents to be properly executed and, where the heir is overseas, properly attested. A power of attorney executed abroad in favour of a relative or representative in India, notarised and, where the country is not a party to arrangements that dispense with it, apostilled or legalised through the Indian embassy or consulate, is the usual mechanism for letting someone in India operate the claim on the heir's behalf. Banks and depositories will scrutinise a foreign power of attorney more closely than a domestically executed one, and some insist on the original or a certified copy rather than a scanned version, so this is not a step to leave until the last stage of a claim.

Where the overseas heir is the sole claimant and nothing requires physical presence, such as signing a claim form, submitting identity documents and providing bank details for remittance, institutions will generally process the claim on the strength of properly attested documents sent by courier or through the Indian mission abroad. Where a succession certificate is needed and the matter is contested, an overseas heir will usually still need to engage an advocate in India to represent them in the District Judge's court, since the petition and any objections raised by other claimants are conducted in person or through counsel, not by correspondence.

Frequently Asked Questions

If my father named my brother as nominee on his savings account, does that mean my brother now owns the whole balance?

No. The bank is protected in paying the nominee, and the nominee can operate the account, but under the general law other legal heirs can still claim their share of the balance from the nominee if their personal law entitles them to one. Nomination settles who the bank pays, not who keeps the money in the end.

Is a legal heir certificate the same as a succession certificate?

No. A legal heir certificate, issued by a revenue or municipal authority, is generally used for smaller claims or specific government purposes and is accepted by some banks below their internal threshold. A succession certificate is issued by a District Judge under the Indian Succession Act specifically to let the holder collect the deceased's debts and securities, and institutions generally insist on it once a claim is above their simplified-procedure threshold or is disputed.

My mother's life insurance nominee was her sister, not a spouse or child. Does the 2015 amendment protect the payout for my sister alone?

Generally no. The beneficial nominee protection under the amended Section 39 of the Insurance Act applies where the nomination is in favour of the policyholder's parents, spouse or children, or spouse and children together. A nomination in favour of a sibling falls outside that list, so the sister collects the payout but may still have to account to the other legal heirs under succession law.

Can an NRI heir complete a bank claim without travelling to India?

Often yes, through a properly executed and attested power of attorney in favour of someone in India, combined with identity documents and a claim form sent through courier or the Indian mission abroad. Where the claim is contested and a succession certificate is needed, the overseas heir will usually still need an advocate to appear in the proceedings in India.

What happens to shares or dividends nobody claimed for years?

Unclaimed shares, and dividends unclaimed for the period fixed under the Companies Act, are transferred to the Investor Education and Protection Fund under Sections 124 and 125. Recovering them afterwards means filing Form IEPF-5 with the IEPF Authority with the company's nodal officer verifying the claim, which takes longer than a transmission claim made before the transfer to the fund.
