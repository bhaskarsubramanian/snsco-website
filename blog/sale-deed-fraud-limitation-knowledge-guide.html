Challenging a Sale Deed for Fraud: Why the Three-Year Clock Rarely Starts When You Think It Does

Why limitation to cancel a fraudulent sale deed under Article 59 runs from knowledge, not registration, and what a plaint must plead to survive.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 17 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A family discovers, years after the fact, that a parcel of land was sold away using a forged power of attorney, or that an elderly relative's signature was obtained on a sale deed while they did not understand what they were signing. The instinctive legal response is to rush to court and plead fraud, on the assumption that fraud erases any limitation problem. It does not. Article 59 of the Limitation Act, 1963 gives three years to cancel or set aside an instrument like a sale deed, and the Supreme Court has been firm, including in a run of decisions in late 2024 and 2025, that simply writing the word fraud into a plaint does not get around that three-year wall.

What actually decides whether a long-delayed challenge survives is when the three years started running, and that question turns out to be more fact-sensitive, and more favourable to a genuinely defrauded plaintiff, than the bare existence of an old registered deed might suggest.

Article 59 and the Three-Year Period

Article 59 of the Limitation Act applies to a suit to cancel or set aside an instrument, and sets the period at three years from when the facts entitling the plaintiff to have the instrument cancelled or set aside first became known to them. This is distinct from Article 65, which gives twelve years for a suit based on title to recover possession, and the two articles can produce very different outcomes depending on which one a court decides actually governs a particular case.

That choice between the two articles often turns on whether the sale deed being challenged is void or merely voidable. Where a deed is void from the outset, for instance because it was never validly executed at all, a plaintiff can sometimes rely on the longer twelve-year period under Article 65 to recover possession based on title, treating the void document as a nullity that needs no formal cancellation. Where the deed is voidable, meaning it has legal effect until a court actually sets it aside, Article 59's three-year period from knowledge is the governing provision, and a September 2025 Supreme Court ruling confirms this distinction is doing real work in how these cases are decided.

Knowledge, Not Registration, Starts the Clock

The most consequential recent clarification came in December 2024, when the Supreme Court held, in Daliben Valjibhai v. Prajapati Kodarbhai Kachrabhai, that limitation in a fraud-based challenge to a sale deed begins when the aggrieved party actually becomes aware of the fraud, not from the date the document was registered. The High Court in that matter had taken the opposite view, treating the registration date itself as fixing the plaintiff's knowledge, and the Supreme Court set that approach aside.

This matters enormously for the family member who only learns of a fraudulent sale years after it was registered, perhaps because they lived abroad, were not involved in managing the property, or were deliberately kept in the dark by a relative who orchestrated the sale. Registration under the Registration Act creates a public record, and lower courts have sometimes treated that public record as constructive notice regardless of whether the plaintiff had any realistic way of finding out. The Supreme Court's December 2024 ruling pushes back against mechanically importing that constructive-notice idea into the specific, fact-driven knowledge inquiry that Article 59 actually requires.

A Bare Plea of Fraud Is Not Enough

None of this means a plaintiff can simply assert fraud and expect the limitation question to be decided in their favour at trial. The same December 2024 decision, and the broader line of authority it sits within, makes clear that the bar of limitation cannot be saved by merely pleading the word fraud. A plaint has to set out the particulars of the alleged fraud with enough specificity, who did what, when, and how it was concealed, that a court can assess at the earliest stage whether the claim of delayed knowledge is credible rather than a device to get around an otherwise time-barred suit.

Courts have been especially unforgiving of vague pleadings on the date of knowledge. Where a plaintiff's own case does not specify with any precision when the fraud was actually discovered, a court can reject the claim that limitation should run from a later date, and this has led to suits being thrown out at the Order VII Rule 11 stage, well before trial, for failing to plead the necessary particulars of fraud and the date of knowledge together.

Why the Order VII Rule 11 Stage Matters So Much Here

A defendant facing a fraud-based challenge to an old sale deed will almost always move to reject the plaint under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure on the ground that it is barred by limitation on its own averments. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that at this stage a court looks only at what the plaint itself says, not at the defendant's version of events or any evidence, so a plaintiff whose plaint clearly and specifically pleads a credible, late date of knowledge, with facts supporting why that knowledge could not reasonably have come earlier, stands a real chance of getting past this hurdle.

Conversely, a plaint that pleads the fraud in general terms and leaves the date of knowledge vague, or internally inconsistent with other facts pleaded in the same document such as evidence that the plaintiff was actually managing or dealing with the property during the intervening years, invites exactly the kind of dismissal at the threshold that the Supreme Court's own authority supports. Drafting this part of the plaint with real precision is therefore not a formality; it is usually the single factor that decides whether the case gets heard on its merits at all.

Building the Record Before Filing

Because so much turns on when knowledge of the fraud actually arose, the groundwork for a case like this starts well before drafting the plaint. A client should be able to point to something concrete marking the date of discovery, such as a certified copy of the registered document obtained on a specific date, correspondence in which the fraud was first raised, or a police complaint or legal notice sent soon after discovery, rather than relying on a general assertion that they only found out recently.

Where the family has had some dealings with the property in the intervening years, such as paying property tax, residing on part of the land, or being named in municipal records, that history needs to be addressed head-on in the plaint rather than left for the other side to raise, since a court weighing a limitation objection will look at the whole of the plaintiff's own pleaded conduct, not just the paragraph that states when the fraud was discovered. A lawyer taking on a case like this early, before the plaint is drafted, can usually tell within the first conversation whether the client's account of delayed discovery is going to hold up against the paper trail the other side will inevitably produce, and that early assessment is worth far more than a confident pleading written without it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do I have to challenge a sale deed obtained by fraud?

Three years under Article 59 of the Limitation Act, running from when you first became aware of the facts entitling you to have the deed cancelled, not from the date the deed was registered. The Supreme Court confirmed the knowledge-based starting point in a December 2024 ruling.

Does registering the sale deed count as my having known about the fraud?

Not automatically. A December 2024 Supreme Court decision rejected the view that the registration date fixes a plaintiff's knowledge for limitation purposes, holding instead that limitation runs from actual discovery of the fraud, which can be considerably later than the registration date.

Is it enough to just plead that the sale deed was fraudulent?

No. The plaint must give specific particulars of the fraud and a clear, credible account of when it was actually discovered. Courts have dismissed suits at the earliest stage where the plea of fraud was vague or where the date of knowledge was not pleaded with enough precision to justify a late-starting limitation period.

What is the difference between a void and a voidable sale deed for this purpose?

A void deed, one that was never validly executed, may let a plaintiff rely on the twelve-year period under Article 65 to recover possession based on title. A voidable deed has legal effect until a court sets it aside, so Article 59's three-year period from knowledge governs a challenge to it instead.

References & Further Reading

The provisions and rulings below were relied on for this guide. The position can change with amendments and later decisions, so check the current text and the latest case law before acting on any of it.

  1. Limitation Act, 1963, Article 59 (suit to cancel or set aside an instrument, three years from knowledge of the facts entitling cancellation)
  2. Limitation Act, 1963, Article 65 (suit for possession based on title, twelve years)
  3. Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Order VII Rule 11 (rejection of plaint, limitation assessed on the plaint's own averments)
  4. Daliben Valjibhai v. Prajapati Kodarbhai Kachrabhai, Supreme Court, 11 December 2024 (limitation for fraud-based cancellation runs from knowledge, not registration; bare plea of fraud insufficient)
  5. Mallavva v. Kalsammanavara Kalamma, Supreme Court, 2024 INSC 1021 (where declaration of title is the primary relief and possession only consequential, the twelve-year period under Article 65, not the three-year period under Article 59, governs the suit)
