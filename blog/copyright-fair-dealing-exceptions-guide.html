Fair Dealing Under the Copyright Act: When Using Someone Else's Work Isn't Infringement

A guide to the fair dealing exceptions under Section 52 of the Copyright Act, 1957: the specific, limited circumstances in which criticism, review, research, reporting and educational use of a copyrighted work do not amount to infringement.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice. See our disclaimer

Copyright Protection Is Not Absolute

Copyright gives the owner exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, perform and adapt a work, but Section 52 of the Copyright Act, 1957 carves out specific exceptions (commonly called 'fair dealing') under which certain uses of a copyrighted work do not amount to infringement, even without the owner's permission.

What Section 52 Actually Covers

The exceptions are not a broad, open-ended 'fair use' standard like in some other jurisdictions: they are a specific, enumerated list. These include fair dealing for the purposes of private or personal use including research, criticism or review, reporting of current events, use in the course of judicial proceedings, reproduction for teaching in an educational institution, and certain uses by libraries. Each ground has its own conditions and is construed by courts on the specific facts of the use in question.

The 'Fairness' Inquiry: Amount, Purpose and Market Effect

Even where a use falls within a recognised Section 52 category, courts examine whether the dealing was genuinely 'fair': considering factors such as the amount and substantiality of the portion used relative to the whole work, whether the purpose was genuinely for criticism, review, research or reporting rather than a pretext for wholesale reproduction, and whether the use is likely to harm the commercial market for the original work.

Fair Dealing Is a Defence, Not a Pre-Clearance

Fair dealing operates as a defence to an infringement claim rather than a right that must be pre-cleared with the copyright owner. This means a user relying on it should be prepared to justify the use if challenged: keeping the portion used proportionate to the stated purpose, attributing the source, and avoiding reproduction that could substitute for the original in the market are all practical ways to strengthen a fair dealing defence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does India recognise a general 'fair use' right like the United States?

No. Indian copyright law under Section 52 sets out specific, enumerated exceptions rather than an open-ended fair use doctrine: a use must fall within one of the listed categories, such as criticism, review, research or reporting.

Can I reproduce an entire copyrighted work under fair dealing if my purpose is review?

Not necessarily. Courts look at whether the amount used was reasonably necessary for the stated purpose: reproducing an entire work where only excerpts were needed can undermine a fair dealing defence.

Is educational use of copyrighted material automatically exempt?

Only within the specific conditions Section 52 sets out, such as reproduction in the course of instructional activities by an educational institution: general commercial use branded as 'educational' does not automatically qualify.

If I am sued for infringement, do I have to prove fair dealing myself?

Yes. Fair dealing operates as a defence that the person relying on it must raise and establish on the facts, rather than something the copyright owner must first disprove.
