Can an NRI Set Up a One Person Company in India? The 2021 Rule Change Explained

Can an NRI incorporate a One Person Company in India? The 2021 amendment, the residency test, mandatory conversion and why foreign nationals still cannot.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Yes, a Non-Resident Indian can now incorporate and be the sole member of a One Person Company in India, a change made by the Companies (Incorporation) Second Amendment Rules, 2021, with effect from 1 April 2021. Before that date, only a person resident in India could form or be a nominee of an OPC, which shut out an Indian citizen who had settled abroad but wanted to hold a wholly owned Indian business in corporate form without bringing in a second shareholder purely to satisfy the two-member minimum that an ordinary private company needs.

The rule that opened the door to NRIs applies only to an NRI who remains an Indian citizen. A foreign national, even one of Indian origin who has taken up citizenship elsewhere and holds only an OCI card, still cannot incorporate an OPC in India, because the eligibility rule is written around citizenship, not merely around residence or ancestry, and a number of secondary commentaries blur this distinction in ways that can mislead an OCI cardholder into thinking the door is open to them too.

Myth: an OPC must be formed by someone living in India

This was true before 1 April 2021 and is the reason the myth persists in older articles and templates still circulating online. The Companies (Incorporation) Second Amendment Rules, 2021 rewrote the eligibility rule to cover an Indian citizen whether resident in India or otherwise, removing the residency condition for the sole member of an OPC. The same relaxation extended to the nominee a member of an OPC has to name, who steps in if the member dies or becomes incapable of contracting; the nominee can now also be an NRI.

Fact: residence in India stopped being a condition for forming an OPC from 1 April 2021. What still matters is citizenship. An Indian citizen living in Dubai, London or Singapore can today be the sole shareholder and director of an Indian OPC, sign the incorporation documents from abroad through a practising professional, and run the company's Indian operations without needing a second person to hold even a single share purely for compliance.

Myth: any person of Indian origin can now form an OPC

This is the more common error, because people often use NRI and OCI loosely as if they mean the same thing. They do not. An NRI is an Indian citizen who lives outside India; an OCI cardholder has typically given up Indian citizenship and taken the citizenship of another country, India not generally permitting dual citizenship in the full sense. The 2021 amendment's language, extending eligibility to an Indian citizen whether resident in India or otherwise, tracks citizenship, and a foreign national, including an OCI cardholder who is legally a citizen of another country, falls outside it.

Fact: a foreign national cannot incorporate an OPC in India regardless of how long they have held an OCI card or how closely connected they are to India by birth or ancestry. Someone in that position who wants a wholly owned Indian vehicle has to use a wholly owned private limited subsidiary instead, which has no citizenship restriction on its shareholders but does need at least one director who is resident in India under the Companies Act's resident director rule, and needs the compliance overhead of a second nominal arrangement that an OPC was designed to avoid.

Myth: an OPC has to convert to a private company once it grows

Before April 2021, an OPC was compulsorily required to convert into a private or public company if its paid up share capital exceeded fifty lakh rupees or its average annual turnover for the preceding three years exceeded two crore rupees, and a member who wanted to convert voluntarily before hitting either threshold had to wait two years from the date of incorporation before doing so. Both restrictions came off the books with the same 2021 amendment.

Fact: there is no longer a size at which an OPC is forced to convert into a private or public company, and there is no longer a minimum two year wait before a member who wants to convert voluntarily can do so. An OPC today can keep growing indefinitely in its own corporate form, or convert earlier than two years if the member decides that a second investor or shareholder structure suits the business better, without either change being driven by a statutory trigger rather than by the owner's own commercial judgment.

What an OPC actually gives an NRI that a sole proprietorship does not

An OPC is a company under the Companies Act, 2013, which means it has a separate legal personality from its member, limited liability protecting the member's personal assets from the company's debts, and perpetual succession through the mandatory nominee arrangement, none of which a sole proprietorship or an unregistered partnership offers. For an NRI who wants to run a single owned Indian business, whether a trading operation, a consultancy, or a small manufacturing unit, without taking on a co-founder purely to meet a minimum shareholder count, the OPC structure gives the legal protection of a company while keeping ownership and control with one person.

The trade-off against a Limited Liability Partnership or a private limited company with more than one shareholder is that an OPC cannot itself hold shares in another company for investment purposes, cannot carry on non-banking financial investment activities, and cannot convert into a Section 8 not-for-profit company, limits that have not been removed by the 2021 amendment. A business plan that depends on holding other companies' shares, or on raising outside equity from more than one investor down the line, will outgrow the OPC form eventually and should plan the conversion into its structure from the start rather than treating the OPC as permanent.

The practical steps for an NRI incorporating an OPC

The process runs through the same SPICe+ web form on the MCA portal used for any other company incorporation, with the member's and nominee's identity and address proof, a registered office address in India, and the usual memorandum and articles of association, except that the member signs and files from wherever they are living, typically using a digital signature certificate obtained with notarised and apostilled or consularised identity documents where the signatory is abroad at the time. Because the sole member is also usually the sole director, the OPC needs a director identification number for that one person, and the nominee named in the incorporation documents needs their consent on record as well.

Banking and day-to-day operations are where an NRI-owned OPC tends to run into friction that a resident-owned company does not: opening a current account, getting a payment gateway approved, and dealing with vendors who expect to meet a director in person can all take longer when the sole decision maker is based abroad, so appointing a resident authorised signatory under a clear power of attorney for banking and routine filings, while keeping ownership and ultimate control with the NRI member, is worth building into the company's operating practice from day one rather than improvising it after the business is already running.

The compliance an OPC still has to keep up with

An OPC gets real relief on the calendar of formal meetings. It does not have to hold an annual general meeting at all, and a one-director OPC is not bound by the usual quorum rule for board meetings, though an OPC with more than one director still has to hold at least one board meeting in each half of the calendar year with a gap of not more than ninety days between the two. The financial statements still have to be filed in Form AOC-4 within the time allowed after the financial year closes, and the annual return in the abridged Form MGT-7A, so an OPC is not exempt from annual filings, only from the meeting formalities that a multi-shareholder company has to go through to get there.

None of this filing relief is automatic if the company misses a deadline. A default on AOC-4 or MGT-7A exposes the company and its officers to the same penalty structure that applies to any other defaulting company, and for an NRI running the OPC from abroad, missing a filing because a reminder email went unread is a routine but entirely avoidable way to lose the benefit of the simplified structure. Engaging a company secretary or chartered accountant in India to track these dates, rather than trying to track an Indian compliance calendar from a different time zone without local help, is the practical answer most NRI-owned OPCs eventually settle on.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an OCI cardholder who was born in India form a One Person Company?

Not under the current rule, because OPC eligibility turns on Indian citizenship rather than on birth or ancestry in India. An OCI cardholder who has taken foreign citizenship is treated as a foreign national for this purpose and cannot incorporate an OPC, even if they were born an Indian citizen and hold an OCI card today.

Is there still a cap on how big an NRI-owned OPC can grow?

No statutory cap forces conversion any more. The Companies (Incorporation) Second Amendment Rules, 2021 removed the earlier fifty lakh rupee paid-up capital and two crore rupee turnover thresholds that used to compel an OPC to convert into a private or public company, so an OPC can now continue indefinitely regardless of size unless its member chooses to convert.

Can an NRI be both the member and the nominee of the same OPC?

No. The member and the nominee have to be different people, because the nominee's role is to step into the member's shoes if the member dies or becomes incapable of contracting, which would serve no purpose if they were the same person. Both the member and the nominee can be NRIs under the current rule, as long as each is an Indian citizen.

Does an NRI-owned OPC still need a resident director?

Every company incorporated in India, including an OPC, must have at least one director who has stayed in India for a total period of not less than one hundred and eighty two days in the previous calendar year under the Companies Act's resident director requirement, so an NRI running a single-member OPC typically has to appoint an additional resident director to satisfy this rule even while remaining the sole shareholder.
