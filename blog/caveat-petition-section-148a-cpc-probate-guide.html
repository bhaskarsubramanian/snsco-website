Filing a Caveat in India: Section 148A CPC in Civil Suits and Caveats in Probate Proceedings

How to file a caveat under Section 148A CPC before an expected suit, how a probate caveat works under the Succession Act, and what each actually prevents.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A caveat is a notice to the court, not a notice to the other side, and that distinction confuses more people than any other part of the procedure. Filing one does not stop anyone from going to court against you. What it does is make sure that if someone does go to court seeking an interim order against you, perhaps an injunction or a stay, the court hears you first instead of passing an ex parte order and leaving you to apply for vacating it afterward.

Two separate kinds of caveat are relevant in Indian practice and families and businesses tend to mix them up. The first is the ordinary civil caveat under Section 148A of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, filed when you expect someone to sue you or seek interim relief against you. The second is a caveat in a probate or letters of administration proceeding under the Indian Succession Act, 1925, filed by someone who objects to a will being probated or an administration being granted. The two serve the same broad purpose, being heard before an order goes against you, but they sit in different statutes, follow different procedure, and have different consequences once filed.

What Section 148A actually gives you

Section 148A of the CPC lets a person who expects to be made a party to a suit, or expects an application to be made in a suit already filed, lodge a caveat in that court. Once lodged, the person who intends to file the suit or application, if they have notice of the caveat, must serve a copy on the caveator, and the court must give the caveator a hearing before making any interim order in the proceeding to which the caveat relates. Courts applying this section have repeatedly held that the notice requirement under sub-section (3) is not a formality, and a court that passes an ex parte interim order without checking whether a caveat is on file, despite notice having reached the applicant, defeats the entire purpose of the provision.

A recurring misconception is that a caveat somehow blocks the other side from filing their case or applying for interim relief at all. It does not. The other side can still sue and can still ask for an injunction or attachment. What changes is procedural: the court is expected to issue notice to the caveator and hear them before deciding the interim application, rather than deciding it ex parte. If the other side's lawyers are diligent about checking the caveat register before moving an urgent application, which they are supposed to be, an ex parte order against a caveator becomes much harder to get and much easier to get vacated if it slips through.

Who can file, and in which court

Any person who has reason to believe that a suit or proceeding has been or is about to be instituted in a particular court, and who has an apprehension of an adverse interim order being sought in it, can lodge a caveat there. The section does not restrict this to parties who are already named anywhere, which is why a caveat is commonly filed by a prospective defendant, by a family member who expects to be impleaded in a pending property suit, or by a company that anticipates a dispute with a vendor or an ex-employee moving for urgent relief.

A caveat must be filed in the specific court, or courts, where the applicant genuinely expects proceedings. A caveat lodged in the wrong court or filed as a blanket precaution across every court in a city achieves nothing, since the obligation to check for a caveat and give notice runs only in the court where it is actually on file. Courts have taken differing views on whether Section 148A applies to writ petitions under Article 226 or 227 of the Constitution, with some High Courts treating it as confined to civil suits and proceedings under the CPC and others extending it by their own rules to writ matters, so the position has to be checked against that specific High Court's rules before assuming a caveat filed there covers an anticipated writ.

What to put in the caveat and how it is filed

The application is short by design: the caveator's name and address for service, the name of the person expected to file the suit or application and, where known, the other side's advocate, a brief statement of the matter in dispute and why an adverse order is apprehended, and a prayer that no order be made without hearing the caveator. Where the caveator is represented, the application goes with a vakalatnama, and a nominal court fee applies, fixed by the relevant court fees schedule or High Court rules.

Serving a copy on the person expected to sue, where that person or their advocate is known, is a good practice step even though the statute puts the notice obligation primarily on the person who later does sue once they have notice of the caveat. In practice, courts and registries maintain a caveat register or diary that the filing section is expected to check whenever an urgent or ex parte application comes in, and a caveator who has only filed the application without making sure it is properly indexed against the correct case type or party names risks it being missed.

How long a caveat lasts

Section 148A(5) fixes the life of a caveat at ninety days from the date it is lodged. If the suit or application the caveator anticipated has not been filed within that period, the caveat lapses on its own. Most practitioners treat the remedy for a lapsed caveat as filing a fresh one rather than relying on any automatic renewal, and a caveator who still expects proceedings after ninety days should simply refile before the old one expires rather than assume continuity.

At least one High Court has held that the ninety-day period should exclude a period during which courts were not functioning normally, which matters for caveats that happened to be running during a court closure, but that is a narrow exception tied to its own facts rather than a general rule a caveator can rely on to stretch the ordinary ninety days.

The separate world of a probate caveat

A will does not need a lawsuit to be contested the way a sale deed does. Once a petition for probate or letters of administration is filed, the court issues citations under Section 283 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 to persons claiming an interest in the deceased's estate, inviting them to appear. A person who wants to object, typically another heir who disputes the will's validity, due execution, or the testator's capacity, enters a caveat in that proceeding rather than filing a separate suit.

Courts have read the word 'interest' in Section 283 narrowly: the caveator must show a direct pecuniary or proprietary stake in the estate, not a general grievance, and cannot use the caveat to assert a title of their own that conflicts with the deceased's own ownership of the property. Once an affidavit supporting the caveat is filed, Section 295 of the Act converts the probate petition into a contentious proceeding, numbered and tried broadly as a suit, with the petitioner for probate as plaintiff and the caveator as defendant. This is the single biggest practical consequence of a probate caveat: a straightforward, unopposed probate petition that might otherwise be disposed of on affidavits turns into full-blown litigation, with pleadings, evidence, and cross-examination of witnesses to the will, and can run for years rather than months.

A person who wants to propound a different, earlier or later will of the same deceased cannot do that merely by entering a caveat against the pending petition. They have to file their own substantive petition for probate of that other will, and can caveat the first proceeding in addition. Filing only a caveat, without a competing petition, when what you actually want is to have a different will recognised, leaves the real relief unclaimed.

Common mistakes

Filing a civil caveat under Section 148A without knowing, or being able to identify with reasonable specificity, which court the anticipated proceeding will land in. A caveat is not a nationwide shield, and most High Courts and district courts maintain separate registers, so the same apprehension may need caveats filed in more than one court if the proceeding could genuinely go to either.

Treating a probate caveat as a quick, low-cost way to delay an estate's distribution without a genuine case on execution or capacity. Courts examining these caveats, once the matter is tried as a suit, look at whether the caveator actually leads evidence to support the objection, and a caveat entered without any real basis exposes the caveator to costs and does not improve their position in the family's eventual distribution of assets.

Forgetting that a caveat is not itself a form of interim relief. It does not stop the other side from approaching an appellate court, does not freeze the underlying asset, and does not substitute for an injunction application the caveator may separately need if they are worried about alienation or waste of property while the main proceeding is pending.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does filing a caveat under Section 148A stop someone from suing me?

No. It does not prevent the suit or the application from being filed at all. It only requires the court to notify the caveator and hear them before passing an interim order, such as an injunction or an ex parte stay, instead of deciding that application without the caveator's side being heard.

How long does a civil caveat under the CPC remain valid?

Ninety days from the date it is lodged, under Section 148A(5). If no suit or application the caveator anticipated is filed within that window, the caveat lapses on its own, and the usual practice is to file a fresh caveat rather than assume it continues automatically.

What happens once someone files a caveat against a probate petition?

Under Section 295 of the Indian Succession Act, once an affidavit supporting the caveat is filed, the probate petition stops being a simple application and is converted into a contentious proceeding, tried like a civil suit with the petitioner for probate as plaintiff and the caveator as defendant, which can take considerably longer to resolve.

Can anyone file a caveat against a will, or only an heir?

The caveator must show an interest in the estate, generally read by courts as a direct pecuniary or proprietary stake, not merely a general objection. Someone with no legal stake in the estate's distribution is unlikely to be treated as having the standing to caveat the probate petition.

Can a caveat be filed against a writ petition in the High Court?

It depends on the High Court. Some High Courts have held that Section 148A applies only to civil suits and proceedings under the Code of Civil Procedure and does not extend to writ petitions under Article 226 or 227, while others apply it through their own rules. Check the specific High Court's current rules before relying on a caveat to cover an anticipated writ.

References & Further Reading

The provisions and rulings below were relied on for this guide. The position can change with amendments and later decisions, so check the current text and the latest case law before acting on any of it.

  1. Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Section 148A, including sub-sections (3) (notice requirement) and (5) (ninety-day validity)
  2. Indian Succession Act, 1925, Section 283 (citations to persons interested) and Section 295 (caveat converts a probate petition into a contentious proceeding tried as a suit)
  3. Deepak Khosla v. Union of India & Ors. (Delhi High Court), on Section 148A applying only to civil proceedings and not to CrPC petitions
  4. Yaseen & Ors. v. Mahendra Yadav, Allahabad High Court, 7 October 2020, on excluding the court-closure period from the ninety-day computation
  5. In re Narendra Narayan Roy Chaudhury (Calcutta High Court, 1945), on a caveator who propounds a different will needing to file a substantive petition of their own
