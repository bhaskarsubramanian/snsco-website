Does a Foreign Employee Working in India Have to Join the Provident Fund? The International Worker Rules

PF rules for foreign staff working in India: who counts as an International Worker, totalisation agreements, and the 2024-2025 High Court split explained.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 13 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An Indian company hires a German engineer on a three-year assignment and puts her on local payroll alongside its Indian staff. The HR team assumes provident fund applies to Indian employees only, and stops there. That assumption is wrong, and it is one of the more consistently litigated points in Indian payroll compliance because, unlike an ordinary employee, a foreign national working in India can be covered by the Employees' Provident Fund scheme from day one, on the full salary, with no wage ceiling, unless a specific exemption applies.

This is governed by a special category called 'International Worker' that has existed since 2008 and has been through a genuine split in the High Courts: one struck it down as unconstitutional in 2024, another upheld it in 2025. As on today the provision stands, employers must comply with it, and the position is carried forward into the new Employees' Provident Funds Scheme, 2026, which replaced the 1952 scheme this year.

Who counts as an International Worker

An International Worker, for provident fund purposes, is either a foreign national working for an establishment in India that is covered by the Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952, or an Indian employee who has worked or is going to work in a foreign country with which India has a Social Security Agreement and is eligible, under that country's social security law, for benefits there on account of that employment. The category was inserted into the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme as Paragraph 83, and into the Employees' Pension Scheme as Paragraph 43A, with effect from 1 October 2008, and the provision was later substituted with modifications by a notification issued in September 2010.

The practical effect of the category is to remove the ordinary wage ceiling for provident fund contributions. A domestic employee's contribution is calculated, for most purposes, on a statutory wage ceiling, but an International Worker's contribution is calculated on full actual salary, which is why the category matters so much to an employer's payroll cost for foreign staff and for Indian staff posted out under an intercompany arrangement.

The two routes out: totalisation and exemption

The scheme does not require double contribution from every foreign worker. India has signed bilateral Social Security Agreements, also called Totalisation Agreements, with a number of countries, and these agreements do two main things. They allow a worker who has contributed to social security in both countries to combine, or 'totalise', the periods of contribution for eligibility to benefits, and they allow a worker on a short-term posting to stay on their home country's social security system instead of the host country's, through a 'detachment certificate', avoiding contribution in both places at once for the same period.

Separately, the government has at times exempted citizens of specific countries from mandatory contribution altogether by notification, generally on a reciprocal basis where that country does not require Indian workers there to contribute to its own scheme. Whether a particular nationality is covered by a Social Security Agreement, and whether a detachment certificate has actually been obtained for a specific employee, has to be checked individually. An employer cannot assume exemption merely because the employee's home country is a developed economy with its own pension system; the exemption exists only where India has actually concluded an agreement with that country or issued a specific notification.

The Karnataka High Court struck the rule down in 2024

In Stone Hill Education Foundation v. Union of India and Others, decided on 25 April 2024 on a batch of writ petitions including WP No. 18486 of 2012, the Karnataka High Court held Paragraph 83 of the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme and Paragraph 43A of the Employees' Pension Scheme unconstitutional and arbitrary, as violating Article 14 of the Constitution. The court's reasoning centred on the removal of the wage ceiling for International Workers while ordinary domestic employees remained subject to a cap, and on a distinction the provision drew between International Workers of Indian origin and those of other nationalities, which the court found unjustified.

That ruling, being a decision of a High Court, binds establishments and officers within Karnataka, and for a period it gave employers there, and employers elsewhere citing it persuasively, a real argument to resist contribution demands on foreign staff.

The Delhi High Court went the other way in 2025

In SpiceJet Ltd. v. Union of India and Another, in writ petitions including W.P.(C) 2941 of 2012 and W.P.(C) 6330 of 2021, the Delhi High Court on 4 November 2025 upheld the validity of the 2008 and 2010 notifications that introduced the International Worker provisions. The court expressly disagreed with the Karnataka High Court's reasoning, holding that International Workers form a separate and distinct class from domestic employees because of the different, often short-term and cross-border nature of their employment and social security arrangements, and that this classification has a rational basis connected to the object of the law, which the Karnataka ruling, in the Delhi court's view, did not adequately address.

The Delhi court also pointed to an earlier Bombay High Court decision, Sachin Vijay Desai v. Union of India, decided in 2019, which had similarly upheld the International Worker provisions as constitutionally valid, giving the Delhi ruling company on the point before the Karnataka decision came along.

What this split means for an employer today

With the High Courts split, the Bombay and Delhi courts upholding the provision and the Karnataka court striking it down, and the question not yet settled by the Supreme Court, an employer's safest course depends heavily on where its covered establishment is located. An establishment within Karnataka has the Stone Hill ruling on its side unless and until a higher court reverses it. An establishment elsewhere, including in Delhi, faces an EPFO that will rely on the SpiceJet ruling and continue to enforce Paragraph 83 as it stands, and a demand for contribution on full salary for foreign staff should be expected rather than resisted on constitutional grounds alone at the field level.

That conflict is now before the Supreme Court, which took up the validity of the International Worker regime in March 2026 with a view to resolving the inconsistency between the High Court rulings. Until that happens, an employer with foreign staff in more than one state cannot rely on a single national answer and should take jurisdiction-specific advice before deciding whether to contribute under protest, appeal an assessment, or simply comply.

A practical first step regardless of jurisdiction is an internal audit: list every foreign national on payroll, check whether a detachment certificate was ever obtained for each one, and confirm whether India has a Social Security Agreement with that employee's home country at all. Many employers discover during this exercise that contributions have been made inconsistently across different foreign hires over the years, with some treated correctly as exempt and others missed entirely, which is its own exposure quite apart from which way the Karnataka-Delhi conflict eventually settles.

The 2026 scheme carries the provision forward

The Ministry of Labour and Employment notified the Employees' Provident Funds Scheme, 2026 in supersession of the 1952 scheme, with the new scheme and its companion Employees' Pension Scheme, 2026 and Employees' Deposit Linked Insurance Scheme, 2026 coming into force around the end of June and the start of July 2026. The core contribution rate and the basic eligibility framework have been retained from the 1952 scheme, and members already enrolled continue without a fresh enrolment. The new scheme carries forward a dedicated International Worker framework in substance, so the Karnataka and Delhi rulings, both decided on the 1952 scheme's version of the provision, remain directly relevant to interpreting its 2026 successor.

Employers should not treat the 2026 renumbering as a reason to revisit compliance decisions taken under the 1952 scheme. Actions already taken under the earlier scheme, including registrations, contributions and pending disputes, are expressly preserved rather than wiped clean by the supersession.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a foreign employee have to contribute provident fund from the first day of Indian employment?

Generally yes, once the Indian establishment is covered by the Act, unless the employee holds a valid detachment certificate under a Social Security Agreement or falls within a specific country exemption notification. There is no initial grace period based on length of stay alone for an International Worker.

Is there a salary ceiling for provident fund contribution on an International Worker?

No. Unlike most domestic employees, whose contribution is calculated against a statutory wage ceiling, an International Worker's contribution is calculated on full actual salary, which is the central financial difference this category creates for employer payroll cost.

My establishment is in Karnataka. Can I stop contributing for foreign staff after Stone Hill?

The Karnataka High Court's 2024 ruling gives establishments there a real argument, but it is one High Court's view against another, and no final word from the Supreme Court exists yet. Stopping contribution without specific advice on your facts and current appellate status carries real risk.

What is a detachment certificate and how does it help?

It is a certificate issued under a bilateral Social Security Agreement confirming that a worker on a short-term posting remains covered by their home country's social security system during the posting, which lets the employer avoid contributing to the Indian scheme for that same period for that employee.

Does the new 2026 provident fund scheme change the International Worker rules?

The 2026 scheme replaces the 1952 scheme but carries the International Worker framework forward in substance, along with the core contribution rate and eligibility norms. The ongoing Karnataka-Delhi court conflict, decided under the 1952 scheme's wording, continues to apply to its interpretation.
