SEBI's Settlement Route: What 'Without Admitting or Denying' Actually Protects, and What It Does Not

How SEBI's settlement mechanism works, why a settled order is not a finding of guilt, and why SEBI can still revive a matter it has supposedly closed.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 17 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A company or individual facing a SEBI show-cause notice has three realistic paths once the investigation is underway: contest it through the full adjudication and appeal process, or apply to settle it under the settlement framework and bring the matter to a close without a formal finding of wrongdoing. The settlement route has become the default choice for a very wide range of SEBI matters, from technical disclosure lapses to minimum public shareholding non-compliance involving some of India's largest listed groups, and understanding what it actually buys, and does not buy, matters as much as deciding to use it.

This guide works through how SEBI's settlement mechanism operates in practice, what the phrase 'without admitting or denying' really means for a respondent, and the circumstances in which a settled matter can still come back.

How the Settlement Application Actually Works

A person against whom SEBI proceedings have been initiated, or against whom proceedings are reasonably apprehended, can apply to settle under the SEBI (Settlement Proceedings) Regulations, 2018, which took effect on 1 January 2019 and replaced the earlier 2014 settlement regime. The application typically proposes a settlement amount and, where relevant, specific undertakings about future conduct, and it is considered by a panel that generally includes Whole-Time Members of SEBI, who evaluate whether the proposed terms are appropriate given the nature and seriousness of the alleged violation.

Approval is not automatic. SEBI retains discretion over whether a particular matter is even suitable for settlement in the first place, and the regulator can reject a settlement proposal it considers inadequate relative to the alleged conduct, sending the respondent back to the full adjudication process if terms cannot be agreed. This discretion means a settlement application is a negotiation, not an entitlement, and a respondent should not assume that offering a settlement amount guarantees SEBI's acceptance of it.

What 'Without Admitting or Denying' Actually Means

The defining feature of a SEBI settlement order is that the respondent neither admits nor denies the underlying findings of fact or conclusions of law that the show-cause notice alleged. This has a real, practical consequence: a settlement order is generally not treated as a factual finding of wrongdoing, which matters for how the episode can be characterised in other contexts, such as disclosures to other regulators, commercial counterparties, or in unrelated litigation.

What it does not do is erase the underlying conduct or the investigation itself. SEBI's own record of having investigated the matter continues to exist, the show-cause notice and the factual allegations within it remain part of the regulatory history, and a settlement is best understood as closing the specific enforcement proceeding rather than rewriting what happened. A respondent who treats a settlement as equivalent to a clean bill of health, rather than as a negotiated closure of one enforcement track, risks misjudging how the episode will be read later by other stakeholders.

Why SEBI Retains the Power to Revive a Settled Matter

A settlement under the 2018 Regulations is not an unconditional, permanently final closure. SEBI has expressly retained, under the settlement regulations, the power to restore or revive the underlying proceedings where it later comes to notice that the applicant did not make full and true disclosure during the settlement process, breached an undertaking or waiver given as part of the settlement, or where there was some discrepancy between the settlement terms agreed and how they were actually implemented.

This revival power is the main reason a settlement should be approached with the same factual rigour as a contested proceeding, even though the respondent is not formally admitting anything. Overstating a position, concealing a relevant fact during settlement discussions, or failing to follow through precisely on an undertaking given as part of the settlement terms all create a live risk that SEBI reopens the exact proceeding the settlement was meant to close, this time with the added complication of the settlement breach itself as a fresh issue. Counsel advising on a settlement application should therefore treat the disclosure obligations within the settlement process itself as seriously as the disclosures that would have been required in a full adjudication, since the shortcut the settlement route offers on process does not extend to a shortcut on candour.

The Adani Minimum Public Shareholding Settlement as an Illustration

The scale at which this route operates was on display in September 2026, when Gautam Adani, four listed Adani group companies, including Adani Enterprises, Adani Power, Adani Ports and Special Economic Zone, and Adani Energy Solutions, and more than a dozen other individuals settled minimum public shareholding non-compliance proceedings with SEBI for a combined amount of roughly ₹1.48 crore. Each of the four company groups paid approximately ₹37.05 lakh, with the company and its named directors held jointly and severally responsible for that amount, and the settlement followed a show-cause notice issued in September 2024 and a supplementary notice in March 2025.

The order was passed without any admission or denial of the underlying allegations, and SEBI retained its usual revival rights under the regulations. A separate order on the same date reportedly found that the central allegations against a related individual, Vinod Adani, in the same long-running matter were not established, a different outcome from the settlement reached by the other respondents and a reminder that settlement is one possible outcome among several in a multi-respondent proceeding, not a single, uniform resolution that necessarily applies to everyone the notice named.

A Framework in the Middle of Change

SEBI issued a consultation paper in August 2026 proposing a substantial overhaul of the settlement mechanism through new Settlement of Proceedings Regulations, which means the precise framework described here may not be the one in force by the time a reader is actually considering a settlement application. Practitioners should check, at the time of any application, whether the 2018 Regulations as amended remain the operative framework or whether a newly notified regime has taken their place, since a consultation paper's proposals are not the same as a finalised regulation and the gap between the two can matter considerably for procedural details such as timelines and panel composition.

This uncertainty is itself a reason to move on a settlement application once the decision to pursue one has been made, rather than treating the timing as indefinitely flexible. A regime change mid-application could alter procedural expectations in ways that are hard to predict in advance, and a respondent already committed to the settlement route is generally better served by proceeding under the clearer, currently understood framework than waiting to see what a still-pending consultation eventually produces. Companies with a show-cause notice already in hand should ask their counsel, as a specific line item in the settlement strategy, whether any part of the proposed consultation changes would materially affect the terms on offer today, rather than discovering the answer only after a new regulation has already taken effect.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does settling with SEBI mean admitting guilt?

No. A SEBI settlement order is passed without the respondent admitting or denying the underlying findings of fact or conclusions of law. The order is generally not treated as a factual finding of wrongdoing, though SEBI's own investigation record and the original allegations remain part of the regulatory history.

Is SEBI required to accept a settlement proposal?

No. SEBI retains discretion over whether a matter is appropriate for settlement at all, and a panel typically including Whole-Time Members evaluates proposed settlement terms before approving them. SEBI can reject an inadequate proposal and send the matter back to the full adjudication process.

Can SEBI reopen a matter after it has been settled?

Yes, in specific circumstances. SEBI has expressly retained the power to revive settled proceedings where the applicant failed to make full and true disclosure, breached an undertaking given during settlement, or where a discrepancy arose between the agreed terms and how they were actually implemented.

Is the settlement route only for minor or technical SEBI violations?

No. The September 2026 settlement by Gautam Adani and four listed Adani group companies over minimum public shareholding non-compliance, involving a years-long investigation and over a dozen respondents, shows the route being used for significant, high-profile matters, not just minor technical lapses.

What regulations currently govern SEBI settlements?

The SEBI (Settlement Proceedings) Regulations, 2018, in force since 1 January 2019. SEBI issued an August 2026 consultation paper proposing a full overhaul of this framework, so practitioners should confirm whether a newly notified regime has since replaced the 2018 Regulations before relying on current procedural details.
