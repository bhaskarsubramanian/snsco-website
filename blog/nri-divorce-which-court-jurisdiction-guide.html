NRI Divorce: Which Indian Court Has Jurisdiction and Where to File

Where NRIs and their spouses can file for divorce in India under Section 19 HMA or Section 31 SMA, and why many petitions fail.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

As the law stands today, an Indian divorce petition involving an NRI spouse can usually be filed in one of four places: where the marriage was solemnised, where the respondent currently resides, where the couple last lived together as husband and wife, or, for a wife filing the petition, where she herself resides on the date she files it. Which of these actually works depends on where each spouse is living when the case is filed, not on where either of them happens to hold a passport or a green card.

This question comes up constantly because one spouse is abroad and the other wants to file in India without waiting for the other side to show up first, or because a wife living with her parents in a different city wants to file close to home rather than travel back to the matrimonial town. Get the forum wrong and the respondent's first move will be an application contesting jurisdiction, which can cost a year before the real case is even heard.

The four grounds under Section 19, Hindu Marriage Act

Section 19 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 lists the district courts that may entertain a divorce, judicial separation or nullity petition. A petition can go to the court where the marriage was solemnised, where the respondent resides at the time of filing, or where the parties to the marriage last resided together. For an NRI couple, the 'last resided together' ground often turns out to be the most useful of the three, because it does not depend on where either spouse happens to be living now. If a couple married in Delhi, lived together in Dubai for six years, and the wife then moved back to her parents' home in Pune while the husband stayed in Dubai, a petition can still be filed in the court that has jurisdiction over wherever the two of them last shared a home before the separation, provided that place is in India.

Where none of these three grounds sits comfortably in India, because the marriage was solemnised abroad and the couple never lived together in India, the case gets harder and a lawyer needs to look closely at whether an Indian court has jurisdiction at all. Courts have generally resisted letting parties manufacture jurisdiction through a short, token visit to India timed around the filing. A petition filed the week after arrival, with no real connection to the place, invites an early challenge and should be avoided.

The wife's own right to file where she lives

Section 19 was amended in 2003 to add a fourth ground: a wife can present her petition in the district court within whose jurisdiction she is residing on the date she files it. This clause was inserted specifically so that a wife in distress, including one who has been deserted or is otherwise estranged from her husband, does not have to travel to the husband's town or to the place of the wedding just to file. For an NRI marriage, this is frequently the clause that actually matters: a wife who has returned to India while her NRI husband remains abroad can file in the city where she is currently living, even if the marriage took place elsewhere and the couple never lived together in that city.

Courts reading this clause have generally taken 'residing' to mean more than a flying visit, though they have not read in a fixed minimum period either. A wife who is only in a city for a few days to attend a family function, with no settled presence there, is on weaker ground than one who has moved back and is staying with her family while the case is pending. The husband can contest this factual question, so the wife's side should be ready to show school admissions, a ration card, Aadhaar address updates, or similar evidence of an actual, not token, residence.

This special right belongs only to the wife. A husband cannot invoke the same clause to file where he resides; he is confined to the marriage-solemnisation, respondent-residence and last-cohabitation grounds.

Filing when the other spouse lives abroad

A separate difficulty arises when the petitioner is in India and the only available ground would otherwise require serving a respondent who lives permanently outside India. Courts have accepted petitions filed where the petitioner resides in India, with the respondent abroad, under the limb that lets a petition proceed where the respondent does not reside within the territories to which the Act extends, so long as the petitioner is not using this purely to avoid a respondent who could just as easily be served through an Indian address or a short visit. Practically, this is less about a special rule for NRIs and more about reading the existing grounds sensibly when one party genuinely lives overseas.

Serving an NRI respondent who is uncooperative is its own separate problem from jurisdiction. Courts allow service through the Ministry of External Affairs and the Indian mission in the respondent's country, and, once ordinary service is shown to be failing, substituted service by publication. None of this changes which court has jurisdiction; it only changes how the respondent is brought to notice once the right court has been identified.

Civil and interfaith marriages under the Special Marriage Act

Where the marriage was registered under the Special Marriage Act, 1954, rather than solemnised as a Hindu marriage, the equivalent jurisdiction provision is Section 31. It mirrors the Hindu Marriage Act's grounds: the court where the marriage was solemnised, where the respondent resides, or where the parties last resided together. A matching wife's-residence clause was added to Section 31 at the same time as the 2003 amendment to Section 19, so a wife married under the Special Marriage Act has the same right to file where she currently lives.

Section 31 also has its own clause dealing with a wife domiciled in India: where she has been ordinarily resident in India for a continuous period of three years immediately before filing, she can seek a decree even though her husband is not residing in India at the time. This is of real use to NRI wives whose husbands have settled abroad and have no remaining presence in India at all, a situation where the 'last resided together' and 'respondent resides' grounds may both be unavailable.

A foreign decree does not settle an Indian jurisdiction question

Couples sometimes assume that because one spouse has already obtained, or could obtain, a divorce decree from a foreign court where they live, an Indian petition becomes unnecessary or can simply be filed anywhere convenient. That assumption causes real trouble. A foreign decree granted on a ground that the Hindu Marriage Act does not recognise, most commonly irretrievable breakdown of marriage where Indian law requires proof of a specific fault ground or a completed mutual-consent process, is not automatically binding in India under Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure, and a party can still be required to litigate the divorce afresh in an Indian court with proper jurisdiction under Section 19 or Section 31. Treating the foreign proceeding as a substitute for choosing the correct Indian forum, rather than as a separate question to be assessed on its own facts, is one of the more expensive mistakes NRI couples make.

The safer course, where there is any possibility that recognition in India will matter later (inheritance, remarriage, a second nationality, custody), is to treat the Indian jurisdiction question as a live issue from the start rather than an afterthought once the foreign decree is already in hand.

What actually goes wrong in these filings

In practice, three mistakes recur. First, petitioners file in the city that is simply most convenient for their own lawyer or family, without checking whether that city actually satisfies one of the statutory grounds; the respondent's counsel will almost always test this at the first hearing. Second, parties wait too long to raise the wife's-residence ground with supporting proof, so that by the time documents are filed to establish genuine residence, the opposing side has already built a narrative of forum shopping. Third, couples who have lived almost their entire married life abroad sometimes find that no Indian ground applies at all, because the marriage was solemnised abroad, the parties never lived together in India, and neither the husband-respondent nor, for the wife's clause, the wife herself has a genuine Indian residence; in that situation, the correct forum may in fact be the foreign court, however much a party may prefer an Indian proceeding.

A court asked to decide a jurisdiction objection will usually want to see documentary proof tied to the specific ground relied on, rather than a general assertion of connection to India. Deciding which of the available grounds is strongest, and gathering the supporting proof for it, is worth doing before the petition is drafted, not after an objection has been filed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an NRI husband file for divorce in India if his wife lives abroad with him?

Yes, provided one of the statutory grounds is met, most commonly that the marriage was solemnised in India or that the couple last resided together in India before separating. If neither of those connects to India and the wife is the respondent living abroad, filing in India becomes harder and depends on the specific facts.

Does a wife have to be living in India for a long time before she can file where she resides?

The clause does not set a fixed minimum period, and courts have read 'residing' flexibly rather than requiring years of stay. What matters is that the residence is genuine and not a brief, engineered visit timed to the filing; supporting proof of an actual address helps if the point is contested.

If we already divorced abroad, can one of us still be dragged into an Indian court?

Yes, if the foreign decree was granted on a ground the Hindu Marriage Act does not recognise and the other spouse chooses to contest its recognition under Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure. An Indian court can then be asked to decide the divorce afresh, in whichever forum has jurisdiction under Section 19 or Section 31.

What if the marriage was registered under the Special Marriage Act rather than as a Hindu marriage?

The jurisdiction rules sit in Section 31 of the Special Marriage Act instead of Section 19 of the Hindu Marriage Act, but the grounds are largely parallel: place of solemnisation, respondent's residence, last shared residence, and a wife's right to file where she currently resides.

Can both spouses agree to file wherever is most convenient for them, regardless of these rules?

Parties cannot confer jurisdiction on a court by agreement alone; the court must actually have jurisdiction under the statute. In an uncontested, mutual-consent case this is rarely tested, but filing in a court with no statutory connection to the marriage leaves the petition open to challenge if either side later changes position.
