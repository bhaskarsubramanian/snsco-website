Can You Enforce an Emergency Arbitrator's Order in India? What Amazon v Future Retail Settled

An emergency arbitrator's order is enforceable in India as a Section 17 order, but only when the arbitration itself is seated in India.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A client calls after losing an urgent application before an emergency arbitrator appointed under an institution's rules, usually SIAC or ICC, and wants to know whether the order can actually be used to stop the other side doing something in India right now, or whether it is just a piece of paper until a full tribunal is formed months later. The honest answer for several years was that nobody was fully sure, because the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 never mentions an emergency arbitrator at all.

That uncertainty narrowed considerably after the Supreme Court's 2021 ruling in the Amazon-Future Retail dispute, though it did not disappear, and the gap that remains matters a great deal if your arbitration happens to be seated outside India rather than inside it.

Why the Act Has Nothing to Say About Emergency Arbitrators

The 1996 Act was drafted around a model where interim relief comes either from a court under Section 9 or from an arbitral tribunal under Section 17, once that tribunal actually exists. Emergency arbitration is a creature of institutional rules, SIAC, ICC, LCIA and others each built their own version, designed precisely to plug the gap between filing a request for arbitration and a tribunal being constituted, which can otherwise take months.

Because the statute was silent, parties facing an emergency order in India had a real fight on their hands whenever the losing side argued the order was a nullity, issued by someone with no status under the Act and therefore nothing a court should lift a finger to enforce. Future Retail Group ran exactly that argument in 2020 after Amazon obtained an emergency award restraining the Future-Reliance transaction, taking the position that emergency arbitration sits entirely outside Part I of the Act and cannot be anchored to Section 17 at all.

What the Supreme Court Actually Decided in 2021

In Amazon.com NV Investment Holdings LLC v. Future Retail Ltd., (2022) 1 SCC 209, decided on 6 August 2021, the Supreme Court held that an award made by an emergency arbitrator under institutional rules, in an arbitration seated in India, qualifies as an order under Section 17(1) of the Act. Parties who agree to arbitrate under a particular institution's rules are taken to have agreed to that institution's emergency arbitrator mechanism as part of the bargain, so there is no separate consent problem to solve.

Because the order counts as a Section 17(1) order, it can be enforced the same way any tribunal's interim order is enforced, through Section 17(2), which lets a party approach the jurisdictional court for enforcement as if the order were a court order. The Court also held that an order enforcing an emergency award under Section 17(2) cannot itself be appealed under Section 37, which closed off a line of satellite litigation that would otherwise have let a losing party drag out enforcement through an appeal.

The ruling settled the core status question for India-seated cases. It did not deal with, and does not help, a party trying to enforce an emergency order made in a foreign-seated arbitration.

The Foreign-Seated Gap Nobody Has Closed Yet

If your arbitration is seated in Singapore, London or anywhere outside India, an emergency arbitrator's order from that proceeding gets no benefit from the Amazon ruling, because Section 17 only speaks to India-seated arbitrations. A party holding a foreign-seated emergency order who needs interim protection in India has to go a different route entirely: file a fresh application under Section 9 before an Indian court, asking the court to grant the same interim relief in its own right, rather than asking the court to simply enforce the emergency arbitrator's order as such.

That route is not foolproof. Section 9 is only available where the parties have not contracted it away, and some institutional or bespoke arbitration clauses do exclude Indian court assistance once the parties have chosen a foreign seat and a foreign curial law. Even where Section 9 is open, the applicant is effectively re-litigating the urgency and the merits before an Indian judge, who may or may not reach the same conclusion the emergency arbitrator did, rather than getting a straightforward enforcement order.

A useful illustration from after Amazon is Ashok Kumar Goel v. Ebixcash, where the Bombay High Court, faced with a Section 9 application tracking relief an emergency arbitrator had already granted, treated the emergency arbitrator's reasoning as persuasive and granted substantially the same relief. That shows the Section 9 route can work in practice, but it is still a fresh application with its own hearing, not a mechanical enforcement step.

Drafting Around the Problem Before It Arises

A client negotiating an arbitration clause today should not assume the Amazon ruling solves the emergency relief question by itself. Choosing an India seat, where available and commercially sensible, gets the clearest benefit of Section 17 enforceability for any emergency order. Choosing an institution whose rules expressly provide for emergency arbitrators, SIAC and ICC both do, matters more than it might seem, since the entire Amazon reasoning rests on the parties having agreed to that institution's rules, emergency mechanism included.

Where a foreign seat is unavoidable, for instance because a foreign counterparty insists on it, the clause should at minimum avoid language that could be read as excluding Section 9 altogether, so an Indian party retains the fallback of approaching an Indian court directly for interim protection even though it will not be able to simply enforce a foreign emergency order as such. It also helps to name the specific emergency arbitrator provision of the chosen institution's rules in the clause itself, rather than a bare reference to the institution, since a court weighing whether the parties truly consented to emergency relief will look closely at exactly what the clause says they agreed to adopt.

The Draft 2024 Amendment Bill, and Why It Is Not Law Yet

The Department of Legal Affairs put out the Draft Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Bill, 2024 for public consultation in October 2024, proposing a new Section 9A that would give emergency arbitration an express statutory footing, letting arbitral institutions provide for emergency arbitrator appointments before a tribunal is constituted, with such an order enforceable as a Section 17(2) order and open to later confirmation, variation or setting aside by the tribunal once it is formed. As of now the bill remains a consultation draft, not an enacted amendment, so the governing law for emergency arbitration is still the Amazon judgment rather than any statutory text.

A party should treat the draft bill as a signal of where the law is heading, useful for arguing policy in a close case, rather than as something a court is bound to apply. Until it is actually passed, the India-seated versus foreign-seated distinction from Amazon remains the operative line, and anyone relying on an emergency order needs to work out which side of that line their arbitration falls on before assuming enforcement will be straightforward.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an emergency arbitrator's order automatically enforceable in India?

Only if the arbitration is seated in India. The Supreme Court in Amazon v. Future Retail treated such an order as a Section 17(1) order, enforceable under Section 17(2) like a tribunal order. A foreign-seated emergency order gets no such benefit and needs a separate Section 9 application.

Can I appeal a court order that enforces an emergency arbitrator's award?

No. The Supreme Court held that an order enforcing an emergency award under Section 17(2) is not appealable under Section 37, which removes one route a losing party might otherwise use to delay compliance while a full appeal is heard and decided on its own timeline.

What do I do if my emergency order came from a Singapore- or London-seated arbitration?

File a fresh Section 9 application before the appropriate Indian court, asking for the same interim relief directly, rather than asking the court to enforce the foreign order itself. Check first whether your clause has excluded Section 9 assistance, since some foreign-seat clauses do exactly that.

Does an arbitration clause need to say anything specific to get emergency arbitrator protection?

It should point to an institution, such as SIAC or ICC, whose rules actually provide for an emergency arbitrator mechanism, since the Amazon reasoning rests on the parties having agreed to that institution's full rule set, emergency provisions included, not on some freestanding right to urgent relief.

Has the law been changed to deal with this directly?

Not yet. A Draft Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Bill, 2024 proposes a new Section 9A giving emergency arbitration express statutory recognition, letting institutions appoint emergency arbitrators before a tribunal is formed, but it remains a public consultation draft and has not been enacted into law.
