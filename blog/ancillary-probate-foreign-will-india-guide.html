A Will Was Already Probated Abroad: Getting Ancillary Probate for Indian Assets

An NRI's will was probated abroad with Indian assets still to deal with: how Section 228 of the Indian Succession Act grants ancillary probate here.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 22 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

When a person dies leaving a will, and a court outside India has already granted probate or letters of administration on that will, the executor still cannot deal with the deceased's bank accounts, shares or property in India on the strength of the foreign grant alone. Section 228 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 lets an Indian court grant what is usually called ancillary probate: a domestic grant, based on the will already proved abroad, that gives the executor authority over the assets actually located in India.

This comes up most often for an NRI family where a parent or relative who had settled in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Singapore, the UAE or elsewhere dies leaving both foreign assets and an Indian bank account, flat or shareholding that still needs to be transferred to the heirs or sold and the proceeds repatriated. Without an ancillary grant from an Indian court, an Indian bank or sub-registrar will generally not accept a foreign probate certificate by itself, however properly it was granted in the country where it was issued.

Why the foreign grant is not enough on its own

India's succession law treats movable and immovable property in India as subject to the jurisdiction of Indian courts for the purpose of administration, whatever court dealt with the estate elsewhere. A probate or letters of administration granted by a court outside India operates only within that court's own territory unless an Indian court separately recognises it, and Section 228 is the mechanism for that recognition where a will has already been proved abroad.

The practical effect is that an executor who already holds a clean, uncontested grant in, say, New York or London still has to go back to an Indian court, file a petition, and get an order before an Indian bank will release funds or before a share transfer or property mutation can go through. This often surprises families who assume that because probate has already been granted once, the Indian side of the estate should follow automatically; it does not, and treating the Indian assets as an afterthought once the foreign estate is closed is one of the more common and avoidable delays in NRI succession matters.

What Section 228 actually requires

Section 228 allows an Indian court to grant probate of a will proved and deposited in a court of competent jurisdiction outside India, with a properly authenticated copy of the will and of the foreign grant filed along with the petition. Courts have described this authentication requirement in practice as being satisfied by an apostilled copy of the foreign grant and will where the country that issued it is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, with one Bombay High Court order going as far as saying that once an apostilled copy is placed before the court, it should ordinarily grant letters of administration or probate on that basis without reopening the question of the will's validity.

The grant that follows is ancillary in nature, meaning it depends on the original foreign grant remaining valid and in force; if the foreign grant were revoked, the Indian ancillary grant would not survive it either, though it is not automatically revoked merely because the primary estate elsewhere is still being administered. One Bombay High Court order, dealing with a grant under Sections 228 and 271 together, held that an ancillary grant is not liable to be revoked merely because proceedings on the original grant are still pending, as long as that original grant is still subsisting.

Proof of the will under Section 228 is treated differently from proving a will from scratch under Section 276, which is the ordinary route for a will that has never been probated anywhere; commentary on a 2024 decision notes that the two provisions ask the court to do different things, because under Section 228 the court is essentially recognising a will already found genuine elsewhere, not independently satisfying itself of due execution and attestation the way it would for a fresh probate petition.

Which court has jurisdiction, and what the petition needs

Section 270 of the Act fixes which Indian court can entertain a Section 228 petition, generally the District Judge within whose jurisdiction the deceased's property, or a part of it, is actually situated at the time of the application, or, in a High Court exercising original jurisdiction over such matters, the equivalent court there. Where the Indian assets are spread across more than one state, for instance a bank account in Delhi and an apartment in Kolkata, the family typically picks the location where the more significant or more immediately needed asset sits, since the grant once issued extends to the deceased's property generally rather than only to assets in that particular court's district.

The petition itself needs the authenticated or apostilled copy of the foreign will and grant, the death certificate, proof of the petitioner's status as executor or administrator under the foreign grant, and the usual schedule of the Indian assets the family wants to deal with, typically bank accounts with their account numbers, demat holdings, and property with its registration details. Because the underlying will was already tested once abroad, the Indian proceeding is usually faster and less adversarial than an ordinary probate petition, provided no one in India disputes the will or the petitioner's standing to represent the estate, in which case it becomes a contested proceeding like any other probate matter.

Repatriating the Indian proceeds once the grant is in hand

Getting the ancillary grant is usually only the first half of what an NRI family actually wants, which is to sell the Indian asset, if that is the plan, and move the money to wherever the heirs live. Once Indian banks and the sub-registrar accept the ancillary grant and the estate's assets are transferred into the names of the legal heirs or liquidated, the tax and foreign exchange rules that apply to any NRI dealing with inherited Indian assets apply here as well, including the tax deducted at source on a sale of inherited property and the documentation an authorised dealer bank will ask for before remitting the sale proceeds abroad under the current repatriation limits.

Families sometimes try to shortcut this by asking an Indian bank to simply accept the foreign grant with a covering letter from the family's foreign lawyer. Most Indian banks will not do this for any meaningful sum, because the bank's own legal risk in releasing funds without a domestic court order is exactly what Section 228 exists to remove; building the time for an ancillary probate petition into the family's overall plan for the estate, rather than assuming it can be skipped once a foreign grant exists, avoids months of back and forth with a branch manager who simply has no authority to accept the foreign paper alone.

When several heirs live in different countries

It is common for one sibling to be named executor under the foreign will while other heirs live in India, in a third country, or have returned to India after years abroad. The ancillary probate petition is usually filed in the executor's name, and a power of attorney authorising a representative in India, often an advocate or a trusted relative, to appear, sign and collect documents on the executor's behalf saves the named executor from having to travel to India purely to attend court dates that are mostly procedural once the paperwork is in order.

Where the heirs disagree about how the Indian assets should eventually be divided, that disagreement does not usually stop the ancillary grant itself from being issued, because Section 228 is concerned with recognising the will and the executor's authority, not with resolving a family dispute over distribution. The distribution dispute, if it cannot be settled between the heirs directly or through mediation, tends to surface later as a separate proceeding once the executor is ready to actually hand over or divide the Indian assets, and families who anticipate this kind of disagreement early do better engaging a mediator or settling the division in writing before the grant is used to transfer anything, rather than after.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does every foreign will need a fresh probate proceeding in India?

Not if the will has already been probated abroad and there is a valid foreign grant; the route is the lighter ancillary probate procedure under Section 228 rather than a fresh probate from scratch under Section 276. If the will was never probated anywhere, an executor or heir generally has to seek probate or letters of administration in India in the ordinary way instead.

What documents need apostille for an ancillary probate petition in India?

Courts have generally accepted an apostilled copy of the foreign will and the foreign grant of probate or letters of administration as sufficient authentication where the issuing country is party to the Hague Apostille Convention. Where the country is not a Convention member, consular legalisation through the Indian embassy or consulate there is typically needed instead of an apostille.

Which Indian court should an ancillary probate petition be filed in?

Section 270 of the Indian Succession Act points to the court, generally the District Judge, within whose jurisdiction the deceased's property or a part of it is actually located at the time of filing. Where assets sit in more than one state, the family generally files where the most significant Indian asset is, since the grant once issued is not limited to that district alone.

Can the Indian ancillary probate be challenged even if no one contested the will abroad?

Yes. An interested person in India can still object to the ancillary petition on grounds available under Indian law, such as a dispute over who the rightful heirs are or doubts about the authenticity of the documents filed, even where the underlying will went unchallenged in the foreign court. In practice this is uncommon once a genuine, properly authenticated foreign grant is produced, but it is not legally foreclosed.
