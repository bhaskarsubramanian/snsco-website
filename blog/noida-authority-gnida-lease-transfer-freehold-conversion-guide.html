Transferring a Noida or Greater Noida Leasehold Plot: Permission, Charges and Freehold Status

How a Noida or Greater Noida Authority lease is transferred, the no-dues and mortgage permission steps, and the real state of freehold conversion.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Almost all residential, commercial and industrial land in Noida and Greater Noida is allotted on a long lease, typically ninety-nine years, by the Noida Authority or the Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority, not sold outright. That single fact drives everything about how a plot or flat built on it changes hands. A buyer is not simply registering a sale deed between two private parties; the transfer has to be permitted by the Authority, recorded against the original lease, and cleared of dues before it is complete, and the underlying title the buyer ends up with is still a leasehold interest unless the specific land in question has actually been converted to freehold, which, for most Noida and Greater Noida residential land, it has not.

This guide sets out what the Authority's transfer permission actually requires, how mortgage permission and the no-dues certificate fit into a sale, and why freehold conversion is a much narrower and less settled area than popular reporting sometimes suggests. Our guides on Buying Property in Noida and Uttar Pradesh: Stamp Duty, Circle Rates and the Registry Process and on Industrial Plot Allotment or Lease Cancelled by a Development Authority cover the stamping side of a purchase and the risk of losing an allotment altogether, and are worth reading alongside this one.

Why the lease, not a sale deed, is the real foundation of title here

Land in Noida and Greater Noida is allotted under the Uttar Pradesh Industrial Area Development Act, 1976, which empowers the Authority to acquire, develop and allot land within its notified area, generally on lease rather than by outright conveyance. The original allottee executes a lease deed with the Authority setting out the term, the premium and ground rent payable, and conditions on use, construction timelines and transfer. Everything a later buyer acquires, whether directly from the Authority or from a chain of earlier transferees, traces back to that lease deed, and the terms of the original lease continue to bind the property even after several transfers, since a transfer assigns the leasehold interest rather than creating a fresh, unconditional title.

This is why a buyer's lawyer has to read the original lease deed, not just the latest transfer paperwork, before advising on a Noida or Greater Noida purchase. Conditions on construction timelines, permitted use, and the Authority's power to cancel the allotment for default travel with the land regardless of how many hands it has passed through, and a buyer who skips this step can end up holding a leasehold interest that the Authority is already entitled to cancel for a default committed by someone several transfers back.

Transfer permission: the documents and the sequence

Selling or transferring a Noida Authority or Greater Noida Authority leasehold plot or flat requires the Authority's prior written permission; a private sale agreement and registration without that permission does not bind the Authority and leaves the buyer's name unrecorded against the lease. The application for transfer permission is typically supported by the original allotment letter, the lease deed, proof of identity of both parties, and the property's payment and construction history, and the Authority issues a No Dues Certificate confirming that ground rent, lease rent and other charges stand paid before it will proceed with the transfer.

Once the Authority is satisfied, it issues what is generally called a transfer memorandum, which records the new transferee against the lease and is the document a buyer actually relies on to show the Authority recognises the change in ownership. Registration of the sale deed at the sub-registrar's office is a separate step under the Registration Act and the Indian Stamp Act as applicable in Uttar Pradesh, and the sequence buyers get wrong most often is registering the deed first and only then approaching the Authority; doing it the other way round, permission and transfer memorandum first, then registration, avoids a mismatch between what the registry shows and what the Authority's own records recognise.

Transfer charges, and why the rate matters to both sides

The Authority levies a transfer charge, calculated as a percentage of the premium or the prevailing value of the property, as a condition of granting permission, and this charge has changed over time; a 2021 reduction cut the residential transfer charge roughly in half and separately reduced the rate for institutional properties, according to contemporaneous reporting on the revision. Because this rate is set by the Authority's own policy and has moved before, a buyer or seller should get the current applicable rate confirmed directly with the Authority or through the transfer application process itself rather than rely on a figure quoted from an older transaction or an online article.

This charge is usually factored into the negotiation between buyer and seller, and who actually bears it is a matter of contract between the parties rather than something the Authority dictates, so this is worth settling explicitly in the agreement to sell before either side assumes the other will pay it. Where a chain of unregistered transfers has built up over the years without each one being formalised with the Authority, the cumulative transfer charge and paperwork needed to regularise the whole chain in one go can be substantial, which is a common reason older Noida flats take longer to sell than their listing price would suggest.

Mortgage permission and what a lender actually needs

Because the underlying land is leasehold, a bank or housing finance company financing a purchase needs the Authority's permission to create a mortgage over the leasehold interest, separate from the transfer permission discussed above. This is typically processed alongside the loan documentation, and the lender's panel lawyer will usually insist on seeing the Authority's mortgage permission, and sometimes a joint mortgage permission where more than one lender or a co-applicant structure is involved, before disbursing the loan. A buyer financing a purchase should flag early in the process that the property is leasehold Authority land, since this step adds time to a home loan sanction compared with freehold property elsewhere, and a lender unfamiliar with the Authority's process can otherwise cause unnecessary delay at the disbursement stage.

A default on the underlying lease conditions, unpaid ground rent or an unauthorised construction, for example, can complicate a mortgage even after it has been created, since the Authority's power to resume the land for breach of lease conditions is not automatically subordinate to a lender's mortgage. Buyers and lenders alike should check the lease compliance history, not just the title documents, before treating an Authority-permitted mortgage as fully secured.

What cancellation of the underlying allotment means for a buyer

The Authority's power to cancel an allotment or resume the land for breach of lease conditions, non-payment of dues, or leaving land unutilised beyond the period the lease or a later amendment allows, sits in the background of every leasehold transfer. A 2022 amendment to the Uttar Pradesh Industrial Area Development Act is understood to have introduced a mechanism under which an allotment and lease on certain older grants is treated as cancelled where the land was not put to use within a specified timeframe, subject to prior notice, with the land then vesting back in the Authority. The Allahabad High Court and the Supreme Court have both upheld cancellations of this kind in cases involving unutilised or defaulted industrial plots, treating the Authority's power as a legitimate response to land being held idle rather than developed.

A buyer taking a transfer of an existing leasehold interest inherits the risk of whatever compliance history sits behind that lease, which is precisely why checking for pending notices, show-cause proceedings, or disputed dues with the Authority is as important as checking the registry records. Our guide on industrial plot allotment and lease cancellation by a development authority sets out the grounds and the remedies available to a sitting allottee in more detail.

Freehold conversion: in-principle approval, and why it has not translated into a general scheme

Noida Authority gave in-principle approval to converting leasehold land to freehold as far back as 2018, subject to the Uttar Pradesh government's own concurrence, and that proposal generated considerable coverage at the time. What the available record does not show is a general, Authority-wide freehold conversion scheme that has actually come into effect for ordinary residential leasehold plots and flats since then; the leasehold structure, with its typical ninety-nine-year term, continues to be described as the standing position for land allotted by the Noida Authority, the Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority and the Yamuna Expressway Industrial Development Authority.

Specific, narrower freehold conversions do happen from time to time under policies the Authority notifies for particular categories or schemes, and residents' welfare associations have periodically pressed the Authority for a freehold option without an additional conversion charge being added on top of what they already paid. Because the position shifts with each notification and has not settled into one consistent, generally available scheme, a buyer or current leaseholder who wants to know whether a specific plot can be converted to freehold should get the Authority's current, written policy for that category of land confirmed before relying on anything reported in the press, rather than assume freehold status is now available simply because it was proposed several years ago.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my Noida flat without the Authority's permission if the lease deed is already in my name?

No. Even where the lease has already been transferred into your name once, every further transfer needs the Authority's prior permission and a fresh no-dues certificate. A sale registered at the sub-registrar's office without that permission does not get recorded against the lease and leaves the buyer's position unclear in the Authority's own records.

Is Noida or Greater Noida leasehold land now freehold?

Not as a general rule. The Noida Authority gave in-principle approval to a freehold conversion proposal in 2018, but the leasehold structure, typically a ninety-nine-year lease, remains the standing position for most residential and other plots. Specific conversions exist under particular schemes, so the current policy for a given plot needs to be confirmed directly with the Authority.

What happens if earlier transfers in the chain were never formalised with the Authority?

The whole unformalised chain usually has to be regularised with the Authority before a further sale can be permitted, which can mean paying transfer charges and furnishing documents for each unrecorded step, not just the most recent one. This is a common reason older resale flats in Noida take longer to close than the listing suggests.

Can the Authority cancel my lease even if I bought the plot from someone else, not directly from the Authority?

Yes. A transfer assigns the leasehold interest subject to the existing lease conditions, so a default in the compliance history, whether it occurred before or after your purchase, can expose the current holder to cancellation or resumption proceedings. Checking for pending notices or disputes with the Authority before buying is as important as checking the title chain.

Do I need the Authority's permission to mortgage a Noida leasehold flat to a bank?

Yes, a mortgage over leasehold Authority land needs separate mortgage permission from the Authority, in addition to the transfer permission for the sale itself. Lenders unfamiliar with this step sometimes cause delay at the loan disbursement stage, so it helps to flag the leasehold status early in the loan process.
