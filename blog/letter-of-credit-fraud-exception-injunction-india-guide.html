Letters of Credit in International Trade: When an Indian Court Will Stop Payment

How a documentary letter of credit works under Indian law, and the narrow fraud and injustice exceptions courts apply before restraining payment on one.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 16 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A letter of credit is a bank's own promise to pay, issued at the request of a buyer, that is independent of whatever dispute the buyer and seller may be having over the underlying contract. That independence is the entire point of the instrument, because a seller who has shipped goods wants certainty of payment on presenting the right documents, not a payment that depends on the buyer being satisfied with the goods. Indian courts have repeatedly held that this independence should not be disturbed except in narrow, well-defined situations, and understanding those situations matters as much to a foreign seller relying on an Indian buyer's letter of credit as it does to an Indian company trying to stop a payment it believes is wrong.

This guide sets out how that independence principle works under Indian law, the two recognised exceptions a court will act on, and why Indian courts are reluctant to grant an injunction against a letter of credit or a bank guarantee even when the underlying commercial dispute looks genuine.

Why a letter of credit is treated as independent of the underlying contract

A documentary letter of credit involves at least three separate contracts: the sale contract between buyer and seller, the contract between the buyer and the issuing bank under which the bank agrees to open the credit, and the credit itself, which is the issuing bank's undertaking to the seller or its bank to pay against specified documents. Indian courts have consistently treated these as autonomous of each other, meaning the issuing bank's obligation to pay against conforming documents does not depend on whether the buyer is happy with the goods, whether there is a dispute over quality or quantity, or whether the buyer alleges a breach of the sale contract. The bank's duty is to examine the documents presented, not to adjudicate the underlying commercial dispute between its customer and the seller.

This is usually described, in Indian case law and in international banking practice, as the autonomy or independence principle, and it is reinforced in practice by the fact that most letters of credit used in India, as elsewhere, incorporate the International Chamber of Commerce's uniform rules for documentary credits by reference, which embed the same separation between the credit and the underlying sale. A buyer who wants to challenge the seller over the goods has to do so through an ordinary damages claim or arbitration under the sale contract; it is not a basis, by itself, to stop the bank from honouring a credit against documents that match what the credit calls for.

The two exceptions Indian courts actually apply

The leading Supreme Court authority on this, U.P. State Sugar Corporation v. Sumac International Limited, decided in 1996 and reported at (1997) 1 SCC 568, set out two situations in which a court may restrain payment under a letter of credit or a bank guarantee despite the autonomy principle. The first is fraud of an egregious nature that would vitiate the very foundation of the credit or guarantee, known to the bank at the time payment is sought. The second is a case of irretrievable injustice, meaning payment would cause harm that could never realistically be reversed or compensated later, even if the party resisting payment eventually won the underlying dispute.

The Supreme Court has returned to and applied this framework in later decisions, including Vinitec Electronics Private Limited v. HCL Infosystems Limited in 2007, which confirmed that these remain the only two grounds on which an injunction can be granted against payment under a letter of credit or bank guarantee. The fraud has to be that of the beneficiary, the party seeking payment, not merely an allegation that the underlying contract was breached or that the goods were defective; a buyer's unhappiness with what it received is not fraud in this sense, however genuine the grievance may be commercially. Courts have made this point repeatedly because it is the allegation buyers reach for most often when a shipment arrives and turns out to be less than what they expected.

Why courts set the fraud bar so high

The Supreme Court in Federal Bank Ltd. v. V.M. Jog Engineering Ltd., decided in 2000, and again in Himadri Chemicals Industries Ltd. v. Coal Tar Refining Company, decided in 2007, held that an injunction was not justified where there was no allegation, or no evidence, of fraud known to the paying or negotiating bank. The reasoning across these decisions is consistent: if courts allowed ordinary contractual disputes to freeze payment under a letter of credit, the instrument would lose the certainty that makes it useful in the first place, and international and domestic trade would become harder to finance, because sellers would no longer be able to rely on a bank's independent promise to pay.

Courts also require clear evidence both of the fraud itself and of the bank's knowledge of it at the relevant time, rather than mere suspicion or an unproven allegation raised for the first time when payment is about to fall due. A party seeking an injunction typically has to move quickly, with documentary evidence ready, because courts are alert to the possibility that an injunction application is really an attempt to delay a payment the applicant should otherwise be making.

What a seller relying on an Indian buyer's credit should do to protect the exception from being misused against it

A seller presenting documents under a letter of credit from an Indian bank should make sure the documents strictly match what the credit calls for, because banks are entitled to refuse payment on a discrepancy that has nothing to do with fraud, and a buyer who wants to delay payment will often seize on a documentary discrepancy rather than attempt the much harder route of alleging fraud in court. Keeping shipping, inspection and compliance records in order before presentation reduces both the risk of a genuine discrepancy and the room for a buyer to manufacture a dispute around one.

Where an Indian buyer does go to court seeking an injunction against the credit, the beneficiary, usually a foreign seller, is entitled to appear and contest the application, and Indian courts generally expect the party seeking the injunction to disclose the application quickly to the beneficiary rather than obtain an order unilaterally. A seller who learns of such proceedings should act promptly through local counsel, because an injunction obtained even temporarily can disrupt payment timelines and knock-on obligations the seller has to its own suppliers or financiers, and because an ex parte order that goes unchallenged for even a short period can harden into a position the seller finds harder to unwind later.

How this differs from, and overlaps with, bank guarantees

Indian courts apply essentially the same two-exception framework to bank guarantees as they do to letters of credit, which is why the same cases are cited for both instruments. The practical difference is in how each is usually used: a letter of credit is the primary payment mechanism in a sale transaction, paid against shipping and commercial documents, while a bank guarantee is typically a secondary security, called upon only if the underlying party defaults on a separate obligation, such as a performance guarantee or an advance payment guarantee. Because both instruments serve the same certainty-of-payment function for the beneficiary, courts have been equally reluctant to interfere with either on anything short of the fraud or irretrievable injustice grounds.

A party structuring cross-border trade with India should treat the choice between a letter of credit and a bank guarantee, and the precise wording of either, as something to get right at the contract stage rather than something to fix through litigation later, because the independence principle that protects a seller's right to be paid applies just as firmly against a seller trying to claim under an instrument that does not, on its own wording, cover the situation that has arisen. Badly drafted trigger conditions in a performance guarantee, for instance, have cost beneficiaries their claim in reported cases even where the underlying default was never seriously disputed, simply because the guarantee's own wording did not match what had actually happened.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an Indian buyer stop a letter of credit payment simply because the goods were defective?

No. A dispute over the quality or condition of goods is a matter for the underlying sale contract, pursued through a damages claim or arbitration, not a ground to restrain the bank from paying against conforming documents. Indian courts have repeatedly held that ordinary contractual disputes do not meet the fraud or irretrievable injustice threshold.

What counts as fraud serious enough to stop a letter of credit payment?

The fraud must be of an egregious nature, committed by the beneficiary, and known to the bank, striking at the very foundation of the credit itself, such as forged or entirely fictitious shipping documents. A buyer's belief that it was cheated commercially, without clear evidence reaching this level, will not meet the test.

Does an Indian court require notice to the seller before granting an injunction against a letter of credit?

Courts generally expect prompt disclosure to the beneficiary and are cautious about unilateral orders, given how disruptive an injunction can be to international trade financing. A foreign seller who learns of such proceedings should engage local counsel quickly to contest the application on the merits.

Is a bank guarantee treated the same way as a letter of credit by Indian courts?

Yes, in substance. Indian courts apply the same fraud and irretrievable injustice exceptions to both instruments, and the leading Supreme Court decisions on bank guarantees and letters of credit are routinely cited together for this reason, even though the two instruments are typically used at different stages of a transaction.
