An Executor's Duties and Personal Liabilities: What Agreeing to Serve Actually Means

Being named executor of a Will carries real legal duties and potential personal liability: this guide explains what an executor must do, and where things can go wrong.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Agreeing to Be an Executor Is Agreeing to a Job, Not an Honour

Being named executor in a friend's or relative's Will is often treated as a mark of trust, and it is, but it is also a genuine legal appointment carrying defined duties, real time commitment, and in some circumstances personal financial exposure if those duties are mishandled. Anyone named as executor, and anyone drafting a Will who is choosing who to name, should understand what the role actually involves before assuming it is a purely honorary position.

The Executor's Core Duties

An executor's fundamental job is to collect and protect the deceased's assets, pay the deceased's genuine debts and liabilities (including funeral expenses and applicable taxes), and then distribute what remains to the beneficiaries strictly according to the terms of the Will. This involves, in practice, locating and securing the deceased's assets and property; identifying and verifying outstanding debts, whether to lenders, vendors, or the tax authorities; obtaining probate of the Will (where required, or otherwise establishing the executor's authority to act) from the appropriate court; and keeping proper accounts of everything received and paid out during the administration of the estate, since beneficiaries are entitled to a proper accounting.

Where Personal Liability Can Arise

An executor who administers the estate carefully and in good faith generally has little to fear. Personal liability tends to arise from specific missteps: distributing estate assets to beneficiaries before all legitimate debts and claims against the estate have been identified and paid, which can leave the executor personally liable to an unpaid creditor who later comes forward; failing to pay taxes properly due from the estate, which can expose the executor to penalties in their personal capacity in some circumstances; mixing estate funds with the executor's own personal funds, which invites both suspicion and genuine accounting difficulty; and simply acting outside the powers the Will actually grants, or contrary to its express terms, which can expose the executor to a claim by an aggrieved beneficiary for the resulting loss.

An Executor Doesn't Have to Act Alone, or Even Accept the Role

A person named as executor in a Will is not legally compelled to accept the appointment: a nominated executor can formally renounce the role, typically before undertaking any significant acts of administration, after which the estate proceeds as though that person had not been named (often with another named executor, or an administrator appointed by the court, stepping in instead). An executor who does accept the role can, and often should, engage professional help, a chartered accountant for tax matters, a lawyer for probate and any contested claims, since doing so competently is not a sign of failure but ordinary prudence, particularly for a larger or more complicated estate.

Multiple Executors and Disagreements Between Them

Wills sometimes name more than one executor, whether to share the workload, to provide a check on any single executor's decisions, or simply because the testator wanted to honour more than one relative or friend. Where executors disagree on a significant decision, they generally need to act jointly for major steps such as selling estate property or making distributions, which can create real practical friction if the relationship between the named executors is not a good one: a point testators should weigh carefully when choosing whether to name a single executor, co-executors, or a primary executor with a named alternate.

Practical Advice for Anyone Named as Executor

Keep meticulous, contemporaneous records of every asset identified, every debt paid, and every distribution made. Do not distribute estate assets to beneficiaries until confident that all legitimate claims against the estate, including tax liabilities, have been identified and addressed, since distributions made too early are the single most common source of an executor's personal exposure. And where the estate is at all complex, or a dispute among beneficiaries seems likely, engage professional advice early rather than after a problem has already crystallised.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an executor personally liable for a deceased person's debts?

Not generally out of the executor's own pocket for debts that exceed the estate's assets, but an executor can become personally liable where they distribute estate assets before properly identifying and paying legitimate debts, leaving an unpaid creditor with a valid claim against the executor personally.

Can a person refuse to act as executor even if named in a Will?

Yes. A nominated executor can formally renounce the role, generally before undertaking significant acts of administration, after which another named executor or a court-appointed administrator typically steps in instead.

What is the biggest practical risk for an executor?

Distributing estate assets to beneficiaries before confirming that all legitimate debts, claims and tax liabilities against the estate have been identified and paid: this is the most common source of personal liability for executors.

Should an executor keep records of the estate administration?

Yes: meticulous, contemporaneous records of assets collected, debts paid and distributions made are essential, since beneficiaries are entitled to a proper accounting and good records are the executor's best protection if that accounting is ever challenged.

Can multiple executors named in the same Will disagree?

They can, and since major decisions generally require the executors to act jointly, disagreement between co-executors can create real practical difficulty: a factor worth weighing when a testator decides whether to name one executor, co-executors, or a primary executor with a named alternate.
