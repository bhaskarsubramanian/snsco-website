Foreign Parent ESOPs: What an Indian Employee Must Report

An Indian employee holding foreign parent stock options faces FEMA filing by the employer, perquisite tax on exercise, and yearly Schedule FA disclosure.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 14 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

When a multinational extends its employee stock option plan to staff of its Indian subsidiary, the shares the employee eventually holds are shares of a foreign company, not an Indian one. That single fact pulls the arrangement out of ordinary domestic ESOP rules and into the Foreign Exchange Management Act framework for outbound investment, on top of the usual income tax on the perquisite. Most of the actual filing burden sits with the Indian employer rather than the employee, but the employee carries the tax and disclosure exposure personally.

This guide sets out which FEMA route the shares fall under, who files what and by when, how the shares are taxed on exercise and later sale, and the Black Money Act risk that catches people who treat the foreign shares as something to deal with only when they are sold. As on 14 November 2026.

Why this is not an ordinary domestic ESOP

A resident Indian acquiring shares of a foreign company, for any reason, is making an overseas investment, and overseas investment by residents is regulated under FEMA just as inbound foreign investment is. The current framework is the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, Regulations and Directions 2022, notified by the Reserve Bank of India on 22 August 2022, which replaced the older and more fragmented overseas direct investment regime.

Under that framework, an employee who receives stock options from a foreign parent and later exercises them is treated differently depending on how much of the foreign company the employee ends up holding, not on the fact that the shares came through an employment relationship. That distinction decides which filing route applies and, in most cases, who has to make it. A group that already runs an ESOP in its home jurisdiction and simply extends the same plan document to Indian staff without adjusting for this is usually the one that discovers the FEMA angle only once an employee asks why a filing has not been made.

Which FEMA route the shares fall under

The 2022 framework splits outbound equity holdings into overseas portfolio investment, where the resident holds less than 10% of the foreign company's paid-up equity and has no control over it, and overseas direct investment, where the holding reaches 10% or more or carries control. An individual employee's shareholding in a large multinational parent, built up through an option grant, will almost always sit well under the 10% line, so the acquisition is classified as overseas portfolio investment rather than overseas direct investment.

This classification matters because OPI carries lighter obligations than ODI, which involves a more detailed approval and reporting trail intended for companies and funds making strategic overseas investments, not individual employees exercising options. An employee should not assume the ODI paperwork applies just because the shares sit in a corporate structure abroad; the test is the size and nature of the individual's own holding. The position can change for a senior executive who is also granted options directly tied to a board seat or a founder-level stake in a smaller foreign group company, where the 10% or control line is realistically within reach, so each grant is worth checking on its own facts rather than assuming the whole workforce sits in the same OPI bucket.

Who actually files with the RBI, and when

For OPI arising from an ESOP, the reporting obligation under the 2022 framework falls on the Indian employer, not on the individual employee. The employer reports the acquisition to the Reserve Bank of India through its own authorised dealer bank, using Form OPI, within 60 days of the end of the half-year in which the shares were acquired, with the two half-years running to 31 March and 30 September. An employee exercising options in, say, June of a given year would expect the employer's reporting to be completed by the end of November of that year.

This is a change from the position before 2022, when a separate annual Form ESOP return to the RBI covered this kind of grant. That older form is no longer the route for shares flowing from a foreign parent to Indian employees; Form ESOP today is used for the reverse direction, where an Indian company grants options to its own non-resident employees, filed on the FIRMS portal within 30 days of the relevant corporate action. An employee should still confirm with the employer's finance or company secretarial team that the Form OPI filing has actually been made for each tranche exercised, since the consequence of a missed filing falls on the company but the employee is the one who will need a clean compliance history if the shares are ever sold and the proceeds brought back to India.

How the shares are taxed on exercise

Income tax treats a foreign parent's ESOP the same way it treats a domestic one for the basic charge. Section 17(2)(vi) of the Income-tax Act brings the value of a specified security or sweat equity share, allotted or transferred directly or indirectly by the employer or a former employer, into the definition of a perquisite. The value taxed as salary income is the difference between the fair market value of the shares on the date the option is exercised and the price the employee actually paid to exercise it, and the employer is expected to account for this through payroll withholding in the same way it would for a domestic grant.

The practical complication is valuation. An Indian employer cannot always get a same-day fair market value for a foreign, often unlisted, parent company's shares as easily as it can for its own shares, so groups typically build a valuation process into the ESOP administration itself, whether that is a listed share price on exercise date for a public parent or a formal valuation certificate for a private one. An employee who changes jobs or leaves the group before this is settled should not assume the tax question disappears; the perquisite is taxed in the year of exercise regardless of subsequent employment status.

What happens on sale, and the Schedule FA trap

A later sale of the foreign shares is taxed separately, as a capital gain, computed from the fair market value already taxed as perquisite at exercise, not from the original exercise price. Because the shares sit outside India, this part of the arrangement is easy to lose track of between exercise and eventual sale, especially where the employee holds the shares for several years through a foreign brokerage account set up by the employer's plan administrator.

The part that catches people out is not the capital gains computation but the annual disclosure obligation. A resident and ordinarily resident taxpayer must report foreign assets, including these shares, in Schedule FA of the income tax return every year the holding exists, whether or not the shares produced any income that year, with any dividend income going into Schedule FSI and any foreign tax credit claimed through Form 67. This is a disclosure requirement independent of the tax computation, and it applies from the year of acquisition through to the year of disposal, not just in the year the shares are sold.

The Black Money Act risk, and how real it is

Failing to disclose foreign assets in Schedule FA exposes the taxpayer to a penalty under Section 43 of the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) Act 2015, commonly reported at up to Rs 10 lakh for each year of non-disclosure, separate from and in addition to any income tax otherwise due. This has caught employees who paid their perquisite tax correctly through payroll but simply never put the foreign shares into Schedule FA, treating the tax deduction at source as the end of their obligation.

The penalty is not automatic in every case. In ACIT v Rohit Krishna, the Mumbai bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal examined a Section 43 penalty imposed over undisclosed foreign ESOP shares and treated the penalty as a matter for the assessing officer's discretion rather than something that follows mechanically from a missed disclosure, particularly where the taxpayer's underlying tax position was not otherwise in question. That is a reason for caution, not for treating the disclosure as optional; an employee should still file Schedule FA correctly every year and treat a tribunal's discretion to waive a penalty as a fallback, not a plan.

What the Indian employer should set up in advance

A multinational extending its ESOP into India should decide, before the first grant to Indian staff, who tracks the OPI classification for each employee, who files Form OPI with the authorised dealer bank each half-year, and who is responsible for getting a usable fair market value on each exercise date so payroll withholding is accurate. Leaving this to be worked out after the first employee exercises options tends to produce exactly the kind of gap, a late or missed FEMA filing, or a perquisite value calculated on guesswork, that surfaces later during an audit or when the employee tries to repatriate sale proceeds.

Employees, for their part, are better served by keeping their own simple record of grant dates, exercise dates, fair market values used for payroll tax, and confirmation from the employer that each FEMA filing was made, rather than relying entirely on the employer's systems years later when the shares are finally sold and the full history needs to be reconstructed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the employee personally have to file anything with the RBI?

For the standard case of an Indian employee receiving overseas portfolio investment shares through a foreign parent's ESOP, the Form OPI filing obligation sits with the Indian employer through its authorised dealer bank, not with the employee individually. The employee's own ongoing obligation is the annual Schedule FA disclosure in the income tax return, not a separate RBI filing.

Is there a cap on how much an employee can pay to exercise foreign stock options?

Outbound investment by a resident individual, including amounts paid to exercise foreign ESOPs, is generally expected to stay within the Liberalised Remittance Scheme ceiling, reported at USD 250,000 per financial year for an individual's overseas investment and remittances taken together. An employee exercising a large option grant should check this against other remittances made in the same year.

What happens if the employee forgets to disclose the shares in Schedule FA?

The exposure is a penalty under Section 43 of the Black Money Act, commonly cited at up to Rs 10 lakh for each year of non-disclosure, on top of any income tax otherwise due. Tribunals have in some cases treated this penalty as discretionary rather than automatic, but the safer course is always to disclose correctly rather than rely on that discretion.

Does it matter whether the foreign parent is a listed or a private company?

It affects valuation more than the legal route. A listed parent gives a ready market price for perquisite valuation and for later capital gains computation. A private parent needs a formal fair market valuation at each exercise date, which the employer should arrange in advance so payroll tax is not calculated on an estimate.

Does the position change if the employee later becomes a non-resident?

The perquisite tax at exercise and any Schedule FA disclosure obligations that arose while the employee was resident and ordinarily resident do not disappear on a later move abroad. Shares acquired while resident must still be accounted for, and the employee's residential status at the time of each later event, including any sale, governs how that specific event is taxed.
