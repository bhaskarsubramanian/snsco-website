A POSH Complaint Was Settled Through Conciliation: Can the Employer Still Take Disciplinary Action

A December 2025 Gauhati High Court ruling held that a POSH conciliation settlement bars further Internal Committee inquiry, not separate discipline.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An employer whose Internal Committee reaches a conciliation settlement under Section 10 of the POSH Act often assumes the matter is closed for every purpose once that settlement is recorded, including any separate departmental action against the employee accused of harassment. A December 2025 Gauhati High Court ruling in Airports Authority of India v. Praveen VS says that assumption goes further than the statute actually requires.

This guide works through what Section 10(4) of the Act actually bars once a conciliation settlement is reached, what the Gauhati High Court held it does not bar, and what this means for an employer's own disciplinary process running alongside, or after, a POSH conciliation.

What Section 10 conciliation is, and its one hard limit

Section 10 lets an Internal Committee attempt conciliation between the complainant and the respondent before initiating a formal inquiry, but only if the complainant herself asks for it; the Committee cannot push conciliation on a reluctant complainant, and the section specifically bars any monetary settlement as the basis of that conciliation. Once a settlement is reached and recorded on these terms, Section 10(4) states that no further inquiry shall be conducted by the Internal Committee or the Local Committee.

Read on its own, that line could be taken to mean the entire matter is closed once conciliation succeeds, which is exactly the reading the single judge in the Airports Authority of India case gave it at first instance, quashing the employer's subsequent departmental proceedings against the employee on the basis that Section 10(4) barred any further action once the conciliation settlement was recorded.

The Gauhati High Court's division bench: a narrower reading

On appeal, a division bench of the Gauhati High Court, deciding Airports Authority of India & Ors. v. Praveen VS on 9 December 2025, partially allowed the employer's appeal and held that Section 10(4) bars only further inquiry by the Internal Committee or Local Committee itself once a conciliation settlement is reached; it does not extinguish the employer's own, separate power to take disciplinary action against the employee under its own service rules.

The bench reasoned that reading Section 10(4) as a blanket bar on any further action at all would work against the POSH Act's underlying purpose of ensuring a safe workplace, since an employer could otherwise find itself unable to act on genuinely serious misconduct merely because the complainant, often under pressure to move on quickly, agreed to a non-monetary conciliation rather than press through a full inquiry. At the same time, the court was careful to preserve the narrower point Section 10(4) does protect: the Internal Committee itself cannot reopen or continue its own inquiry once a valid conciliation settlement is in place.

The condition that actually did the work in this case

Commentary on the ruling has flagged that the employer's own service rules in this case did not treat the POSH Internal Committee's findings or settlement as automatically binding on, or a substitute for, the employer's separate disciplinary process; the two tracks were structurally independent of each other under the airport authority's own rules. Where an employer's service rules do expressly tie disciplinary outcomes to the Internal Committee's findings, or treat a POSH settlement as conclusively disposing of the disciplinary question as well, the analysis could come out differently, since the court was interpreting the statute alongside this particular employer's own rules rather than announcing an outcome that applies regardless of how any given employer has structured its own disciplinary framework.

This is the detail an employer should actually check before assuming the Gauhati ruling gives it an unqualified green light to proceed with discipline after any POSH conciliation: whether its own service rules or standing orders already fold POSH outcomes into the disciplinary process in a way that could be read as making the conciliation settlement determinative there too.

Why this still needs to be treated with some caution

The ruling is from a division bench of a High Court, and commentary on it has specifically flagged that the position should be approached carefully until the Supreme Court has had an opportunity to settle the question definitively, since the single judge's contrary reading at first instance shows that reasonable disagreement on how far Section 10(4) extends already exists at the judicial level. An employer in a different state is not bound by a Gauhati High Court ruling in the way it would be by a Supreme Court decision, though the reasoning is likely to be cited persuasively elsewhere until a conflicting view emerges or the Supreme Court weighs in.

An employer relying on this ruling to pursue disciplinary action after a POSH conciliation should also be alert to natural justice requirements in the separate disciplinary proceeding itself; the Gauhati High Court's holding addresses whether Section 10(4) bars the proceeding at all, not whether any particular disciplinary process that follows was conducted fairly, which remains a separate question an employer still has to get right on its own facts.

What an employer should actually do with a conciliation settlement going forward

Before relying on a conciliation settlement to close a POSH matter entirely, check what the employer's own service rules say about the relationship between POSH outcomes and the separate disciplinary process, since that relationship, not the bare text of Section 10(4) alone, is what the Gauhati High Court treated as doing much of the real work in its reasoning.

Where disciplinary action is genuinely warranted on facts independent of what the Internal Committee itself found, document that independence clearly: the disciplinary charge sheet and process should be framed around the employer's own service rules and its own assessment of the underlying conduct, rather than simply restating the Internal Committee's conciliation record as if it were a finding the employer is now acting on. Treating the two processes as genuinely separate, both procedurally and in how the case for discipline is actually framed, is the safest way to use the Gauhati High Court's reasoning rather than overreach it.

An employer should also review its own standing orders and certified service rules now, rather than waiting for the next POSH complaint to find out how those rules actually interact with an Internal Committee outcome. Where the rules are silent or ambiguous on the point, as the airport authority's were, an employer retains more room to run the two processes independently; where the rules already fold POSH findings into the disciplinary framework, amending them deliberately, with proper consultation, is a more defensible path than simply assuming a favourable reading will be available if the question is ever litigated.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a POSH conciliation settlement stop the employer from taking any further action against the employee?

According to a December 2025 Gauhati High Court ruling, no. Section 10(4) of the POSH Act bars further inquiry by the Internal Committee or Local Committee itself once a conciliation settlement is reached, but it does not bar the employer from pursuing separate disciplinary action under its own service rules.

Can the Internal Committee reopen its own inquiry after a conciliation settlement?

No. That is the one thing Section 10(4) clearly bars: once a valid conciliation settlement is recorded, the Internal Committee or Local Committee cannot conduct further inquiry into the same complaint. The Gauhati High Court's ruling preserved this specific limit while reading the wider bar narrowly.

Does this ruling apply to every employer regardless of its own service rules?

Not necessarily. Commentary on the ruling notes that the employer's own service rules in this case did not treat the Internal Committee's outcome as binding on the disciplinary process. Where an employer's rules do tie the two together, the analysis of whether separate discipline can proceed could differ.

Is the Gauhati High Court's ruling binding across India?

No. It is a High Court division bench ruling, not a Supreme Court decision, and the single judge in the same case had taken a contrary view at first instance. Commentary advises treating the position with some caution until the Supreme Court addresses the question, though the reasoning may be cited persuasively elsewhere.

What should an employer do before disciplining an employee after a POSH settlement?

Check whether its own service rules link disciplinary outcomes to the Internal Committee's findings, and if proceeding, frame the disciplinary case around the employer's own independent assessment of the conduct and its own rules, rather than simply treating the conciliation record as the basis for the disciplinary charge.
