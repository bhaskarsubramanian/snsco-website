Is Probate Still Mandatory in India? What the Repeal of Section 213 Actually Changed

What the 2025 repeal of Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act changes for executors and legatees, and where the old probate rule still applies.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 16 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

For close to a century, a legatee under a will who wanted to enforce rights in a Mumbai, Chennai or Kolkata court, or whose will concerned property within the original civil jurisdiction of those High Courts, could not simply walk in and claim under the will. Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 stood in the way, requiring probate or letters of administration before any right as an executor or legatee could be established in court. That bar applied to Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists and Parsis within its territorial scope; Christians and Muslims were governed by different rules entirely and this specific restriction did not touch them in the same way.

The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 omitted Section 213 altogether. A Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh High Court ruling has already applied the change to set aside a mutation rejection that rested solely on the absence of probate. This guide sets out what changed, what has not changed, and where a family dealing with an inherited estate still needs to be careful.

What Section 213 used to require

Before the 2025 amendment, Section 213(1) barred a court from recognising a right as executor or legatee under a will unless probate of the will, or letters of administration with the will annexed, had first been obtained from a court of competent jurisdiction. The bar was not about the will's validity in the abstract; a perfectly genuine will simply could not be acted upon in litigation, or relied upon to resist another party's claim in court, until it had gone through probate.

The territorial and community limits mattered as much as the rule itself. The requirement applied where the will was made by a Hindu, Sikh, Jain, Buddhist or Parsi and either was executed within the local limits of the ordinary original civil jurisdiction of the Calcutta, Madras or Bombay High Courts, or related to immovable property situated within those limits. A will executed in, say, Pune concerning agricultural land in Maharashtra outside that specific jurisdiction fell outside Section 213's bar even before the 2025 change, which is why practitioners outside the three metros sometimes found the rule less relevant to their daily practice than those operating within it.

What the Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 did

The Second Schedule to the Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 directs that Section 213 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 shall be omitted, with a consequential change removing the reference to Section 213 carried in Section 3(1) of the principal Act. Reports differ slightly on the precise notification timeline, with one tracking presidential assent to around 20 December 2025, but the direction itself, that the section stands omitted, is consistent across the commentary.

The practical effect is that a legatee or executor can now found a claim in court on an unprobated will, including in the three metros where the old bar used to apply, without first obtaining probate or letters of administration. This does not mean probate has become pointless. It means probate is no longer a precondition to litigating under the will; a party can still choose to obtain probate for other reasons, chiefly because a probate decree is a judgment in rem, binding on the whole world once granted, which an unprobated will founding a bare civil claim is not.

The savings clause and pending matters

The Repealing and Amending Act carries a general savings provision preserving rights, obligations and liabilities that had already accrued before the change took effect. This matters for anyone mid-litigation: a suit already dismissed, or a right already lost, for want of probate before the amendment took effect is not automatically revived by the later change in law, and the savings clause needs to be checked against the specific procedural posture of any pending matter rather than assumed to apply uniformly.

A 2026 Kerala High Court ruling, Premila Surendran v Prasanna Chandran, took up exactly this question for a Hindu will executed before the 2025 amendment over property in Kannur, with one side arguing probate was mandatory on those facts; the reported outcome of that appeal does not make clear whether the court accepted that argument, so the point should be checked against the full judgment rather than assumed either way. What is clear is that the timing of execution and the stage of the underlying dispute both matter. A family with a will executed years ago, where a probate requirement attached under the old law, should not assume the 2025 repeal retroactively cures a defect in title or revives a claim that had already failed under the earlier regime.

Why probate still matters even though it is no longer compulsory

A probate decree operates against the whole world, not just the parties before the court. For high value estates, disputed families, or situations where a bank, a land registry, or a co-owner might later question the legatee's title, a probate decree remains the strongest document available, precisely because it cannot be reopened by someone who was not a party the way an ordinary civil decree can be collaterally attacked.

Banks and financial institutions, land registries, and corporate share transfer agents are all free to set their own internal documentation requirements independent of what Section 213 required in court. A legatee who no longer needs probate to file a suit may still find that a bank will not release a large fixed deposit, or a land registry will not process a mutation, without either probate or a succession certificate or legal heir certificate, depending on the asset class and the state's own revenue practice. The 2025 change affects what a court requires before entertaining a claim; it does not bind every non-judicial authority a family will actually need to deal with.

How the J&K&L High Court applied the change

In the ruling applying the new position, a Tehsildar had rejected mutation of inheritance in favour of a legatee solely because the will had not been probated. The High Court set aside that rejection, holding that since Section 213 had been omitted, obtaining probate was no longer a mandatory precondition to claiming rights under a will, and directed the revenue authority to reconsider the mutation application on its merits rather than reject it on the probate ground alone.

The decision is useful precisely because it shows the change reaching beyond civil suits in the formal sense into revenue and mutation proceedings as well, though a revenue officer is still entitled to examine whether the will itself is genuine and validly executed under Sections 59 and 63 of the Indian Succession Act. Removing the probate precondition did not remove the underlying requirement that a will actually be proved when its genuineness is disputed; it removed only the separate hurdle of a prior probate decree.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the 2025 change mean I never need to go to court over a will anymore?

No. It removes probate as a mandatory precondition to litigating under a will in the areas where Section 213 used to apply. If the will's genuineness, execution, or the testator's capacity is disputed, the matter can still end up in court, whether through a probate petition filed voluntarily or through the underlying civil claim itself.

I have a will executed in 2019 concerning property in Mumbai. Does the repeal help me now?

The repeal applies going forward and the courts are still working out exactly how the savings clause interacts with wills executed, or disputes that arose, before the amendment took effect. A 2026 Kerala High Court ruling took up this exact question for a pre-amendment will, but the reported outcome does not confirm how the court resolved it, so this needs case-specific advice rather than a blanket assumption that the repeal cures every earlier situation.

If probate is not mandatory, should I still get one for a high value estate?

Generally yes, for a disputed or high value estate. A probate decree binds the whole world and cannot be collaterally attacked the way an ordinary decree in a civil suit can be, which matters enormously when multiple relatives, creditors, or purchasers might later question the legatee's title to valuable assets.

Will my bank or the land registry still ask for probate even though the law no longer requires it for court claims?

Very possibly. Banks, registries and transfer agents set their own documentation standards independent of Section 213, and many will continue to insist on probate, a succession certificate, or a legal heir certificate before releasing funds or processing a mutation, regardless of what a court would now require to entertain a suit.
