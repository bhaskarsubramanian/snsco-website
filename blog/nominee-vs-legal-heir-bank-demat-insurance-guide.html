Nominee vs Legal Heir: Who Actually Gets a Deceased Person's Bank, Demat and Insurance Money?

A nominee on a bank account, demat account or insurance policy is not automatically its owner after the account-holder's death: this guide explains the real legal difference between a nominee and a legal heir.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A Very Common, Very Costly Misunderstanding

Families frequently assume that whoever is named as 'nominee' on a bank account, demat account, mutual fund folio or insurance policy simply inherits that money when the account-holder dies: full stop, no further questions. That assumption is wrong, and acting on it can lead to real disputes, and even to a nominee having to hand over money to people who were never on the account at all. Nomination and inheritance are related but legally distinct concepts, and understanding the difference matters both for someone naming a nominee today and for a family sorting out an estate after a death.

What a Nominee Actually Is: A Trustee, Not an Owner

A nomination under banking regulations, the Insurance Act or the Companies Act (for demat holdings) exists primarily to give the bank, insurer or depository a person they can safely and quickly pay or transfer assets to on the account-holder's death, without having to independently determine who the legal heirs are. It is a payment-facilitation mechanism, not a mechanism for changing who owns the underlying money in law. As Indian courts, including in a 2026 Orissa High Court ruling in Snigdha Patnaik v. Susama Mohanty, have repeatedly held: a nominee receives the proceeds as a trustee for the legal heirs, not as the beneficial owner in their own right. The nominee is legally obliged to hand the proceeds over to the persons who are actually entitled to inherit under succession law, or under the deceased's Will if there was one.

Who the Legal Heirs Actually Are

Where the deceased left a valid Will, the Will governs who inherits, regardless of who was named as nominee: the nominee simply becomes the conduit through which the named beneficiaries receive the asset. Where the deceased died intestate (without a Will), succession is governed by the applicable personal law: for a Hindu male dying intestate, Section 8 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 sets out Class I heirs, the widow, sons, daughters, and the deceased's mother, among others, who inherit simultaneously and in equal shares, ahead of any Class II heir or more distant relative, and well ahead of a nominee who happens to be, say, a sibling or parent rather than the spouse or children.

Where This Actually Causes Disputes

The dispute pattern shows up repeatedly in reported cases: an employee dies, their bank or provident-fund records list a parent as nominee (perhaps from years earlier, before marriage, and never updated), and the surviving spouse and children, the actual Class I legal heirs, find the nominee resisting handover of the proceeds on the basis of the nomination alone. Courts have consistently sided with the legal heirs in this scenario. In the Orissa High Court's 2026 ruling, a widow and a mother both claimed a deceased bank employee's terminal benefits of roughly Rs. 62.90 lakh, with the mother relying on her status as the recorded nominee; the Court held that nomination does not confer ownership, and that the widow, as a Class I heir under Hindu succession law, held the superior claim.

Practical Steps to Avoid the Problem

Because nomination and inheritance frequently diverge in practice, both account-holders and families should treat nomination as one piece of estate planning, not the whole of it. Account-holders should review and update nominations after major life events, marriage, divorce, the birth of children, a spouse's or nominee's death, since a stale nomination from decades earlier is a common source of exactly this kind of dispute. Families settling an estate should not assume a listed nominee automatically has the final say; the nominee's role is to receive and then properly distribute the proceeds according to a valid Will or the applicable succession law, and legal heirs who believe a nominee is wrongly withholding funds have a clear basis, backed by consistent case law, to press their claim.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a bank nominee automatically inherit the money in the account?

No. A nominee receives the funds from the bank as a trustee for the legal heirs, not as the beneficial owner. The nominee is legally obliged to distribute the funds according to the deceased's Will, or, if there is no Will, according to the applicable succession law.

What did the Orissa High Court hold in Snigdha Patnaik v. Susama Mohanty?

The Court held that nomination in a bank account does not amount to ownership, a nominee merely holds the proceeds as a trustee for the legal heirs, and that under Hindu succession law a deceased Hindu man's property devolves first upon his Class I heirs, including his widow, ahead of a nominee's claim.

Who are Class I heirs under the Hindu Succession Act?

Under Section 8 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, Class I heirs of a Hindu male dying intestate include his widow, sons, daughters and mother, among certain other specified relatives, who inherit simultaneously and in equal shares.

If I've already named a nominee, do I still need a Will?

Yes, if you want to control who actually ends up with the asset. Nomination only determines who the bank or insurer pays out to first; a Will (or, absent one, succession law) determines who is legally entitled to keep it. Relying on nomination alone leaves the outcome to default succession rules, which may not match your wishes.

What should a family do if a nominee refuses to hand over funds to the rightful legal heirs?

The legal heirs can rely on the settled legal position: reinforced by rulings such as Snigdha Patnaik v. Susama Mohanty: that a nominee holds funds in trust, and can pursue a civil claim or succession proceeding to establish their entitlement and compel proper distribution.

References & Further Reading

This guide describes the general legal position on nomination versus inheritance. Personal-law succession rules vary by religion and family circumstance, and readers should confirm the applicable rules for their own situation.

  1. Snigdha Patnaik v. Susama Mohanty, Orissa High Court (2026): nomination does not confer ownership; a nominee holds proceeds in trust for the legal heirs.
  2. Hindu Succession Act, 1956, Section 8 and the Class I heirs schedule.
  3. Banking Companies (Nomination) Rules, 1985, framed under Section 45ZA of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949.
  4. Insurance Act, 1938, Section 39, as amended: nomination and beneficial-nominee provisions for life insurance policies.
