Electronic Evidence in Indian Courts: What Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam Requires

What Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam requires before a WhatsApp chat or email is admitted in court, and who must sign the certificate.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 16 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

If a case turns on a WhatsApp chat, an email thread, a CCTV recording or a bank statement printed from a server, the single most common way that evidence gets thrown out, or never gets in at all, is a missing or defective certificate. Since 1 July 2024, the rule that governs this is Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which replaced Section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 for any proceeding started on or after that date. The underlying idea has not changed much, electronic evidence that is not the original device needs a certificate to be admitted, but the certificate itself now asks for more than it used to.

This guide sets out what Section 63 actually requires, who has to sign the certificate, when it can be skipped altogether, and why the Supreme Court's older rulings on this point still matter even though the statute they interpreted has been replaced.

The basic rule: a copy of an electronic record needs a certificate

Indian evidence law has long drawn a line between primary evidence, the original, and secondary evidence, a copy. For an electronic record, the original is usually the device itself, the phone, the server, the hard drive. A party rarely brings the entire server or phone to court, and instead relies on a printout, a screenshot, a forensic image, or a CD or pen drive containing extracted data, all of which count as secondary evidence of the electronic record. Section 63 of the BSA, like Section 65B before it, says such secondary evidence can only be admitted if it satisfies a set of conditions about how the copy was produced and is accompanied by a certificate confirming those conditions were met.

Where the original device itself is produced and proved in court, this certificate requirement does not apply, because the device is primary evidence and is proved the way any other physical object is proved, through a witness who can speak to what it is and where it came from. The certificate route exists specifically for situations where producing the original device in court is impractical or simply not how litigants and investigators normally work, which in practice is most situations involving digital evidence today.

What changed from Section 65B to Section 63

The core structure carries over: the party relying on the electronic record still has to show the computer or device that produced it was in regular use, that the information was fed into it in the ordinary course of activities, that the device was operating properly during the relevant period, and that the output reproduces or is derived from the information fed in. What Section 63 adds is a more detailed, two-part certificate. One part has to be given by a person occupying a responsible official position in relation to the operation of the device, or who is otherwise qualified to speak to how the record was produced, and it now has to identify and disclose the hash value of the electronic record and the device, a technical fingerprint meant to show the file has not been altered since it was captured.

The second part is an expert's declaration confirming the accuracy of the device and process described. This dual-signature structure is new compared to the older single-certificate practice under Section 65B, and it reflects a deliberate tightening aimed at reducing disputes over whether digital evidence, particularly video and audio, has been tampered with or deepfaked, a concern that has grown considerably as such technology has become easier to use convincingly.

Why the certificate requirement is treated as mandatory, not optional

Under the old Section 65B regime, the Supreme Court went through a confusing back-and-forth on whether the certificate was strictly mandatory or could be dispensed with in some circumstances. The Constitution Bench in Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal, decided in 2020, settled the question by holding that the certificate under Section 65B(4) is a condition precedent to the admissibility of electronic evidence, and it expressly overruled an earlier decision, Shafhi Mohammad, which had suggested courts could relax the requirement in some cases. This followed and reaffirmed the position the Supreme Court had first taken in Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer, decided in 2014 and reported at (2014) 10 SCC 473, which had itself corrected an even earlier, looser approach to admitting electronic copies.

Commentary on Section 63 of the BSA treats this mandatory-certificate principle as having carried over directly, since the statutory language and structure are substantially the same, just expanded. This means a litigant who turns up at trial with a key WhatsApp screenshot but no certificate from a person in a responsible position regarding the device it came from is very likely to find that evidence excluded, however genuine the screenshot actually is, because the court's focus at the admissibility stage is on whether the statutory conditions were met, not on whether the content looks credible. Lawyers building a case around digital communications should treat the certificate as something to chase down at the start of the matter, not as paperwork left for the week before trial.

The hash value requirement and why it survived a constitutional challenge

Section 63(4)'s requirement that the certificate disclose the hash value of the electronic record was challenged as unworkable or unfair in Pune Bar Association v. Union of India, a case reported in 2026, where the Supreme Court upheld its constitutionality. The practical effect is that a party relying on electronic evidence, or more often the forensic expert or investigating agency assisting them, has to generate and record a hash value, essentially a unique digital signature of the file, at the point the evidence is captured or extracted, and reproduce that hash value in the certificate. Evidence captured without this step from the outset becomes much harder to certify properly later, which is why parties involved in disputes likely to turn on digital records, commercial fraud, cheque dishonour backed by electronic communications, employment disputes involving emails or chat logs, are increasingly advised to involve a forensic expert early rather than after a dispute has already crystallised.

For businesses, this has a quiet but real operational consequence: internal systems that log or store communications relevant to disputes, customer service chats, approval emails, access logs, should ideally be set up so that a hash value or equivalent integrity check can be generated on demand, because retrofitting this after evidence has already been collected informally is far harder than building it in from the start.

The transition rule and what it means for ongoing cases

Because the BSA took effect on 1 July 2024, a transitional question arises for evidence connected to events or proceedings that straddle that date. The general approach taken in commentary and by courts has been that Section 65B continues to govern proceedings initiated before 1 July 2024, while Section 63 applies to those initiated on or after that date, meaning the applicable certificate regime usually tracks when the case itself was filed or the relevant proceeding commenced, not when the underlying electronic record was created. Litigants and their counsel handling matters that span the transition should check which regime applies to their specific proceeding rather than assume, since getting this wrong can mean producing the wrong form of certificate at the wrong stage.

In practical terms for anyone preparing a case today, that means almost all fresh filings will fall under Section 63 of the BSA rather than the old Section 65B, so the dual-certificate, hash-disclosure format should be treated as the default to prepare for, with the older single-certificate format relevant mainly to matters that were already pending before the transition date.

What this means for a foreign company litigating or arbitrating from India

A foreign company that becomes a party to Indian court proceedings, whether as claimant or defendant, often holds the relevant electronic records on servers outside India, managed by IT teams unfamiliar with Indian evidence rules. This is a frequent, avoidable source of delay, because a certificate signed by someone with only a loose connection to the system, or one that omits the hash value Section 63 now requires, can be challenged by the opposing side even where the content of the record is not seriously disputed. Building in enough lead time to get the right person, someone who can genuinely speak to how the system is administered, to sign the certificate, and to loop in a forensic expert for the second declaration, is worth doing as soon as litigation looks likely rather than once a hearing date is close.

Arbitration seated in India, or governed by Indian procedural choices, often borrows from these evidentiary standards even though the Evidence Act and the BSA do not strictly bind an arbitral tribunal the way they bind a court. Tribunals frequently look to the same certificate practice as a marker of reliability when weighing digital evidence, so treating Section 63 as a floor to meet, rather than a court-only technicality, tends to serve a foreign party better in either forum.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a certificate to produce a printout of my own WhatsApp chat in court?

In almost all cases, yes, because a printout or screenshot is secondary evidence of the electronic record on the device. The certificate under Section 63 has to come from a person in a responsible official position in relation to the phone or system the chat came from, and now also needs the record's hash value, not just a signature from the party relying on it.

Who is qualified to sign the expert declaration under Section 63?

The provision calls for a declaration by an expert, generally understood to mean someone with genuine technical competence to speak to the device's operation and the integrity of the extraction process, rather than any person who happens to be familiar with the device. Courts have increasingly expected this to be a qualified forensic expert rather than an in-house IT staff member with limited digital forensics training.

What happens if I forget to get a Section 63 certificate before trial?

The Supreme Court has treated the certificate as a condition precedent to admissibility, so evidence offered without one is at serious risk of being excluded outright, regardless of how relevant or authentic it appears to be. Some reported decisions have allowed a certificate to be filed at a later stage if obtainable, but this should never be relied upon as a fallback plan.

Does Section 63 apply to evidence that was created before the BSA came into force?

What matters under the generally followed transitional approach is when the proceeding itself was initiated, not when the underlying record was created. A case filed on or after 1 July 2024 is expected to follow Section 63, even if the WhatsApp message, email or recording in question dates from well before that.
