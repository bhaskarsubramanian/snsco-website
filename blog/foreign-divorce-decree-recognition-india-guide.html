Is a Foreign Divorce Decree Automatically Valid in India? What Section 13 CPC Actually Requires

A divorce granted abroad is not automatically recognised in India — this guide explains when a foreign divorce decree is valid here, and when it can be challenged.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

No, a Foreign Decree Is Not Automatically Recognised

A common and consequential misunderstanding among NRI couples is the assumption that once a court abroad — say, in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada or the Gulf — grants a divorce, that divorce is simply valid everywhere, including in India. It is not automatic. Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 sets out the conditions under which a foreign judgment, including a foreign divorce decree, will be treated as conclusive and recognised in India, and a decree that fails to meet those conditions is not binding here, however validly it may have been granted under the law of the country that issued it.

The Section 13 Conditions

Section 13 provides that a foreign judgment is conclusive as to any matter directly adjudicated upon, except in six specified situations: where the foreign court lacked competent jurisdiction; where the judgment was not given on the merits of the case; where the judgment appears, on its face, to be founded on an incorrect view of international law or a refusal to recognise Indian law where applicable; where the proceedings were opposed to natural justice; where the judgment was obtained by fraud; and where it sustains a claim founded on a breach of Indian law. For a divorce decree specifically, the jurisdiction and natural-justice grounds tend to generate the most disputes: whether the foreign court actually had proper jurisdiction over the marriage (which turns partly on where the parties were domiciled or ordinarily resident), and whether the respondent spouse was given proper notice and a genuine opportunity to be heard, rather than the divorce being granted essentially unilaterally, without the other spouse's meaningful participation.

Jurisdiction: Where This Most Often Goes Wrong

Indian courts, including the Supreme Court in long-settled precedent on this question, have held that a foreign court's jurisdiction to dissolve an Indian marriage governed by Indian personal law is not automatically established merely because one spouse is domiciled or resident in that foreign country, if the marriage itself was solemnised in India under Indian law (typically the Hindu Marriage Act or the Special Marriage Act) and the other spouse did not submit to that foreign court's jurisdiction. A spouse — often the one who moved abroad — who unilaterally files for and obtains a foreign divorce, without the other spouse genuinely appearing or consenting to that court's jurisdiction, faces a real risk that Indian courts will decline to recognise that decree as valid, since Indian matrimonial law generally requires dissolution to proceed under the law actually governing the marriage, not merely the law of wherever one spouse happens to be living.

What Happens If a Foreign Decree Isn't Recognised

If a foreign divorce decree fails the Section 13 test, it is simply not treated as having dissolved the marriage as a matter of Indian law — which creates serious downstream complications. A spouse who remarries in India relying on an unrecognised foreign decree can face allegations that the remarriage is void, or worse, exposure to a bigamy complaint. Maintenance, custody and property claims connected to the marriage can remain open in India notwithstanding the foreign decree, since Indian courts are not treating the marriage as having ended. This is precisely why a spouse relying on a foreign divorce, particularly one obtained without full participation by both parties, should not simply assume the matter is closed and should instead have the foreign decree's validity in India properly assessed before taking any further step — such as remarriage — that depends on it.

The Safer Route: Mutual, Participatory Proceedings

Where both spouses genuinely participate in the foreign proceedings — submitting to the foreign court's jurisdiction, being properly represented or at least properly notified, and the decree being granted on a genuinely contested or mutually consented basis rather than by default — the resulting decree is considerably more likely to satisfy Section 13 and be recognised in India. Couples who anticipate needing their divorce recognised in India (because one or both parties, or their assets, remain connected to India) should, where realistically possible, structure the foreign proceeding with this in mind, including ensuring the other spouse is properly served and given a genuine opportunity to participate, rather than pursuing a purely unilateral or default process that may look procedurally sound abroad but vulnerable in India.

Confirming Recognition Formally

Because the stakes of getting this wrong are high — a defective remarriage, an unresolved maintenance claim, or unexpected exposure to a bigamy allegation — a spouse relying on a foreign divorce decree for any significant step in India (remarriage, property settlement, or otherwise) should have an Indian court formally confirm the decree's validity where there is any real doubt, rather than proceeding on the assumption that a foreign court's decree is simply, automatically, good enough.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a divorce granted by a foreign court automatically valid in India?

No. Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 sets out specific conditions a foreign judgment must satisfy to be recognised in India, and a foreign divorce decree that fails to meet them is not treated as binding here.

What is the most common reason a foreign divorce decree isn't recognised in India?

Jurisdiction and natural-justice defects are the most common: Indian courts have held that a foreign court's jurisdiction over an Indian marriage isn't automatically established merely by one spouse's foreign residence, particularly where the other spouse did not submit to that court's jurisdiction or was not properly heard.

What happens if someone remarries in India relying on an unrecognised foreign divorce decree?

The remarriage can be alleged to be void, and the person can face exposure to a bigamy complaint, since Indian law does not treat the original marriage as having ended if the foreign decree fails the Section 13 test.

What kind of foreign divorce proceeding is more likely to be recognised in India?

One where both spouses genuinely participated — submitting to the foreign court's jurisdiction, being properly notified or represented, and the decree being granted on a contested or mutually consented basis rather than by unilateral default.

How can someone be sure a foreign divorce decree will be treated as valid in India?

Where there is any real doubt, the safest step is to have an Indian court formally confirm the decree's validity before relying on it for a significant step such as remarriage or a property settlement, rather than assuming recognition.

References & Further Reading

This guide provides a general overview of foreign divorce decree recognition under Indian law. Recognition is highly fact-sensitive, turning on the specific foreign proceeding's jurisdictional basis and procedural fairness, and should be assessed individually before relying on a foreign decree.
