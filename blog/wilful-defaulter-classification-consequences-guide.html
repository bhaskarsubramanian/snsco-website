Wilful Defaulter Classification in India: Process, Consequences and How to Respond

How banks classify a borrower or promoter as a wilful defaulter under RBI's Master Direction, the two-stage committee process, and the practical and legal consequences that follow.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 April 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

What 'Wilful Default' Means

Under the Reserve Bank of India's Master Direction on Treatment of Wilful Defaulters and Large Defaulters, a default is treated as 'wilful' where the borrower has defaulted despite having the capacity to honour its obligations, has diverted or siphoned the borrowed funds for purposes other than those for which finance was granted, has disposed of financed assets or provided security without the lender's knowledge, or has otherwise acted in a manner that shows deliberate, rather than merely financially-driven, default. Genuine business failure or inability to pay, without more, does not amount to wilful default.

The Two-Stage Internal Process

RBI's framework requires banks to run classification through two internal bodies. An Identification Committee first examines the evidence and, if it forms a prima facie view of wilful default, issues a show-cause notice to the borrower and, where applicable, to guarantors and promoters, calling for a written response within a reasonable period, generally not less than 21 days. Only after considering that response does a separate Review Committee, chaired by an official senior in rank to the Identification Committee's members, pass a final, reasoned order either confirming or rejecting the classification.

Consequences of Classification

Classification as a wilful defaulter carries serious consequences: the borrowing entity and its promoters or whole-time directors become ineligible for fresh institutional finance for a prescribed period, cannot participate in floating new ventures using bank finance for a period, and the classification is reported to credit information companies, affecting the group's broader access to credit. In more serious cases, wilful default findings can also feed into separate criminal or regulatory proceedings, including under laws dealing with financial fraud.

A Borrower's Options When Facing a Show-Cause Notice

A borrower or promoter served with a show-cause notice should respond in writing within the time allowed, addressing each specific allegation with supporting documentation, particularly on end-use of funds, since diversion allegations are the most common basis for wilful default findings. If the Review Committee confirms the classification despite a substantive response, the classified entity or individual can approach an appropriate appellate forum within the bank's own grievance mechanism where available, and separately has recourse to challenge the classification before a court where the process itself was procedurally flawed: for instance, where the Review Committee did not independently consider the response or the show-cause notice lacked adequate particulars.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a company be classified as a wilful defaulter even if it has genuinely run out of money?

No. Genuine inability to pay due to business failure, without evidence of diversion of funds, disposal of secured assets, or similar conduct showing deliberate default despite capacity to pay, does not meet RBI's definition of wilful default.

Does wilful defaulter classification stop a borrower from raising equity or non-bank funding?

The restriction under RBI's framework is specifically on fresh institutional finance from banks and financial institutions and on floating new ventures using such finance for a prescribed period: it does not, by itself, statutorily bar non-institutional funding sources, though the classification's effect on credit reports can practically affect a wider range of financing relationships.

What is the minimum response time a borrower must be given after a show-cause notice?

RBI's Master Direction contemplates a reasonable period, generally not less than 21 days, for the borrower and any concerned promoters or guarantors to submit their written representation before the Review Committee takes a final decision.
