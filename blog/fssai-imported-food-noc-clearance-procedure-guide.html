Clearing Imported Food Consignments Through FSSAI: The NOC Procedure Explained

A guide for importers on the FSSAI No-Objection Certificate process for imported food consignments, from documentation to port clearance and common causes of delay.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 22 January 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Why an FSSAI NOC Is a Separate Step From Customs Clearance

Importing a food consignment into India involves two distinct regulatory tracks that must both be satisfied before goods can leave the port: customs clearance under ordinary import regulations, and a food-safety-specific No-Objection Certificate (NOC) from FSSAI confirming the consignment meets applicable Indian food safety standards. An importer cannot substitute one for the other. Customs clearance alone does not authorise release of a food consignment without the separate FSSAI NOC.

Documentation an Importer Should Have Ready

A complete NOC application typically requires the import licence details, the specific product's compositional and labelling information, and, critically, a laboratory test report from an FSSAI-recognised or NABL-accredited laboratory confirming the consignment's compliance with the applicable product standard. Since port testing and clearance already add time to an import timeline, importers frequently arrange pre-shipment testing at origin or promptly on arrival, rather than waiting for FSSAI's own port-level testing process to identify a compliance issue for the first time.

Common Causes of Rejection or Delay

FSSAI's Food Import Rejection Alert data has consistently shown non-conforming pesticide residue levels, contamination findings, and labelling non-compliance, including missing or inaccurate mandatory declarations required specifically for imported food: among the most common reasons a consignment is rejected or held pending further clarification at the port. A consignment held for labelling deficiencies alone, where the underlying product is otherwise compliant, is generally a faster issue to resolve than a genuine compositional or contamination finding, but both routes stop the consignment from moving until resolved.

Managing Demurrage Risk While an Application Is Pending

Since demurrage and container detention charges continue to accrue while a consignment sits at the port awaiting an NOC decision, an importer whose application has been complete for an extended period without any query from FSSAI has a basis to press for a time-bound decision, including, in an appropriate case, seeking judicial intervention where prolonged, unexplained inaction is itself causing escalating commercial loss.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is customs clearance sufficient to release an imported food consignment?

No. A food consignment additionally requires a separate FSSAI No-Objection Certificate confirming compliance with Indian food safety standards; customs clearance alone does not authorise release.

What documentation does an FSSAI NOC application typically require?

Import licence details, the product's compositional and labelling information, and a laboratory test report from an FSSAI-recognised or NABL-accredited laboratory confirming compliance with the applicable standard.

What are the most common reasons an imported food consignment is rejected or delayed?

Non-conforming pesticide residue levels, contamination findings, and labelling non-compliance, including missing or inaccurate mandatory declarations specific to imported food.

What can an importer do if an NOC application remains pending for an extended period without any query?

Press the authority for a time-bound decision, and in an appropriate case seek judicial intervention where prolonged, unexplained delay is causing escalating demurrage and detention costs.
