Why a Higher Offer After an IBC Liquidation Auction Usually Doesn't Reopen the Sale

A later bidder offering more money is a familiar temptation in liquidation sales. A recent NCLAT ruling explains why that temptation is, on its own, not a legal basis to undo a concluded auction.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 12 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice, see our disclaimer

The Recurring Problem in Liquidation Sales

Liquidation under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 typically proceeds through a public e-auction conducted by the liquidator, run under reserve prices and terms set out in the auction notice and governed by the applicable IBBI liquidation process regulations. A familiar pattern in these sales is that a bidder emerges as the successful bidder, deposits the earnest money or initial part payment required, and only then does a disappointed party, sometimes a secured creditor unhappy with the price, sometimes a rival bidder who missed the deadline, surface with an offer to pay more.

The tension this creates is real. Liquidators and creditors naturally want to maximise recovery for the stakeholders of the corporate debtor. But allowing a concluded auction to be reopened every time a higher number appears afterward would make every liquidation sale provisional indefinitely, discouraging serious bidders from participating in the first place, since no one would want to commit capital to a bid that a later, higher offer could simply unwind.

What the NCLAT Held in the Maithan Alloys Matter

In State Bank of India v. Maithan Alloys Limited, the liquidator had conducted a second e-auction after an initial round drew no qualifying bid, at a reduced reserve price, and Maithan Alloys Limited was declared the successful bidder, depositing the twenty-five percent earnest money the auction terms required. When the Adjudicating Authority at Kolkata interfered with that concluded process on the basis that a higher offer had since come in, the NCLAT reversed that order, holding that the auction had not been challenged on grounds of fraud, collusion or irregularity, and that the auction terms contained no provision allowing the process to be reopened simply because a better price later emerged.

The Appellate Tribunal's reasoning rests on protecting the sanctity and finality of the public auction process itself. A successful bidder who has complied with the auction terms, including making the required deposit, is entitled to expect that compliance will be honoured, not treated as merely provisional pending whether a better offer eventually turns up.

What Bidders, Liquidators and Creditors Should Take From This

For bidders, the ruling is a reason for confidence in participating seriously in IBC liquidation auctions: a compliant, successful bid is not easily undone after the fact by a rival's later, higher number. For liquidators, it reinforces that the auction terms and process actually followed matter a great deal, since the finality the NCLAT protects depends on the auction having been conducted properly in the first place, with no fraud, collusion or material irregularity. For creditors unhappy with a sale outcome, the practical lesson is that objections to the process, pricing methodology, or conduct of the auction need to be raised before or during the auction, or promptly afterward on a recognised ground, rather than being deferred until a better offer happens to appear.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a creditor reopen a liquidation auction simply because someone later offers more?

Generally not. The NCLAT has held that a concluded, properly conducted auction cannot be disturbed merely because a higher offer surfaces afterward, absent fraud, collusion or a material irregularity in the auction process itself.

What counts as a ground to challenge a liquidation auction after it closes?

Fraud, collusion, or a material irregularity in how the auction was actually conducted remain recognised grounds; a later, higher price offer on its own is not treated as such a ground.

Does this mean liquidators have unchecked discretion in conducting auctions?

No. Liquidators must still follow the applicable IBBI liquidation process regulations and the published auction terms; the finality the NCLAT protects is the finality of an auction that was itself conducted properly and in accordance with those terms.
