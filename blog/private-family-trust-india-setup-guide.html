Setting Up a Private Family Trust in India: Revocable or Irrevocable, and What It Costs in Tax

How a private family trust is created under the Indian Trusts Act, revocable versus irrevocable, and the tax and FEMA issues for NRI settlors.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 13 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Families with property in more than one city, a business that several children will eventually run together, or a member who needs lifelong financial protection often ask the same question after they have already made a will: is a will enough, or do we also need a trust. A will only operates on death and only once, through probate or a succession certificate, and it says nothing about how assets are managed while the person who made it is still alive but wants to step back from running them. A private family trust does something a will cannot: it lets a person hand over legal ownership of specific assets, right now, to a trustee who manages them for named beneficiaries according to rules written down in advance.

A private trust is not a tax shelter and not a way to defeat creditors or a future claim under succession law, and courts have struck down trusts created for exactly those purposes. Used for its real purpose, which is orderly management and succession of family assets, it is one of the older and better tested structures in Indian law, governed by a statute that has barely changed since 1882.

What has to be in the trust deed for it to actually be a trust

Section 6 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 requires the person creating the trust, called the author of the trust or the settlor, to show with reasonable certainty four things: an intention to create a trust, the purpose of the trust, the beneficiary, and the trust property. The Act's own illustrations make the point that vague language defeats a trust. A bequest to a relative made in the hope that he will 'continue the family business' for the benefit of others, without naming who those others are, does not create a trust, because the beneficiary is not identified with reasonable certainty. A trust deed drafted loosely, in the hope that the family will simply understand what was meant, is the single most common reason private trusts fail when tested in court.

A trust over movable property, such as shares, mutual fund units or cash, can in principle be created without a written instrument, though in practice nobody does this for a family trust meant to last. A trust over immovable property is different: Section 5 of the Act requires it to be declared by a non-testamentary instrument in writing, signed by the author or the trustee, and registered. That registration requirement, combined with the Registration Act, 1908's own rule that instruments creating or declaring rights in immovable property above a nominal value must be registered, means a family trust holding a house or land cannot exist informally. Stamp duty on the trust deed is a state subject and the applicable schedule of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 as adopted or amended by the relevant state has to be checked for the property's location.

Revocable or irrevocable: the choice that drives everything else

A revocable trust lets the settlor change the terms or cancel the trust and take the assets back. That flexibility has a tax cost. Under the clubbing provisions of the Income-tax Act, 1961, income from assets transferred to a trust that the settlor can revoke is generally taxed as the settlor's own income, not the trust's or the beneficiary's, because the law treats a revocable arrangement as not having really parted with ownership for tax purposes.

An irrevocable trust cannot be altered or cancelled by the settlor once created, except strictly as the deed itself permits. This is the structure usually meant when people talk about family trusts for succession planning, because it is the only version that genuinely removes the assets from the settlor's own estate for future succession purposes, with the trade-off that the settlor gives up the ability to change their mind. Families who are not certain about the final shape of the arrangement sometimes start with a revocable trust and convert it to irrevocable later, once the structure has been tested for a few years, though each conversion needs its own deed and its own stamp duty and registration where immovable property is involved.

Specific trusts versus discretionary trusts, and the tax difference

A trust where each beneficiary's share is fixed and known in advance, called a specific trust, is taxed broadly as if the income belonged directly to each beneficiary in their fixed share, at that beneficiary's own slab rate. A discretionary trust, where the trustee decides how much each beneficiary receives, or where the beneficiaries or their shares are not determinate at the time the income arises, is taxed differently under Section 164 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, which charges such income at the maximum marginal rate, a rate defined in Section 2(29C) of the Act and pegged to the highest tax bracket plus applicable surcharge and cess. One point of currency matters here. The Income-tax Act, 2025 came into force on 1 April 2026 and replaced the Income-tax Act, 1961, carrying these trust provisions forward in renumbered form. The 1961 section numbers used in this guide govern the earlier tax years, while income arising from 1 April 2026 onwards falls under the corresponding provisions of the 2025 Act, whose numbering should be checked before filing.

This is a deliberate policy choice. The scheme of Section 164(1) is understood to discourage indeterminate, discretionary arrangements by taxing them at the highest rate, except in specific situations the proviso to that section carves out, such as where the trust is the only one created by that settlor and none of the beneficiaries has other income above the basic exemption threshold. Families who want the flexibility of a discretionary trust, so that the trustee can respond to a beneficiary's changing needs over the years, should go in with a clear understanding that this flexibility is usually taxed at the highest rate unless the arrangement fits squarely within one of the proviso's exceptions.

NRI settlors, trustees and beneficiaries: the FEMA layer

A trust does not sit outside the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 merely because it is a domestic instrument. Where the settlor, a trustee or a beneficiary is a non-resident, each transfer into or out of the trust has to independently satisfy the FEMA rules that would apply to that same transfer if it were made directly rather than through a trust. Settling Indian immovable property into a trust where the beneficiaries are all relatives who qualify as NRIs under the current Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) Rules, 2019 is generally treated as a permitted transfer, but the position changes, and can require specific approval, where a beneficiary is a foreign national who is not of Indian origin or where the property would effectively pass to a non-resident outside the relative categories the rules recognise.

Practitioners also flag that a trustee's power to invest trust funds in Indian immovable property is itself constrained when the beneficiaries are a mixed group of residents and non-residents, since the FEMA analysis has to be done beneficiary by beneficiary rather than for the trust as a single entity. A family with members settled in different countries should get the FEMA position checked against the trust deed's actual beneficiary list before, not after, property is transferred in, since unwinding a non-compliant transfer is far harder than structuring it correctly at the start.

Choosing and controlling the trustee

The trustee holds legal title to the trust property and owes fiduciary duties to the beneficiaries under the Act, including duties to carry out the trust's purpose, to protect trust property, to keep proper accounts, and not to profit personally beyond what the deed allows. Families commonly appoint a mix of trustees, often including the settlor during their lifetime alongside a professional or a trusted relative, with the deed specifying how a trustee can be removed or replaced and who decides when there is disagreement among co-trustees.

A deed that names only one trustee, with no mechanism for replacement if that person dies or becomes incapable, is a recurring drafting failure that forces the family back to court years later to get a new trustee appointed. The deed should also say plainly whether a trustee can resign, how a resigning trustee hands over accounts and property, and what approval, if any, a trustee needs from beneficiaries before selling or mortgaging trust property.

When a trust is the wrong tool

A trust is not a substitute for a will covering assets the family does not intend to transfer during their lifetime, and most families end up needing both. A trust created shortly before death, structured to defeat an existing maintenance obligation, a pending claim, or the statutory shares of heirs under personal succession law, invites exactly the kind of challenge that a will drafted for the same improper purpose would invite, and courts examining such a trust will look past the deed's language to the real intention behind it.

For a family whose only goal is to avoid the delay of probate on a modest, single-city estate, a carefully drafted will, or in some cases a registered family settlement, usually does the job at a fraction of the ongoing compliance cost a trust carries, since a trust needs its own PAN, its own bank account in many cases, and continuing tax filings for as long as it exists. The trust route earns its cost mainly where there is a business to be run as a unit, a minor or special-needs beneficiary who needs management rather than a lump sum, or assets spread across jurisdictions that a single probate proceeding cannot efficiently cover.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a private family trust avoid the need for probate?

Only for the assets actually transferred into the trust during the settlor's lifetime. Probate is a court process for a will, and a trust is a separate instrument that operates by transferring ownership now, not on death, so assets left outside the trust and covered only by a will still go through whatever succession process applies to a will in that place.

Can an NRI be the settlor of a private trust holding Indian property?

Yes, but every transfer of Indian immovable property into the trust has to independently satisfy the Foreign Exchange Management Act rules that would apply to a direct transfer, and the analysis depends on the residential status of the beneficiaries named in the deed, not just the settlor.

Is a trust deed for immovable property compulsorily registered?

Yes. Section 5 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 requires a trust over immovable property to be declared by a written, signed, registered instrument, and the Registration Act, 1908 separately requires registration of instruments creating or declaring rights in immovable property above a nominal value.

What tax rate applies to income earned by a discretionary family trust?

Section 164 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 generally taxes such income at the maximum marginal rate because the beneficiaries' shares are not fixed in advance, subject to specific exceptions in the proviso to that section, such as where it is the settlor's only trust and no beneficiary has other income above the basic exemption limit.

Can a family trust be challenged the way a will can be challenged?

Yes, particularly where it was created to defeat an existing legal claim, a maintenance obligation, or the statutory inheritance rights of heirs. A court will look past the language of the deed to the real purpose behind the trust before deciding whether to uphold it.
