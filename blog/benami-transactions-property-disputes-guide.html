Benami Transactions and Property Disputes: What the Law Actually Restricts

A practical guide explaining what a benami transaction is, why the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act restricts recovery of such property, and the narrow statutory exceptions that remain available.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice. See our disclaimer

What Counts as a Benami Transaction

A benami transaction, in general terms, is one where property is held by one person (the benamidar) in their name, while the consideration for acquiring it was provided by another person (the real owner), who is intended to enjoy the actual benefit of the property. Historically common as an informal way of holding family property, or of keeping the identity of the true owner out of the public record, such arrangements are now tightly restricted under the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988, substantially strengthened by amendments in 2016.

The Core Restriction

Section 4 of the Act bars the real owner (the person who actually funded the acquisition) from bringing any suit, claim, or action to enforce a right in respect of benami property against the benamidar (the person in whose name it is held) or anyone claiming through them. In practice, this means a person who funded a property purchase in another's name generally cannot go to a civil court to recover that property, even where they can otherwise prove they provided the money.

The Narrow Statutory Exceptions

The Act recognises two principal exceptions where a claim for benami property remains permissible: first, where a member of a Hindu Undivided Family holds property for the benefit of other coparceners; and second, where a person holds property in a genuine fiduciary capacity (such as a trustee, executor, partner, or director) for the benefit of another for whom they stand in that specific fiduciary relationship. These exceptions are construed strictly and narrowly by courts, and a party relying on one bears the burden of establishing that the specific statutory conditions are genuinely met.

Why This Matters Before, Not After, a Purchase

Because the restriction on recovery is so significant, the practical lesson is almost entirely preventive: property should be acquired and registered in the name of the person actually intended to be its owner. Relying on an informal family understanding to later 'sort out' who really owns a benami-held property is a substantially riskier proposition than it may first appear, given how narrowly the statutory exceptions are read.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I paid for a property but it's registered in someone else's name, can I claim it back?

Generally, no: Section 4 of the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act bars such a claim, except within the narrow statutory exceptions for coparcenary and genuine fiduciary holdings.

Does it matter that I can prove I actually paid for the property?

Not by itself: proof of having provided the consideration is not, on its own, a defence or basis for recovery outside the specific statutory exceptions.

What are the exceptions where a benami-style claim can still succeed?

Property held by a coparcener in a Hindu Undivided Family for the benefit of other coparceners, and property held in a genuine fiduciary capacity (such as by a trustee or partner) for the benefit of another: both construed narrowly.

What is the safest way to avoid a benami problem?

Register and hold property in the name of the person actually intended to own it: avoid informal arrangements to fund a purchase in someone else's name with the intention of later asserting ownership.
