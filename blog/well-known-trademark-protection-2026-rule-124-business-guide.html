Protecting a Well-Known Brand in India: What the Delhi High Court's Columbia Pictures Ruling Means for Businesses

An explainer on the Delhi High Court's clarification that a Rule 124 well-known mark declaration is not a precondition to Section 11(2) protection, and what that means for brand owners defending their marks against lookalikes in unrelated product categories.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 July 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice. See our disclaimer.

Why This Question Matters for Brand Owners

A trademark normally only blocks a confusingly similar mark within the same or closely related category of goods or services. Section 11(2) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999, is an exception: it allows the owner of a mark that qualifies as well known to object to a similar mark even in a completely unrelated class of goods, on the theory that use of a similar mark anywhere risks diluting or unfairly riding on the reputation of a famous brand. The practical question that had divided opinion, and that reached the Delhi High Court in Columbia Pictures Industries, Inc. v. Registrar of Trade Marks, was whether a brand owner must first obtain a formal declaration from the Registry that its mark is well known, under Rule 124 of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017, before it can invoke this cross-class protection in any later proceeding.

What the Delhi High Court Reportedly Held

The dispute arose when Columbia Pictures opposed the registration of the mark "GHOST BUSTER" in a pharmaceutical and sanitary products class, relying on the fame of its "GHOSTBUSTERS" entertainment franchise even though the contested mark covered an entirely different line of goods. The Registrar had rejected the opposition partly because Columbia had not separately obtained a Rule 124 declaration for its mark. On appeal, the Delhi High Court is reported to have clarified that Rule 124 provides an optional, facilitative route for a brand owner to seek formal recognition in advance, but is not itself a gatekeeping requirement that a mark must clear before Section 11(2) protection becomes available in a specific opposition, infringement or cancellation proceeding.

Under this reading, whether a mark is well known is a factual question to be proved on the evidence placed before the specific tribunal hearing the dispute, drawing on the statutory factors in Section 11(6), including the extent and duration of the mark's use, its recognition among the relevant section of the public, and the extent to which it has been promoted. A mark owner does not lose cross-class protection merely because it has not previously filed and secured a formal Rule 124 declaration.

What This Means in Practice

For a business that believes a competitor is trading on the reputation of its brand in an unrelated product category, this means it does not need to have first completed a separate, standalone Rule 124 application before it can raise well-known mark protection in an opposition or infringement proceeding. What it does need is a solid evidentiary record: sales figures, advertising spend, media coverage, consumer survey data where available, and a documented history of enforcement against other infringers, since this evidence is what the tribunal will actually weigh in deciding whether the mark qualifies as well known for the purposes of that specific case.

At the same time, a Rule 124 declaration, once obtained, remains useful. It creates a standing, citable finding that can be referred to in future disputes without re-litigating the well-known status question from scratch each time, which can save considerable time and cost in later proceedings even though it is not a strict precondition.

Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a brand need a Rule 124 declaration before it can stop a similar mark in an unrelated product category?

According to the Delhi High Court's reported decision in Columbia Pictures Industries, Inc. v. Registrar of Trade Marks, no. A Rule 124 declaration is an optional route to formal recognition, not a precondition for Section 11(2) cross-class protection, which can be established on the evidence in any specific proceeding.

What evidence helps establish that a mark is well known under Section 11(2)?

Relevant evidence typically includes the duration and extent of the mark's use, advertising and promotional spend, sales figures, media recognition, and a track record of enforcement against infringing or similar marks, assessed against the factors set out in Section 11(6) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999.

Is it still worth applying for a Rule 124 well-known mark declaration?

Yes. Even though it is not strictly required before invoking Section 11(2) protection, a Rule 124 declaration creates a standing finding of well-known status that a brand owner can rely on in future disputes without re-proving the point each time, which can reduce the time and cost of later enforcement actions.

Does well-known mark protection apply automatically to every class of goods once recognised?

A mark found to be well known is assessed in light of recognition among the relevant section of the public and the specific circumstances of each dispute; the protection is not a blanket, automatic bar on every conceivable use by a third party, and tribunals still examine the facts of each case, including the degree of similarity and the likelihood of a connection being assumed by consumers.
