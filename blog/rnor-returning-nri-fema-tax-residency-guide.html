RNOR Status for Returning NRIs: The Tax Window Most People Miss

Returning NRIs often get a multi-year transitional tax status that shields foreign income and assets from full Indian taxation — this guide explains how RNOR status works and how to plan around it.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

Three Residential Statuses, Not Two

Indian income tax law recognises three residential statuses, not simply 'resident' and 'non-resident': Non-Resident (NR), Resident and Ordinarily Resident (ROR), and a transitional middle category, Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR). Many returning NRIs assume they move directly from NR to full ROR status the moment they resettle in India, which overlooks a genuinely valuable planning window: RNOR status, available for a limited period after return, under which a person's global tax exposure and foreign asset disclosure obligations are materially lighter than they will eventually become as a full ROR.

How RNOR Status Is Determined

A person qualifies as RNOR (rather than the more burdensome ROR) if they satisfy the basic residency test for being 'resident' in India in a given year, but also satisfy either of two specific conditions: they were non-resident in India in nine out of the ten previous years, or they were physically present in India for 729 days or less during the seven previous years. In practice, this generally gives a returning NRI who has spent many consecutive years abroad an RNOR window of roughly two to three years after their return, though the precise duration depends on the specific pattern of the individual's presence in India in the years immediately preceding return, and should be calculated carefully rather than assumed.

What RNOR Status Actually Shields

The core benefit of RNOR status is that foreign-sourced income — income earned or accrued outside India, other than income from a business controlled from India or a profession set up in India — is not taxable in India during the RNOR period, unlike for a fully ROR taxpayer, whose global income is taxable in India regardless of source. This can matter significantly for a returning NRI who still holds foreign investments, a foreign pension, foreign rental income, or other overseas income streams generating ongoing returns — during the RNOR window, that income generally remains outside the Indian tax net, giving the returning NRI valuable time to reorganise their financial affairs (repatriating funds, restructuring investments, or winding down foreign income sources) before full worldwide taxation begins.

RNOR and Foreign Asset Disclosure

RNOR status also affects, though does not entirely eliminate, foreign asset disclosure considerations connected to the Black Money Act framework, discussed in our companion guide — the more onerous full Schedule FA disclosure regime and associated exposure attach specifically once a taxpayer becomes resident and ordinarily resident, so understanding precisely when the RNOR window ends is important for knowing when those fuller obligations begin, not only for tax planning but for compliance planning as well.

Practical Planning During the RNOR Window

Returning NRIs should use the RNOR window deliberately rather than letting it pass unused: this is the natural time to consider whether and how to repatriate foreign funds, to review and potentially restructure foreign investments in light of the fact that their tax treatment in India will become materially less favourable once ROR status attaches, and to plan the timing of any large foreign income realisation (such as selling a foreign investment with substantial accrued gains) to fall, where legitimately possible, within the RNOR period rather than after it ends. Because the RNOR window is time-limited and its exact end date depends on the specific facts of a person's residence history, returning NRIs should calculate their own RNOR eligibility and likely end date early, ideally before or immediately upon returning, rather than discovering after the fact that the window has already closed.

Getting the Calculation Right

Because RNOR eligibility depends on a specific look-back calculation across the preceding seven to ten years, and because the underlying day-count and residency rules themselves are technical, returning NRIs should have their specific RNOR eligibility and expected duration confirmed by a tax professional based on their actual travel and residence history, rather than relying on a general rule of thumb that may not match their individual case.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is RNOR status?

Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident — a transitional Indian tax residential status, available to a person who is resident in India in a given year but who was non-resident in nine of the previous ten years, or present in India for 729 days or less in the previous seven years.

How long does RNOR status typically last for a returning NRI?

Commonly around two to three years after return for someone who spent many consecutive years abroad, though the precise duration depends on the individual's specific residence history and should be calculated rather than assumed.

What tax benefit does RNOR status provide?

Foreign-sourced income (other than income from a business controlled from India or a profession set up in India) is generally not taxable in India during the RNOR period, unlike for a fully Resident and Ordinarily Resident taxpayer, whose global income is taxable in India regardless of source.

Does RNOR status affect foreign asset disclosure obligations?

It affects the timing of when the fuller Schedule FA disclosure regime under the Black Money Act framework begins to apply — those more onerous obligations generally attach once a taxpayer becomes fully Resident and Ordinarily Resident, so knowing when RNOR ends matters for compliance planning too.

Why should a returning NRI calculate their RNOR window early?

Because the window is time-limited and depends on a specific look-back calculation, planning steps such as repatriating funds or realising foreign investment gains are best timed to fall within the RNOR period where possible — discovering the window has already closed after the fact forecloses that planning opportunity.
