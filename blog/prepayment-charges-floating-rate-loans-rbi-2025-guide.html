Can Your Bank Charge You for Repaying a Loan Early? The RBI's 2025 Prepayment Rules

From loans sanctioned on or after 1 January 2026, most floating-rate individual and MSE loans cannot carry a prepayment or foreclosure charge at all.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A borrower who wants to close a loan early, by refinancing with a cheaper lender or simply paying off the balance once they have the funds, has historically run into a foreclosure or prepayment charge buried in the loan agreement, often calculated as a percentage of the outstanding principal. The Reserve Bank of India's Pre-payment Charges on Loans Directions, 2025 change this for a large category of loans, and a borrower or a lender who has not updated its documentation to the new position is likely to get the charge wrong in one direction or the other.

This guide sets out what the 2025 Directions actually prohibit, which loans are covered and which are not, and when the new rule starts to bite.

What the Directions actually ban

The Reserve Bank of India (Pre-payment Charges on Loans) Directions, 2025, issued on 2 July 2025, prohibit regulated entities, meaning most commercial banks, certain categories of urban co-operative banks, non-banking financial companies, and All India Financial Institutions, from levying a prepayment or foreclosure charge on a floating-rate loan given to an individual for a purpose other than business.

The same prohibition is extended to floating-rate loans given to individuals and micro and small enterprises for business purposes, subject to the lender falling within the categories the Directions cover and, in the draft version of the rule, subject to a sanctioned-limit threshold for the loan. This matters because it is a meaningfully wider ban than earlier RBI guidance, which had focused mainly on home loans to individuals; the 2025 Directions bring a broader set of floating-rate retail and small-business credit within the no-charge rule.

Which loans and lenders fall outside the ban

The prohibition applies only to floating-rate loans; a fixed-rate loan is outside the scope of these Directions entirely, and a lender remains free to levy a prepayment charge on a fixed-rate loan as its own commercial terms and any other applicable regulation allow.

The Directions also carve out certain categories of smaller or specialised lenders from the business-loan limb of the ban, with Tier 1 and Tier 2 urban co-operative banks and base-layer non-banking financial companies falling outside that part of the prohibition in the proposed rule. A borrower dealing with a lender in one of these categories should not assume the blanket no-charge position applies to a business loan from that specific lender without checking where that lender actually sits in the Directions' categories.

Co-borrowers on a loan do not take it outside the ban: the Directions make clear that a floating-rate individual loan for non-business purposes stays covered even where there is more than one borrower on the facility, which matters for home loans and similar facilities commonly taken jointly by family members.

When the new rule actually starts to apply

The 2025 Directions apply to loans and advances that are sanctioned or renewed on or after 1 January 2026. A loan already running on terms agreed before that date is not automatically brought under the new no-charge rule merely because the Directions have come into force; the triggering event is the sanction or renewal date of that specific facility, not the date the Directions themselves took effect.

This timing point matters in practice for a borrower close to a renewal date: a facility due for renewal shortly after 1 January 2026 will fall under the new rule from that renewal, while the same facility renewed just before that date would not, at least until its next renewal. A borrower planning a prepayment around that boundary should check the facility's own sanction or renewal date against the Directions' effective date rather than assuming the ban applies simply because the calendar has turned.

What a lender can still do

Outside the categories the Directions cover, such as a fixed-rate loan or a loan from a lender category the business-loan limb excludes, the position before the 2025 Directions continues to apply: a lender can levy a prepayment or foreclosure charge if it is disclosed in the loan agreement and follows a board-approved policy, since the broader regulatory expectation that any such charge be transparent and consistently applied, rather than imposed arbitrarily case by case, predates and survives the 2025 Directions.

A lender's loan documentation, sanction letters, and fee schedules drafted before July 2025 should be reviewed against the new Directions well before January 2026, since a facility sanctioned or renewed on or after that date carrying a prepayment charge the Directions no longer permit exposes the lender to a straightforward regulatory compliance gap, not merely a commercial dispute with an individual borrower.

Why the Reserve Bank moved on this

The draft circular preceding the 2025 Directions framed foreclosure charges as a responsible-lending conduct issue: a charge calculated as a percentage of the outstanding loan can discourage a borrower from refinancing with a cheaper lender even where refinancing would genuinely save them money, locking the borrower into their existing lender's rate rather than letting ordinary competition between lenders bring rates down for the borrower's benefit.

Floating-rate loans were the particular target because a borrower on a floating rate is, in principle, already exposed to rate movements either way, and a punitive exit charge on top of that exposure was seen as weighting the relationship further in the lender's favour. A fixed-rate loan was treated differently because the lender has typically taken on its own interest-rate risk in agreeing to a fixed rate for the loan's term, which is part of why fixed-rate loans remain outside the 2025 ban.

What a borrower planning to prepay should actually do

Before requesting a foreclosure statement, a borrower should check three things: whether the loan is floating or fixed rate, when it was last sanctioned or renewed relative to 1 January 2026, and whether the lender falls into one of the categories the Directions cover for that type of loan. A borrower assuming the new no-charge rule automatically applies, without checking these three points against their own facility, risks being surprised by a charge that remains lawful on the specific facts of their loan.

Where the rule does apply and a lender nonetheless proposes to levy a charge, the borrower's strongest first step is simply to cite the specific Direction and ask the lender, in writing, to confirm its position, since most regulated lenders will correct an internal error of this kind once it is pointed out rather than risk a supervisory finding over a single borrower's foreclosure fee; escalation to the Reserve Bank's own grievance channels is available if the lender does not correct course, but is rarely the first step that is actually needed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a bank charge a fee for prepaying a home loan taken for personal use?

Not for a floating-rate home loan to an individual for a non-business purpose, where the loan is sanctioned or renewed on or after 1 January 2026, under the RBI's 2025 Pre-payment Charges on Loans Directions. This applies even where the loan has more than one borrower.

Does the prepayment ban cover business loans too?

Yes, for floating-rate loans to individuals and micro and small enterprises for business purposes, from the categories of lenders the Directions cover, subject to exceptions for certain smaller urban co-operative banks and base-layer NBFCs on the business-loan side of the rule.

Does this apply to a fixed-rate loan?

No. The 2025 Directions apply only to floating-rate loans. A lender can still levy a prepayment or foreclosure charge on a fixed-rate loan under its own commercial terms, subject to whatever other disclosure and consistency requirements already applied to such charges before these Directions.

My loan was sanctioned before January 2026. Does the new rule protect me?

Only from the facility's next sanction or renewal on or after 1 January 2026. The Directions apply based on when the specific loan is sanctioned or renewed, not simply on the date the Directions themselves came into force, so an existing loan is not automatically covered until it reaches that point.

What should I do if my lender tries to charge a prepayment fee that the Directions prohibit?

Check the loan's sanction or renewal date against 1 January 2026 and confirm it is a floating-rate facility to an individual or an eligible micro or small enterprise from a covered lender category, then raise the specific Direction with the lender in writing before escalating it as a regulatory compliance issue rather than a purely contractual dispute.
