How to File a Motor Accident Compensation Claim: A Guide to MACT Proceedings

How a Motor Accident Claims Tribunal petition actually works — who can file, where to file, what documents you need, and how compensation is calculated.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 25 August 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

What the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal Is

A road accident that causes death, injury or property damage gives rise to a specific statutory remedy in India, separate from an ordinary civil suit for damages: a claim petition before the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal, constituted under Section 165 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988. Each state government sets up one or more of these Tribunals, usually presided over by a judicial officer of the rank of a District Judge, and their jurisdiction is confined to adjudicating claims for compensation arising out of accidents involving the death of, or bodily injury to, persons, or damage to property, resulting from the use of a motor vehicle.

The Tribunal's procedure is deliberately less rigid than a regular civil trial. It is not bound by the strict rules of evidence and procedure under the Code of Civil Procedure and the Evidence Act in the same way a civil court is, and is meant to follow a summary process aimed at resolving compensation claims with less delay and formality than ordinary litigation. In practice, contested claims — particularly those involving disputed liability, quantum disputes with the insurer, or multiple claimants — still take real time and benefit considerably from being properly pleaded and evidenced from the outset.

Who Can File, and Against Whom

Under Section 166 of the Act, a claim can be filed by the person who has sustained injury, by the owner of property that has been damaged, or, where the accident has resulted in death, by all or any of the legal representatives of the deceased. In a fatal accident case, this ordinarily means the spouse, children and dependent parents, though the precise composition of eligible claimants — and how compensation is apportioned among them — is decided with reference to who was actually dependent on, or legally entitled to inherit from, the deceased.

The petition is typically directed against three parties together: the driver of the offending vehicle, its registered owner, and the insurer that issued the third-party insurance policy covering that vehicle. Impleading the insurer is what makes the claim practically recoverable in most cases, since a compulsory third-party insurance policy is what the Act mandates every motor vehicle to carry, and it is usually the insurer that actually satisfies the award once passed. Where the vehicle or driver responsible cannot immediately be traced, a claim can still be pursued, including against a fund set up for uninsured and unidentified vehicle cases, though the mechanics of that route are more involved and worth discussing with counsel early.

Immediate Steps After the Accident

What is done — or not done — in the days immediately following an accident often shapes how smoothly the eventual claim proceeds. A First Information Report at the local police station, recording the basic facts of how the accident happened, is usually the foundation document a claim is built on. Under Sections 158 and 159 of the Act, the police officer investigating a motor vehicle accident is required to forward information about the accident to the Claims Tribunal, and in many states this information — culminating in what is often called a Detailed Accident Report — is used to register a claim proceeding even before the victim or family has formally filed a petition of their own. This mechanism is a useful safety net, particularly for claimants who are unaware of their rights or unable to act immediately due to the severity of injuries, but it should not be relied upon as a substitute for promptly engaging counsel and filing a properly pleaded petition, since the police-forwarded report will not by itself capture the full extent of loss, dependency and future prospects that a well-drafted claim petition should.

Beyond the FIR, it is worth preserving: photographs or video of the accident scene and vehicles involved, contact details of any independent eyewitnesses, the registration number and, where obtainable, the insurance particulars of the offending vehicle, and complete medical records from the first point of treatment onward — the medico-legal certificate, discharge summary, treatment bills, and, in a fatal case, the post-mortem report. These records are what ultimately substantiate both liability and the quantum of compensation claimed.

Filing the Claim Petition: Where and With What

Section 166(2) gives a claimant a genuine choice of forum: the petition can be filed before the Tribunal within whose local limits the accident occurred, or before the Tribunal within whose local limits the claimant resides or carries on business, or before the Tribunal within whose local limits the vehicle owner (the defendant) resides. This flexibility matters in practice — a family that has moved, or a victim who is being treated in a different city, is not forced to litigate in the town where the accident happened purely because that is where it occurred.

A typical claim petition sets out the facts of the accident, the parties involved, the basis on which each respondent is said to be liable, and a computation of the compensation claimed under each head, supported by an affidavit and the documents that substantiate the claim — the FIR and any charge-sheet, medical records and bills, proof of income (salary slips, income tax returns, or, for the self-employed, other reliable evidence of earnings), the deceased's or injured person's age proof, dependents' and legal heirs' proof, and, where relevant, a disability certificate assessing the degree of permanent disablement. Many states provide a court-fee exemption or concession for motor accident claim petitions given their welfare character, though the exact position varies and should be confirmed with the Tribunal's own registry or counsel at the time of filing.

The Limitation Question — and Why It Is Currently Unsettled

The time limit for filing a MACT claim has genuinely moved more than once, and readers should not rely on an outdated position. The Act originally prescribed a limitation period, which was removed altogether by an amendment in 1994 — for close to three decades afterward, claims could be filed under Section 166 without a fixed statutory deadline, subject only to the general expectation that undue and unexplained delay could count against a claimant when the Tribunal exercised its discretion. The Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019 reinstated a limitation period, requiring a claim under Section 166(3) to ordinarily be filed within six months of the accident. That reinstated provision has, however, been stayed by the Supreme Court, leaving its practical enforcement unsettled while the question works its way through the courts. Given that uncertainty, the sound practical approach is to treat prompt filing as the safer course in every case, rather than to assume that the absence of an enforced deadline gives claimants unlimited time.

No-Fault and Structured-Formula Routes

Section 166 is the primary route for a compensation claim and generally requires the claimant to establish that the accident was caused by rash or negligent driving of the offending vehicle. The Act separately provides, in Chapter X, for a no-fault liability route under Section 140 and a structured-formula route under Section 163A, both of which were designed to let certain claimants recover a defined amount of compensation without having to prove negligence — a genuine advantage where liability is disputed or difficult to establish quickly. The 2019 amendment has altered the statutory landscape around these provisions, and the current position on their availability, applicable amounts and interaction with a Section 166 claim can differ depending on when the accident occurred and which provisions have been brought into force in a given state. This is an area where the specifics genuinely matter and are worth confirming with counsel rather than assumed from an older source.

How Compensation Is Actually Calculated

Compensation under Section 166 is assessed under a set of recognised heads. In a fatal accident case, the principal component is usually the loss of dependency — what the deceased's family has lost by way of financial support — computed using the multiplier method: the deceased's annual income, with an addition for future prospects, is multiplied by a figure drawn from an age-based multiplier table (higher for younger victims, tapering with age), reflecting the number of years of dependency the compensation is meant to cover. The framework for this calculation, including the percentage addition for future prospects based on the deceased's age and whether they held permanent employment, were self-employed, or drew a fixed salary, was authoritatively settled by the Supreme Court's Constitution Bench in National Insurance Company Ltd. v. Pranay Sethi (2017), building on the multiplier approach the Court had earlier laid down in Sarla Verma v. Delhi Transport Corporation.

To the loss-of-dependency figure, the Tribunal adds what are called conventional heads — funeral expenses, loss of consortium (to the spouse, and, following later decisions extending the principle, in some cases to children and parents as well), and loss of the deceased's estate — for which the Pranay Sethi bench fixed uniform baseline amounts, to be enhanced periodically. In an injury case rather than a fatal one, the heads instead include actual medical expenditure, loss of earnings during the period of treatment, loss of future earning capacity where the injury has caused permanent disability (again generally computed using a multiplier), and compensation for pain and suffering, loss of amenities of life, and, where relevant, disfigurement. Because the exact conventional-head amounts and multiplier tables are subject to periodic judicial revision, the current figures should always be checked against the latest position rather than an older source.

Interim Compensation, the Award, and Appeals

Section 168 empowers the Tribunal to make an award directing compensation to be paid, and in appropriate circumstances tribunals have the power to grant interim compensation during the pendency of the claim rather than making the claimant wait for final adjudication before receiving any relief at all — particularly relevant where a family has lost its principal earning member and needs support to survive the litigation itself. Once a final award is passed, a party aggrieved by it — typically the insurer or the vehicle owner, though a claimant dissatisfied with the quantum awarded can equally appeal — can appeal to the High Court under Section 173, subject to conditions the Act attaches to such an appeal, including a pre-deposit requirement in certain circumstances that is meant to discourage appeals filed only to delay payment.

Tribunals are generally expected to resolve claims with reasonable expedition given the summary nature of the proceeding, but actual timelines vary considerably depending on the Tribunal's pendency, whether liability is contested, and how many claimants and respondents are involved — a claimant should treat any given timeline as indicative rather than assured, and focus instead on ensuring the petition is properly pleaded and evidenced from the outset, since that is what most directly affects both the pace and the eventual outcome of the claim.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who can file a claim before the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal?

The person injured in the accident can file the claim themselves. Where the accident results in death, the claim is filed by the deceased's legal representatives — typically the spouse, children, or parents who are dependent on or entitled to inherit from the deceased. Owners of damaged property can also file for the property loss. A claim can be filed even where the exact identity of the driver or vehicle is not immediately known, and can later be amended once that information is established.

Where do I file a MACT claim petition?

Under Section 166(2) of the Motor Vehicles Act, a claimant has a choice of jurisdiction — the Tribunal within whose local limits the accident occurred, or the Tribunal within whose limits the claimant resides or carries on business, or the Tribunal within whose limits the defendant (the vehicle owner) resides. This flexibility exists precisely so a claimant is not forced to litigate far from home simply because the accident happened elsewhere.

Is there a time limit for filing a motor accident claim?

The position has shifted more than once. A statutory limitation was removed by amendment in 1994, after which claims could be filed without a fixed deadline. The Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019 reinstated a six-month limitation period under Section 166(3), but the Supreme Court has stayed the operation of that provision, so its practical enforcement remains unsettled at present. Given this uncertainty, the safest course is still to file as promptly as possible after the accident rather than relying on the absence of a hard deadline.

How is compensation actually calculated?

For loss of dependency in a fatal accident or loss of future earning capacity in a permanent disability case, tribunals generally use the multiplier method — the deceased or injured person's annual income (with an addition for future prospects, calibrated by age and employment type) is multiplied by a figure drawn from an age-based multiplier table, following the framework the Supreme Court settled in National Insurance Company Ltd. v. Pranay Sethi. This is added to actual medical expenses, funeral expenses, loss of consortium and other conventional heads, and — for injury cases — compensation for pain, suffering and loss of amenities.

Do I need to prove the driver was negligent to get compensation?

Under the primary route, a claim under Section 166, the claimant must generally show that the accident was caused by the negligent or rash driving of the offending vehicle. Separately, the Act also provides limited no-fault and structured-formula routes for certain categories of claims that do not require proof of negligence, though the scope and current amounts under these provisions have been affected by the 2019 amendment and should be checked at the time of filing rather than assumed.
