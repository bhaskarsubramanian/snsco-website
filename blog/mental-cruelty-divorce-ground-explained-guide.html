Mental Cruelty as a Ground for Divorce in India: What Courts Actually Look For

A guide explaining how Indian courts assess mental cruelty as a ground for divorce, what kinds of conduct have been treated as cruelty, and what is generally not enough.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 29 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Overview

A guide explaining how Indian courts assess mental cruelty as a ground for divorce, what kinds of conduct have been treated as cruelty, and what is generally not enough.

What You Need to Know

  • Cruelty, including mental cruelty, is a ground for divorce under Section 13(1)(ia) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 and under the broadly parallel provisions of other personal-law and civil marriage statutes
  • Mental cruelty is not defined exhaustively by statute; courts assess it on a case-by-case basis, looking at whether the cumulative conduct of one spouse has caused such mental pain and suffering that it becomes impossible for the other spouse to continue living with them
  • Conduct that courts have, in appropriate factual contexts, treated as capable of amounting to mental cruelty includes persistent false accusations, sustained humiliation or degrading treatment, prolonged and unjustified denial of conjugal relations, repeated unsubstantiated criminal complaints, and a prolonged period of separation without genuine attempts at reconciliation
  • Ordinary wear-and-tear of married life: occasional quarrels, temperamental incompatibility, or isolated incidents of friction. Is generally not treated as cruelty; courts look for a sustained pattern rather than isolated lapses
  • Because mental cruelty is fact-specific, courts consider the totality of the parties' conduct, their social and educational background, and the cumulative effect of the alleged behaviour, rather than applying a fixed checklist

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a single argument or disagreement count as mental cruelty?

No: courts distinguish ordinary marital friction from cruelty; a pattern of sustained, serious conduct is generally required, not an isolated incident.

Can false criminal complaints against a spouse amount to cruelty?

Courts have treated a pattern of unsubstantiated criminal complaints, causing serious reputational and emotional harm, as capable of amounting to cruelty, though this depends on the specific facts.

Is there a fixed legal definition of mental cruelty?

No exhaustive statutory definition exists; courts assess mental cruelty based on the cumulative facts of each case rather than a fixed checklist.
