How a Foreign Company Protects Trade Secrets in India Without a Dedicated Statute

India has no trade secrets law. How confidential business information is protected through contract, equity and limited IT Act remedies, as on Nov 2026.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A foreign company setting up a subsidiary, a joint venture, or an outsourcing arrangement in India often asks the same question once it learns that India has signed no dedicated trade secrets law. The honest answer is that protection still exists, built out of contract law, the equitable doctrine of breach of confidence, and a couple of statutes that were not written with trade secrets in mind, rather than out of a single statute a company can simply cite in a complaint.

This note sets out what that patchwork actually does and does not cover, the handful of cases Indian courts keep returning to, and what a foreign business should put in place before, rather than after, a departing employee or a former vendor walks off with its formulas, client lists or source code.

There is no Indian Trade Secrets Act

Unlike patents, trademarks, copyright and designs, each of which has its own statute, trade secrets have never had a dedicated law in India. A private member's bill and an earlier draft National Innovation Act were floated years ago and went nowhere. Courts fill the gap with common law principles borrowed mostly from English equity, chiefly the doctrine of breach of confidence, under which a person who receives information in circumstances implying an obligation of confidence cannot use or disclose it to the discloser's detriment.

This matters practically because a claim for misappropriation of a trade secret in India is almost always pleaded as something else: breach of contract, breach of confidence, or occasionally passing off or unfair competition, rather than as a free-standing statutory trade secret claim. The relief available, usually an interim injunction followed by damages, flows from whichever of those causes of action the facts support, which means the quality of the underlying contract and the evidence of confidentiality matters more in India than it might in a jurisdiction with a dedicated statute.

What the contract has to do and Section 27's limit

A non-disclosure or confidentiality clause, standing on its own contract, is the single strongest tool available and courts enforce it routinely once a breach is shown. Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 voids any agreement that restrains a person from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business, and this provision has repeatedly tripped up employers who tried to stop a former employee from working for a competitor after the employment ended. A plain confidentiality obligation, which restrains the use of specific information rather than restraining the employee from working at all, generally survives Section 27 scrutiny, while a post-employment non-compete clause usually does not, though courts have upheld narrowly framed restrictions where they protect a legitimate interest rather than simply blocking competition.

The practical consequence is that a confidentiality clause should be drafted to describe the information being protected and the obligation not to use or disclose it, and should avoid dressing itself up as a disguised non-compete. A breach, once proved, gives a damages claim under Section 73 of the Contract Act and can support a permanent injunction under Section 38 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, restraining further use or disclosure.

Where there is no contract: the breach of confidence doctrine

The harder case is a third party who had no contract with the business at all, a competitor who receives leaked information, or a contractor's employee who was never asked to sign anything directly with the business whose secrets are at stake. Indian courts have extended relief here under the equitable doctrine of breach of confidence, which does not require privity of contract, only that the information was received under circumstances where an obligation of confidence could reasonably be implied.

Diljeet Titus, Advocate v. Alfred A. Adebare, reported at 2006 (32) PTC 609 (Delhi), remains the case most often cited for this. Associates who left a law firm were restrained from using client lists, proprietary records and a computer-generated database they had copied, even though the court recognised their freedom to use the general skills and knowledge they had picked up while working there. The distinction the court drew, between information someone has mentally absorbed through experience and material they have physically copied or removed, continues to shape how Indian courts approach departing-employee disputes.

The customer database line of cases

Burlington Home Shopping Pvt. Ltd. v. Rajnish Chibber, decided by the Delhi High Court on 20 October 1995 and reported at 61 (1996) DLT 6, is the other case that comes up constantly, usually in disputes over customer lists and databases. The court granted an interim injunction against a former associate who had set up a competing mail-order business using a customer database compiled with real investment of time and effort, treating the compilation as protectable both as a literary work under copyright law and, in some later commentary, as a trade secret in its own right.

Later Delhi High Court decisions have narrowed how far that reasoning travels. Courts applying it since have been clear that an ordinary customer list, assembled without real effort or carrying no independent commercial value, does not automatically qualify for protection merely because a business would prefer a competitor not have it. A foreign company relying on a database or client list as a trade secret should expect an Indian court to ask what work went into compiling it and what commercial harm its disclosure would actually cause, not simply accept that the list exists and was taken.

Where the IT Act helps, and where it does not

Two provisions of the Information Technology Act, 2000 sometimes come into play alongside a confidentiality claim. Section 72 punishes a person who, while providing services under a contract, discloses electronic information without the subject's consent, but it is narrow: it applies to breach of confidentiality by someone acting under a contract for services, chiefly intermediaries and government functionaries, and does not generally reach a competitor who simply receives stolen information from someone else. Sections 43 and 66 address unauthorised access to a computer system and can support a claim where a trade secret was taken through hacking or unauthorised copying from a protected system, giving both a civil remedy for damages and potential criminal exposure.

None of these sections were drafted with trade secrets specifically in mind, and a company relying on the IT Act alone, without a confidentiality contract or a breach of confidence claim running alongside it, is relying on a narrower and less tested remedy than the contract and equity routes described above.

What a foreign company should actually put in place

Every employment contract, consultancy agreement, and vendor or manufacturing agreement touching confidential processes, formulas, pricing, source code or customer data should carry a clause that defines the information being protected with some specificity, states the obligation not to use or disclose it during and after the relationship, and survives termination of the broader agreement. A separate, standalone non-disclosure agreement should sit alongside any due diligence process, licensing discussion or outsourcing arrangement, signed before any real information changes hands rather than after.

Internally, access to sensitive material should be limited to people who actually need it, with some record kept of who had access and when, because an Indian court assessing a breach of confidence claim will look for evidence that the information was in fact treated as confidential rather than left freely accessible within the business. None of this converts trade secret protection in India into a registered right the way a patent or trademark is, but it gives a court the contractual and factual basis it needs to grant an injunction quickly, which in most of these disputes matters far more than the prospect of damages years later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a Trade Secrets Act in India?

No. India has no dedicated trade secrets statute as of November 2026. Protection is built from contract law, the equitable doctrine of breach of confidence, and limited provisions of the Information Technology Act and the Contract Act, rather than a single standalone law.

Can an Indian employment contract stop an employee from working for a competitor after leaving?

A post-employment non-compete clause usually fails under Section 27 of the Contract Act, which voids restraints on lawful trade or profession once employment ends. A confidentiality clause restraining the use of specific information, rather than restraining the person from working at all, generally survives this test.

What can a company do if a third party with no contract leaks or uses its confidential information?

Indian courts allow a breach of confidence claim even without a contract, where the information was received under circumstances implying an obligation of confidence. Diljeet Titus v. Alfred A. Adebare is the case most often cited for this kind of claim against someone who copied or removed confidential material.

Is a customer database automatically protected as a trade secret in India?

Not automatically. Burlington Home Shopping v. Rajnish Chibber protected a database that took real effort to compile, but later decisions require the list to carry genuine commercial value and confidentiality, not simply exist, before a court will treat it as protectable.

Does the IT Act protect trade secrets in India?

Only narrowly. Section 72 covers disclosure by someone bound by a contract for services, and Sections 43 and 66 address unauthorised computer access, but neither was written for trade secrets specifically, and both work best alongside a confidentiality contract rather than in place of one.
