Registered vs Unregistered Wills: Does Registration Actually Change Anything?

Registration of a Will is optional under Indian law, not compulsory — this guide explains what registering a Will actually adds, and what it doesn't, so you can decide whether it's worth doing.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 30 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

Registration Is Optional, Not Compulsory

Of the documents Indian law routinely asks people to register, a Will stands out as an exception. Section 18 of the Registration Act, 1908 lists documents for which registration is optional, and a Will is one of them — unlike a sale deed or gift deed of immovable property, which the Registration Act makes compulsory to register, a Will is legally complete and enforceable whether or not it is ever taken to a Sub-Registrar's office. This surprises many people, who sometimes assume — wrongly — that an unregistered Will carries less legal weight or can be challenged more easily merely for want of registration.

What Actually Makes a Will Valid

A Will's validity turns on execution and attestation, not registration. Section 63 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 requires that the testator sign or affix a mark to the Will (or direct someone to sign on their behalf, in their presence), and that at least two witnesses each see the testator sign (or acknowledge their signature) and then sign the Will themselves in the testator's presence. Section 68 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 additionally requires that, to prove a Will in court, at least one attesting witness be called to testify to its execution, where such a witness is available. Get execution and attestation right, and the Will is valid — registered or not. The Supreme Court has repeatedly reaffirmed this position, including in its 2026 decision in Parvathi Nairthi v. Laxmi Nairthy (2026 INSC 521), where it held that a Will need not be registered to be valid and that proving a Will does not require mathematical accuracy, only that it was properly executed and attested.

So What Does Registration Actually Add?

If registration doesn't affect legal validity, what is it for? The honest answer is: evidentiary comfort, not legal necessity. When a Will is registered under Section 17 of the Registration Act, the Sub-Registrar retains a copy, records the date, and notes the testator's and witnesses' presence and identity at the time of registration. That creates an independent, official, time-stamped record that is much harder for anyone to later allege was fabricated, ante-dated, or tampered with after the testator's death. An unregistered Will, kept in a drawer or with a lawyer, still needs to be proved the same way — through witness testimony about execution — but it lacks that independent institutional record backing up its existence and date.

The Sealed-Cover Alternative: Depositing a Will

Between full registration and simply keeping a Will at home, the Registration Act offers a middle path testators sometimes overlook: deposit of a sealed Will under Section 42. A testator can hand a sealed cover containing the Will to the Registrar, who records the date of deposit without opening or reading the contents. This preserves complete confidentiality during the testator's lifetime — nobody, including the Registrar, knows what the Will says — while still creating an official, dated record that a Will exists and was deposited on a specific date. For testators uncomfortable with the idea of registration (which involves the Will's contents, though not necessarily its full text, being part of a public registration process) but who still want an institutional record, deposit is worth considering.

What Registration Does Not Cure

Registering a Will is not a shortcut past a genuine challenge. If a Will was executed under undue influence, or by a testator who lacked testamentary capacity at the time, or amid other real suspicious circumstances — an unexplained departure from a testator's earlier stated intentions, unusual involvement of a beneficiary in preparing the Will, or an unnatural exclusion combined with other red flags — registration does not immunise it from being challenged on those grounds. Courts examine the circumstances of execution regardless of whether a Will was registered; registration only makes it harder to dispute that the document itself is genuine and dates from when it claims to.

Weighing Whether to Register

For most straightforward estates, an unregistered but properly executed and safely stored Will, with reliable witnesses who can be traced later, works perfectly well. Where a contested or high-value estate is more likely — blended families, an estranged heir, a business succession, or a testator who anticipates that someone may challenge the Will after their death — the modest cost and effort of registration (or, at minimum, sealed-cover deposit) is usually worth the added evidentiary comfort it provides. The decision is a risk-management one, not a legal-validity one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it compulsory to register a Will in India?

No. Section 18 of the Registration Act, 1908 makes registration of a Will optional, not compulsory, unlike a sale deed or gift deed of immovable property, both of which the Act requires to be registered.

Is an unregistered Will legally valid?

Yes, provided it is properly executed and attested under Section 63 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 and can be proved under Section 68 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. The Supreme Court reaffirmed this in Parvathi Nairthi v. Laxmi Nairthy (2026 INSC 521), holding that a Will need not be registered to be valid.

What is the real benefit of registering a Will?

Registration's benefit is evidentiary, not legal: it creates a dated, official record at the Sub-Registrar's office that is harder to allege was fabricated or tampered with after the testator's death, since the office independently retains a copy.

Can I keep a Will confidential and still get an official record of it?

Yes — Section 42 of the Registration Act allows a testator to deposit a Will in a sealed cover with the Registrar, who records the date of deposit without opening or reading the contents, preserving confidentiality during the testator's lifetime while creating a dated official record.

Does registering a Will protect it from being challenged?

No. Registration does not cure genuine suspicious circumstances such as undue influence or lack of testamentary capacity — a registered Will can still be challenged on those grounds, though registration does make it harder to dispute the document's authenticity and date.
