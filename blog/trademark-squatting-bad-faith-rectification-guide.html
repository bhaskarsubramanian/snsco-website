Trademark Squatting in India: Recovering a Mark Someone Else Registered in Bad Faith

How Indian courts treat trademark squatting as bad faith, and how a rectification petition under Sections 47 and 57 can get a squatted mark removed.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 5 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

As the law stands today, a person who registers someone else's brand name or logo in India, usually a foreign or not-yet-launched Indian brand, with no genuine intention of using it themselves and simply to block the real owner or extract a payment, is treated by the Delhi High Court as acting in bad faith under Section 11(10)(ii) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999, and the remedy is a rectification petition asking the Registrar's register to be corrected to remove that registration under Section 57, often alongside Section 47 on the ground that the squatter never had, and never used, a genuine intention to use the mark.

The difficulty with squatting is less about winning the legal argument once the matter reaches court, since Indian courts have been fairly consistent in calling it out, and more about the years it can take to get there, and about everything a brand owner has to do, and prove, in the meantime.

What counts as squatting, and why it slips through at the application stage

Squatting is not a term defined anywhere in the Trade Marks Act itself, but Indian courts have had no trouble treating it as a form of bad faith. The Delhi High Court has described it as registering a well-known foreign trademark with no genuine intent to use it, solely to prevent the rightful owner from entering the market or to extract a monetary benefit from them. The application itself usually looks unremarkable on paper, filed on a 'proposed to be used' basis in a class that matches the real owner's business, often before the real owner has entered India or completed its own registration there.

Because the Trade Marks Registry's examination at the application stage is not designed to investigate a foreign brand's reputation or an applicant's actual intentions, squatted marks regularly proceed to registration unopposed, particularly where the real owner has not yet filed its own application or watch service in India and so never sees the advertisement in time to oppose it. The practical effect is that bad faith, in most of these cases, becomes relevant only after the fact: either when someone opposes the application before it is granted, or, more commonly, once the genuine owner discovers the registration later and has to apply to have it removed.

The Delhi High Court's Godai Global ruling

In a decision reported in early 2025, the Delhi High Court dealt with a rectification petition brought by the owner of the Korean skincare brand Beauty of Joseon against a respondent who had registered a near-identical device mark in India. The respondent did not appear or file a substantive reply, so the court accepted the petitioner's case on the facts before it; it held that there was no plausible reason for the respondent to have adopted a mark so similar to the Korean brand's other than to ride on its established goodwill, and directed the Trade Marks Registry to remove the registration under Section 57.

The court's reasoning relied on two earlier Delhi High Court decisions that had already treated squatting as bad faith: a 2023 ruling in a dispute over a well-known mark, and BPI Sports LLC v. Saurabh Gulati, a 2023 decision in which the respondent, an importer of the petitioner's products, had registered the petitioner's word mark in his own name in India. In that earlier case, with infringement unavailable because the petitioner held no Indian registration of its own, the court granted rectification under Section 57(2) on the footing that the registration was wrongly remaining on the register, since the facts underlying the respondent's bad faith had never been placed before the Registrar at the application stage.

A second, independent line: fabricated evidence of use

A separate Bombay High Court decision in 2025, concerning the 'LE SHARK' mark, illustrates a related but distinct pattern. There, the registered mark's logo was reported to be a near-identical copy of a mark the petitioner had used abroad since the 1980s, and the registrant's defence relied on invoices and other material said to show prior use in India, which the court found to be unreliable. Where a party relying on a disputed registration cannot produce genuine proof of honest adoption and use, and instead puts forward unreliable material to fill that gap, the resulting rectification case becomes considerably easier for the petitioner, since the court is no longer just weighing competing claims of first use but is confronted with evidence that actively undermines the respondent's position.

The common thread across both the Delhi and Bombay rulings is that courts are willing to infer bad faith from the surrounding circumstances, close copying of a mark with no credible business reason, an importer or distributor registering a principal's mark in their own name, or an inability to substantiate claimed use, rather than requiring the petitioner to produce direct proof of the registrant's state of mind, which is almost never available in a squatting case.

Running the rectification petition

A rectification petition under Section 57 is filed by a person aggrieved, asking that an entry wrongly made or wrongly remaining on the register be removed or corrected; it is commonly pleaded together with Section 47, which allows removal on the ground that the registered proprietor had no bona fide intention to use the mark when it was registered, and in fact made no genuine use of it for the statutorily relevant period. The two grounds work well together in a squatting case, since the squatter typically has neither used the mark nor can show a credible reason for having registered it in the first place.

A foreign brand owner bringing such a petition should also expect to establish standing as a 'person aggrieved', showing that the squatted registration genuinely stands in the way of its own business or future registration in India, a point the respondent will often contest as a preliminary objection before the substantive bad-faith case is even reached. Evidence of the petitioner's own reputation, prior use outside India, and the closeness of the copying remains central to the case, and should be assembled and documented well before the petition is filed rather than gathered in response to the respondent's defence.

What this costs in time, and how to avoid needing it at all

Even where the bad-faith case is strong, commentary on these disputes consistently flags how long the full process can take: years spent first on the rectification petition itself, and then, if the squatter has gone on to use the mark commercially in the meantime, a separate infringement or passing-off suit to actually stop that use. Rectification removes the registration from the register; it does not, by itself, award damages or injunct ongoing use, so a brand owner who discovers a squatted registration only after the squatter has built up a real business under it may need both proceedings rather than one.

The more reliable protection is to avoid ever being on the back foot in the first place: filing an Indian trademark application, even on a 'proposed to be used' basis, well before launch, and running a watch service on new applications in the relevant classes, so that a squatting attempt can be opposed at the application stage rather than unwound years later through rectification. A brand that waits until it is ready to launch in India before filing its own application is, in effect, leaving the window open for exactly the kind of registration these cases are about.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is trademark squatting illegal under Indian law?

The Trade Marks Act does not use the word 'squatting', but the Delhi High Court has held that it falls within bad faith under Section 11(10)(ii) of the Act, and squatted registrations have been ordered removed under Section 57. It is treated as a recognised ground for rectification, not as a separate standalone offence.

Can a foreign brand with no Indian registration still get a squatted mark removed?

Yes, through a rectification petition under Section 57, as in BPI Sports LLC v. Saurabh Gulati, where the petitioner had no Indian registration of its own and so could not sue for infringement, but still succeeded in having the respondent's registration removed on bad-faith grounds.

What has to be proved to win a trademark squatting rectification case?

Typically that the respondent's mark closely copies the petitioner's, that there is no credible honest reason for the respondent to have adopted it, and that the respondent has made no genuine use of it, or that any claimed use is unreliable. Courts have inferred bad faith from these circumstances rather than requiring direct proof of intent.

Does winning a rectification petition stop the squatter from using the mark going forward?

Not by itself. Rectification corrects the register by removing the registration; stopping active use of the mark commercially generally requires a separate passing-off or infringement proceeding, which is why brand owners who discover squatting late may need both.

What is the best way to prevent trademark squatting before it happens?

File an Indian trademark application early, even before commercial launch, on a proposed-to-be-used basis, and run a trademark watch service so a squatting attempt can be opposed during the application stage rather than challenged years later through rectification.
