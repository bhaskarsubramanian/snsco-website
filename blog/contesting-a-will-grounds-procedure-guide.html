Contesting a Will in India: Grounds, Procedure and Who Can Challenge It

What it actually takes to challenge a will in India — the recognised legal grounds, who has standing to object, and how a caveat and probate proceeding work.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

Disagreeing With a Will Is Not the Same as Having a Ground to Set It Aside

Families frequently feel a will is "unfair" — one sibling received more than another, a long-serving domestic caretaker was left out, a late-life bequest surprised everyone. None of that, by itself, is a legal ground to set a will aside. Indian law respects a testator's freedom to dispose of their property as they choose, subject to specific statutory exceptions (such as a Muslim testator's restriction on bequeathing more than one-third of the estate without heirs' consent, under Islamic personal law). A successful challenge has to be built on one or more of a limited set of recognised legal grounds, not simply on the outcome feeling unfair.

The Recognised Grounds for Challenge

Lack of testamentary capacity. The testator must have been of sound mind and capable of understanding the nature and effect of the document, the extent of the property being disposed of, and the claims of those who might ordinarily expect to benefit, at the time the will was executed. A will signed while the testator was suffering from a condition that genuinely impaired this understanding can be challenged on this ground — though a mere general diagnosis, such as an age-related illness, is not automatically sufficient; the impairment must be shown to have actually affected capacity at the relevant time.

Improper execution. Section 63 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 sets out the formalities a will (other than certain privileged wills made by specific categories of persons, such as soldiers in actual service) must satisfy: it must be signed by the testator (or signed by another person in the testator's presence and by their direction), and that signature must be made or acknowledged in the presence of at least two witnesses who must each attest the will in the testator's presence. A will that skips these formalities — signed by only one witness, or witnessed without the testator actually present — is vulnerable to challenge on grounds of improper execution.

Undue influence, coercion or fraud. A will procured by exerting improper pressure on a testator — particularly one who was old, ill, isolated or otherwise dependent on the person benefiting — so that the document reflects that person's wishes rather than the testator's own free will, can be challenged on grounds of undue influence. Coercion (threats or force) and fraud (deception as to the nature of the document being signed, or as to material facts) are related but distinct grounds, and each requires specific facts to be pleaded and proved, not a general suspicion.

Forgery. A challenge that the signature is not genuinely the testator's, or that the document itself is fabricated, is a forgery challenge and typically calls for expert evidence — commonly a forensic handwriting comparison — alongside other circumstantial evidence.

Who Can Challenge — and the Caveat Mechanism

Standing to contest a will generally belongs to a person with a genuine interest in the estate — typically someone who would have inherited under the applicable law of intestate succession had there been no will, or a beneficiary under an earlier will that the disputed will supersedes. A stranger with no connection to the deceased or the estate cannot ordinarily maintain a challenge.

Where a will is likely to be presented for probate (the court's formal validation of the will and the executor's authority to administer the estate) or for letters of administration, anyone who intends to contest it should file a caveat under Section 283 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 with the court where the proceeding is expected to be filed. A caveat is a formal notice ensuring the court will not grant probate without first hearing the caveator's objections. Filing this proactively — as soon as a dispute is anticipated, rather than after a grant has already been made — is one of the most important practical steps in this area, since reopening a grant that has already gone through is significantly harder than contesting a petition before it is decided.

The Burden of Proof — and Why "Suspicious Circumstances" Matter

The person seeking to rely on the will (the propounder, typically the executor named in it) carries the burden of proving its due execution — that the formalities under Section 63 were actually followed, generally through the testimony of an attesting witness where available. Indian courts have consistently held that where the circumstances surrounding a will's execution are "suspicious" — for example, the will unnaturally excludes natural heirs without explanation, the propounder played an unusually active role in preparing or procuring the will, the testator's signature appears markedly different from known exemplars, or the will was executed shortly before death under circumstances suggesting isolation from family — the propounder carries an enhanced burden to remove that suspicion through clear and satisfactory evidence before the will can be accepted as genuine. This "suspicious circumstances" doctrine is often the practical battleground on which contested probate matters are actually won or lost.

Practical Points Worth Keeping in Mind

Act promptly. Filing a caveat before a grant is made, and gathering contemporaneous medical records, correspondence and witness accounts close in time to the will's execution, materially strengthens a challenge — memories fade and documents go missing the longer a dispute is left unaddressed. Identify precisely which ground (or grounds) the facts actually support, since a vague, unparticularised challenge is unlikely to succeed; courts expect specific facts to be pleaded for undue influence, fraud or incapacity, not general assertions. And because these disputes sit at the intersection of family relationships and formal court procedure, and the applicable limitation period depends on the specific proceeding and forum, anyone considering a challenge — or anyone whose will as executor has been challenged — should take specific legal advice early rather than waiting for a dispute to escalate.

Frequently Asked Questions

On what grounds can a will be challenged in India?

The commonly recognised grounds are: the testator lacked testamentary capacity (unsound mind or incapacity) at the time of execution; the will was not validly executed under Section 63 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 (improper signing, absence of two attesting witnesses, or a failure to follow the required formalities); the will was procured by undue influence, coercion or fraud; or the will is a forgery. Mere suspicion or a belief that the distribution is unfair is not, by itself, a ground to set a will aside.

Who has legal standing to contest a will?

Generally, a person with a legitimate interest in the deceased's estate — typically a legal heir who would have inherited under intestate succession, or a beneficiary under an earlier will — has standing to object. A person with no connection to the deceased or the estate ordinarily cannot maintain a challenge.

What is a caveat and when should one be filed?

A caveat under Section 283 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 is a formal notice filed with the court to ensure that no probate or letters of administration are granted without the caveator being heard first. Anyone who anticipates that a will may be presented for probate and who intends to contest it should file a caveat promptly, since a grant made before a caveat is on record can be far harder to reopen.

What is the burden of proof in a will dispute?

The propounder of the will (the person seeking to establish and rely on it) bears the initial burden of proving its due and valid execution, including proof of attestation as required by Section 63 of the Indian Succession Act and the Indian Evidence Act framework. Where suspicious circumstances surrounding execution are shown — such as an unexplained departure from natural bequests, or the propounder's own active role in preparing the will — courts require that those suspicious circumstances be satisfactorily explained before the will can be accepted.

Is there a time limit for challenging a will?

There is no single, universal limitation period that applies identically to every will challenge — the applicable period depends on the specific proceeding involved (such as a probate petition, a suit for a declaration, or objections within a pending probate case) and is governed by the relevant provisions of the Limitation Act, 1963. Because this varies by the specific relief sought and the facts of the case, anyone considering a challenge should get specific advice promptly rather than assuming a general limitation period applies.

References & Further Reading

This article describes the general legal framework for contesting a will in India. Whether a specific challenge succeeds turns entirely on the facts and evidence in that case — readers facing an actual dispute should take specific legal advice.

  2. Indian Evidence Act framework on proof of attestation of documents required by law to be attested.
