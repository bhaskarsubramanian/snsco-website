Can a Bank Be Branded a Defaulter for Not Honouring a Garnishee Order Over a Frozen Account?

When a bank can resist an ESI garnishee order on an account, and what 'inoperative account' means as a defence, per a 2026 Calcutta HC ruling.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 17 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A statutory recovery officer, whether from the ESI Corporation, EPFO or the Income Tax Department, has a blunt instrument available when a defaulting establishment owes money: attach whatever the establishment's bank is holding on its behalf. The bank gets a prohibitory order, sometimes called a garnishee notice, directing it to pay over the amount from the named account instead of allowing the account holder to operate it. Banks generally comply without argument, because arguing with a statutory recovery officer is expensive and the bank is, after all, holding someone else's money. But what happens when the account named in the order has nothing in it, or cannot be operated at all because it was frozen months earlier for an unrelated reason? Can the recovery officer still brand the bank a defaulter and proceed against the bank itself for the unpaid sum?

The Calcutta High Court answered that question against a recovery officer in 2026, and the reasoning has wider application than the ESI dispute in which it arose. It touches every bank that is served a statutory garnishee order on an account that is not, in fact, operable in the ordinary course.

What a garnishee order actually asks a bank to do

A garnishee or prohibitory order served on a bank under a statutory recovery mechanism, whether the ESI Act, the EPF Act or income tax recovery rules, directs the bank not to allow the named account holder to withdraw funds, and instead to remit the amount (up to the sum demanded) to the recovery officer. The obligation created is not an open-ended liability on the bank's own resources. It is limited strictly to what the account actually and lawfully holds, and to what can lawfully be paid out of it, at the time the order takes effect and afterwards.

That distinction matters because banks sometimes receive these orders on accounts that are dormant, closed in substance though not formally, or already restricted for reasons that have nothing to do with the recovery demand. A bank cannot pay out money that is not there, and it cannot operate an account that its own systems, or a court order, have put beyond ordinary transactions. The recovery officer's remedy in that situation lies against the establishment and its other assets, not against the bank for a shortfall the bank never caused.

The Calcutta High Court ruling on SBI's inoperative account

The dispute arose out of ESI dues owed by a cooperative society that banked with the State Bank of India's Murgasol branch. The ESI Recovery Officer had issued a prohibitory order against the society's account in August 2024. SBI's response was that the account was inoperative and that it could not lawfully remit any sum from it. The recovery officer was not satisfied, and proceeded to treat SBI itself as a deemed defaulter over the unpaid ESI dues, exposing the bank to recovery action in its own right.

SBI challenged that finding before the Calcutta High Court, and succeeded. Justice Shampa Dutt (Paul) held that branding a bank a deemed defaulter in these circumstances was a finding made without application of mind, and that it violated principles of natural justice. The Court's reasoning rested on a simple point that recovery officers frequently skip past: a bank cannot be made to answer for funds that an account, verifiably, does not and cannot hold. Treating a bank as a defaulter without first testing whether the underlying account was capable of yielding the demanded sum is not a technical slip; it converts a mechanism meant to recover money that exists into a liability on a third party for money that never existed in the first place.

When 'the account was frozen' is a real defence, and when it is not

Not every claim that an account was restricted will carry the day. A bank that simply refuses to pay out of a functioning, funded account, perhaps because it would rather not inconvenience a valued customer, is in an entirely different position from a bank reporting that the account has genuinely been inoperative, frozen or closed before the recovery demand arrived. Courts scrutinise the reason for non-payment closely, and a self-serving or unsupported claim of restriction will not survive examination.

The cleanest cases are those where the bank itself froze the account earlier for independent reasons, such as suspected fraud or a know-your-customer lapse, and can show the freeze predates the garnishee order. A harder case arises where the bank has frozen an account specifically because of suspected money-mule activity connected to the very funds the recovery officer is chasing; there, a court will look more closely at whether the freeze is a genuine compliance action or a convenient excuse for delay. Either way, the bank's position is only as strong as its paper trail.

Why natural justice matters before a bank is branded a defaulter

A recovery officer escalating against a bank is exercising what is, in substance, a penal power: the bank moves from being a neutral custodian of someone else's money to a party facing recovery action against its own assets. The Calcutta High Court's reasoning reflects a position taken by banks in related litigation as well, namely that because the escalation is penal in character, the bank is entitled to a genuine opportunity to explain its non-compliance before any adverse finding is recorded against it, not a notice that is issued and decided in the same breath.

In practice this means a recovery officer who receives a bank's explanation that an account is inoperative cannot simply disregard it and move straight to a deemed-defaulter finding. The officer has to actually test the explanation, typically by calling for account statements or a status certificate, and address why that explanation is accepted or rejected. A finding that skips this step, as happened with SBI, is vulnerable to being quashed regardless of how large or well-founded the underlying recovery demand against the establishment itself might be.

This is not the same as an ordinary decree-holder's garnishee order

Two distinct mechanisms deserve to be kept apart here. What has been discussed above concerns statutory recovery garnishee orders, issued by bodies like EPFO, ESI or a tax recovery officer under their own governing statutes, each with its own escalation ladder against a non-compliant bank. An ordinary civil decree-holder attaching a judgment-debtor's bank account instead proceeds under Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, a different procedural framework altogether, with its own notice requirements, its own timelines for the bank to disclose the account balance, and its own set of defences available to the bank as garnishee.

The bank is a third party in both situations, and some of the underlying logic, that the bank cannot be made to pay what the account does not hold, carries across. But the statutory basis, the escalation mechanism, and the available challenge route differ sharply between the two, and a bank's counsel should not assume an argument developed for one route transfers automatically to the other without checking the specific provisions actually invoked in the notice served.

What a bank, and the account holder, should document

For a bank facing this situation, the practical lesson is to document the account's status contemporaneously and keep that record ready. A certificate showing the date from which an account became inoperative, dormant or frozen, ideally generated from the bank's core system rather than drafted after the fact, is the single most persuasive piece of evidence in any later challenge. Where the bank itself imposed a freeze, the internal note recording the reason and date for that freeze should be preserved as carefully as any customer-facing record.

For an establishment whose account has been garnisheed while already inoperative, there is a parallel lesson: raise the point early, in writing, directly with the recovery officer, rather than waiting for the bank to be dragged into deemed-defaulter proceedings before the issue surfaces. A prompt, documented objection narrows what the recovery officer can later claim was never brought to their attention, and strengthens any subsequent writ challenge to an adverse order.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a bank simply refuse to honour a statutory garnishee order?

No. A bank must comply with a valid garnishee order to the extent the named account actually holds recoverable funds. What it can resist is being treated as a defaulter for a shortfall caused by the account being inoperative, frozen or empty, provided it can show and explain that status to the recovery officer.

What should an establishment do if its account was already frozen when a garnishee order arrived?

Notify the recovery officer in writing immediately, with documentary proof of when and why the account was restricted. Waiting until the bank itself is threatened with deemed-defaulter action is the wrong sequence; raising it early keeps the dispute focused on the correct facts from the outset.

Is this the same as a garnishee order in ordinary civil execution proceedings?

No. A decree-holder enforcing a money decree against a judgment-debtor's bank account proceeds under Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, a separate framework from a statutory recovery officer's prohibitory order under the ESI Act, EPF Act or tax recovery rules. The defences available to a bank differ between the two routes.

What relief can a bank seek if it is wrongly branded a deemed defaulter?

A writ petition before the jurisdictional High Court challenging the deemed-defaulter finding, on the ground that it was recorded without properly considering the account's actual status and without giving the bank a genuine opportunity to be heard, as the Calcutta High Court accepted in the SBI matter.

Does a frozen account ever justify permanent non-payment of a garnishee demand?

Not necessarily. If the freeze is lifted, or funds later become available in the account, the bank's obligation to remit under the garnishee order can revive. The defence works for the period the account was genuinely inoperative, not as a permanent discharge of the underlying demand.
