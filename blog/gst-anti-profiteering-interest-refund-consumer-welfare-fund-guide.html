GST Refunds and Anti-Profiteering Interest After Union of India v. Torrent Power: A Guide for Businesses

What the Supreme Court's decision in Union of India v. Torrent Power Ltd. means for businesses facing an anti-profiteering interest demand, or seeking a GST refund where the tax burden may already have been passed on to customers.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 25 February 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice. See our disclaimer.

Two Questions, One Case

The Supreme Court's decision in Union of India v. Torrent Power Ltd., reported as decided on 10 February 2026, touches two separate issues that frequently come up for businesses dealing with GST: when can the tax authorities charge interest on a profiteered amount under the anti-profiteering rules, and who is entitled to a GST refund where the tax in question has already been collected from, and effectively borne by, the business's own customers.

Interest on Profiteered Amounts: Not Retrospective

Rule 133(3)(c) of the CGST Rules empowers the authority examining an anti-profiteering complaint, where it finds that a business failed to pass on the benefit of a GST rate reduction or input tax credit to consumers through a commensurate price reduction, to direct that interest be paid on the profiteered amount from the date it was collected until it is returned. The question before the Supreme Court was whether this interest-direction power, inserted by a later amendment, could be applied to profiteering periods that occurred before the amendment was notified.

The Court is reported to have held that this power operates prospectively only. A business facing an anti-profiteering interest demand calculated as though the amended rule applied to an earlier period has, on this reasoning, a basis to resist that portion of the demand, since a provision creating a new financial liability is not ordinarily read backwards in time unless Parliament has said so explicitly.

Refunds and the Consumer Welfare Fund

The second issue concerned the destination of a GST refund where the tax originally charged had, as a matter of fact, been passed on to the business's own customers rather than absorbed by the business itself. Sections 54(5) and 57 of the CGST Act embody a principle against unjust enrichment: a taxpayer should not receive back money that it has already recovered from someone else down the supply chain. The Supreme Court reportedly reaffirmed that where this factual passing-on is established, the refund must be credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund rather than paid to the claiming business.

For a business, this means that a refund claim should be prepared with a clear, documented position on whether the relevant tax cost was built into the price charged to customers or absorbed internally. A business that genuinely bore the cost itself, without passing it on, remains entitled to receive the refund directly, but it should be ready to demonstrate that with pricing and accounting records if the department raises the question.

Practical Takeaways

A business that is the subject of an anti-profiteering proceeding spanning a period before the Rule 133(3)(c) amendment was notified should specifically flag the prospective-only nature of the interest power when responding to any demand. Separately, a business filing a refund claim should maintain clear documentation tracing whether the relevant tax was passed on to customers, since that single factual question will determine whether any refund actually reaches the business or is instead credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund.

Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the GST department charge interest on a profiteered amount for a period before Rule 133(3)(c) was amended?

According to the Supreme Court's reported decision in Union of India v. Torrent Power Ltd., the amendment empowering an interest direction under Rule 133(3)(c) of the CGST Rules operates prospectively only, and should not be applied to profiteering periods that preceded the amendment.

What happens to a GST refund if the tax was already collected from customers?

Under Sections 54(5) and 57 of the CGST Act, 2017, where the incidence of the tax has been passed on to the ultimate consumer, the refund amount is credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund rather than paid to the business claiming the refund, to prevent the business from being unjustly enriched by recovering the same amount twice.

How can a business show that it did not pass on a tax cost to customers?

Businesses typically rely on pricing records, invoices and accounting documentation that trace whether a specific tax component was built into the price charged to customers or absorbed internally; a clear, contemporaneous paper trail is important since the department can be expected to scrutinise this factual question closely.

Does this ruling affect anti-profiteering proceedings generally, or only interest demands?

The reported ruling specifically addresses the prospective application of the interest-direction power under Rule 133(3)(c) and a related refund question; it does not, on the reports available, disturb the underlying anti-profiteering framework itself, which continues to apply to price-reduction obligations following a GST rate cut or an input tax credit benefit.

References & Further Reading

This article summarises a reported Supreme Court decision in general terms for informational purposes. Readers should consult the full judgment in Union of India v. Torrent Power Ltd. and the current text of the CGST Act and Rules before relying on any specific point.

  3. Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, Sections 54(5) and 57 (refund and the Consumer Welfare Fund).
