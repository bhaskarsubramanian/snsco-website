Rent Agreement Essentials: Registration, Notice Period and Security Deposit Disputes

Why most rent agreements run eleven months, when registration is compulsory, and what actually happens when a landlord withholds the security deposit.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Most rent agreements in India run for eleven months not because of some special landlord-tenant custom, but because of a provision in the Registration Act, 1908 that makes registration compulsory only above a one-year term. Below that, registration is optional, though stamping is not. Security deposit disputes sit on a separate track entirely, generally outside rent-control legislation unless your state has specifically adopted a cap, and are usually fought in the ordinary civil or small-causes forum rather than before any rent authority.

This guide covers three practical questions that come up constantly: whether your agreement needs to be registered, what an unregistered agreement can and cannot do for you later, and what actually happens if a landlord refuses to return the deposit.

Why eleven months, and what registration actually requires

Section 17 of the Registration Act makes registration compulsory for a lease of immovable property from year to year, for a term exceeding one year, or reserving a yearly rent. An agreement for eleven months, or any term up to and including one year, falls outside this requirement and does not have to be registered. A longer term, or an agreement that renews automatically into a year-to-year arrangement, brings registration back into play.

Registration is not the same as stamping. Stamp duty is a tax on the instrument itself and generally applies whether the agreement runs for eleven months or eleven years; an unstamped or under-stamped document faces its own separate problems in court, independent of whether it needed to be registered. Parties are also free to register an eleven-month agreement voluntarily under Section 18 of the Act even though the law does not require it, usually because a registered document gives both sides a clearer, more easily provable record. Maharashtra is a notable exception to the general pattern: its state rent-control legislation requires a leave-and-licence or tenancy agreement to be in writing and registered regardless of term, and places that registration duty specifically on the landlord, so the eleven-month route does not avoid registration there the way it does elsewhere.

What an unregistered agreement can still do for you

Where registration was legally required but never done, Section 49 of the Registration Act bars the document from being used as evidence of the transaction it was meant to record, with a narrow exception for proving a collateral purpose unconnected with the main terms of the lease. Courts have read this exception tightly: an unregistered lease has generally been allowed in evidence to show the nature and character of a tenant's possession, but not to prove the specific terms the lease itself was supposed to fix, such as the exact tenancy period, the agreed rent figure, or a specific undertaking like vacating without notice.

For an eleven-month agreement that never needed registration in the first place, none of this applies, since Section 17 was never triggered. The practical point that actually matters for most tenants and landlords is simpler: whether registered or not, keep the signed agreement, the payment record for rent and deposit, and any written communication about renewal or termination, since disputes are won far more often on what can be shown than on what the law technically required.

Notice periods and renewal

The notice period for ending a tenancy is primarily a matter of contract: whatever the agreement specifies, whether that is one month, two months, or some other period, generally governs, subject to any state rent-control law that independently restricts eviction or renewal where that law applies to the premises. Where the agreement is silent, the general notice requirement under the Transfer of Property Act for a tenancy from month to month typically fills the gap, though this depends on how the tenancy was actually structured and should not be assumed without checking the specific agreement and, where relevant, the applicable state rent law.

A common source of dispute is an agreement that lapses on its stated date but where the tenant simply continues in possession and keeps paying rent that the landlord accepts. Depending on the facts, this can be read as a holding over on the old terms or as creating a fresh tenancy, and the characterisation affects what notice, if any, is needed to end it. Where continued occupation after the term is intended to be on a month-to-month basis pending a fresh agreement, it is worth recording that in writing at the time, rather than leaving it to be argued over later.

Security deposit disputes: what the law actually caps

There is no single national ceiling on residential security deposits that applies everywhere. The Model Tenancy Act, 2021, a central framework law, proposes a cap of two months' rent for residential premises and six months' rent for non-residential premises, along with a district-level Rent Authority, Rent Court and Rent Tribunal structure, but it only takes effect in a state once that state adopts it in some form, and adoption and implementation vary considerably. Depositing or demanding more than two months' rent is common practice in many parts of the country and is not automatically unlawful unless your specific state has adopted a binding cap.

What is more consistently enforceable is the underlying contractual obligation: a deposit is refundable, net of any lawful deductions for damage beyond normal wear and tear or unpaid dues, once the tenant vacates and hands back possession. A landlord who simply withholds the deposit without a specific, documented basis for a deduction is exposed to a straightforward recovery claim, and the absence of a national cap does not affect that underlying right to a refund.

Where to actually file a deposit dispute

Where a specific rent-control statute and its rent authority or controller apply to the premises, a deposit dispute connected to that tenancy may fall within that authority's jurisdiction rather than the ordinary civil court; this depends on the particular state law and, as with eviction, getting the forum wrong can cost time even where the underlying claim is sound. Where no such statute applies, the claim is an ordinary money claim, and in cities that have a Small Causes Court, that court often has a faster, simpler procedure for sums within its pecuniary jurisdiction, which varies by state and by court and should be checked locally rather than assumed.

Before filing anywhere, a written demand for the refund, referencing the agreement, the amount paid, and the date of vacating, is worth sending and keeping on record regardless of which forum eventually hears the matter; it establishes the date from which the landlord was put on notice and often resolves straightforward cases without any filing at all. Where an arbitration clause exists in the agreement, courts have in at least one reported decision treated a security deposit dispute as arbitrable, so check the agreement for such a clause before assuming the civil or small-causes route is the only option.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my eleven-month rent agreement need to be registered?

Generally no, since registration under the Registration Act is compulsory only for a lease exceeding one year or reserving a yearly rent. You can still register it voluntarily for a clearer record, and in Maharashtra registration is required regardless of the term.

Can I use an unregistered agreement to prove my tenancy in court?

If registration was never legally required for your term, this question doesn't arise. If it was required and skipped, the document generally cannot prove the specific terms it was meant to record, though it may still help show the basic nature of your possession.

Is there a legal limit on how much security deposit a landlord can charge?

Not nationally. A two-month cap exists under the Model Tenancy Act, 2021, but only binds tenancies in states that have adopted it, and adoption varies and is still developing in several states, so check your specific state's position rather than assuming the cap applies.

My landlord won't return my deposit and isn't giving a reason. What can I do?

Send a written demand first, referencing the amount, the agreement and your vacating date. If that doesn't work, a money recovery claim in the small-causes court or the ordinary civil court, or the relevant rent authority where one applies to your tenancy, is the usual next step.

My agreement ended but I'm still living there and paying rent. Am I protected?

Possibly, but the position depends on the facts: continued occupation with accepted rent can be read as a holding over on the old terms or as a new tenancy, and this affects what notice is needed to end it. Put the arrangement in writing if you want certainty rather than relying on how a court might later read your conduct.
