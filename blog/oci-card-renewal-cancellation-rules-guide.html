OCI Card Renewal and Cancellation: Myths NRIs and OCIs Still Believe

What actually triggers OCI card reissue after the 2021 relaxation, and the real grounds and process for cancelling OCI registration under Section 7D.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 20 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

An OCI card does not expire and does not need to be reissued every time its holder gets a new passport. Since a relaxation that took effect in April 2021, reissue is required at only two points in a person's life, tied to age rather than to each new passport. Separately, the government can cancel an OCI registration, but only on specific grounds under Section 7D of the Citizenship Act, 1955, and only after giving the holder a real opportunity to respond, as several Delhi High Court rulings between 2024 and 2026 have reaffirmed.

What follows sets out the common beliefs OCI holders carry about renewal and cancellation, and what the rules and recent rulings actually say against each one.

Myth: the OCI card must be reissued every time a new passport is obtained

This was true before April 2021 and is a major reason OCI holders still get this wrong. Under the pre-2021 position, a foreign passport renewal meant a fresh OCI card had to be obtained to match it, which created real friction at airports when the card still carried an old passport number.

Guidelines issued around 16 and 20 April 2021 relaxed this. An OCI cardholder now needs a fresh OCI card linked to a new passport only twice: once after a passport is issued following the holder turning 20, and again once after a passport is issued following the holder turning 50. Between those two points, a passport can be renewed any number of times without triggering a fresh OCI card. Indian missions abroad circulated these guidelines directly, and the relaxation remains the current position.

A person who got their OCI card as a child, say at age eight, and whose next passport renewal came at age fifteen, does not need a fresh card at fifteen under these rules; the trigger is tied to turning 20 or turning 50, not to every passport renewal in between. There was also a transitional window, reported as running from 31 December 2021 to 31 December 2022 on account of pandemic-related disruption, during which affected cardholders already due for reissue under the old rule were given extra time; that transitional window has since lapsed and should not be relied on today.

Myth: you must still carry the old passport along with the OCI card

Before 2021, an OCI holder travelling on a card linked to an old passport number was expected to carry that old passport too, as proof of the link between the card and the person. This caused obvious practical problems for anyone who had lost or discarded an expired passport.

The 2021 guidelines removed this requirement. A holder travelling on an OCI card that still shows an old passport number is not required to produce that old passport; carrying the current, valid passport is what matters. This is a travel-document point rather than a change to the underlying OCI registration, so it does not affect whether the card itself is valid.

This mattered in practice for anyone who had misplaced an old, expired passport years after it ceased to be useful for any other purpose, and who previously risked being stopped at immigration purely over a mismatch between the passport number printed on the OCI card and the passport actually in hand. Airlines and immigration counters still sometimes ask about the mismatch out of habit, which is why it helps to carry a printed or digital copy of the actual guideline, or at minimum to know the April 2021 date to cite, when the question comes up.

Myth: an OCI card is just a long-stay visa, nothing more

An OCI card functions as a multiple-entry, multi-purpose lifelong visa to visit India, which already distinguishes it from an ordinary tourist or business visa that has to be renewed on its own schedule. It also replaced the older PIO card scheme, which the government discontinued.

OCI status is not citizenship. An OCI holder cannot vote in Indian elections, cannot hold most government jobs, and cannot buy agricultural land or a farmhouse in India in the same way an Indian citizen can, a restriction covered in more detail in a separate guide on NRI and OCI purchase of agricultural land under FEMA. Property, banking and succession rights that flow from OCI registration are real, but they sit alongside these specific exclusions rather than replicating citizenship.

The PIO card scheme that OCI effectively absorbed had its own separate registration and renewal cycle, and the two are not the same document even though both were historically aimed at persons of Indian origin settled abroad. Anyone still holding only an old PIO card, rather than a current OCI card, should treat converting to OCI as something to actually finish rather than an optional upgrade, since the two schemes are not run or recognised identically today.

Myth: OCI registration can only be cancelled for a criminal conviction

Section 7D of the Citizenship Act, 1955 lists several independent grounds for cancelling OCI registration, not just conviction for an offence. Clause (e) allows cancellation where the central government is satisfied that it is necessary in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of India, friendly relations with a foreign country, or the general public. A separate clause, 7D(da), inserted by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019, covers violation of specified laws the government notifies for this purpose.

These grounds give the government a wide discretion on paper, which is exactly why the procedural safeguard that accompanies Section 7D has mattered so much in recent litigation, discussed next. The two clauses are also conceptually different: clause (e) turns on the government's own satisfaction about sovereignty, security, foreign relations or public interest, which is inherently harder for a court to second-guess on the merits, while clause (da) turns on an actual violation of a specified law, which is at least capable of being tested against facts and evidence in the way any factual allegation is.

Myth: once a cancellation order is passed, it is final and no hearing was needed

Section 7D carries a proviso requiring that no order cancelling OCI registration be passed unless the holder has been given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. The Delhi High Court has read this proviso with some teeth. In the case concerning academic Ashok Swain, the Court set aside a cancellation order after finding the government's process mechanical, holding that a cancellation under clause (e) requires disclosing the material particulars and evidence relied on before the holder responds, not simply issuing a conclusion.

In a separate matter, Khalid Jahangir Qazi, the Delhi High Court found that broad, unparticularised allegations do not give an OCI holder an adequate opportunity to be heard, since a person cannot meaningfully respond to vague accusations. The pattern across these rulings is consistent: the government may have wide substantive grounds, but it cannot skip disclosure and a genuine chance to respond before acting on them.

For an OCI holder actually facing this situation, the practical takeaway is to ask, in writing, for the specific material and evidence the government is relying on as soon as any adverse communication arrives, rather than responding generally to a vague notice. A response that engages with specifics tends to put the authority in the position of either producing the material it relied on or conceding it has none, which is closer to what these rulings were actually testing.

Myth: blacklisting under the Foreigners Act is a separate process with no safeguards

Some OCI holders are placed on a blacklist or denied entry under the Foreigners Act rather than through a formal Section 7D cancellation order, and the government has sometimes treated this as a lighter-touch process without the same safeguards. The Delhi High Court has not accepted that distinction where the substance is the same. In a 2025 ruling, the Court held that where blacklisting rests on the same underlying grounds that would otherwise justify a Section 7D cancellation, the statutory safeguards under Section 7D apply regardless of which label the government uses for the action.

The practical point for an affected OCI holder is that the label the government puts on an adverse action does not decide what procedure was owed; the substance of what is actually being done to the person's status does. An OCI holder denied entry at the border, or informed that they have been placed on a watch list, should ask promptly and in writing for the specific grounds, and should not assume that because no formal 'cancellation order' was ever issued, the Section 7D safeguards have no bearing on the action taken against them.

What an affected OCI holder should actually do

If a cancellation notice, blacklisting communication or entry denial arrives, the first step is to request the specific grounds and the material relied on in writing, and to keep a dated record of that request. Responding substantively, rather than generally, within whatever time is granted matters more than the length of the response itself, since these rulings have turned on whether the holder was actually given something concrete to respond to.

Where a hearing is denied outright, or where the grounds given remain vague despite a specific request, the Delhi High Court has been the forum these cases have actually gone to, generally by way of a writ petition challenging the process rather than the government's underlying discretion on sovereignty, security or public interest grounds, which courts are naturally more reluctant to second-guess. This distinction, between challenging the process followed and challenging the substance of the government's assessment, is central to why these petitions have succeeded where they have: they focused on the missing hearing and missing disclosure, not on relitigating the underlying security or policy judgment itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my OCI card have an expiry date?

No. The card itself does not expire with a validity period in the way a visa does. What triggers a fresh card is a new passport obtained after turning 20, and again after turning 50; between those points, no reissue is required on a passport renewal alone.

Can the government cancel my OCI card without telling me why?

No, not lawfully. Section 7D's proviso requires a reasonable opportunity to be heard, and Delhi High Court rulings have required the government to disclose the specific material and evidence it relies on before cancelling, rather than issuing only a conclusion.

I was blacklisted and denied entry, but no formal cancellation order was passed against my OCI status. Does that matter?

It may not, if the blacklisting rests on grounds that overlap with Section 7D cancellation grounds. Courts have applied the same hearing safeguard to blacklisting in that situation, treating the substance of the action as what counts rather than its label.

I lost my old passport that my OCI card is linked to. Can I still travel?

Yes, generally. Since the 2021 relaxation, you are expected to carry your current valid passport, not the old one the card references, so losing the old passport does not by itself stop you from travelling on an unreissued OCI card.

If my OCI registration is cancelled, do I lose my property and banking rights in India too?

Largely yes, because those rights generally flow from the OCI registration itself rather than existing independently of it. This is a serious consequence, which is part of why the hearing safeguard before cancellation matters so much in practice, and why getting legal grounds and evidence disclosed early, before a final order, is worth pursuing actively.
