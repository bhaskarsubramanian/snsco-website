Medical Negligence in India: The Consumer, Civil and Criminal Routes to a Claim

How a medical negligence claim proceeds in India — the consumer forum, civil suit and criminal routes, the Bolam/Bolitho standard, and NMC complaints.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice — see our disclaimer

Four Different Routes, Serving Different Purposes

A patient (or, where the patient has died, their legal heirs) harmed by substandard medical care in India generally has up to four distinct routes available, and they are not mutually exclusive in the sense of being forced to pick only one forever — though each has its own standard of proof, its own remedy, and its own practical trade-offs. These are: a complaint before the consumer forum for deficiency in service; a civil suit for damages; a criminal complaint in cases of genuinely gross negligence; and a regulatory complaint to the medical council over the practitioner's professional conduct. Understanding what each route can and cannot deliver is the first step in deciding how to proceed.

The Consumer Forum Route

In Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha (1995), the Supreme Court held that medical services rendered for consideration fall within the definition of "service" under consumer protection law, opening the door for patients to bring a deficiency-in-service complaint before the consumer commission structure now established under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 — the District, State or National Commission, depending on the value of the claim under the applicable pecuniary jurisdiction thresholds. This route is often the most accessible for patients: it does not require the more elaborate civil-suit procedure, allows the patient to appear in person if they choose, and can award compensation for medical expenses, pain and suffering, and related losses. It is worth noting that free treatment rendered at a government hospital may sit outside this consumer-forum route depending on the specific facts (whether any consideration was paid, directly or indirectly, by or on behalf of the patient), so that particular scenario should be assessed on its own facts.

The Standard: What Actually Counts as Negligence

Indian courts have generally applied a version of the English Bolam test, as refined in Bolitho, to judge medical negligence: a doctor's conduct is not negligent if it conforms to a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical practitioners skilled in that particular field, provided that accepted practice itself would withstand logical scrutiny by the court. This standard exists because medicine, unlike many other fields, routinely involves genuine clinical judgment calls where more than one course of action can be reasonable — the law does not treat a bad outcome, or even a treatment approach that, in hindsight, was not the optimal choice, as automatically negligent. A negligence claim needs expert medical evidence to establish that the treating doctor's conduct fell below what a reasonably competent practitioner in that specialty would have done in the same circumstances — this is why expert opinion is almost always central to how these cases are actually contested and decided.

The Civil Suit Route

A patient can also bring an ordinary civil suit for damages founded in the tort of negligence, filed before the civil court having jurisdiction, rather than (or in addition to, depending on strategy and the specific facts) a consumer complaint. A civil suit follows the ordinary Code of Civil Procedure timeline and evidentiary process, which is typically slower and more procedurally involved than a consumer complaint, but may be the more appropriate route for very high-value or legally complex claims, or where the specific relief sought does not fit neatly within the consumer forum's more summary process.

The Criminal Route — A Deliberately Higher Bar

Criminal liability for medical negligence — typically prosecuted under the provisions of the criminal code dealing with causing death or grievous hurt by a negligent or rash act — is available in India, but the Supreme Court in Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab (2005) held that the threshold for criminal liability is deliberately set higher than for civil or consumer liability: the negligence must be gross or the conduct must amount to recklessness, not the ordinary degree of negligence sufficient to establish civil liability. The Court also laid down procedural safeguards recognising the specific risks of over-criminalising medical practice — including that a doctor should generally not be arrested in a routine negligence complaint unless it is necessary for furthering the investigation, and that a private complaint alleging negligence against a doctor should generally not proceed without an independent and competent medical opinion supporting a prima facie case of recklessness. This deliberately higher bar reflects a policy concern that treating ordinary clinical misjudgment as a crime would push doctors toward excessively defensive practice, rather than genuinely improving patient safety.

The Regulatory (NMC/State Medical Council) Route

Separately from any claim for compensation, a patient can complain about a registered medical practitioner's professional conduct to the relevant State Medical Council, and in appropriate cases to the Ethics and Medical Registration Board of the National Medical Commission, which replaced the erstwhile Medical Council of India's regulatory functions. This route can result in disciplinary consequences for the practitioner — a warning, suspension, or removal from the medical register — but does not itself award compensation to the patient; it exists to protect the integrity of the profession and public safety, not to compensate the individual complainant, so it is generally pursued alongside, not instead of, one of the compensation-focused routes above where the patient is also seeking damages.

Choosing a Route — Practical Considerations

The consumer forum route is generally the fastest and most accessible path to compensation for most patients, and is the appropriate starting point for the great majority of medical negligence grievances. A civil suit may be worth considering for particularly high-value or complex claims. A criminal complaint should be pursued only where the facts genuinely support gross negligence or recklessness — not as a routine add-on to every claim — both because the legal threshold is high and because the Jacob Mathew safeguards mean an unsupported criminal complaint is unlikely to progress. And a regulatory complaint to the medical council is worth filing where the primary concern is the practitioner's fitness to continue practising, independent of any compensation claim. In every route, securing the complete medical records — case sheets, test reports, consent forms, discharge summaries and prescriptions — as early as possible, and obtaining an independent expert medical opinion on the standard of care, are the two steps that most consistently determine whether a claim can actually be substantiated.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I file a medical negligence complaint before a consumer commission?

Yes. Since the Supreme Court's ruling in Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha (1995), medical services rendered for consideration are treated as a 'service' under consumer protection law, so a patient (or their legal heirs) can bring a deficiency-in-service complaint before the appropriate District, State or National Consumer Commission, subject to the ordinary pecuniary jurisdiction and limitation rules that apply to any consumer complaint. Free treatment at a government hospital may fall outside this route depending on the facts, and should be assessed specifically.

What legal standard is used to judge whether a doctor was negligent?

Indian courts have generally applied the Bolam test, as refined by Bolitho — a doctor is not negligent if their conduct conforms to a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical practitioners skilled in that field, provided that accepted practice itself withstands logical scrutiny. A bad outcome or an honest error of clinical judgment, without more, does not by itself establish negligence; the claim has to show the doctor's conduct fell below what a reasonably competent practitioner in that field would have done in the circumstances.

Can a doctor be criminally prosecuted for medical negligence?

Yes, but the threshold is deliberately higher than for a civil or consumer claim. Following the Supreme Court's ruling in Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab (2005), criminal liability under provisions dealing with causing death or hurt by a negligent act requires gross negligence or recklessness — a high degree of negligence, not the ordinary negligence sufficient for civil liability — and the Court laid down procedural safeguards, including that a doctor should generally not be arrested in a routine negligence case without an independent medical opinion supporting a prima facie case of recklessness.

Is there a separate route to complain to a medical regulator?

Yes — a complaint about a registered medical practitioner's professional conduct can be made to the State Medical Council, and in appropriate cases to the National Medical Commission's Ethics and Medical Registration Board, which can investigate professional misconduct and, where warranted, take disciplinary action including suspension or removal from the medical register. This regulatory route is separate from, and does not itself award, monetary compensation to the patient — that requires the consumer or civil route.

How is compensation calculated in a medical negligence claim?

There is no single fixed formula; compensation is assessed on the specific facts, typically covering medical expenses already incurred and reasonably anticipated future treatment, loss of income and earning capacity, and non-pecuniary damages for pain, suffering and loss of amenities — analogous in structure to compensation principles used in personal injury and wrongful death claims generally, adapted to the medical negligence context, and determined by the forum hearing the specific case.
