Demolition or Sealing Notice for Unauthorised Construction: The Notice, Hearing and Appeal Route

What a show cause notice for unauthorised construction must say, the hearing, the appeal window, and what courts now say on regularisation.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A demolition or sealing notice rarely arrives out of nowhere. It usually follows an inspection, a complaint from a neighbour, or a routine survey, and it is meant to be a show cause notice, not a final order. The owner is entitled to be told what the alleged violation is, given an opportunity to respond or appear for a hearing, and given a recorded order before any demolition or sealing actually happens. Skipping that sequence, or demolishing before the time given to respond has run out, is itself a ground to challenge the action, independent of whether the construction is in fact unauthorised.

This guide sets out the general shape of that process under the statutes development authorities and municipal bodies most commonly rely on, in Uttar Pradesh under the Urban Planning and Development Act, 1973 and in Delhi under the Municipal Corporation Act, 1957, the appeal routes those laws provide, and the more recent and considerably less forgiving stance the Supreme Court has taken on compounding and regularisation. It is written for an owner who has received a notice and needs to understand the process, not as a way to avoid compliance with a genuinely unauthorised structure.

The show cause notice has to say what you are accused of

Under Section 27 of the Uttar Pradesh Urban Planning and Development Act, 1973, no demolition order can be passed without first issuing a show cause notice that identifies the nature of the unauthorised construction, essentially which part of the structure deviates from the sanctioned plan or was raised without sanction at all. A notice that simply alleges unauthorised construction in general terms, without specifying what the authority says is wrong, makes it difficult for the owner to respond meaningfully and has been challenged successfully on that basis. The same logic applies where sealing rather than demolition is contemplated: a separate show cause notice is required before sealing under the applicable provision, and that notice too has to give the owner a defined opportunity to respond.

In Delhi, the equivalent machinery sits in the Delhi Municipal Corporation Act, 1957, where demolition has been ordered under Sections 343 and 344 following a show cause notice, sealing is handled under a distinct provision requiring its own notice, and a separate vacation notice power exists to require occupants to leave premises that have been sealed or are to be demolished. The precise section numbers and time limits in individual notices should always be checked against the notice itself and the current text of the Act, since municipal practice varies and notices are sometimes challenged successfully simply because they cite the wrong provision or misdescribe the property.

The personal hearing and what the authority is supposed to consider

Once a show cause notice is issued, the owner is ordinarily given a window to file a reply and, if the authority proceeds to a hearing, to appear and explain the position, whether that is producing a sanctioned plan that the authority missed, showing that the construction falls within a permissible deviation, or arguing that no unauthorised construction exists at all. State guidance issued in some jurisdictions in the past couple of years has added procedural buffers around this stage, including a minimum period that must elapse after the notice is received before a demolition order can even be made, separate from the later period that must elapse after the order itself before demolition is carried out. Owners should read their notice carefully for the specific timelines it states, since these buffer periods have been introduced at different times and are not uniform across every authority or every type of notice.

The authority is expected to actually consider the reply and any documents filed, not treat the hearing as a formality before an outcome that was already decided. Where an order recites that a hearing was given but the order itself shows no engagement with the specific points the owner raised, that is a recognised ground to challenge the order on appeal or in a writ petition, though courts remain reluctant to interfere merely because the owner is unhappy with the outcome rather than because the process was genuinely deficient.

The appeal: where it goes and the time you have

Under the Uttar Pradesh Act, an aggrieved person can appeal a demolition order to the Chairman of the development authority within thirty days of the order, and the Chairman, after hearing the parties, can allow the appeal, dismiss it, or vary the order. Where the demolition order is never properly served, or only a bare order without the detailed reasoning is communicated, the thirty-day clock and the practical ability to file a meaningful appeal can both be affected, and this has been argued before the Allahabad High Court as frustrating the statutory right of appeal altogether.

The Delhi Municipal Corporation Act similarly gives demolition and sealing orders their own appeal route, including an appeal before the Appellate Tribunal constituted under the Act for certain categories of orders. The appeal provisions and the specific tribunal or authority to approach differ depending on whether the order is for demolition, sealing, or vacation of premises, so the notice itself, or a quick check with the issuing office, should confirm which appellate forum applies before an owner spends the limited appeal window approaching the wrong one.

Writ jurisdiction: a limited backstop, not a substitute for the appeal

A writ petition under Article 226 can be filed where the notice or order suffers from a jurisdictional defect, a complete failure to follow the mandatory notice and hearing requirements, or patent illegality, and courts have intervened in cases where service of the order was disputed or where a notice named the wrong property or the wrong owner. Courts are considerably less willing to use writ jurisdiction to re-examine disputed questions of fact, such as whether a particular structure actually matches the sanctioned plan, since that kind of dispute is generally better suited to the statutory appeal where evidence can be led and examined.

It is also not a reliable way to buy time once the statutory appeal period has lapsed without an appeal being filed. Courts have been clear that a party who sleeps on the appeal remedy and then approaches the High Court only once demolition is imminent faces a steep hurdle in showing that intervention is still warranted, and several reported orders simply direct the party back to the statutory forum, or require urgent compliance with interim steps, rather than deciding the underlying dispute themselves.

Compounding and regularisation: why courts have become far stricter

Municipal laws in several states allow compounding of certain building violations on payment of a fee, and this has historically given owners of minor deviations a route to retain the structure rather than face demolition. The Supreme Court has recently cautioned lower courts against treating the mere existence of a compounding provision as a reason to stay or set aside a demolition order, holding in one 2025 matter that the availability of compounding is not by itself a ground to interfere with an otherwise valid demolition direction. In a separate 2025 order, the Court went further, declining to allow an owner to seek regularisation of an unauthorised two-floor structure after the fact, observing that someone who has shown no regard for the sanctioned plan cannot expect a court to then let them regularise what they built without permission.

This does not mean regularisation is never available; where a statute or scheme genuinely provides for it, and especially where an earlier court order has already protected the structure pending a regularisation application, courts have allowed that process to run its course rather than short-circuiting it with a fresh demolition direction. The practical takeaway for an owner facing a notice is that betting on an eventual compounding fee as a fallback has become considerably less reliable than it may once have been, and the stronger position is almost always to show, at the show cause stage itself, that the construction was in fact sanctioned or falls within a permitted deviation, rather than to concede the violation and hope for leniency later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an authority demolish my property without any notice at all?

No. Both the Uttar Pradesh Urban Planning and Development Act and the Delhi Municipal Corporation Act require a show cause notice describing the alleged violation before a demolition or sealing order can be made, and demolishing without that notice and without giving the stated response period is itself a ground to challenge the action.

How long do I have to appeal a demolition order in Uttar Pradesh?

Generally thirty days from the date of the order under Section 27(2) of the 1973 Act, running to the Chairman of the development authority. If the order was not properly served on you, that can affect when the clock actually starts, but do not rely on that argument instead of filing promptly.

Will paying a compounding fee stop the demolition?

Not reliably anymore. The Supreme Court has recently held that the mere availability of compounding under municipal regulations is not, by itself, a reason to stay or set aside a demolition order, so compounding should not be treated as a guaranteed fallback.

Can I go straight to the High Court instead of filing the statutory appeal?

You can file a writ petition where the notice or order suffers a genuine jurisdictional or procedural defect, but courts are reluctant to use writ jurisdiction to decide disputed factual questions and even more reluctant once the statutory appeal period has been allowed to lapse without any appeal filed.

Does an occupant who is a tenant, not the owner, get any separate notice?

Municipal laws typically provide a separate vacation notice mechanism requiring occupants to leave premises that are to be sealed or demolished, distinct from the notice issued to the owner on the underlying violation. A tenant served with such a notice should check it against the specific provision cited rather than assume it mirrors the owner's notice.
