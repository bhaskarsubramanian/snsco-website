Section 22 of the Hindu Succession Act: A Co-Heir's Right to Buy Before a Stranger Does

Section 22 of the Hindu Succession Act lets a Class I heir buy out a co-heir's share before an outsider does. Scope, price and limits, as on Nov 2026.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 27 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Three siblings inherit their father's house in equal shares after he dies without a will. One of them wants out, and is ready to sell their one-third share to a neighbour who has made an offer. The other two would rather keep the house in the family, or at least decide who the new co-owner is going to be. Most people assume there is nothing they can do about it once a willing buyer and a willing seller agree on a price. For property that came to all three of them by inheritance, that assumption is wrong.

Section 22 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 gives a co-heir in this position a right to buy the departing heir's share before it goes to an outsider. The Supreme Court revisited the provision on 14 July 2026 in Mahinder and Others v. Puran Singh, holding that the right applies to agricultural land just as it applies to a house or a shop. This guide sets out what the right actually covers, how the price gets fixed, and what a co-heir who wants to use it, or resist it, needs to do, as the law stands in November 2026.

What Section 22 Actually Gives a Co-Heir

Section 22 applies in a narrow set of circumstances. Two or more people have to have inherited an interest in immovable property, or in a business carried on by the deceased, through intestate succession, meaning the deceased person left no will and the property passed under the Act's own rules of devolution to the Class I heirs listed in the Schedule: a son, a daughter, a widow, a mother, and the other specific relatives the Schedule names. If even one of those conditions is missing, the section has nothing to say. Property left by a will does not attract it. Property the heirs bought together out of their own money, rather than inherited, does not attract it either.

Where the conditions are met, the provision gives every other Class I heir a preferential right to acquire the interest that one heir proposes to transfer, whether by sale, gift, or otherwise, to someone outside the group. It is usually described as a statutory right of pre-emption built into the law of succession itself, rather than a general rule about family property. The right exists only among the heirs who took the same inheritance together, and it falls away once the property has already been formally partitioned between them, since at that point each heir holds a separate, clearly demarcated share rather than an undivided interest in the whole.

The 2026 Ruling: Mahinder and Others v. Puran Singh

The question reached the Supreme Court after the Punjab and Haryana High Court, and the courts below it, had already held, across more than one round of litigation, that a co-heir's preferential right under Section 22 extends to agricultural land inherited from the same intestate. A bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and Nongmeikapam Kotiswar Singh dismissed the appeal and upheld that position on 14 July 2026, in a judgment reported as 2026 INSC 698.

The appellants had argued that Section 22 could not stand alongside, or had effectively been overtaken by, the Constitution Bench's 1986 ruling in Atam Prakash v. State of Haryana, which struck down a different pre-emption right, the one a landowner's neighbour or co-sharer held under Punjab's own pre-emption law, as discriminatory and arbitrary. The Court rejected that argument. It held that Atam Prakash dealt with the Punjab Pre-emption Act's right, which arises simply from owning adjoining land or being a co-sharer, and never examined or decided the validity of Section 22 of the Hindu Succession Act at all. The two provisions, in the Court's reasoning, are not the same kind of right: Section 22 is tied to intestate succession and arises only among heirs who inherited together, a materially different basis from a neighbour's or co-sharer's general right of pre-emption under the Punjab statute.

The Court also confirmed that Parliament had the legislative competence to enact Section 22 as part of a central succession law, a point the appellants had tried to reopen. For a family working out what to do with inherited agricultural land anywhere in India, the practical effect is straightforward: a sibling or other Class I co-heir cannot be told that farmland sits outside Section 22's reach merely because it is agricultural rather than a house or a shop.

How the Price Gets Fixed When Heirs Disagree

Section 22(1) creates the preferential right itself. Section 22(2) deals with what happens when the heirs cannot agree on a price for the share being bought out: the statute lets the court step in and fix the consideration, rather than leaving the transaction to collapse for want of agreement. In practice this usually means the heir exercising the right has to file a suit, and the court then has to value the share, typically on the basis of a valuation report, prevailing sale prices for comparable property in the area, and any offer the outside buyer had actually put on the table.

Section 22(3) deals with a different problem: two or more of the remaining heirs both want to buy the departing heir's share, and neither will give way. The provision resolves this through a bidding process, under which the heir offering the highest price for the share is preferred over the others. This keeps the right from becoming a fresh source of dispute among the heirs trying to exercise it, by substituting a price contest among themselves for a dispute with the court.

When a Co-Heir Sells Without Telling the Others

The statute says nothing about how a departing heir is supposed to notify the others before transferring their share, and the Supreme Court has itself observed, in an earlier ruling interpreting the section, that it does not provide for any notice being given to the co-heirs who hold the preferential right. This gap matters in practice, because the usual case reaching a court is not a departing heir asking permission in advance, but a sale that has already happened, with the aggrieved co-heirs finding out about it only afterward, sometimes from the buyer trying to take possession.

Where that happens, the established route is a suit by the aggrieved heir or heirs for enforcement of the right conferred by Section 22, asking the court to treat them as the rightful purchaser of the share on the same or a court-fixed price, rather than a suit to declare the earlier sale void outright. Courts have differed on exactly which limitation period applies to such a suit: some decisions have treated it as a suit to enforce a right of pre-emption, attracting the shorter one-year period under Article 97 of the Limitation Act, 1963, while others have applied a longer residuary period instead. An heir who learns of an unauthorised sale should not assume a generous window and should move to file as soon as the sale comes to light.

What the Right Does Not Cover

Section 22 is easy to overstate. It gives no right to block a sale altogether, only a right to step into the outside buyer's shoes on the same terms, or on terms a court fixes. It does not apply to a transfer between the co-heirs themselves, since the point of the right is to keep the property within the group of heirs rather than letting an outsider in. It does not revive once the heirs have partitioned the property and each holds a separately demarcated portion, because at that stage there is no longer an undivided interest for the section to attach to.

It is also best kept separate from other pre-emption rights that exist under different statutes, such as a co-sharer's or neighbour's right under some State pre-emption laws, or a tenant's statutory right of first refusal under certain rent or tenancy laws. Those rights carry their own conditions and their own fate in the courts, and Atam Prakash's striking down of the Punjab Pre-emption Act's version does not touch Section 22, as Mahinder confirms. Mixing up the two is a common source of wasted argument in these cases.

Practical Steps for a Family Facing This Situation

An heir who wants to sell their share to an outsider should tell the other Class I heirs in writing before doing so, even though the statute does not demand it, because it heads off a dispute that is otherwise likely to follow and because a buyer who later discovers the property came with an unresolved Section 22 claim attached to it will usually walk away or demand a lower price. An heir who wants to exercise the right should act quickly once a transfer to an outsider becomes known, gather independent valuation evidence early, and be ready to deposit or demonstrate the funds to pay the price a court fixes, since courts are generally unwilling to let an heir hold the right open indefinitely without being able to pay for it.

Anyone buying a share in inherited property from one heir, where other Class I heirs exist, should check before paying whether the property devolved by intestate succession and whether the other heirs were informed, because a purchase that ignores Section 22 can be unwound later even though the sale deed itself gets registered without any objection from the registering authority, which does not examine succession rights before accepting a document for registration.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Section 22 apply to a house my father left me and my siblings under his will?

No. Section 22 only applies where the property devolved by intestate succession, meaning the person died without a will and the property passed under the Hindu Succession Act's own rules. Property left by a registered will is governed by the will and the law of wills, not by Section 22.

Can I use Section 22 to stop my brother from selling his share to anyone at all?

No. The right only lets you buy the share yourself, on the same terms the outside buyer offered or on a price a court fixes, instead of letting the outsider in. It does not let you block the sale outright if you are unwilling or unable to pay for the share.

Does Section 22 apply to agricultural land?

Yes. The Supreme Court confirmed this in Mahinder and Others v. Puran Singh (2026 INSC 698), decided on 14 July 2026, rejecting the argument that the 1986 ruling in Atam Prakash, which struck down a different pre-emption right under Punjab's own law, had any bearing on Section 22.

What if two of us both want to buy out the third heir's share?

Section 22(3) resolves this through a bidding process between the heirs who both want to buy, with the one offering the higher price preferred. The court steps in to run this process if the heirs cannot settle it between themselves.

How long do I have to go to court if I only find out about the sale after it happened?

Courts have taken different views on the exact limitation period, with some applying the one-year period under Article 97 of the Limitation Act for suits enforcing a right of pre-emption and others applying a longer period. Given that split, treat the deadline as short and move to file as soon as you learn of the sale.
