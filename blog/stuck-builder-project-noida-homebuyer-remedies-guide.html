A Stalled Builder Project in Noida or Greater Noida: What Homebuyers Can Actually Do

What UP RERA, the Consumer Commission, an IBC filing and the courts can and cannot do for a buyer stuck with a stalled Noida or Greater Noida flat.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 3 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A buyer whose Noida or Greater Noida flat has been stuck for years is not without options, but which one actually moves the project depends on why it stopped. As on today's date, four routes exist side by side: a complaint before UP RERA that ends in a Recovery Certificate executed through the District Magistrate, a compensation claim before the Consumer Commission, an insolvency application under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code if enough buyers join together, and, in the largest stalled projects, a case already pending before the Allahabad High Court or the Supreme Court that an individual buyer can try to join rather than start afresh. None of these is quick, and the IBC route in particular only works once a project crosses a certain scale of default.

The complication that is specific to Noida and Greater Noida is the Authority's lease. A developer here does not own the land outright; it holds a lease from the Noida, Greater Noida or Yamuna Expressway Industrial Development Authority, and it is supposed to pay the Authority lease premium and dues in instalments out of what it collects from buyers. When a builder stops paying the Authority, the Authority typically refuses to register the conveyance deed for any flat in that project, even for a buyer who has paid the builder in full. That single fact explains most of what follows.

Why Noida and Greater Noida Projects Stall More Often Than Elsewhere

Three things combine here that do not always combine elsewhere. First, the leasehold structure means a buyer's title is only as good as the builder's standing with the Authority; a buyer cannot get a clean registry if the builder owes the Authority money, no matter how current the buyer's own payments are. Second, many Noida and Greater Noida projects from the 2010-2014 boom were launched on borrowed land premium, with developers betting on future sales to pay the Authority later. When sales slowed, the dues piled up and construction stopped at the same time. Third, several large developers in this belt, including Amrapali, Supertech and a number of smaller groups, ended up simultaneously facing RERA complaints, consumer cases, bank recovery suits and eventually insolvency, so a single project's fate often gets tied up in proceedings a buyer never started and cannot control.

This is why advice that works for a stalled project in another city, such as simply filing a RERA complaint and waiting for a refund order, does not fully answer the Noida or Greater Noida buyer's problem. A refund or compensation order against an insolvent, dues-defaulting builder is often a paper victory unless it is paired with pressure on the Authority to release the project, or the project is taken over by someone who can pay off the Authority.

A UP RERA Complaint and the Recovery Certificate

The first and least expensive step is still a complaint to the UP Real Estate Regulatory Authority. A buyer can seek possession with delay compensation, a refund with interest, or a direction compelling the promoter to complete specified work, depending on what is realistic for that project. UP RERA has reported resolving well over fifteen thousand such complaints, with a large share ending either in direct recovery or in a Recovery Certificate against the promoter. Once an order is passed and not honoured, the buyer applies for a Recovery Certificate, which is then sent to the District Magistrate for execution as if it were an arrear of land revenue.

In practice this execution step is where delay creeps back in. Buyers have had to approach the Allahabad High Court to get a District Magistrate to act on a Recovery Certificate already issued by UP RERA; in one reported case the High Court directed the Noida administration to ensure the certificate was honoured within a set period. A buyer should treat the Recovery Certificate as the start of execution, not the end, and track its status on the UP RERA portal rather than assume it will enforce itself.

Filing Under Section 7 of the IBC When the Builder Is Clearly Insolvent

Homebuyers count as financial creditors under the Code because the explanation to the definition of financial debt treats amounts raised from allottees under a real estate project as such. This lets a group of buyers file an application under Section 7 before the National Company Law Tribunal to put the developer into the corporate insolvency resolution process. The catch is a threshold added by the 2019 ordinance and carried into the 2020 amendment: an application by allottees must be filed jointly by not fewer than one hundred allottees of the project, or ten percent of the total allottees, whichever is lower, and the Supreme Court has held that this count runs by unit or allotment rather than by individual buyer.

This route suits a large, clearly stalled project where many buyers can coordinate, not a lone buyer with one flat. It also carries a genuine risk for a buyer who already holds a RERA Recovery Certificate: appellate rulings have gone both ways on whether a decree holder or recovery certificate holder can still be treated as a financial creditor for this purpose, so a buyer in that position should get specific advice on their own facts before relying on this route rather than assuming the two remedies simply stack.

The Authority Dues Deadlock and What the Supreme Court Has Actually Ordered

The Supreme Court has intervened directly in some of the largest stalled projects in this belt, and its directions are worth knowing even though they bind specific projects rather than laying down a rule for every stalled site. In the Amrapali matter, decided in February 2023, the Court held that the Noida and Greater Noida Authorities could not sell homebuyers' flats or the underlying leased land to recover their dues from the developer; instead the leases granted to the Amrapali companies were cancelled and the project rights were transferred to a Court-appointed receiver, with the Authorities directed to execute tripartite agreements and arrange completion certificates and electricity connections for buyers already living in completed blocks.

In the Supertech matter, the Court in 2026 upheld an NCLAT order directing the public sector entity NBCC to complete a set of stalled Supertech projects, invoking Article 142 to craft relief that an ordinary insolvency resolution could not have delivered on its own. In a separate dispute over the Lotus 300 project in Sector 107, the Court's 2024 order required the Noida Authority to proceed with registry permissions even while the Authority separately pursued the developer for unpaid dues, rather than holding buyers hostage to that recovery. None of this means every stalled Noida project will get a Court-ordered rescue; these are outcomes reached after years of litigation in specific, large, politically visible projects, and a buyer in a smaller project should not assume the same intervention is coming.

The Consumer Commission as a Parallel, Not a Substitute

A buyer can also sue the developer before the District, State or National Consumer Commission for deficiency in service, seeking a refund with interest or compensation for delay, instead of or alongside a RERA complaint. The Consumer Commission route tends to suit a buyer who wants damages and is prepared to litigate against an active developer; it is less useful against a developer who has gone silent, has no assets left outside the stalled project, or is already before the NCLT, because an order for money against an empty company changes little. Buyers often use RERA for possession-linked relief and the Consumer Commission for compensation once the possession question is settled, rather than treating the two as alternative paths to the same end.

Which commission to approach, District, State or National, depends on the value of the claim including the compensation sought, and the forum changes periodically as the pecuniary limits are revised, so a buyer should check the current threshold before filing. A consumer complaint also carries its own limitation period running from when the cause of action arose, usually tied to the date the builder was contractually bound to hand over the flat, so a buyer who has already waited years should not assume there is no urgency left in choosing this route.

What a Buyer Stuck in Such a Project Should Actually Check First

Before choosing a forum, a buyer should establish three facts: whether the project is still RERA-registered and what its extended completion date is, whether the builder or the project is already the subject of an NCLT insolvency filing by a bank or other creditor, and what the Authority's own records show about outstanding lease dues on that specific project, which can usually be sought through an RTI application to the Authority. Buyers in the same project do better to coordinate informally, through a residents' or allottees' association, even if they do not intend to file a joint IBC application immediately, because UP RERA's own data shows a meaningful share of recoveries have come through settlements the Authority facilitated between promoters and groups of complainants rather than through contested orders alone.

A buyer should also keep original payment receipts, the builder-buyer agreement, and correspondence with the Authority or the builder in one organised file, since every forum described above, whether RERA, the Consumer Commission, the NCLT or a writ court, asks for the same basic documents to establish payment history and the builder's default. Gathering this early saves real time, particularly if several buyers in the same project decide to pool their evidence before approaching a forum together.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get my registry done if I have paid the builder in full but the builder owes the Authority money?

Not automatically. Noida and Greater Noida Authorities have in practice withheld registry across an entire project until the builder's dues are cleared or restructured, regardless of an individual buyer's own payment status, unless a court or the Authority itself directs otherwise for that project.

Should I file with UP RERA or go straight to the Consumer Commission?

Start with UP RERA if you still want possession, completion or a Recovery Certificate against the promoter; use the Consumer Commission mainly for a compensation claim, particularly once it is clear possession will not happen or you have decided to exit the project.

Can I file an IBC case against the builder on my own?

No. A real estate allottee's Section 7 application must be filed jointly with at least one hundred allottees of the project or ten percent of the total allottees, whichever is fewer, counted by unit rather than by person, so you need other buyers to join you.

Does a Supreme Court order in another Noida project help my own stalled project?

Only indirectly. Orders in the Amrapali, Supertech or Lotus 300 matters bind those specific projects; they can be cited as persuasive precedent on how courts have treated Authority dues and registry, but they do not automatically extend relief to an unrelated stalled project.

What should I check before deciding how to proceed?

Confirm the project's current RERA registration and extended timeline, check whether any bank or creditor has already filed an NCLT case against the builder, and seek the Authority's own dues record for that project through an RTI request before choosing a forum.
