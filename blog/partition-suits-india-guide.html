How Partition Suits Work in India: A Practical Guide for Co-Owners

A practical guide for a co-owner of jointly or ancestrally held property considering partition, covering who can file, how shares are determined, the difference between partition by metes and bounds and by sale, and what to expect procedurally.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 September 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

What a Partition Suit Actually Does

A partition suit is a civil proceeding through which a co-owner of jointly held property seeks to convert an undivided share into a separately identifiable, exclusively held portion. It does not create ownership rights that do not already exist: it assumes co-ownership is established and unresolved only as to how the property should be physically or financially divided among the co-owners. This distinguishes it from a title suit, which is used where ownership itself, rather than the division of an agreed ownership, is in dispute.

Partition suits most commonly arise in relation to ancestral or inherited property, where multiple legal heirs hold undivided shares by operation of succession law, but they can equally arise between co-purchasers, business partners who jointly acquired property, or any other group holding property in common.

Who Can File, and Against Whom

Any co-owner or co-sharer with a defined or undivided share in the property may file a partition suit, provided that share itself is not disputed by the other parties: if it is, the more fundamental question of entitlement typically has to be resolved first, often within the same suit, before partition can proceed. Every other person holding a share in the property should generally be joined as a party, either as a co-plaintiff or as a defendant, so that the eventual decree binds all co-owners and achieves a final, effective division rather than one that a left-out co-owner could later reopen.

Where the property has passed through succession, it is important to correctly identify all legal heirs entitled to a share: an incomplete array of parties is one of the most common reasons a partition decree is later challenged or found ineffective against a person who was never joined.

Partition by Metes and Bounds Versus Partition by Sale

Where a property is capable of physical division without materially reducing its overall value or usability, a large plot of agricultural land, for instance, a court will typically order partition by metes and bounds, physically demarcating separate portions corresponding to each co-owner's share, often with the assistance of a court-appointed commissioner or surveyor.

Where physical division is not reasonably practicable, a single residential flat, for example, cannot sensibly be split into several self-contained units, the court may instead order partition by sale, directing that the property be sold (through a court-supervised process, or by permitting co-owners the first right to buy out the others' shares) and the proceeds divided among the co-owners according to their respective shares. Which route applies depends heavily on the nature of the specific property, and is often a contested question in its own right.

What the Process Involves

A partition suit is generally instituted in the civil court having jurisdiction over the location of the property, and proceeds broadly as any other civil suit: filing of the plaint identifying the property, the shares claimed, and the relief sought; written statements from the other co-owners, who may dispute the shares claimed or raise other defences; framing of issues; trial, including evidence on title, shares and possession; and finally a preliminary decree determining the shares of each party, followed (where physical division is ordered) by a final decree effecting the actual division, often carried out with the assistance of a court commissioner.

Where one co-owner has been in exclusive possession of the property, or has collected rent or income from it, the suit frequently also involves a claim for accounting: requiring that co-owner to account for the income or benefit derived from the property, to be adjusted against the eventual division of shares.

Common Complications

Partition suits often become contested not over the basic right to partition, but over ancillary questions: the exact extent of each party's share (particularly where some heirs have received advances or gifts that may need to be accounted for), whether improvements made by one co-owner in possession should be compensated, and whether a family settlement or earlier informal arrangement should be treated as binding in place of a fresh judicial partition. Co-sharers who have relocated abroad can also complicate the timeline through service requirements, though this is a procedural, not a substantive, bar to relief.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who can file a partition suit?

Any co-owner or co-sharer with a defined or undivided share, including a legal heir who has inherited a share, provided their right to a share is not itself disputed.

What is the difference between partition by metes and bounds and partition by sale?

Partition by metes and bounds physically divides the property matching each co-owner's share. Partition by sale directs sale of the property and division of proceeds where physical division is not reasonably possible.

Can a co-owner in possession of a larger portion resist partition?

They cannot resist a properly framed partition suit on that basis alone, though the value of any improvements made, or their existing occupation, may be relevant to the final division or accounting.

Does a partition suit require every co-owner to be a party?

Yes: every person with a share generally must be joined as a party so the final decree binds all co-owners and effectively resolves the division.
