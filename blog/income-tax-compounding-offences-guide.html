Facing Prosecution for a TDS or Tax Default? How Compounding of Offences Under Section 279(2) Works Now

What changed in CBDT's October 2024 compounding guidelines, which income tax offences can be compounded, and how a taxpayer actually applies.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A company director who discovers that a delayed TDS deposit or a return not filed on time has turned into a criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act is usually more alarmed than the underlying default warrants, because most of these defaults can still be resolved without a trial through the compounding mechanism under Section 279(2). What has changed, materially, is that the CBDT rewrote the rules governing this process in October 2024, replacing a structure that had grown complicated over four successive sets of guidelines issued between 2008 and 2022.

For a taxpayer or a tax professional who last looked closely at compounding before that revision, several of the old assumptions, about which offences qualify, how many times an application can be made, and what it costs in interest, no longer hold in the same form.

What Compounding Actually Means Here

Section 279(2) of the Income Tax Act allows an offence under Chapter XXII of the Act, which covers the various prosecution provisions such as those for TDS defaults, failure to file returns, and false statements, to be compounded either before or after prosecution proceedings have been launched. Compounding is not a right. The provision leaves the decision to the discretion of the Principal Chief Commissioner, Chief Commissioner, Principal Director General or Director General, as the case may be, and that discretion has historically been structured and constrained by guidelines the CBDT issues from time to time.

In practical terms, compounding lets a taxpayer pay a prescribed compounding charge, calculated according to the guidelines then in force, in exchange for the prosecution being dropped or not pursued, without the matter needing to go to trial. It is generally the faster and more predictable route compared to litigating the prosecution, particularly for a business that wants to put the matter behind it rather than spend years in criminal court over what is often, commercially, a cash flow or compliance lapse rather than a case of deliberate evasion.

Why the October 2024 Guidelines Mattered

The CBDT issued revised guidelines on compounding of offences under direct tax laws, effective from 17 October 2024, expressly superseding the four previous sets of guidelines dated 2008, 2014, 2019 and 2022. The stated reason, following an announcement in the July 2024 budget, was to simplify a process that had become, over successive rounds of amendment, difficult for taxpayers and tax officers alike to apply consistently.

One structural change stands out above the others. The earlier guidelines split offences into separate categories for the purpose of deciding eligibility and charges, broadly distinguishing more technical defaults from more serious ones. The 2024 guidelines eliminate that categorisation entirely, moving to a more unified framework for deciding whether an offence can be compounded, rather than sorting applications first by which category the alleged offence fell into.

Other Practical Changes a Taxpayer Should Know About

The revised guidelines removed the cap that had previously limited how many compounding applications a taxpayer could file, and they also removed the earlier time limit, reported as a thirty-six month window measured from the date of the complaint, within which an application had to be filed. Both changes matter for a taxpayer who missed an earlier filing window under the old rules or whose first application was rejected on a technical defect, since a corrected, fresh application is now more clearly contemplated under the revised framework rather than being time-barred outright.

They also now expressly permit compounding of offences under Sections 275A and 276B, which deal respectively with contravention of certain search and seizure related orders and with failure to deposit tax deducted at source, expanding the universe of offences available for compounding compared to the narrower earlier position on at least some of these provisions. Commentary following the revision also describes a removal of interest charged on delayed payment of the compounding charge itself, which, if the amount in question is large, can be a meaningful saving compared to the earlier regime.

What Happens to Applications Already Pending When the Rules Changed

The 2024 guidelines apply to pending applications as well as new ones, which is a point worth flagging for anyone who filed before 17 October 2024 and assumed their application would be decided purely under the older rules. Where a taxpayer has already paid a compounding charge, or where the charge determined under the earlier guidelines was actually lower than what the new guidelines would produce, the CBDT's position is that there is no redetermination, meaning the taxpayer is not asked to pay more simply because the rules changed midway. This is a reasonably taxpayer-favourable transition rule, and it is worth checking carefully if an older application is still working its way through the system.

A taxpayer in this position should write to the jurisdictional authority asking for written confirmation of which guideline version is being applied to their pending file, rather than assuming the transition rule has been applied correctly as a matter of course. Tax officers handling a large backlog of older applications do not always flag this distinction proactively, and a taxpayer who later discovers a higher charge was applied in error has a straightforward basis to seek correction, provided the point is raised while the application is still pending rather than after an order has already been passed.

How an Application Actually Gets Made

An application for compounding is made to the jurisdictional authority empowered under the guidelines, typically the Principal Chief Commissioner or Chief Commissioner for the region where the case is being handled, setting out the offence, the circumstances of the default, and the request for compounding. The authority examines the application against the current guidelines, including whatever eligibility conditions and exclusions the 2024 revision sets out, and decides whether to accept it and, if so, on what compounding charge.

Because this remains a discretionary process rather than an automatic entitlement, the quality of the application itself matters. A clear, honest account of why the default occurred, prompt compliance once the default was discovered, and a clean record on cooperation with the department all tend to support an application, whereas a history of repeated similar defaults or evidence suggesting deliberate concealment makes compounding considerably harder to secure, whatever the current guidelines say about which offences are technically eligible.

Why 'Not a Matter of Right' Still Matters Even Under the Simplified Rules

Earlier guidelines were explicit that compounding is not a matter of right, and that the authority's discretion takes into account the taxpayer's conduct and the nature and magnitude of the offence, not simply whether the offence falls on a list of compoundable provisions. Nothing in the 2024 simplification removes this underlying discretion, it only changes how the process around exercising that discretion is structured. A taxpayer should not read the removal of the old category system, or the relaxation of the filing-window and application-count limits, as meaning compounding has become automatic. It remains an application for the authority's favourable exercise of discretion, just through a less rigid procedural structure than before.

This is worth stressing because the simplified rules can give an impression of entitlement that the underlying provision never actually grants. A taxpayer advising a client, or a client instructing counsel, should treat the application as an exercise in persuasion rather than a formality to be completed and ticked off, gathering the strongest possible account of the circumstances and the steps taken to remedy the default before submitting it, rather than relying on the relaxed procedural conditions alone to carry the application through.

Frequently Asked Questions

We missed the earlier deadline to apply for compounding under the old guidelines. Can we still apply now?

The 2024 guidelines removed the previous time limit for filing a compounding application, reported as a thirty-six month window from the date of the complaint. This generally opens the door to applications that would have been time-barred under the earlier rules, though the application is still subject to the authority's discretion on the merits.

We already paid a compounding charge under the old 2022 guidelines before the October 2024 revision. Will we now owe more?

No. The CBDT's position is that the revised guidelines do not require redetermination where payment has already been made, or where the charge fixed under the earlier guidelines was lower than what the new guidelines would otherwise produce.

Can a TDS default under Section 276B actually be compounded?

Yes, the revised guidelines expressly permit compounding of offences under Section 276B, which covers failure to deposit tax deducted at source, as well as Section 275A. Confirm the current position and any specific conditions with the jurisdictional authority before applying, since eligibility conditions can still vary by the facts of the case.

Is there still a limit on how many times we can apply for compounding?

The 2024 guidelines removed the earlier limit on the number of applications a taxpayer could file. This does not guarantee a later application will succeed on the merits, but it removes a procedural bar that previously existed under the earlier guidelines.

Does compounding mean the department accepts we did nothing wrong?

No. Compounding resolves the prosecution without a trial but does not amount to an acquittal or a finding that no default occurred. The guidelines themselves describe compounding as discretionary relief from prosecution, not a vindication of the taxpayer's conduct, and clarifications on non-admission of guilt are among the points the revised guidelines address.
