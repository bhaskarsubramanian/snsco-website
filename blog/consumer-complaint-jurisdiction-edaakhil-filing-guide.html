Which Consumer Commission Should You File In, and How Does e-Daakhil Filing Work?

Pecuniary and territorial jurisdiction under the Consumer Protection Act 2019, the 2021 Rules' revised limits, the two-year limit, and e-Daakhil filing.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 28 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A consumer complaint goes to the District Commission, the State Commission or the National Commission depending on the value of the goods or services paid for, not the amount of compensation being claimed, and the current pecuniary bands were revised downward from the figures printed in the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 itself by a set of 2021 Rules that most people reading only the Act's bare text will miss. Getting the commission wrong means the complaint can be returned for refiling, which costs time the two-year limitation period does not forgive.

This is relevant wherever a buyer in Delhi, Noida, Kolkata or anywhere else in India has a dispute with a builder, an e-commerce seller, an insurer, a bank or a service provider and is deciding where and how to file. Most commissions across the country now accept filing through the e-Daakhil portal, so a complainant does not need to physically visit the commission's registry to start the case.

Pecuniary Jurisdiction: Value of Consideration, Not Compensation Claimed

Sections 34, 47 and 58 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 fix pecuniary jurisdiction by reference to the value of the goods or services for which consideration was paid, a different measure from the compensation a complainant might go on to claim for defect, deficiency or loss. A buyer who paid Rs. 40 lakh for a flat and claims Rs. 15 lakh in compensation for delay and defects is assessed against the Rs. 40 lakh consideration figure for jurisdiction purposes, not the Rs. 15 lakh claimed.

The Supreme Court upheld this consideration-based basis for fixing jurisdiction on 29 April 2025, dismissing a constitutional challenge to Sections 34(1), 47(1)(a)(i) and 58(1)(a)(i) of the Act, so the basic structure is settled law rather than an open question. What a complainant still has to get right is which of the three bands the consideration figure actually falls into, since those bands were changed after the Act was passed.

The Current Bands Under the 2021 Rules

The Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction of the District Commission, the State Commission and the National Commission) Rules, 2021, notified on 30 December 2021 under Section 101 of the Act, revised the pecuniary bands that had been printed in the original 2019 Act. Under the 2021 Rules, the District Commission has jurisdiction where the value of goods or services paid as consideration does not exceed Rs. 50 lakh, the State Commission where it exceeds Rs. 50 lakh but does not exceed Rs. 2 crore, and the National Commission where it exceeds Rs. 2 crore.

This is lower at the District Commission level than the figure of Rs. 1 crore that appears in the bare text of Section 34(1) of the 2019 Act, which is why checking only the Act's printed section number without checking whether a later rule has changed the figure is a common and avoidable mistake. The government's stated reason for the 2021 revision was that the original bands were pushing too many cases that properly belonged at the District level up to the State Commission, adding to the backlog there. A complainant or an advocate checking an older article, a template complaint drafted before 2021, or a bare reading of the Act without checking the Rules is the most common way this mistake happens, and it is worth re-checking the current figure even where a prior matter with a similar value was correctly filed before the Rules changed.

Territorial Jurisdiction: Where You Can File

Section 34(2) of the Act gives a complainant more than one place to file. A complaint against a seller or service provider can be filed where the opposite party, or one of several opposite parties, actually or voluntarily resides, carries on business, or has a branch office, or where the opposite party personally works for gain. It can also be filed where the cause of action, wholly or in part, arose, which for a builder-buyer dispute is often the location of the project itself.

The 2019 Act added a further option that the 1986 Act did not have: a complaint can also be filed in the District Commission within whose local limits the complainant themselves resides or personally works for gain, which matters directly to a buyer who purchased a property or service in one city but has since moved to or always lived in another. This consumer-friendly addition means a Delhi resident who bought a flat in a different state can often still file the complaint before a Delhi Commission, rather than having to travel to where the builder or the project is located. A clause in the purchase agreement that tries to name a different, exclusive forum does not override this statutory choice; parties cannot by private agreement take away a commission's jurisdiction where the Act otherwise gives it.

The Two-Year Limitation Period

Section 69(1) of the Act bars a commission from admitting a complaint unless it is filed within two years from the date the cause of action arose. For a continuing wrong, such as a builder's ongoing failure to hand over possession, identifying the correct date the cause of action arose is itself often disputed, and getting this wrong by filing too late without addressing it is one of the more common reasons a complaint is dismissed at the threshold stage.

Section 69(2) allows a commission to entertain a complaint filed after the two-year window, but only where the commission records its reasons for condoning the delay, and this is not automatic merely because a complainant asks for it. In practice a complainant filing close to or after the two-year mark should file a separate application explaining the delay alongside the complaint itself, with whatever documentary explanation is available for why the complaint could not have been filed earlier, rather than leaving the point to be addressed only if the opposite party raises it.

Filing Online Through e-Daakhil

e-Daakhil is the online portal launched by the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission on 7 September 2020 under the Consumer Protection (Consumer Dispute Redressal Commissions) Rules, 2020, letting a complainant or their advocate file a complaint electronically with the District, State or National Commission, along with the requisite fee, rather than filing physically at the registry. A complainant first registers on the portal using an OTP-based login, then files the complaint with scanned copies of the supporting documents, and the portal generates a tracking reference for the filing.

Coverage has expanded since the portal's 2020 launch, and reporting through 2024 describes the Department of Consumer Affairs as having rolled out e-Daakhil across all states and union territories, though complainants should still confirm that the specific commission they intend to file before is actually live on the portal rather than assuming nationwide coverage applies uniformly. A newer, integrated portal called e-Jagriti, which brings together e-Daakhil with the National Commission's earlier case-monitoring systems, has also been referenced in recent reporting, so a complainant filing today should check which of the two portals the relevant commission is currently using before starting the filing.

Practical Points Before You File

Before filing, work out the value of consideration paid, not the compensation being claimed, and check that figure against the 2021 Rules' bands rather than the Act's original section numbers, since filing in the wrong commission because of this gap is avoidable with a few minutes of checking. Decide on the territorial basis for filing as well, since a complainant now typically has a choice between the place of business of the opposite party, the place the cause of action arose, and the complainant's own place of residence or work, and the most convenient of these is usually the complainant's own location given the 2019 Act's added ground.

Keep the purchase documents, correspondence with the seller or service provider, and any expert or valuation evidence organised and scanned before starting the e-Daakhil filing, since the portal expects these to be uploaded as part of the filing itself rather than submitted later in physical form. Where the two-year period is close to expiring or has already passed, prepare the delay-condonation application with a clear explanation at the same time as the main complaint, rather than filing the complaint alone and hoping the point is not raised.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is pecuniary jurisdiction decided for a consumer complaint?

By the value of the goods or services for which consideration was paid, not the compensation claimed. Under the Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction) Rules, 2021, the District Commission hears cases up to Rs. 50 lakh, the State Commission from above Rs. 50 lakh up to Rs. 2 crore, and the National Commission above Rs. 2 crore.

Can I file a consumer complaint in my home city even if the seller is elsewhere?

Often yes. Section 34(2) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 lets a complainant file in the District Commission within whose limits they themselves reside or personally work for gain, in addition to where the opposite party carries on business or where the cause of action arose, and a contract clause naming a different exclusive forum does not override this.

What is the time limit for filing a consumer complaint?

Two years from the date the cause of action arose, under Section 69(1) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. A commission can admit a complaint filed later under Section 69(2), but only if it records its reasons for condoning the delay, so a complainant filing late should submit a separate condonation application explaining the delay.

What is e-Daakhil and who can use it?

e-Daakhil is the online portal the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission launched in September 2020 for filing consumer complaints electronically with the District, State or National Commission. A complainant or their advocate registers with an OTP-based login, uploads the complaint and supporting documents, and pays the fee online, without needing to file physically at the registry.

Are the pecuniary limits in the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 still current?

No, not as printed in the Act's original sections. The Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction) Rules, 2021 revised the bands downward, most notably lowering the District Commission's upper limit from the Act's original Rs. 1 crore figure to Rs. 50 lakh, so the 2021 Rules, not the Act's bare text, give the current figures.
