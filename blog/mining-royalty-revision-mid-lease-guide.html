Can the State Raise Mining Royalty Mid-Lease Even If Your Lease Deed Says Nothing About It?

The Supreme Court has confirmed states can revise royalty and dead rent during a running mining lease. What this means for lessees budgeting lease costs.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 15 October 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Most mining lease deeds in India are silent on what happens to the royalty rate once the lease is running. A lessee who negotiated hard on the rate at the time of grant tends to read that silence as a kind of protection, on the theory that if the deed does not say the rate can go up, it cannot go up. A string of High Court rulings over the years gave that reading some support. The Supreme Court has now closed the question, at least for the type of lease most commonly affected, in State of Haryana and Others v. M/s Faridabad Gurgaon Minerals and Another, decided on 13 July 2026.

The ruling matters to anyone holding, bidding for, or financing a mining lease for minor minerals such as road metal, masonry stone, slate, sand or morrum, because it settles that the rate fixed at the time of grant is not fixed for the life of the lease, deed or no deed.

Why Lessees Assume the Rate Is Locked In

The assumption is understandable. A mining lease looks and reads like a contract. It has a term, a leased area, a set of conditions the lessee has to meet, and a schedule of payments. Parties to an ordinary contract expect that what is not written is not owed, so a lease deed that mentions a royalty figure but says nothing about revising it reads, on its face, as fixing that figure for the term.

The error in that reading is treating a mining lease as purely a creature of contract. It is granted under a statutory licensing framework, and the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 builds the power to revise royalty into the framework itself, not into the individual lease document. In Faridabad Gurgaon Minerals, the lessees had been granted seven-year leases for minor minerals in 2001 and 2002, and the Haryana government issued a notification in June 2005 raising both royalty and dead rent by fifty per cent partway through the term. The Punjab and Haryana High Court had earlier accepted the lessees' argument that the deed's silence barred the increase and quashed the notification. The Supreme Court disagreed, and the reasoning it gave applies well beyond Haryana's facts.

Minor Minerals: Why Section 15 and the State Rules Control

For minor minerals, the relevant provision is Section 15 of the MMDR Act, not any term the parties wrote into the deed. Section 15(1) lets a state government make its own rules governing minor mineral leases, and Section 15(3) requires the lessee to pay royalty or dead rent at the rate prescribed from time to time in those rules, subject to a proviso that the state cannot enhance the rate of royalty or dead rent for a minor mineral more than once in any period of three years. In Haryana's case the governing rules are the Punjab Minor Mineral Concession Rules, 1964, as adopted and continued in Haryana after the state's formation, and the Court anchored the power specifically in Rule 21 for royalty and Rule 10(2) for dead rent, the latter allowing dead rent to be enhanced by up to fifty per cent once the lease has run three years.

The Court's point was that these rules are not something a lessee can contract out of by silence. They operate as a statutory incident attached to every minor mineral lease granted under the framework, in the same way a tenancy created under a rent control statute carries statutory terms whether or not the tenancy agreement repeats them. A lease deed that says nothing about revision has not frozen the rate; it has simply left the question to the rules that were already governing the grant before the deed was signed. The Court also noted that the auction notice and letter of acceptance under which these particular leases were granted had told bidders in advance that the lease would remain governed by the statutory rules, which made it harder for the lessees to argue they had bargained for a fixed rate.

Major Minerals: The Parallel Rule Under Section 9

Major minerals sit under a separate but structurally similar provision. Section 9 of the MMDR Act fixes royalty by reference to the Second Schedule, which the Central Government can revise under Section 9(3), subject to its own cap of twenty per cent of the sale price of the mineral at the pit's head and a bar on enhancing the rate more than once in four years. Section 9(1) applies to pre-existing leases notwithstanding anything in the lease instrument, so a lessee holding an older major mineral lease cannot point to the lease's vintage as a shield either.

The mechanics differ by mineral category and by which level of government holds the revision power, state rules for minor minerals, the Central Government's Second Schedule for major minerals, but the underlying principle is the same for both. A lessee negotiating a major mineral lease should treat the twenty per cent and four-year figures as the outer limit on any single revision, not as a promise that revision will happen only that often or only up to that ceiling every time; the Central Government still decides whether and when to exercise the power within those limits.

What the Supreme Court Actually Decided

The bench of Justices Dipankar Datta and Satish Chandra Sharma allowed the state's appeals in Faridabad Gurgaon Minerals, along with the connected matter involving Ganpati Enterprises Slate Mines, and set aside the High Court's judgments quashing the 2005 notification. The Court held that a lessee does not acquire a vested right to a static royalty rate for the full term of the lease merely because the deed is silent on revision, and that the power to revise flows from the statute and the rules framed under it, which continue to bind the lease regardless of what the deed does or does not say.

The Court was equally clear that this is not an unlimited power. Section 15(3)'s proviso restricts how often a minor mineral rate can move, once in three years, and the state rules themselves set the ceiling on how much it can move in a single revision. A notification that breaches either constraint, by revising twice inside three years or by exceeding the enhancement cap the rules allow, would be vulnerable on a different footing than the argument the lessees actually ran and lost in this case.

Royalty and Dead Rent Are Not the Same Payment

A lessee reading this judgment for the first time can get confused between royalty and dead rent, since both get revised together in practice and both appear in the same notifications. Dead rent is a fixed minimum sum payable for the leased area regardless of whether any mineral is actually extracted in a given period, while royalty tracks the quantity of mineral actually raised and removed. Mining statutes generally require the lessee to pay whichever of the two is higher for any given period, under Section 9A for major minerals and the equivalent state rule provisions for minor minerals, so that a lessee cannot avoid payment simply by not working the lease in a slow period.

This dual structure is why a government revising rates mid-lease usually touches both figures in the same notification, and why a lessee checking its exposure after a revision notice needs to work out both numbers and pay the higher one, not assume the lower, more familiar royalty figure automatically controls. It is also worth flagging that how a mining lease is stamped for registration purposes, generally by reference to anticipated royalty yield over the term rather than dead rent, is a related but separate question from the one this judgment answers, and should not be confused with it when budgeting for a new lease.

What This Means for Budgeting a Lease Going Forward

The practical consequence for anyone taking a fresh minor mineral lease, or holding one already, is that the figure quoted at the time of grant should be treated as a starting point, not a ceiling for the term. A seven-year lease, the term at issue in the Haryana case, can reasonably be expected to see at least one, and possibly two, rate revisions over its life, since the three-year gap the proviso requires fits comfortably within that span. Financial models, project cost estimates and lender covenants built around mining leases should price in that risk rather than treating the grant-date royalty as fixed input data.

A lessee who thinks a particular revision notification has gone further than the rules allow, by compressing the three-year gap or exceeding whatever enhancement ceiling the applicable state rules fix, still has a live argument, but it has to be framed as a breach of the specific statutory and rule-based limits on the power, not as a claim that the deed's silence bars revision altogether. That second argument is the one Faridabad Gurgaon Minerals has now closed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Our mining lease deed does not mention royalty revision at all. Does that protect us from a rate increase during the term?

No. The Supreme Court held in State of Haryana v. Faridabad Gurgaon Minerals that a lease deed's silence on revision does not stop the state from revising royalty and dead rent, because the power comes from the MMDR Act and the rules framed under it, not from the deed itself.

How often can the government actually raise royalty on a minor mineral lease?

Section 15(3) of the MMDR Act bars enhancing the royalty or dead rent rate for a minor mineral more than once in any three-year period. A notification that revises the rate more frequently than that, or exceeds the enhancement ceiling fixed in the applicable state rules, is open to challenge on that specific ground.

Is the rule the same for major minerals like iron ore or limestone?

The mechanism differs. Major mineral royalty is fixed by the Second Schedule to the MMDR Act, revisable by the Central Government under Section 9(3), subject to a cap of twenty per cent of the sale price at the pit's head and a bar on revising more than once in four years. The underlying principle that the statute, not the lease deed, controls revision is the same.

We pay dead rent even in months we extract no mineral. Can we ask to pay royalty instead since it would be lower?

No. Mining lease provisions requiring payment of whichever of royalty or dead rent is higher exist specifically to prevent that outcome. A lessee has to pay the higher figure for the relevant period regardless of which one it would prefer.

A revision notification increased our dead rent by more than we think the rules allow. What is the actual remedy?

The remedy is a writ petition challenging that specific notification as exceeding the statutory or rule-based limits on the state's revision power, supported by the applicable rule's own enhancement cap and the record of when the lease's last revision took place. A general objection that the deed was silent on revision will not succeed after this ruling.
