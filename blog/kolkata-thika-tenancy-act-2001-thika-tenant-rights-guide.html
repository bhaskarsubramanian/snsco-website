Rights of a Thika Tenant in Kolkata and When a Thika Tenancy Can Be Ended

Who counts as a thika tenant in Kolkata, what the 2001 Act vests in the State, the Controller's powers, eviction limits and the 1981 Act's repeal.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 12 November 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

A thika tenancy is a particular kind of landholding found mainly in and around Kolkata, where a tenant takes land on lease and puts up their own structure on it, usually a hut or a modest building, rather than renting a structure already built by the landlord. Because the tenant owns the building but the land itself belonged to a private landlord, this arrangement produced decades of disputes over rent, eviction and, eventually, over who the tenant should even be dealing with once the State stepped into the landlord's position. The law governing it today is the West Bengal Thika Tenancy (Acquisition and Regulation) Act, 2001, which extends to Kolkata and replaced an earlier 1981 statute that had itself replaced older legislation going back to 1949.

This guide sets out who qualifies as a thika tenant under the current Act, what it means for the land to vest in the State, what the Thika Controller can and cannot decide, and how far a thika tenant is protected against eviction. Readers dealing with ordinary tenancy rather than a thika holding should see our guide on Evicting a Tenant in Kolkata Under the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, since the two regimes are different and a tenancy is one or the other, not both.

Who is a thika tenant

The 2001 Act defines a thika tenant by reference to who holds land under a tenancy and has built, or is entitled to build, a structure on it for residential, business or manufacturing purposes, with the holding typically originating as a lease from a private landlord for that purpose. The defining feature is the separation between ownership of the land, originally with the landlord, and ownership of the structure standing on it, which belongs to the tenant, a split that does not arise under an ordinary tenancy where the landlord owns both the land and whatever stands on it.

Not every long-term occupant of land in Kolkata is a thika tenant merely because they call themselves one, and disputes over whether a particular holding actually satisfies the statutory definition, including whether the structure on it is of the kind the Act contemplates, are common. The Supreme Court has in reported cases gone through the statutory conditions in detail before accepting or rejecting a claim to thika tenant status, which shows that qualifying is a matter of meeting specific conditions in the definition, not simply a matter of how long someone has been on the land.

Vesting: dealing with the State, not the original landlord

The core mechanism of the 2001 Act, carried forward from its 1981 predecessor, is that land comprised in thika tenancies is deemed to have vested in the State, free from the encumbrances that previously attached to it, with effect from the date the thika tenancies were created, which in practical terms displaces the original private landlord's ownership of the land itself. This does not disturb the tenant's own structure, which remains the tenant's property, but it does mean the tenant's landlord relationship going forward runs through the machinery the Act sets up, rather than through whatever family or entity originally granted the lease.

For a tenant or a landlord trying to work out where they stand today, this vesting is the starting point: a landlord cannot simply continue collecting ground rent and dealing with the tenant as though the 2001 Act had not intervened, and a tenant relying on an old lease document alone, without reference to how the Act has treated that holding, is working from an incomplete picture of their own position.

The Thika Controller: registration and the limits of its power

The Act sets up a Thika Controller with authority over registration of thika tenancies and related determinations, including deciding, as a threshold question, whether a particular occupant actually qualifies as a thika tenant under the statutory definition. This power was widened by the West Bengal Thika Tenancy (Acquisition and Regulation) (Amendment) Act, 2010, which substituted Section 5(3) with effect from 1 November 2010: before that amendment the Controller could decide only whether a claimant was a thika tenant, not whether the land itself was thika land, and had no provision to adjudicate a dispute between an owner and an alleged thika tenant over that status; the 2010 amendment gave the Controller power to decide both. Reported cases describe occupants submitting returns before the Controller claiming thika tenant status, which the Controller then examines and either accepts or rejects, and that determination carries real consequences, since the protections and procedures the Act provides only apply to someone who is in fact a thika tenant within its meaning.

What the Controller still cannot do, even after the 2010 widening of Section 5(3), is adjudicate a dispute over who actually owns the underlying property as between rival claimants to title, since that remains a question for the ordinary civil court; this is distinct from the Controller's power, since 2010, to decide whether land is thika land and to resolve a dispute between an owner and an alleged thika tenant over thika tenant status itself. A party litigating a genuine ownership dispute should not expect the Controller's forum to resolve it, and should bring that question before the civil court while using the Controller's process only for what it is actually meant to decide, namely thika tenant status and registration.

Protection against eviction

A recognised thika tenant has statutory protection against being removed from the land at the landlord's discretion, reflecting the Act's broader purpose of securing settled occupation for tenants who have themselves invested in building a structure on leased land, rather than leaving them exposed to the kind of summary eviction that might apply to an ordinary short-term tenancy. The protections operate through the procedures and grounds the Act itself sets out, administered through the Controller and the appellate structure under the Act, rather than through an ordinary civil suit for eviction of the kind a landlord would bring against a tenant under general tenancy law.

Separately, the Supreme Court has in a reported decision upheld a High Court ruling that a thika tenant cannot have a pucca, meaning permanent masonry, structure on the land, based on the statutory definitions and the kind of structure the Act contemplates a thika tenancy being used for. A tenant who has gone beyond what the statutory definition of a thika tenancy permits, by constructing a permanent structure where the Act does not allow one, risks the tenancy itself being treated as falling outside the Act's protection rather than within it.

Transfer, succession and subletting

A thika tenancy, like other statutorily protected tenancies, is subject to its own rules on whether and how it can be transferred, inherited by the tenant's family, or sublet to someone else, and these rules sit within the 2001 Act's own scheme rather than following the general law that would apply to an ordinary leasehold interest. A family member continuing to occupy and use a structure after the recognised thika tenant's death is a common scenario, and the Act's succession provisions, together with the Controller's registration records, are what determine whether that continued occupation is itself protected as a thika tenancy or needs to be separately established.

Subletting by a thika tenant, where it occurs, raises its own questions about whether the subtenant acquires any protection under the Act or remains exposed to the thika tenant's own rights and obligations toward the State, and this is an area where the specific facts, including what the Controller's records show and what the original registration covered, matter more than any general statement of principle.

What the 2001 Act's repeal of the 1981 Act changed, and what carried over

Section 27 of the 2001 Act repeals the earlier 1981 statute, formally the Calcutta Thika and Other Tenancies and Lands (Acquisition and Regulation) Act, 1981, from the commencement of the new Act, but the 2001 Act is best understood as continuing the same underlying policy, State acquisition and regulation of thika tenancy land, with refined definitions and procedure, rather than as a break from the earlier scheme. The vesting mechanism under the 2001 Act closely mirrors the equivalent provision in the 1981 Act, so a tenancy that vested in the State under the earlier Act generally continues on the same footing under the new one.

The 2001 Act's own repeal and savings provision addresses what happens to proceedings and rights that existed under the 1981 Act at the point of repeal, and the Supreme Court has in reported litigation that straddled the two statutes treated the transition as one of continuity rather than requiring parties to restart proceedings from scratch under the new Act. A tenant or landlord with an unresolved matter that began under the 1981 Act should check, with reference to the specific saving provision rather than assuming either outcome, whether that matter survives under the 2001 Act or needed to be freshly brought under it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my thika tenancy be inherited by my children after my death?

The 2001 Act provides for succession to a thika tenancy within its own scheme, and a family member who continues the occupation after the tenant's death can generally continue the tenancy, but whether that continuation is itself recognised depends on the Controller's registration records and the Act's specific succession provisions, not on occupation alone.

Can the Thika Controller decide who actually owns the land, as between two rival claimants?

No. Since a 2010 amendment to Section 5(3), the Controller can decide whether land is thika land and resolve a dispute between an owner and an alleged thika tenant over tenant status, but a High Court has held its jurisdiction does not extend to adjudicating who actually owns the underlying property as between rival claimants to title. That remains a matter for the ordinary civil court.

Can a thika tenant be evicted for putting up a permanent, pucca structure on the land?

The Supreme Court has upheld a ruling that a thika tenant cannot hold a pucca structure under the statutory scheme, and building one beyond what the Act contemplates risks the holding falling outside the Act's protection, which can expose the tenant to removal rather than protect them.

What happened to cases that were still pending when the 1981 Act was repealed and replaced by the 2001 Act?

The 2001 Act's repeal and savings provision addresses pending matters, and reported Supreme Court litigation spanning both statutes has treated the transition as continuous rather than requiring a fresh start. The specific saving clause should be checked for any particular pending matter rather than assuming the outcome.

Does the original private landlord still collect rent from a thika tenant today?

Generally no in the way they once did, since the land itself is deemed to have vested in the State free of the landlord's earlier encumbrance, and the tenant's ongoing relationship runs through the statutory machinery the Act sets up rather than through the original landlord as before.
