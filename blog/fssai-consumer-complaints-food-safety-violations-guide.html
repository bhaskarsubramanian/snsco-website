Consumer Complaints for Food Safety Violations: How the FSSAI and Consumer Protection Routes Differ

A guide explaining how a consumer's complaint about unsafe or adulterated food can proceed under the FSS Act's own enforcement machinery, distinct from a parallel Consumer Protection Act complaint.

By S&S Co. Advocates & Solicitors · Published 19 August 2026 · Informational content, not legal advice: see our disclaimer

Two Distinct, Overlapping Routes

A consumer who receives adulterated, contaminated, or otherwise unsafe food has, broadly, two distinct legal routes available, which can in some circumstances be pursued in parallel: a complaint to the food safety enforcement machinery under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, triggering inspection, sampling and potentially prosecution or adjudication against the food business operator; and a complaint before a Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, seeking compensation for the deficiency in the specific transaction.

What an FSS Act Complaint Achieves

A complaint routed to the Food Safety Officer or Designated Officer for the relevant jurisdiction can trigger a formal inspection and sampling of the food business's stock, and, depending on what the resulting analysis shows, can lead to prosecution under Section 59 for adulterated or unsafe food, or penalty proceedings under Section 68 before an Adjudicating Officer for other contraventions. This route is oriented toward regulatory enforcement against the business generally. It does not, by itself, award the individual complainant compensation.

What a Consumer Protection Act Complaint Achieves

A parallel or separate complaint before a Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission is oriented toward the individual consumer's own loss: compensation for injury, cost of medical treatment, or, in an appropriate case, an order restraining unfair trade practice. And does not depend on securing a conviction or adjudication finding under the FSS Act first, though evidence developed in an FSS Act proceeding (such as a laboratory analysis report) can often be relevant supporting material in the consumer complaint as well.

Practical Sequencing for a Consumer

Where a consumer has suffered actual injury or loss from unsafe food, promptly preserving the product, packaging, and any receipt, and reporting the incident to the local Food Safety Officer so that a sample can be drawn and tested while stock is still available, strengthens both the regulatory complaint and any later consumer compensation claim: testing delayed until after the product is exhausted or discarded significantly weakens the evidentiary basis for either route.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a consumer pursue both an FSS Act complaint and a Consumer Protection Act complaint over the same incident?

Yes, the two routes serve different purposes: regulatory enforcement against the business under the FSS Act, and individual compensation under the Consumer Protection Act. And can generally be pursued in parallel.

Does a consumer need a conviction under the FSS Act before filing a consumer complaint for compensation?

No, a Consumer Protection Act complaint for compensation does not depend on first securing a conviction or adjudication finding under the FSS Act, though supporting evidence like a laboratory report can strengthen either proceeding.

What should a consumer preserve if they suspect food they purchased is unsafe or adulterated?

The product itself where possible, its packaging, and the purchase receipt, and the incident should be reported promptly to the local Food Safety Officer so a sample can be tested while stock is still available.

Who investigates a food safety complaint made to FSSAI's enforcement machinery?

The Food Safety Officer or Designated Officer for the relevant jurisdiction, who can draw samples for testing and initiate prosecution or adjudication proceedings depending on the outcome.
